Units, Dimensions, and Measurement

How physics turns the world into numbers you can trust: the SI and its defining constants, the algebra of dimensions, and the honest bookkeeping of error and uncertainty.

Technical article with worked examples, exercises, trivia, code and diagrams·References in Vancouver style

Abstract. Every physical statement that involves a number is really a statement about a ratio between a quantity and a chosen reference, the unit. This article develops that idea from the ground up. It covers the 2019 International System of Units (SI), which now rests on seven exactly fixed constants of nature; the distinction between base and derived quantities; dimensional analysis and the Buckingham Π theorem; reliable unit conversion; significant figures and rounding; accuracy, precision, and the vocabulary of metrology; random and systematic errors; the propagation of uncertainty (analytic and Monte Carlo); Fermi-style order-of-magnitude estimation; the CODATA physical constants; and natural and Planck units. Each section closes with worked examples, and the article ends with graded exercises, trivia questions, and full solutions.

1What a measurement is

The International Vocabulary of Metrology (VIM) defines a quantity as a "property of a phenomenon, body, or substance, where the property has a magnitude that can be expressed as a number and a reference"1. The reference is usually a measurement unit, a real scalar quantity "defined and adopted by convention, with which any other quantity of the same kind can be compared to express the ratio of the two quantities as a number"1. Writing a quantity value is therefore a product:

Q = {Q} · [Q](1.1)

Here {Q} is the numerical value and [Q] is the unit. This notation, called quantity calculus, is standard in the SI Brochure and in ISO 80000-12,3. The quantity itself does not change when you change units. Only the split between number and unit changes. A rod of length L = 2.5 m is also L = 250 cm. The number grew by a factor of 100 because the unit shrank by a factor of 100.

A measurement is the "process of experimentally obtaining one or more quantity values that can reasonably be attributed to a quantity"1. The word "reasonably" carries a lot of weight. No measurement yields the exact value of a quantity, so a complete result must state a best estimate together with an uncertainty, which describes the spread of values that could reasonably be attributed to the measurand4. Sections 6 to 9 of this article are about doing that honestly.

Core idea

A complete measurement result has three parts: a number, a unit, and an uncertainty. For example, g = 9.801 m s−2 with standard uncertainty 0.053 m s−2, written compactly as g = 9.801(53) m s−2. Leaving out any one of the three makes the statement incomplete4,5.

2The International System of Units (SI)

2.1 A short history

The modern system began with the Metre Convention, a treaty signed in Paris on 20 May 1875 by seventeen states. It created the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) at Sèvres, near Paris, together with the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) that governs it6. The 1st CGPM (1889) sanctioned the international prototype of the metre and the international prototype of the kilogram (IPK), a cylinder of platinum–iridium alloy2. The name "Système international d'unités", abbreviated SI, was adopted by the 11th CGPM in 19602.

Over the twentieth century, artefact definitions were replaced one at a time by definitions tied to nature. The second was tied to a caesium hyperfine transition in 1967, and the metre was tied to a fixed speed of light in 19832. The kilogram held out longest. Comparisons between the IPK and its official copies showed relative drifts of order 5 × 10−8 over a century, which is a real problem for a unit that is supposed to be invariant7.

Milestone · the 2019 revision

The 26th CGPM (November 2018) adopted a revised SI in which all seven base units are defined by fixing the numerical values of seven defining constants. The revision took effect on 20 May 2019, World Metrology Day8,7. Since then, no SI unit depends on a physical artefact. The kilogram is now realized from the Planck constant, for example with a Kibble balance, which compares mechanical power with electrical power measured through quantum electrical standards9.

2.4 SI prefixes

Decimal multiples and submultiples are formed with prefixes. The 27th CGPM (2022) added four new prefixes, ronna (1027), quetta (1030), ronto (10−27) and quecto (10−30), which brings the total to 2410. A prefix attaches to the unit to form a single new symbol, so km2 means (km)2 = 106 m2, not k(m2). Compound prefixes such as "μμF" are not allowed2,11.

2.5 Writing quantities correctly

The SI Brochure and NIST Special Publication 811 lay down a short list of style rules that remove ambiguity2,11:

  • Unit symbols are upright (roman) and quantity symbols are italic: m = 3 kg, never m = 3 kg.
  • A space separates the number from the unit: 25 °C and 10 kg. The only exceptions are the plane-angle symbols °, ′ and ″.
  • Symbols are not pluralized and take no period: 5 kg, not "5 kgs." or "5 Kg".
  • Symbols for units named after people start with a capital letter (N, Pa, W). The unit names themselves are lowercase (newton, pascal, watt). The exception is "degree Celsius".
  • Products are written with a space or a half-high dot (N m or N·m), and quotients with a solidus or a negative exponent (m/s or m s−1). Use at most one solidus: write J/(kg K), not J/kg/K.
  • Digits may be grouped in threes with thin spaces, and either a point or a comma may serve as the decimal marker2.

3Fundamental and derived quantities

The International System of Quantities (ISQ), on which the SI is built, chooses seven base quantities that are treated as mutually independent by convention: length, mass, time, electric current, thermodynamic temperature, amount of substance and luminous intensity3. Every other quantity is a derived quantity, defined through equations of physics in terms of the base quantities. The choice of base is a matter of convenience, not of nature. Gaussian CGS units, for instance, use only three base dimensions (length, mass, time) and make electric charge a derived quantity, so Coulomb's law has no 4πε012.

3.1 Dimensions

The dimension of a quantity Q expresses how it depends on the base quantities. It is written as a product of powers3,2:

dim Q = Lα Mβ Tγ Iδ Θε Nζ Jη(3.1)

The exponents are the dimensional exponents. Force follows from Newton's second law, F = ma, so dim F = M L T−2. Energy follows from work, W = Fd, so dim E = M L2 T−2. A quantity for which every exponent is zero is dimensionless, or more precisely a quantity of dimension one. Examples are refractive index, strain, the Reynolds number and the fine-structure constant2.

Subtlety

Same dimension does not mean same kind of quantity. Torque (N m) and energy (J = N m) share the dimension M L2 T−2, but they are different physical quantities. The SI asks that torque be written in N m and not in joules2. In the same way, the hertz (frequency, s−1) and the becquerel (activity, s−1) are kept separate. Plane angle (radian, m/m) and solid angle (steradian, m2/m2) are dimensionless but have special names so that the reader can tell what is being counted.

4Dimensional analysis

Dimensional analysis exploits one rule: a physically meaningful equation must be dimensionally homogeneous. Every term that is added, subtracted or equated must have the same dimension. The arguments of transcendental functions such as exp, ln, sin and cos must be dimensionless13,14. This rule is a cheap and powerful tool for three tasks: checking equations, guessing the form of physical laws, and organizing experiments.

4.1 Checking equations

Worked example 4.1

Is v2 = u2 + 2as dimensionally consistent?

[v2] = (L T−1)2 = L2 T−2. [u2] = L2 T−2. [2as] = (L T−2)(L) = L2 T−2. All three terms match, so the equation is homogeneous. Note that dimensional analysis cannot detect the factor 2. A wrong pure number passes the test unnoticed. Consistency is necessary for correctness but not sufficient.

4.2 Deriving relations: the Rayleigh method

Suppose a quantity depends on a few others as a power law. Writing down the dimensions and matching exponents often fixes the law up to a numerical constant.

Worked example 4.2 · the pendulum

Assume the period is T = C la mb gc, with C dimensionless. Matching dimensions gives T1 = La+c Mb T−2c.

From M: b = 0. From T: −2c = 1, so c = −½. From L: a + c = 0, so a = ½. Therefore

T = C √l/g(4.1)

The period does not depend on the mass. Solving the equation of motion for small swings gives C = 2π. In general C is a function of the dimensionless amplitude θ0, and dimensional analysis alone cannot find it.

4.3 The Buckingham Π theorem

Edgar Buckingham stated the general result in 191415. If a physical relation involves n variables whose dimensions are built from k independent base dimensions, the relation can be rewritten as a relation among n − k independent dimensionless products Π1, …, Πn−k:

f(q1, …, qn) = 0  ⟺  F(Π1, …, Πn−k) = 0(4.2)

More precisely, k is the rank of the dimension matrix, whose columns are the exponent vectors of the variables. The Π groups span its null space. This is why dimensionless numbers such as the Reynolds number Re = ρvL/μ organize whole fields: two flows with the same Re are physically similar. That principle lets engineers test scale models in wind tunnels and towing tanks14.

Famous example · G. I. Taylor and the first atomic explosion

In 1950 G. I. Taylor published an estimate of the energy of the 1945 Trinity test using little more than dimensional reasoning and a sequence of declassified photographs of the expanding fireball16. If a very strong blast wave of radius R at time t depends only on the released energy E and the air density ρ, the only dimensionally consistent combination is

R = C (E t2 / ρ)1/5  ⇒  E ≈ ρ R5t2(4.3)

The constant C is close to 1 for air. With illustrative values R ≈ 140 m at t ≈ 25 ms and ρ ≈ 1.2 kg m−3, this gives E ≈ 1.0 × 1014 J, or about 25 kilotons of TNT (1 kt = 4.184 × 1012 J). Taylor's published figure was close to the then-classified yield16,14.

4.4 Code: dimension-aware arithmetic and the Π theorem

The short Python program below stores a quantity's dimension as a vector of seven exponents. It refuses to add incompatible quantities and finds the Π groups as the rational null space of the dimension matrix. It uses only the standard library.

from fractions import Fraction

BASE = ("M", "L", "T", "I", "Θ", "N", "J")   # SI base dimensions

class Q:
    """A number carrying its dimension as a tuple of 7 exponents."""
    def __init__(self, value, dim):
        self.value, self.dim = value, tuple(Fraction(d) for d in dim)
    def __mul__(self, o):
        o = o if isinstance(o, Q) else Q(o, (0,)*7)
        return Q(self.value*o.value, [a+b for a, b in zip(self.dim, o.dim)])
    __rmul__ = __mul__
    def __truediv__(self, o):
        o = o if isinstance(o, Q) else Q(o, (0,)*7)
        return Q(self.value/o.value, [a-b for a, b in zip(self.dim, o.dim)])
    def __pow__(self, p):
        return Q(self.value**p, [a*Fraction(p) for a in self.dim])
    def __add__(self, o):
        if self.dim != o.dim:
            raise TypeError(f"cannot add {self.sig()} and {o.sig()}")
        return Q(self.value+o.value, self.dim)
    def sig(self):
        s = " ".join(f"{b}^{d}" for b, d in zip(BASE, self.dim) if d)
        return s or "dimensionless"
    def __repr__(self):
        return f"{self.value:.6g} [{self.sig()}]"

m  = Q(1, (0,1,0,0,0,0,0)); kg = Q(1, (1,0,0,0,0,0,0)); s = Q(1, (0,0,1,0,0,0,0))
N  = kg*m/s**2
J  = N*m

v = 3.0*m/s; mass = 2.0*kg
print("kinetic energy:", 0.5*mass*v**2)     # 9 [M^1 L^2 T^-2]
print("same as joule?", (0.5*mass*v**2).dim == J.dim)
try:
    mass + v
except TypeError as err:
    print("caught:", err)

# ---- Buckingham pi: null space of the dimension matrix ----
def nullspace(A):
    """Rational null space of matrix A (list of rows) by Gauss-Jordan."""
    A = [[Fraction(x) for x in row] for row in A]
    rows, cols = len(A), len(A[0])
    pivots, r = [], 0
    for c in range(cols):
        p = next((i for i in range(r, rows) if A[i][c] != 0), None)
        if p is None:
            continue
        A[r], A[p] = A[p], A[r]
        A[r] = [x / A[r][c] for x in A[r]]
        for i in range(rows):
            if i != r and A[i][c] != 0:
                A[i] = [a - A[i][c]*b for a, b in zip(A[i], A[r])]
        pivots.append(c); r += 1
    free = [c for c in range(cols) if c not in pivots]
    basis = []
    for f in free:
        vec = [Fraction(0)]*cols; vec[f] = Fraction(1)
        for i, pc in enumerate(pivots):
            vec[pc] = -A[i][f]
        basis.append(vec)
    return basis

# Pendulum: variables period T, length l, mass m, gravity g, amplitude θ0
names = ["T", "l", "m", "g", "θ0"]
#         T  l  m  g  θ0
D = [[0, 0, 1, 0, 0],    # M
     [0, 1, 0, 1, 0],    # L
     [1, 0, 0,-2, 0]]    # T
for vec in nullspace(D):
    print("Π =", " · ".join(f"{n}^{e}" for n, e in zip(names, vec) if e))
kinetic energy: 9 [M^1 L^2 T^-2] same as joule? True caught: cannot add M^1 and L^1 T^-1 Π = T^2 · l^-1 · g^1 Π = θ0^1

The two Π groups, gT2/l and θ0, give gT2/l = F(θ0), which is equation (4.1) with C = √F.

4.5 Limits of dimensional analysis

  • It cannot determine dimensionless constants (2π, ½, C in Taylor's formula) or functions of dimensionless groups.
  • It fails if a relevant variable is left out, and it gives spurious freedom if an irrelevant one is put in. Physical insight decides the variable list13.
  • It cannot tell apart quantities that share a dimension, such as torque and energy.
  • Results depend on the system of base dimensions. A formula that is homogeneous in SI may look different in Gaussian units because charge has different dimensions there12.

5Unit conversions

Since Q = {Q}[Q] is invariant, converting units is just multiplying by 1 written in a clever form. Because 1 in = 2.54 cm exactly, the ratio (2.54 cm)/(1 in) equals 1, and multiplying by it changes the units without changing the quantity. Chaining such ratios, and cancelling units like algebraic symbols, is called the factor-label method.

Worked example 5.1 · highway speed

Convert 60 mi/h to m/s.

60 mih × 1609.344 m1 mi × 1 h3600 s = 26.8224 ms

Both conversion factors are exact, so the result is as precise as the original "60". If that number was itself a rounded value, report about 27 m/s.

Worked example 5.2 · powers of units

Convert a density of 1.00 g cm−3 to kg m−3. The factor for cm must be cubed:

1.00 gcm3 × 1 kg103 g × (102 cm1 m)3 = 1.00 × 103 kgm3

5.2 Temperature: an affine conversion

Temperature scales differ by an offset as well as a factor, so you cannot convert them by multiplication alone. By definition t/°C = T/K − 273.152. The Fahrenheit relation is tF/°F = (9/5)(t/°C) + 3211. A temperature difference has no offset: a change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C, which equals a change of 1.8 °F. Mixing up a temperature with a temperature interval is a classic source of error.

When conversions fail

Mars Climate Orbiter (1999). The spacecraft was lost on arrival at Mars on 23 September 1999. The investigation board found the root cause was that ground software produced small-thruster impulse data in English units (pound-force seconds), while the navigation software expected metric units (newton seconds). The resulting trajectory errors, a factor of about 4.45, accumulated over the cruise and brought the orbiter far too low into the Martian atmosphere19.

The "Gimli Glider" (1983). On 23 July 1983 an Air Canada Boeing 767 ran out of fuel in flight. The fuel load had been calculated with a density in pounds per litre where kilograms per litre were needed, so the aircraft carried less than half the fuel required. The crew glided to a safe landing at a former air base at Gimli, Manitoba20.

6Significant figures

Significant figures are a convention for showing roughly how well a number is known, without writing out an explicit uncertainty. They are a shorthand and a rough one. The GUM and NIST recommend stating an explicit uncertainty whenever it matters4,5. The convention is still universal in teaching and in everyday technical work, so it pays to apply it consistently21.

6.1 Counting rules

  1. All nonzero digits are significant: 4.56 has three.
  2. Zeros between nonzero digits are significant: 1002 has four.
  3. Leading zeros are not significant. They only place the decimal point: 0.004 56 has three.
  4. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant: 2.500 has four.
  5. Trailing zeros in an integer with no decimal point are ambiguous. "1500" could have two, three or four. Scientific notation removes the doubt: 1.5 × 103 (two) or 1.500 × 103 (four).
  6. Exact numbers, such as counts, defined conversion factors and the defining constants of the SI, have unlimited significant figures.

6.2 Arithmetic rules of thumb

  • Multiplication and division: keep as many significant figures as the factor with the fewest. Example: 3.24 × 1.2 = 3.888, reported as 3.9.
  • Addition and subtraction: keep as many decimal places as the term with the fewest. Example: 12.11 + 0.3 + 1.234 = 13.644, reported as 13.6.
  • Carry one or two extra guard digits through intermediate steps and round only the final answer. Rounding at every step lets errors build up.
  • Subtracting nearly equal numbers destroys significant figures (catastrophic cancellation): 1.0003 − 1.0001 = 0.0002 has only one significant figure.

6.3 Rounding and reporting with uncertainty

NIST recommends rounding so that the final uncertainty has one or two significant digits, and then rounding the value to the same decimal place5. When the digit dropped is exactly 5 followed by nothing, the "round half to even" rule avoids a systematic upward bias11. Under that rule 2.25 becomes 2.2 and 2.35 becomes 2.4.

import math
from decimal import Decimal, ROUND_HALF_EVEN

def round_sig(x, n):
    """Round x to n significant figures (ties to even, as NIST SP 811 recommends)."""
    if x == 0:
        return 0.0
    exp = math.floor(math.log10(abs(x)))
    q = Decimal(1).scaleb(exp - n + 1)
    return float(Decimal(repr(x)).quantize(q, rounding=ROUND_HALF_EVEN))

def report(value, unc, unc_digits=2):
    """Format 'value(uncertainty)' with the uncertainty to unc_digits figures."""
    exp = math.floor(math.log10(abs(unc))) - unc_digits + 1
    v = round(value, -exp); uu = round(unc / 10**exp)
    decimals = max(0, -exp)
    return f"{v:.{decimals}f}({uu})"

print(round_sig(299792458, 3))        # 300000000.0
print(round_sig(0.00456789, 2))       # 0.0046
print(round_sig(2.25, 2), round_sig(2.35, 2))   # 2.2 2.4  (half to even)
print(report(9.80087819, 0.0526207))  # 9.801(53)
300000000.0 0.0046 2.2 2.4 9.801(53)

7Measurement precision and accuracy

In everyday speech "accurate" and "precise" are synonyms. In metrology they are not. The VIM defines them as follows1:

  • Measurement accuracy is the closeness of agreement between a measured quantity value and a true quantity value of the measurand. It is a qualitative concept and is not given a number.
  • Measurement trueness is the closeness of agreement between the average of an infinite number of replicate measured values and a reference value. Poor trueness signals systematic error, or bias.
  • Measurement precision is the closeness of agreement between measured values obtained by replicate measurements under specified conditions. Poor precision signals random error. Precision is usually expressed as a standard deviation.

Precision depends on the conditions. Repeatability refers to the same procedure, operator, instrument and location over a short time. Reproducibility refers to different locations, operators or instruments1. Neither should be confused with resolution, the smallest change in a quantity that causes a perceptible change in the indication. A digital scale that reads to 0.01 g can still be off by a gram.

8Random and systematic errors

The measurement error is the measured value minus a reference value1. It has two components:

  • Random error varies unpredictably between replicate measurements. Examples are electronic noise, reaction-time scatter in hand timing and small fluctuations in the environment. It averages down when you repeat the measurement.
  • Systematic error stays constant, or varies predictably, across replicates. Examples are a miscalibrated thermometer, a zero offset, parallax always from the same side and an uncorrected thermal expansion. Averaging does nothing to it. Once it is identified, it should be corrected, and the uncertainty of the correction should be included4.

8.1 Statistics of repeated measurements

For N independent readings x1, …, xN, the best estimate is the mean, and the scatter is described by the experimental standard deviation4,21:

x̄ = 1N ∑ xi,    s = √∑ (xi − x̄)2N − 1,    u(x̄) = s√N(8.1)

The quantity u(x̄) is the standard uncertainty of the mean, often called the standard error. To halve it you need four times as many readings. If the readings are normally distributed, about 68 % of them fall within ±s of the mean and about 95 % within ±2s. The GUM's expanded uncertainty U = k u with coverage factor k = 2 corresponds to roughly 95 % coverage under those conditions4.

8.2 Type A and Type B evaluations

The GUM sorts uncertainty components by how they are evaluated, not by where they come from4. Type A evaluations use statistics of repeated observations, as in equation (8.1). Type B evaluations use other information, such as calibration certificates, manufacturer specifications, resolution limits and earlier data. A common Type B case is a digital display with resolution δ. If the true value is equally likely anywhere within ±δ/2, the rectangular distribution gives u = δ/√124.

A systematic error that made headlines

In 2011 the OPERA experiment reported muon neutrinos from CERN arriving at Gran Sasso about 60 ns earlier than light would. That would have meant faster-than-light travel. Further checks traced the anomaly to faults in the timing chain, including a badly connected optical-fibre cable. After the corrections, the neutrino velocity was found to agree with the speed of light within uncertainty22. Statistical precision was never the issue. The problem was a hidden systematic error.

9Uncertainty propagation

Most results are computed from several measured inputs, y = f(x1, …, xn). To first order, small changes in the inputs map linearly onto the output (Figure 5). For uncorrelated inputs, the GUM's law of propagation of uncertainty gives the combined standard uncertainty4:

uc2(y) = ∑i (∂f∂xi)2 u2(xi)(9.1)

The partial derivatives are called sensitivity coefficients. If inputs are correlated, cross terms 2(∂f/∂xi)(∂f/∂xj)u(xi, xj) must be added, where u(xi, xj) is the covariance4.

9.1 Rules you will use every day

Worked example 9.1 · measuring g with a pendulum

A student measures l = 1.000 m with u(l) = 0.002 m, and T = 2.007 s with u(T) = 0.005 s. From g = 4π2l/T2:

So u(g) = 0.053 m s−2 and g = 9.801(53) m s−2, or 9.80 ± 0.05 m s−2. The period contributes more than twice as much as the length, because it enters squared. To improve the result, time 20 swings instead of one, which divides the timing uncertainty per period by about 20.

9.2 Code: analytic versus Monte Carlo propagation

GUM Supplement 1 describes how to propagate whole probability distributions by Monte Carlo sampling23. You draw many random input sets, evaluate the function for each, and take the spread of the outputs. This needs no derivatives and handles nonlinearity correctly. For the pendulum the two methods agree:

import math, random, statistics

def propagate(f, x, u, h=1e-6):
    """First-order (GUM) propagation for uncorrelated inputs.
    f: function of a list; x: best estimates; u: standard uncertainties."""
    y = f(x)
    var = 0.0
    for i in range(len(x)):
        dx = h * (abs(x[i]) or 1.0)
        xp = list(x); xp[i] += dx
        xm = list(x); xm[i] -= dx
        dfdx = (f(xp) - f(xm)) / (2*dx)          # sensitivity coefficient
        var += (dfdx * u[i])**2
    return y, math.sqrt(var)

def monte_carlo(f, x, u, n=200_000, seed=1):
    """GUM Supplement 1 style: sample inputs, look at the output spread."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    ys = [f([rng.gauss(xi, ui) for xi, ui in zip(x, u)]) for _ in range(n)]
    return statistics.fmean(ys), statistics.stdev(ys)

g = lambda p: 4*math.pi**2 * p[0] / p[1]**2      # g = 4π² L / T²
x, u = [1.000, 2.007], [0.002, 0.005]            # L in m, T in s
print("linear  : g = %.4f ± %.4f m/s²" % propagate(g, x, u))
print("monte-c : g = %.4f ± %.4f m/s²" % monte_carlo(g, x, u))

10Order-of-magnitude estimation

The order of magnitude of a quantity is, roughly, the power of ten nearest to it. Order-of-magnitude estimation, also known as a Fermi problem, means getting an answer within a factor of about ten using rough figures and simple reasoning. Enrico Fermi was famous for it. At the Trinity test he dropped scraps of paper as the blast wave passed and, from how far they were carried, estimated a yield of about 10 kilotons of TNT, within a factor of two or three of the true value24.

10.1 Method

  1. Break the unknown into a chain of factors you can estimate.
  2. Estimate each factor to within a factor of about 3, using round numbers and powers of ten.
  3. Multiply. If the factor errors are independent, they partly cancel, so the product is often better than any single guess. This is the same quadrature logic as Section 9, applied in log space25.
  4. Check the answer against a known benchmark or a second method.
Worked example 10.1 · heartbeats in a lifetime

Take about 70 beats per minute, 60 min/h, 24 h/day, 365 days/year and 80 years. Then 70 × 60 × 24 × 365 × 80 ≈ 3 × 109. The answer is about three billion heartbeats. A useful shortcut: one year ≈ π × 107 s, which is accurate to 0.5 %.

Worked example 10.2 · piano tuners in a large city

This is the classic Fermi question25. Suppose a city has 3 × 106 people in about 106 households, and 1 in 20 households has a piano, giving 5 × 104 pianos. Each piano is tuned about once a year, so the city needs 5 × 104 tunings per year. One tuner does about 4 tunings a day for 250 days, or 103 per year. The estimate is about 50 tuners: certainly more than 5 and fewer than 500.

11Physical constants

The fundamental physical constants are quantities that are believed to be universal and unchanging. Every four years the CODATA Task Group on Fundamental Constants carries out a least-squares adjustment of all relevant data and publishes a self-consistent set of recommended values26,27. Since 2019 the constants fall into three groups:

  • Exact by definition: the seven SI defining constants, plus anything computed only from them. Examples are ħ = h/2π, the molar gas constant R = NAk, the Faraday constant F = NAe, the Josephson constant KJ = 2e/h and the von Klitzing constant RK = h/e2.
  • Measured: for example the Newtonian constant of gravitation G, the electron mass and the fine-structure constant.
  • Formerly exact, now measured: the magnetic constant μ0 was exactly 4π × 10−7 N A−2 before 2019. It is now measured through μ0 = 2αh/(e2c), and still agrees with 4π × 10−7 to within about one part in 10102,26.

Relative standard uncertainty, ur = u/|y|, is the fair way to compare how well constants are known. The Rydberg constant is known to about 1 part in 1012 and α to about 1.5 parts in 1010. G, by contrast, is known only to about 2 parts in 105, which makes it by far the least precisely known of the commonly used constants26. Gravity is extraordinarily weak, and laboratory measurements of it are plagued by systematic effects.

Dimensionless constants

Numbers such as α ≈ 1/137.036 and mp/me ≈ 1836.15 have the same value in every system of units. They are the parameters that a fundamental theory would have to explain. Dimensional constants such as c or h, by contrast, have numerical values that are partly an accident of our choice of units28,29.

12Natural units and Planck units

The SI is designed for human-scale engineering. Theorists often prefer units in which certain constants equal 1, so that those constants disappear from equations and the remaining numbers carry physical meaning. Such systems are called natural units. Setting a dimensional constant to 1 is equivalent to declaring two base dimensions to be the same. Setting c = 1, for example, measures time in units of length28.

12.1 Particle-physics natural units (ħ = c = 1)

With ħ = c = 1, every quantity has a dimension that is a power of energy, usually expressed in electronvolts. Mass, energy and momentum are all measured in eV (or GeV), while length and time are measured in eV−1. To return to SI, reinsert ħ and c using the conversion constant30:

ħc = 197.326 980 4… MeV fm   (exact since 2019)(12.1)

So 1 GeV−1 of length is 1.973 × 10−16 m, and 1 GeV−1 of time is ħ/(1 GeV) = 6.582 × 10−25 s. Thermal physicists often also set k = 1, which makes 1 eV equivalent to about 11 604.5 K.

12.3 Planck units from dimensional analysis

In 1899, one year before his quantum hypothesis, Max Planck noticed that G, c and his new constant could be combined to form units of length, mass and time "independent of special bodies or substances", which would "necessarily retain their significance for all times and for all cultures, even extraterrestrial and non-human ones"34. The derivation is a direct application of Section 4. To find a length ℓ = ħa cb Gd, use dim ħ = M L2 T−1, dim c = L T−1 and dim G = M−1 L3 T−2. Solving the exponent equations gives a = ½, b = −3/2, d = ½.

All Planck-unit uncertainties come from G. Since lP ∝ G1/2, its relative uncertainty is half that of G, about 1.1 × 10−5, in line with the rules in Table 6. Physically, the Planck scale is where quantum effects on spacetime geometry are expected to become important. At that scale a particle's Compton wavelength becomes comparable to its Schwarzschild radius, and no established theory describes it28. The Planck mass is surprisingly ordinary, about 22 μg. It looks "large" only by comparison with elementary particles.

13Exercises with solutions

Difficulty: ● basic, ●● intermediate, ●●● challenging. Try each problem before opening its solution.

E1. Show that s = ut + ½at2 is dimensionally homogeneous. ●

Solution

[s] = L. [ut] = (L T−1)(T) = L. [at2] = (L T−2)(T2) = L. Every term has dimension L, so the equation is homogeneous. The factor ½ cannot be checked this way.

E2. Use Newton's law of gravitation, F = Gm1m2/r2, to find the dimension and SI unit of G. ●

Solution

G = Fr2/(m1m2), so dim G = (M L T−2)(L2)/M2 = M−1 L3 T−2. The unit is m3 kg−1 s−2, equivalently N m2 kg−2, which matches Table 7.

E3. The speed v of deep-water ocean waves depends only on the wavelength λ and on g. Find the form of v(λ, g). Then estimate v for λ = 100 m, given that the exact result has a factor 1/√2π. ●●

Solution

Write v = C λagb. Then L T−1 = La+b T−2b, so b = ½ and a = ½. Hence v = C√gλ. With C = 1/√2π: v = √9.81 × 100/(2π) ≈ 12.5 m/s, about 45 km/h. Long waves travel faster, which is why ocean swell arrives sorted by wavelength.

E4. A sign shows a speed limit of 65 mi/h. Express it in km/h and in m/s. ●

Solution

65 × 1.609 344 km/h = 104.607 36 km/h, about 105 km/h. Dividing by 3.6 gives 29.0576 m/s, about 29 m/s. Both factors are exact (Table 5), so the precision is set by the "65".

E5. Compute and round correctly: (a) 3.24 × 1.2; (b) 12.11 + 0.3 + 1.234; (c) the number of significant figures in 0.020 50. ●

Solution

(a) 3.888 → 3.9 (two significant figures, set by 1.2). (b) 13.644 → 13.6 (one decimal place, set by 0.3). (c) Four: 2, 0, 5 and the trailing 0. The leading zeros only place the decimal point.

E6. Five stopwatch readings of a pendulum period are 2.01, 1.98, 2.03, 2.00 and 1.99 s. Find the mean, the standard deviation and the standard uncertainty of the mean. ●●

Solution

Mean x̄ = 10.01/5 = 2.002 s. The deviations are 0.008, −0.022, 0.028, −0.002 and −0.012. Their squares sum to 1.48 × 10−3 s2. Then s = √1.48 × 10−3/4 = 0.019 s and u(x̄) = 0.019/√5 = 0.0086 s. Result: T = 2.002(9) s, or 2.002 ± 0.009 s. This covers only random error. Reaction-time bias in starting and stopping the watch is a separate systematic component.

E7. A rectangle measures (5.0 ± 0.1) cm by (3.0 ± 0.1) cm, with independent uncertainties. Find the area and its uncertainty. ●

Solution

A = 15.0 cm2. Relative uncertainty = √(0.1/5.0)2 + (0.1/3.0)2 = √0.0004 + 0.00111 = 0.039. So u(A) = 0.58 cm2, and A = 15.0 ± 0.6 cm2. Adding the relative uncertainties linearly would give 0.053, an overestimate.

E8. A cart has m = 2.00 ± 0.02 kg and v = 3.0 ± 0.1 m/s. Find its kinetic energy and uncertainty. Which input dominates? ●●

Solution

K = ½mv2 = 9.0 J. ur(K) = √(0.02/2.00)2 + (2 × 0.1/3.0)2 = √(0.010)2 + (0.067)2 = 0.067. So K = 9.0 ± 0.6 J. The velocity dominates by a factor of nearly 7, because it enters squared and its relative uncertainty is already larger.

E9. A digital thermometer reads in steps of 0.1 °C. Estimate the Type B standard uncertainty that comes from the resolution alone. ●●

Solution

Treat the true value as uniformly distributed over ±0.05 °C around the reading. The standard uncertainty is δ/√12 = 0.1/3.464 ≈ 0.029 °C4. Calibration error, which is often much larger, is a separate component.

E10. Estimate to an order of magnitude how many breaths a person takes in a lifetime. ●

Solution

Take about 15 breaths/min × 60 × 24 × 365 × 80 years ≈ 6 × 108, so of order 109. A cross-check against Example 10.1: breathing is about 5 times slower than the heart rate, and 3 × 109/5 ≈ 6 × 108.

E11. Using me from Table 7, compute the electron rest energy in MeV. ●●

Solution

E = mec2 = 9.109 383 7139 × 10−31 kg × (299 792 458 m/s)2 = 8.187 × 10−14 J. Dividing by 1.602 176 634 × 10−19 J/eV gives 0.510 999 MeV, the familiar "0.511 MeV".

E12. In natural units a particle-physics paper quotes a length of 5 GeV−1. Convert it to femtometres. ●●

Solution

Length = 5 × ħc/(1 GeV) = 5 × 0.197 327 GeV fm/GeV = 0.987 fm, roughly the size of a proton.

E13. Derive the Planck mass by dimensional analysis from ħ, c and G, and evaluate it. ●●●

Solution

Set m = ħacbGd. For M: a − d = 1. For L: 2a + b + 3d = 0. For T: −a − b − 2d = 0. Adding the L and T equations gives a + d = 0, so a = ½, d = −½ and b = ½. Thus mP = √ħc/G = √(1.0546 × 10−34 × 2.9979 × 108)/(6.6743 × 10−11) = 2.18 × 10−8 kg, matching Table 9.

E14. A thermometer always reads 0.8 °C high, and repeated readings of a stable bath scatter with s = 0.05 °C. Comment on its precision and its accuracy. What does averaging 100 readings achieve? ●●

Solution

It is precise (small scatter) but not accurate, because it has a 0.8 °C bias. This is panel 2 of Figure 3. Averaging 100 readings shrinks the random part to 0.005 °C but leaves the 0.8 °C bias untouched. The fix is calibration against a reference, followed by a correction of −0.8 °C that carries its own uncertainty.

E15. Using G. I. Taylor's scaling E ≈ ρR5/t2, how much does a 10 % error in measuring R change the energy estimate? ●●●

Solution

Because E ∝ R5, ur(E) ≈ 5 × ur(R) = 50 % to first order. The exact factor for a 10 % overestimate is 1.15 = 1.61, so the first-order rule understates the effect. Steep power laws amplify errors, and a log-log fit over many frames (Taylor's approach) is far more robust than a single measurement.

14Trivia questions with answers

T1. Which SI base unit was the last to be defined by a physical artefact, and when did that end?

Answer

The kilogram, defined by the International Prototype Kilogram from 1889 until 20 May 2019, when the definition based on the Planck constant came into force2,8.

T2. Why is World Metrology Day celebrated on 20 May?

Answer

The Metre Convention was signed on 20 May 18756. The revised SI was also deliberately brought into force on that date in 2019.

T3. Name the four newest SI prefixes and the year they were adopted.

Answer

Ronna (R, 1027), quetta (Q, 1030), ronto (r, 10−27) and quecto (q, 10−30), adopted by the 27th CGPM in 202210. The mass of the Earth is about 6 ronnagrams.

T4. Which SI base unit's definition does not involve the second at all?

Answer

The mole. It is defined solely by fixing NA, so one mole contains exactly 6.022 140 76 × 1023 specified entities2 (Figure 1).

T5. Why is an inch exactly 25.4 mm?

Answer

Under the 1959 international yard and pound agreement, the yard was defined as exactly 0.9144 m and the pound as exactly 0.453 592 37 kg. That makes 1 in = 0.9144 m/36 = 25.4 mm17,11.

T6. Which 1999 NASA mission was lost because one team used pound-force seconds and another used newton seconds?

Answer

Mars Climate Orbiter19.

T7. What is the approximate value of the inverse fine-structure constant, and why does its value not depend on units?

Answer

α−1 ≈ 137.03626. Since α = e2/(4πε0ħc) is dimensionless, every unit cancels.

T8. Did Planck propose his natural units before or after he introduced the quantum of action in his radiation law?

Answer

Before. The units appeared in an 1899 paper to the Prussian Academy34, a year before his December 1900 derivation of the black-body law.

T9. What turned out to cause the OPERA experiment's apparently faster-than-light neutrinos?

Answer

Systematic timing errors, most notably a poorly connected optical-fibre cable in the timing system22.

T10. How many SI coherent derived units have special names?

Answer

22, from the radian to the katal (Table 4)2.

T11. Which is the only SI unit whose full name contains a capital letter?

Answer

The degree Celsius. Names such as newton or kelvin are written in lowercase even though they honour people2,11.

T12. Which base unit is the only one whose name includes an SI prefix?

Answer

The kilogram. This is why prefixes for mass attach to "gram"2.

T13. Who estimated the Trinity yield from published photographs using dimensional analysis?

Answer

Sir Geoffrey Ingram (G. I.) Taylor, in two papers in Proceedings of the Royal Society A in 195016.

T14. Which of the commonly used fundamental constants is the least precisely known?

Answer

The Newtonian constant of gravitation, G, with a relative standard uncertainty of about 2.2 × 10−526.

T15. Before 2019, the magnetic constant μ0 was exactly 4π × 10−7 N A−2. Is it still exact?

Answer

No. Fixing e and h made μ0 a measured quantity tied to α. It still equals 4π × 10−7 to about 1 part in 10102,26.

15Key takeaways

  • A quantity is a number times a unit, and a measurement result is incomplete without an uncertainty.
  • Since 2019, the SI is built on seven exactly fixed constants (ΔνCs, c, h, e, k, NA, Kcd). No artefacts remain.
  • Seven base dimensions generate all derived quantities. Equations must be dimensionally homogeneous, and the Π theorem reduces n variables to n − k dimensionless groups.
  • Convert units by multiplying by exact ratios equal to one. Raise those ratios to the same power as the unit, and treat temperature offsets separately.
  • Significant figures are a rough proxy for uncertainty. Round the uncertainty to one or two digits and the value to match.
  • Precision (scatter) and trueness (bias) are different things. Random errors average down as 1/√N, and systematic errors do not.
  • Independent uncertainties add in quadrature. Relative uncertainties combine for products, weighted by the exponents. Monte Carlo handles strongly nonlinear cases.
  • Fermi estimates are reliable because independent errors in many factors partly cancel.
  • Natural units set chosen constants to 1. Planck units, built from ħ, c and G, mark the scale at which quantum gravity is expected to matter.
