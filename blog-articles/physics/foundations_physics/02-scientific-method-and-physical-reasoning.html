1What the scientific method is (and is not)

There is no single algorithm that every physicist follows step by step. What exists instead is a family of practices held together by one commitment: claims about nature must be answerable to observation, and they must be stated precisely enough that observation could show them to be wrong. Francis Bacon argued in 1620 for building knowledge up from careful, systematic observation rather than from authority [1]. Isaac Newton added four “rules of reasoning” to the second edition of the Principia, including the principle that we should admit no more causes than are true and sufficient to explain the phenomena, and that propositions gathered by induction should be held as accurate until other phenomena make them more accurate or liable to exceptions [2]. In the twentieth century Karl Popper put falsifiability at the centre of what makes a theory scientific [3], while Thomas Kuhn showed that real science alternates between long stretches of puzzle-solving inside an accepted framework (“normal science”) and rarer revolutions in which the framework itself is replaced [4].

Physics textbooks often present this as a cycle. The cycle is a useful map as long as you remember that real research enters it anywhere, loops back often, and runs several loops in parallel.

Common misconceptions
  • “Theories become laws once proved.” In physics a law is usually a compact empirical regularity (Hooke's law, Snell's law); a theory is an explanatory framework (electromagnetism, general relativity). Neither is ever proved in the mathematical sense.
  • “Science starts with a blank mind.” Every observation is made with instruments and concepts that already embody theory.
  • “One failed prediction kills a theory.” Logically it refutes the bundle of theory plus auxiliary assumptions, which is why the response to an anomaly is often a hunt for an error or a missing ingredient (§6).

Richard Feynman summarized the core in a single sentence: the test of all knowledge is experiment, which is the sole judge of scientific “truth” [5]. He also warned that the first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool [6]. Most of the techniques in this article are tools for keeping that promise.

2Observation and experimentation

2.1 Passive observation versus controlled experiment

An observation records what nature does without our intervention: the positions of planets, the spectrum of a star, the arrival times of cosmic rays. An experiment deliberately sets up conditions, changes one or more independent variables, holds others fixed (controlled variables), and records the response (dependent variables). Astronomy and cosmology rely mostly on observation; laboratory physics relies mostly on experiment; both need the same care about bias and uncertainty.

Definition: anatomy of a controlled experiment
  • Independent variable: the quantity you set (e.g. ramp angle).
  • Dependent variable: the quantity you measure (e.g. travel time).
  • Controlled variables: quantities kept fixed (ball, surface, release method).
  • Confounding variable: an uncontrolled factor that varies with the independent variable and could mimic or hide the effect (e.g. room temperature drifting during a run).
  • Control run / blank: a measurement with the effect “switched off” to reveal background.

2.2 Galileo's inclined plane

In Two New Sciences (1638) Galileo described rolling a bronze ball down a long, smooth groove and timing it with a water clock, repeating the trial “a full hundred times” and finding that the distances covered were always as the squares of the times [7]. The ramp “dilutes” gravity so that motion becomes slow enough to time. Historians long doubted that his apparatus could achieve the claimed precision, until Thomas Settle rebuilt it in 1961 and showed that it could [8], an early example of reproducing a historical result.

2.3 The value of a null result

Experiments that find nothing can be decisive. Michelson and Morley's 1887 interferometer was sensitive enough to detect the expected shift from Earth's motion through a hypothetical “luminiferous ether”, yet the observed shift was far smaller than predicted [9]. The null result became one of the empirical pillars of special relativity [10]. A well-designed null experiment states in advance how large an effect it could have seen, so that “nothing” becomes a quantitative upper limit.

2.4 Isolating a tiny effect: Cavendish

Henry Cavendish's 1798 torsion balance measured the gravitational attraction between lead spheres, a force tens of millions of times smaller than their weight. To avoid air currents and his own body heat, he enclosed the apparatus in a sealed room and read the deflection by telescope from outside [11]. His result for Earth's mean density, 5.48 times that of water, is within about 1% of the modern value. The lesson: much of experimental skill is the identification and suppression of disturbances.

Worked example: is the ramp effect real?

A student times a ball over 1.00 m of a ramp at 5° (five trials: 1.47, 1.45, 1.49, 1.46, 1.48 s) and at 10° (1.03, 1.06, 1.04, 1.05, 1.02 s). The means are 1.470 s and 1.040 s; the sample standard deviations are both 0.016 s, giving standard errors of 0.016/√5 ≈ 0.0071 s. The difference, 0.430 s, is about 43 combined standard errors, so the angle effect is unmistakable. Ideal frictionless sliding predicts t = √(2d/g sinθ) = 1.53 s and 1.08 s; a rolling solid sphere has a = (5/7)g sinθ, which predicts longer times (1.81 s and 1.28 s). The data are therefore inconsistent with both idealized models, which is exactly the signal to look for a systematic error (here, a starting gate that releases the ball with a small initial speed, or a timing offset).

Exercises

Exercise 2.1 Identify the independent, dependent and two controlled variables in an experiment measuring how the period of a mass-spring system depends on the mass.

Solution

Independent: the mass m. Dependent: the period T. Controlled: the spring (same spring constant k), the amplitude (kept small and similar), the method of timing (e.g. always time 20 oscillations), the mounting. A possible confounder is that the spring's own mass contributes, which shows up as a non-zero intercept in a plot of T2 against m.

Exercise 2.2 Show that if d = ½at2, the distances covered in successive unit time intervals are in the ratio 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 …

Solution

Distance in the nth interval is d(n) − d(n−1) = ½a[n2 − (n−1)2] = ½a(2n − 1). For n = 1, 2, 3, 4 the factor 2n − 1 is 1, 3, 5, 7.

Exercise 2.3 Why did Cavendish observe his balance from outside the room? Name two other disturbances a modern torsion-balance experiment must control.

Solution

To avoid convection currents and gravitational and thermal disturbances from his own body. Modern experiments must also control seismic vibration, temperature gradients that twist the fibre, magnetic and electrostatic forces on the test masses, tilt of the building, and anelasticity of the fibre.

3Hypothesis formulation

3.1 Three routes to a hypothesis

A hypothesis is a conjectured answer to a question, stated so that it has testable consequences. Philosophers distinguish three modes of reasoning that lead to (or from) one:

ModeFormPhysics exampleGuarantee
DeductionGeneral rule + case ⇒ resultNewton's laws + initial state ⇒ future orbitTruth-preserving: true premises give a true conclusion
InductionMany cases ⇒ general ruleEvery measured electron has the same charge ⇒ all electrons doAmpliative but fallible
Abduction (inference to the best explanation)Surprising fact + “if H, the fact would follow” ⇒ suspect HUranus's orbit is anomalous; an unseen planet would explain itGenerates candidates; needs testing

David Hume pointed out that no finite number of observations logically guarantees the next one, the “problem of induction” [12]. Charles Sanders Peirce named and analysed abduction as the logical step that introduces new ideas [13]. Popper's response to Hume was to recast science as a process of bold conjecture followed by attempted refutation, so that induction is not needed to justify a theory, only deduction to test it [3,14].

3.2 What makes a good hypothesis?

  1. Testable and falsifiable. It forbids some possible observations.
  2. Precise. It predicts a number (with uncertainty), not just a direction.
  3. Parsimonious. It introduces no more entities than needed (Newton's first rule [2]).
  4. Consistent with well-established results outside the domain being tested, or explicit about which ones it challenges.
  5. Fertile. It suggests new experiments beyond the one that motivated it.
Case study: Neptune and Vulcan

By the 1840s Uranus deviated from its predicted Newtonian orbit. Rather than abandon Newton, Urbain Le Verrier hypothesized an unseen outer planet and computed where it should be. Johann Galle found Neptune on 23 September 1846 within about a degree of the predicted position, on the first night of searching [15]. Le Verrier then tried the same move on Mercury's anomalous perihelion advance, postulating an inner planet, “Vulcan”. No such planet exists; the anomaly was eventually explained by general relativity [16]. The same reasoning pattern (abduction to an auxiliary hypothesis) succeeded once and failed once. Only testing could tell the cases apart.

Exercises

Exercise 3.1 Rewrite “Heavier objects fall differently” as a precise, falsifiable hypothesis.

Solution

One version: “In vacuum, the free-fall acceleration of a body near Earth's surface is independent of its mass, to within a fractional difference of 10−3, for masses between 1 g and 10 kg.” It names the conditions, the quantity, the claimed relation, the tolerance and the range of validity, so a single well-made measurement could refute it.

Exercise 3.2 Classify each as deduction, induction or abduction: (a) “All 40 samples of copper conducted electricity, so copper conducts.” (b) “The detector counts rose when the lab door opened; perhaps radon from the corridor is responsible.” (c) “Energy is conserved; this pendulum loses no energy to friction; so it returns to its starting height.”

Solution

(a) Induction. (b) Abduction: a candidate explanation for a surprising observation, to be tested (e.g. by ventilating the corridor). (c) Deduction.

Exercise 3.3 Why was the Vulcan hypothesis scientifically respectable even though it was wrong?

Solution

It was precise and falsifiable: it predicted a body of a certain mass at a certain range of orbits that should transit the Sun or be visible during eclipses. Repeated searches failed to find it, so the hypothesis was refuted. Being wrong in a testable way is what science requires; being unfalsifiable is the real failing.

4Mathematical modeling

4.1 Idealization

A physical model replaces the messy world with a simplified mathematical object: the point mass, the frictionless plane, the ideal gas, the rigid body. Idealization is not a flaw to apologise for; it is what makes the problem solvable and the key causal structure visible. George Box's dictum applies: since all models are wrong, the scientist must be alert to what is importantly wrong [17]. Eugene Wigner called the success of mathematics in describing nature “unreasonable” precisely because nothing guarantees it in advance [18].

4.2 A modeling workflow

  1. Identify the variables and the question.
  2. List assumptions explicitly (small angles, no air resistance, massless string).
  3. Choose governing principles (Newton's second law, conservation of energy).
  4. Formulate equations and boundary or initial conditions.
  5. Check dimensions and limits before solving.
  6. Solve analytically or numerically.
  7. Interpret and test against data; revisit assumptions if they fail.

4.3 Dimensional analysis and the Buckingham Π theorem

Every valid physical equation must be dimensionally homogeneous. The Buckingham Π theorem formalizes the consequence: if a relation involves n variables built from k independent base dimensions, it can be rewritten as a relation among n − k dimensionless groups [19].

Worked example: pendulum period without solving anything

Suppose the period T of a pendulum might depend on length L [m], mass m [kg], gravitational acceleration g [m s−2] and amplitude θ0 [dimensionless]. Five variables, three base dimensions (M, L, T), so two dimensionless groups: T√(g/L) and θ0. Mass cannot appear because nothing else carries the kilogram. Hence

T = √(L/g) · f(θ0)(4.1)

Solving the small-angle equation of motion d2θ/dt2 = −(g/L)θ then fixes f(0) = 2π. Dimensional analysis did most of the work.

Case study: G. I. Taylor's blast wave

In 1950 G. I. Taylor estimated the energy of the first atomic explosion from photographs of the expanding fireball. If the radius R depends only on energy E, time t and air density ρ, dimensional analysis forces R ≈ C(Et2/ρ)1/5 with C of order one [20]. Fitting published images gave an energy in the range of the (then classified) yield, a striking demonstration of what a well-chosen model can extract.

4.4 Knowing when a model breaks: the small-angle approximation

The small-angle model gives T0 = 2π√(L/g). The exact period of the frictionless pendulum is T = T0/AGM(1, cos(θ0/2)), where AGM is the arithmetic–geometric mean, and the first correction is T ≈ T0(1 + θ02/16). The table below was computed with the code that follows it.

Python · exact pendulum period via the AGM
import math

def agm(a, b, tol=1e-15):
    """Arithmetic-geometric mean of a and b."""
    while abs(a - b) > tol:
        a, b = (a + b) / 2, math.sqrt(a * b)
    return a

for deg in [5, 10, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90]:
    th = math.radians(deg)
    exact = 1 / agm(1, math.cos(th / 2))     # T / T0
    first_order = 1 + th**2 / 16
    print(f"{deg:3d} deg  exact={exact:.5f}  series={first_order:.5f}")

The practical rule: the model's validity depends on the accuracy you need. For a 1% measurement of g, amplitudes up to about 20° are safe; for 0.01% you must stay below about 2° or apply the correction.

Exercises

Exercise 4.1 The speed v of deep-water surface waves depends on wavelength λ and g; shallow-water waves depend on depth h and g. Use dimensions to find both forms.

Solution

Only √(length × acceleration) has dimensions of speed. Deep water: v = C1√(gλ) (exact theory gives C1 = 1/√(2π)). Shallow water: v = C2√(gh) (exact C2 = 1). Dimensional analysis gives the scaling but not the constant.

Exercise 4.2 Using Taylor's scaling R = (Et2/ρ)1/5 with C = 1, estimate E if R = 140 m at t = 0.025 s and ρ = 1.2 kg m−3. Express the answer in kilotons of TNT (1 kt = 4.184 × 1012 J).

Solution

E = ρR5/t2 = 1.2 × (140)5 / (0.025)2 = 1.2 × 5.38 × 1010 / 6.25 × 10−4 ≈ 1.03 × 1014 J ≈ 25 kt. This is of the same order as the roughly 20 kt yield usually quoted for the 1945 Trinity test; the uncertain constant C and the fifth power make the estimate sensitive to R.

Exercise 4.3 A model predicts that drag on a sphere is F = kρv2r. Is it dimensionally consistent? What must the dimensions of k be?

Solution

ρv2r has dimensions (kg m−3)(m2 s−2)(m) = kg s−2, which is force divided by length. A force needs kg m s−2, so k would need dimensions of length; the model is wrong as written with dimensionless k. The correct quadratic-drag scaling is F ∝ ρv2r2.

5Theoretical predictions

A prediction is a deductive consequence of a model plus specified conditions. The scientifically valuable predictions are risky: they would be surprising if the theory were false, and they are made before the result is known. Popper stressed that confirmations count only if they are the result of risky predictions [14].

5.1 Retrodiction, prediction and novel prediction

  • Retrodiction: explaining something already known. Einstein's 1915 calculation of Mercury's anomalous perihelion advance, about 43 arcseconds per century, from general relativity is the classic example [16].
  • Prediction: forecasting a future measurement of a known kind (next year's eclipse times).
  • Novel prediction: a phenomenon not previously imagined. General relativity predicted that starlight grazing the Sun is deflected by 1.75 arcseconds, twice the value from a Newtonian corpuscular argument. The 1919 eclipse expeditions reported 1.98″ ± 0.12″ (Sobral) and 1.61″ ± 0.30″ (Príncipe), favouring Einstein [21].

5.2 Long-range predictions

Some predictions wait decades for technology. Gravitational waves, predicted from general relativity in 1916, were directly detected by LIGO on 14 September 2015, with a waveform matching numerical-relativity predictions for two merging black holes [22]. The Higgs boson, proposed in 1964, was observed by the ATLAS and CMS collaborations in 2012 [23,24]. In both cases the theory specified in advance what signal to look for, which is what made the searches meaningful.

Checklist: a usable prediction

States the observable, its predicted value with a theoretical uncertainty, the conditions under which it applies, and what result would count against the theory. A prediction without an error bar cannot be compared with a measurement.

Exercises

Exercise 5.1 The relativistic light deflection is δ = 4GM/(c2b). With GM⊙ = 1.327 × 1020 m3 s−2, c = 2.998 × 108 m s−1 and b = R⊙ = 6.96 × 108 m, compute δ in arcseconds.

Solution

δ = 4(1.327 × 1020)/[(8.988 × 1016)(6.96 × 108)] = 5.308 × 1020/6.256 × 1025 = 8.49 × 10−6 rad. Multiply by 206 265″/rad: δ ≈ 1.75″.

Exercise 5.2 Using the 1919 values, by how many standard deviations does the Sobral result differ from 1.75″ and from 0.87″?

Solution

From 1.75″: (1.98 − 1.75)/0.12 ≈ 1.9σ. From 0.87″: (1.98 − 0.87)/0.12 ≈ 9.3σ. The data clearly disfavour the Newtonian half-value, though the Sobral value itself sits nearly 2σ above Einstein's. Later radio-interferometric measurements confirmed the relativistic value far more precisely.

Exercise 5.3 Explain why a retrodiction such as Mercury's perihelion is weaker evidence than a novel prediction, and give one reason it can still be strong.

Solution

With a known target, a theorist may consciously or unconsciously tune the theory to hit it. However, if the theory has no free parameters that could be adjusted to fit (general relativity gave 43″/century with no tuning), even a retrodiction is a severe test.

6Falsifiability and reproducibility

6.1 Popper's criterion

Popper proposed that a theory is scientific if and only if it is falsifiable: it must prohibit some conceivable observations [3]. The logic is asymmetric. No number of agreeing observations can prove a universal statement, but a single genuine counter-instance can refute it, by modus tollens.

6.2 Complications: Duhem–Quine, Kuhn and Lakatos

Pierre Duhem observed that a physicist never tests an isolated hypothesis, only a whole group of hypotheses together with the theory of the instruments used [25]. When a prediction fails, logic alone does not say which member of the group to give up. Uranus's anomaly was resolved by adding Neptune (an auxiliary hypothesis), not by rejecting Newton (§3). Kuhn documented that scientists routinely tolerate anomalies during normal science [4]. Imre Lakatos reconciled these observations with Popper by evaluating research programmes: a programme is progressive if its modifications predict new facts that are then confirmed, and degenerating if it only patches itself after the fact [26].

Case study: the OPERA “faster-than-light” neutrinos

In 2011 the OPERA experiment reported neutrinos arriving from CERN at Gran Sasso about 60 ns earlier than light would, apparently a 6σ violation of special relativity. The collaboration publicly asked for scrutiny rather than claiming a discovery. Two equipment faults were found, including a badly connected optical-fibre cable in the timing system; the corrected analysis published in 2012 found the neutrino speed consistent with c [27]. This was falsification working as intended: the auxiliary assumption (“the timing chain is correct”) was the part that failed.

6.3 Reproducibility

The International Vocabulary of Metrology distinguishes repeatability (same procedure, operators, system and location, over a short period) from reproducibility (different locations, operators and measuring systems) [28]. In practice, a physics result is accepted when independent groups using different methods agree. Cold fusion illustrates the reverse: Fleischmann and Pons's 1989 report of excess heat from electrolysis of heavy water [29] could not be reproduced reliably by laboratories worldwide, and the claim did not enter mainstream physics. Reproducibility problems are not confined to fringe claims; in a 2016 Nature survey of 1,576 researchers, more than 70% said they had tried and failed to reproduce another scientist's experiment [30].

Practices that protect reproducibility
  • Pre-register the analysis, or use blind analysis: hide the signal region or add a secret offset until all cuts and corrections are frozen.
  • Publish raw data, code, and the full uncertainty budget.
  • Report null results and the sensitivity of the search.
  • Seek independent confirmation with a different method (Higgs: two detectors with different technologies [23,24]).

Exercises

Exercise 6.1 Which of these statements are falsifiable? (a) “All copper wires have resistivity below 2 × 10−8 Ω m at 20 °C.” (b) “There exist forces that never affect any measurement.” (c) “The proton has a lifetime greater than 1034 years.”

Solution

(a) Falsifiable: one copper wire above the limit refutes it. (b) Not falsifiable: by construction no observation can count against it. (c) Falsifiable in principle: observing proton decays at a rate implying a shorter lifetime would refute it. Large water-Cherenkov detectors test exactly this.

Exercise 6.2 List three auxiliary assumptions in the OPERA measurement that could each, if wrong, produce an apparent superluminal result.

Solution

Examples: the 730 km baseline distance measured by geodesy is correct; the GPS-based clock synchronization between sites is correct; the delays in cables and electronics (including the fibre-optic link) are correctly calibrated; the proton-pulse time profile is a faithful proxy for neutrino emission times.

Exercise 6.3 Explain the difference between repeatability and reproducibility, using measurement of g with a pendulum as the example.

Solution

Repeatability: the same student, same pendulum, same stopwatch, measuring several times in one session; the scatter reveals random timing error. Reproducibility: a different group in another lab with a different pendulum and photogate; agreement tests whether systematic effects specific to one setup were present.

7Measurement and uncertainty

7.1 Units: the 2019 SI

A measurement compares a quantity to a unit. Since 20 May 2019 the SI defines all seven base units by fixing the numerical values of seven defining constants, including the Planck constant h = 6.626 070 15 × 10−34 J s, the elementary charge e = 1.602 176 634 × 10−19 C, the Boltzmann constant k = 1.380 649 × 10−23 J K−1 and the Avogadro constant NA = 6.022 140 76 × 1023 mol−1 [31]. These values are now exact by definition; the kilogram is no longer defined by a metal artefact. Other constants such as the gravitational constant G remain measured, with CODATA 2018 giving a relative standard uncertainty of 2.2 × 10−5 [32].

When units go wrong

NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter was lost in 1999 because ground software supplied thruster impulse data in pound-force seconds where the navigation software expected newton seconds, a factor of 4.45 [33]. Every quantity in this article is written with its unit for this reason.

7.2 Error, uncertainty, accuracy, precision

The error of a measurement is its unknown difference from the true value. The uncertainty is a quantified statement of how large that error might reasonably be. The Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM) classifies evaluations as Type A (by statistical analysis of repeated observations) or Type B (by other means: calibration certificates, instrument resolution, physical reasoning) [34]. This replaces the older, ambiguous labels “random” and “systematic” for the method of evaluation, though those words remain useful for describing the behaviour of an effect.

7.3 Type A: statistics of repeated measurements

For N independent readings xi, the best estimate is the mean x̄, the scatter is the sample standard deviation s, and the standard uncertainty of the mean is

u(x̄) = s√N,   s = √[ 1N − 1 Σ(xi − x̄)2 ](7.1)

The 1/√N law means that a tenfold improvement costs a hundredfold more data, which is why precision physics invests in better apparatus rather than just longer runs [35].

7.4 Propagation of uncertainty

If y = f(x1, …, xn) with uncorrelated inputs, the GUM's law of propagation of uncertainty gives, to first order [34]:

uc2(y) = Σi ( ∂f/∂xi )2 u2(xi)(7.2)

For products and powers, y = c x1ax2b, this becomes (u/y)2 = a2(u1/x1)2 + b2(u2/x2)2. When the function is strongly nonlinear or the inputs are not Gaussian, GUM Supplement 1 recommends propagating whole distributions by Monte Carlo sampling [36].

Worked example: g from a pendulum, two ways

L = 1.000 ± 0.002 m and T = 2.007 ± 0.005 s. Then g = 4π2L/T2 = 9.801 m s−2. Linear propagation: u/g = √[(0.002)2 + (2 × 0.005/2.007)2] = 0.00537, so u(g) = 0.053 m s−2. The Monte Carlo code below gives 9.801 ± 0.053 m s−2, agreeing because the relative uncertainties are small. Note that the period dominates the budget through the factor of 2 from T2.

Python · linear vs Monte Carlo uncertainty propagation
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
N = 200_000
L = rng.normal(1.000, 0.002, N)        # m
T = rng.normal(2.007, 0.005, N)        # s
g = 4 * np.pi**2 * L / T**2

print(f"Monte Carlo: g = {g.mean():.3f} ± {g.std():.3f} m/s^2")

g0 = 4 * np.pi**2 * 1.000 / 2.007**2
u_lin = g0 * np.hypot(0.002 / 1.000, 2 * 0.005 / 2.007)
print(f"Linear GUM : g = {g0:.3f} ± {u_lin:.3f} m/s^2")
# Monte Carlo: g = 9.801 ± 0.053 m/s^2
# Linear GUM : g = 9.801 ± 0.053 m/s^2

7.5 Fitting a model: weighted least squares and χ2

Most measurements are not of one number but of a relation. Linearizing the pendulum model gives T2 = (4π2/g)L, a straight line through the origin. A weighted least-squares fit estimates the slope and its uncertainty, and the χ2 statistic tests whether the scatter matches the claimed uncertainties: χ2 per degree of freedom near 1 is expected, much greater suggests underestimated errors or a wrong model, and much less suggests overestimated errors [37].

Python · weighted fit of synthetic pendulum data
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
L = np.array([0.20, 0.40, 0.60, 0.80, 1.00, 1.20])     # m
g_true, sigma_T = 9.81, 0.01                              # m/s^2, s
T = 2*np.pi*np.sqrt(L/g_true) + rng.normal(0, sigma_T, L.size)

y  = T**2                    # model: y = a*L, a = 4 pi^2 / g
sy = 2*T*sigma_T             # propagated uncertainty of T^2
w  = 1/sy**2
a  = np.sum(w*L*y) / np.sum(w*L*L)
sa = np.sqrt(1/np.sum(w*L*L))
g, sg = 4*np.pi**2/a, 4*np.pi**2/a * sa/a
chi2 = np.sum(w*(y - a*L)**2)

print(f"g = {g:.3f} ± {sg:.3f} m/s^2,  chi2 = {chi2:.2f} for {L.size-1} dof")
# g = 9.869 ± 0.048 m/s^2,  chi2 = 6.65 for 5 dof

The fitted value is 1.2 standard uncertainties from the true 9.81 m s−2 used to generate the data. That is exactly the kind of deviation honest uncertainties should produce about a third of the time, and a useful reminder not to “fix” results that look slightly off.

The precision frontier

The electron's magnetic moment has been measured to a relative uncertainty of 1.3 × 10−13 (0.13 parts per trillion) using a single electron in a Penning trap [38]. Comparison with quantum electrodynamics at this level is one of the most stringent tests of any physical theory.

Exercises

Exercise 7.1 Ten timings of 20 pendulum swings give a mean of 40.12 s and a sample standard deviation of 0.15 s. What is the period and its standard uncertainty?

Solution

Uncertainty of the mean: 0.15/√10 = 0.047 s. Dividing by 20 swings: T = 2.006 ± 0.0024 s. Timing many swings divides the reaction-time error by the number of swings.

Exercise 7.2 A cylinder has radius r = 2.00 ± 0.02 cm and height h = 5.00 ± 0.05 cm. Find its volume and uncertainty.

Solution

V = πr2h = 62.83 cm3. Relative uncertainty √[(2 × 0.01)2 + (0.01)2] = 0.0224, so u(V) = 1.4 cm3. Report V = 62.8 ± 1.4 cm3.

Exercise 7.3 A digital thermometer reads to 0.1 °C. Assuming a rectangular distribution of half-width 0.05 °C, what is the Type B standard uncertainty from resolution?

Solution

For a rectangular distribution of half-width a, the GUM gives u = a/√3 = 0.05/1.732 ≈ 0.029 °C [34].

Exercise 7.4 A fit returns χ2 = 48 for 10 degrees of freedom. Give two possible explanations.

Solution

χ2/dof = 4.8 is far above 1. Either the individual uncertainties are underestimated (e.g. a neglected Type B component), or the model is inadequate (e.g. a straight line fitted to curved data, an omitted offset). Plotting residuals usually distinguishes the two: random scatter suggests the first, a systematic pattern the second.

8Model validation and verification

As soon as a model is implemented on a computer, two separate questions arise. Patrick Roache's compact phrasing is widely used: verification asks “are we solving the equations right?”, and validation asks “are we solving the right equations?” [39]. The AIAA guide defines verification as determining that a model implementation accurately represents the developer's conceptual description and its solution, and validation as determining the degree to which a model is an accurate representation of the real world from the perspective of its intended uses [40].

8.1 Verification techniques

  • Code verification: compare with exact analytic solutions, or use the method of manufactured solutions (choose a solution, compute the source term it requires, and check the code reproduces it) [39,41].
  • Order-of-accuracy tests: halve the step size and check the error falls by the theoretically expected factor 2p.
  • Conservation checks: total energy, momentum or charge should be conserved to the expected tolerance.
  • Solution verification: estimate the discretization error of a particular run, for example by grid refinement.
Worked example: measuring observed order of accuracy

Integrate the oscillator ẍ = −x, x(0) = 1, ẋ(0) = 0 to t = 10 and compare with cos 10. The observed order is p = log2(Eh/Eh/2).

Method (formal order)Error, h = 0.1h = 0.05h = 0.025Observed p
Forward Euler (1)5.70 × 10−12.44 × 10−11.13 × 10−11.22, 1.11
Velocity Verlet (2)2.28 × 10−35.67 × 10−41.42 × 10−42.00, 2.00
Runge–Kutta 4 (4)3.94 × 10−62.65 × 10−71.71 × 10−83.89, 3.95

Each method converges towards its formal order as h shrinks (Euler is still outside its asymptotic range at these step sizes). If a supposedly fourth-order code showed p ≈ 1, that would reveal a bug, often in a boundary condition or a single mis-weighted term.

Python · order-of-accuracy test
import math

def run(step, h, T=10.0):
    x, v = 1.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(round(T / h)):
        x, v = step(x, v, h)
    return x

def euler(x, v, h):  return x + h*v, v - h*x
def verlet(x, v, h):
    v_half = v - 0.5*h*x
    x_new  = x + h*v_half
    return x_new, v_half - 0.5*h*x_new
def rk4(x, v, h):
    f = lambda x, v: (v, -x)
    k1 = f(x, v)
    k2 = f(x + h/2*k1[0], v + h/2*k1[1])
    k3 = f(x + h/2*k2[0], v + h/2*k2[1])
    k4 = f(x + h*k3[0],   v + h*k3[1])
    return (x + h/6*(k1[0] + 2*k2[0] + 2*k3[0] + k4[0]),
            v + h/6*(k1[1] + 2*k2[1] + 2*k3[1] + k4[1]))

exact = math.cos(10)
for name, step in [("Euler", euler), ("Verlet", verlet), ("RK4", rk4)]:
    e = [abs(run(step, h) - exact) for h in (0.1, 0.05, 0.025)]
    p = [math.log2(e[i] / e[i+1]) for i in range(2)]
    print(name, [f"{x:.2e}" for x in e], [round(q, 2) for q in p])

8.2 Validation

Validation requires experiments designed for the purpose, with quantified uncertainties on both the measurements and the simulation inputs. The outcome is not “valid” or “invalid” in the abstract but an assessment of accuracy over a domain of applicability: a turbulence model validated for subsonic flow over wings says nothing about hypersonic re-entry [41]. Validation also cannot be done before verification, because an unverified code may match data for the wrong reasons, with numerical errors cancelling modeling errors.

Exercises

Exercise 8.1 Classify as verification or validation: (a) comparing a heat-conduction code with the analytic solution for a semi-infinite slab; (b) comparing predicted and measured temperatures in a test furnace; (c) checking that total energy is conserved to 10−10 in an N-body code.

Solution

(a) Verification (code against exact solution of the same equations). (b) Validation (model against reality). (c) Verification (a property the equations guarantee).

Exercise 8.2 A code's error is 4.0 × 10−3 at h and 1.0 × 10−3 at h/2. What is the observed order? Use Richardson extrapolation to estimate the error at h/4.

Solution

p = log2(4.0/1.0) = 2. At h/4 the error should be about 1.0 × 10−3/4 = 2.5 × 10−4.

Exercise 8.3 Why is a match between an unverified simulation and an experiment weak evidence for the model?

Solution

Numerical error and modeling error can cancel. A coarse grid with excess numerical diffusion might mimic a turbulence effect the model omits. Refining the grid would then destroy the agreement. Verification first isolates the modeling error so that validation tests the physics.

9Thought experiments

A thought experiment (Gedankenexperiment) reasons through an imagined scenario, usually idealized beyond what can be built, to expose a contradiction, illustrate a principle or motivate a new theory [42]. It cannot replace real data, but it can show that a set of beliefs is inconsistent, or that a principle has surprising consequences worth testing.

9.1 Galileo's falling bodies

Aristotle held that heavier bodies fall faster. Galileo, through his character Salviati, asked what happens if a heavy stone and a light stone are tied together [7]. The light stone should slow the heavy one, so the pair falls slower than the heavy stone alone. But the pair is heavier than either, so it should fall faster. The contradiction shows that the speed of fall cannot depend on weight in the assumed way. The argument leaves out air resistance, which is why it is a statement about an idealization.

9.2 Einstein's elevator and light

Einstein recalled asking, as a teenager, what he would see if he chased a light beam. Maxwell's equations allow no frozen electromagnetic wave, which pointed towards the principle, made the foundation of his 1905 paper, that the laws of physics are the same for all inertial observers [10]. In 1907 he imagined an observer in a closed box: no local experiment can distinguish uniform acceleration in empty space from rest in a uniform gravitational field. This equivalence principle [43] implies that light must bend in gravity, the prediction tested in 1919 (§5).

9.3 Thought experiments that became real experiments

Thought experimentQuestionOutcome
Maxwell's demon (1871) [44]Could an intelligent being sorting molecules beat the second law?Landauer (1961) showed erasing information has an unavoidable thermodynamic cost, at least kT ln 2 per bit [45], restoring the second law.
EPR (1935) [46]Is quantum mechanics complete if measurements on one particle fix properties of a distant one?Bell (1964) derived an inequality obeyed by local hidden-variable theories [47]; Aspect and colleagues (1982) observed its violation [48].
Schrödinger's cat (1935) [49]What does superposition mean for macroscopic objects?Still a touchstone for interpretations; it motivated experiments on decoherence and macroscopic superpositions.
Limits of thought experiments

A thought experiment is only as good as its hidden premises. Many classic paradoxes dissolve once an overlooked physical constraint is added (for the demon, the physics of the demon's own memory). Treat a thought experiment as an argument whose assumptions should be listed and checked, just like a model's.

Exercises

Exercise 9.1 In Einstein's accelerating box of width 3 m with a = 9.8 m s−2, how far does a horizontal light beam “fall” while crossing?

Solution

Crossing time t = 3/(3.0 × 108) = 1.0 × 10−8 s. Drop = ½at2 = 0.5 × 9.8 × 10−16 ≈ 4.9 × 10−16 m, smaller than a proton. The effect is real but tiny on human scales, which is why it took solar-scale masses to see it.

Exercise 9.2 Compute the Landauer limit for erasing one bit at room temperature (300 K) in joules and electronvolts.

Solution

kT ln 2 = 1.380 649 × 10−23 × 300 × 0.6931 ≈ 2.87 × 10−21 J ≈ 0.018 eV.

Exercise 9.3 Galileo's tied-stones argument assumes the speed of the combined body is between the speeds of its parts when they “pull” on each other. Is this assumption justified in Aristotelian physics? Why does the argument still have force?

Solution

An Aristotelian might reply that the tied pair is a new single body whose speed depends only on its total weight, rejecting the “dragging” premise. But then a vanishingly thin string would change the falling speed abruptly, which is implausible. The argument shows that weight-dependent fall requires an arbitrary rule for when bodies count as one, and that is its real force [42].

10Computational simulations

Simulation is now often called a third pillar of science alongside theory and experiment. It lets us explore models too complex for closed-form solutions, run “experiments” impossible in the lab, and propagate uncertainties through nonlinear systems.

Origins
  • Monte Carlo methods were developed at Los Alamos in the 1940s by Stanislaw Ulam, John von Neumann, Nicholas Metropolis and others, and published in 1949 [50]. Metropolis later recalled that the name was inspired by the Monte Carlo casino, where Ulam's uncle used to gamble [51]. The Metropolis algorithm of 1953 made it possible to sample complicated equilibrium distributions [52].
  • The Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou problem (1955) simulated a chain of nonlinearly coupled masses on the MANIAC computer. Instead of the expected spreading of energy across all modes, the energy returned almost entirely to the initial mode, a surprise that seeded the study of solitons and nonlinear dynamics [53].
  • Chaos: Edward Lorenz showed in 1963 that a simple three-equation model of convection is deterministic yet extremely sensitive to initial conditions, so that tiny differences grow until long-range prediction becomes impossible [54].

10.1 Sources of error in a simulation

  1. Model error: the equations omit relevant physics (addressed by validation).
  2. Discretization error: finite step sizes and grids (addressed by verification and convergence studies).
  3. Round-off error: finite-precision floating-point arithmetic, about 1.1 × 10−16 relative for IEEE double precision [55].
  4. Statistical error: finite samples in Monte Carlo, scaling as 1/√N.
  5. Input uncertainty: imprecisely known parameters and initial conditions; amplified exponentially in chaotic systems.

10.2 Integrator choice matters: an energy test

For Hamiltonian systems such as a frictionless pendulum, symplectic integrators (symplectic Euler, velocity Verlet) preserve the geometric structure of phase space, so their energy error stays bounded over long times, while the forward Euler method steadily pumps in spurious energy [56]. Running the code below for a 1 m pendulum released from 0.5 rad with h = 0.01 s for 20 s gives a relative energy error of +4.7 (energy multiplied by almost six) for forward Euler, +1.2 × 10−2 for symplectic Euler and −2.0 × 10−4 for velocity Verlet.

Python · energy drift of three integrators
import numpy as np

g, L = 9.81, 1.0
def energy(th, om):   # per unit mass
    return 0.5*L**2*om**2 + g*L*(1 - np.cos(th))

def euler(th, om, dt):
    return th + dt*om, om - dt*(g/L)*np.sin(th)

def symplectic_euler(th, om, dt):
    om = om - dt*(g/L)*np.sin(th)
    return th + dt*om, om

def verlet(th, om, dt):
    om_half = om - 0.5*dt*(g/L)*np.sin(th)
    th = th + dt*om_half
    return th, om_half - 0.5*dt*(g/L)*np.sin(th)

for name, step in [("Euler", euler), ("Symplectic Euler", symplectic_euler),
                   ("Velocity Verlet", verlet)]:
    th, om, dt = 0.5, 0.0, 0.01
    E0 = energy(th, om)
    for _ in range(int(20/dt)):
        th, om = step(th, om, dt)
    print(f"{name:18s} relative energy error: {(energy(th, om) - E0)/E0:+.2e}")

10.3 Monte Carlo estimation

Monte Carlo methods replace an integral by an average over random samples. The classic toy: scatter points in a unit square; the fraction inside the quarter circle estimates π/4. The statistical uncertainty falls as 1/√N regardless of dimension, which is why Monte Carlo dominates high-dimensional problems such as particle transport and statistical mechanics [50,52].

Python · Monte Carlo estimate of π with error bar
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
for n in [100, 10_000, 1_000_000]:
    x, y = rng.random(n), rng.random(n)
    p = np.mean(x*x + y*y <= 1)            # fraction inside
    est, err = 4*p, 4*np.sqrt(p*(1 - p)/n)  # binomial standard error
    print(f"N={n:>9,}  pi ≈ {est:.5f} ± {err:.5f}  (true error {abs(est-np.pi):.5f})")

Exercises

Exercise 10.1 A Monte Carlo estimate of π with 104 points has a standard error of about 0.016. How many points are needed for a standard error of 0.0001?

Solution

Error scales as 1/√N. Reducing the error by a factor 160 requires 1602 = 25 600 times more points: about 2.6 × 108.

Exercise 10.2 Show that forward Euler multiplies the energy of a harmonic oscillator (ẍ = −ω2x) by exactly (1 + ω2h2) each step.

Solution

With E ∝ ω2x2 + v2, forward Euler gives x′ = x + hv, v′ = v − hω2x. Then ω2x′2 + v′2 = ω2(x2 + 2hxv + h2v2) + (v2 − 2hω2xv + h2ω4x2) = (1 + ω2h2)(ω2x2 + v2). The cross terms cancel. Energy grows geometrically for any h > 0, so forward Euler is unconditionally unstable for undamped oscillators.

Exercise 10.3 In a chaotic system nearby trajectories separate as δ(t) ≈ δ0eλt. With λ = 0.9 per time unit, how much longer can you predict if you reduce initial error from 10−3 to 10−6, for a tolerance of 1?

Solution

Prediction horizon t = ln(1/δ0)/λ. For 10−3: 6.91/0.9 = 7.7. For 10−6: 13.8/0.9 = 15.4. A thousandfold better measurement only doubles the horizon, the practical meaning of Lorenz's discovery [54].

11Scientific inference

Inference is the step from data to conclusions about models and parameters. Physicists use two broad statistical frameworks, often side by side [57].

11.1 Frequentist inference and p-values

A p-value is the probability, computed assuming a null hypothesis H0 is true, of obtaining data at least as extreme as those observed. The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement emphasized that a p-value does not measure the probability that the hypothesis is true, nor the size or importance of an effect, and that scientific conclusions should not rest on whether it crosses a single threshold [58].

SignificanceOne-sided tail probabilityRoughly 1 inTypical use in physics
1σ0.1596.3Quoted standard uncertainty
2σ0.022844Common threshold in other sciences (p ≈ 0.05 two-sided)

5σ2.87 × 10−73.5 million“Observation” / discovery in particle physics

The 5σ convention used for the Higgs boson [23,24] and for GW150914 [22] guards against the look-elsewhere effect (searching many places makes a local fluctuation likely somewhere) and against unmodelled systematics [57].

11.2 Bayesian inference

Bayesian inference treats probability as a degree of belief and updates it with data via Bayes' theorem [59,60]:

P(H | D) = P(D | H)  P(H)P(D)(11.1)

Here P(H) is the prior, P(D|H) the likelihood, P(H|D) the posterior, and P(D) the evidence, which normalizes the result and, for comparing models, automatically penalizes unnecessary complexity (a quantitative Occam's razor) [60].

Worked example: frequentist and Bayesian views of the same data

A coin gives 7 heads in 10 tosses. Frequentist: under H0: p = 0.5, the probability of 7 or more heads is (120 + 45 + 10 + 1)/1024 = 0.172, so the data are unremarkable. Bayesian: with a uniform prior the posterior is Beta(8, 4), with mean 8/12 = 0.667 and mode 0.70; the posterior probability that p > 0.5 is about 0.89. The two answers address different questions and are not in conflict.

Python · grid Bayesian update and σ-to-probability conversion (standard library + NumPy)
import math
import numpy as np

p = np.linspace(0, 1, 1001)
prior = np.ones_like(p)                     # uniform prior
heads, n = 7, 10
likelihood = p**heads * (1 - p)**(n - heads)
post = prior * likelihood
post /= np.trapezoid(post, p)               # normalise (NumPy ≥ 2.0; use np.trapz before)

print("posterior mean:", np.trapezoid(p * post, p))     # 0.667
print("P(p > 0.5 | data):", np.trapezoid(post[p > 0.5], p[p > 0.5]))

# One-sided tail probability for an n-sigma excess
for s in (1, 2, 3, 5):
    tail = 0.5 * math.erfc(s / math.sqrt(2))
    print(f"{s} sigma: p = {tail:.3g}  (1 in {1/tail:,.0f})")

11.3 Common inferential errors

  • Transposed conditional: confusing P(data | H0) with P(H0 | data).
  • Optional stopping and p-hacking: tuning the analysis or the sample size until a threshold is crossed. Blind analysis is the physicist's main defence (§6).
  • Ignoring systematics: statistical precision is meaningless if a systematic offset dominates (Figure 5).
  • Overfitting: adding parameters always improves a fit; use χ2 per degree of freedom, information criteria or Bayesian evidence to judge whether they are warranted [37,60].

Exercises

Exercise 11.1 A detector flags a particle as “signal” with 99% efficiency for true signal and a 1% false-positive rate on background. If only 1 event in 10 000 is truly signal, what fraction of flagged events are real?

Solution

Per 106 events: 100 signal, of which 99 flagged; 999 900 background, of which 9 999 flagged. Fraction real = 99/(99 + 9 999) ≈ 0.98%. A tiny prior swamps an excellent test, which is why base rates (priors) cannot be ignored [59].

Exercise 11.2 A search examines 100 independent mass bins. What is the probability that at least one shows a 3σ (one-sided) upward fluctuation purely by chance?

Solution

1 − (1 − 1.35 × 10−3)100 ≈ 1 − e−0.135 ≈ 0.126, about 13%. This is the look-elsewhere effect; a local 3σ is not rare in a broad search [57].

Exercise 11.3 Two measurements of a quantity are 10.2 ± 0.3 and 9.6 ± 0.4. Are they consistent? Compute the weighted mean and its uncertainty.

Solution

Difference 0.6 with combined uncertainty √(0.09 + 0.16) = 0.5, i.e. 1.2σ: consistent. Weights 1/0.09 = 11.1 and 1/0.16 = 6.25. Mean = (11.1 × 10.2 + 6.25 × 9.6)/17.36 = 9.98; uncertainty 1/√17.36 = 0.24. Result 9.98 ± 0.24 [35].

Exercise 11.4 Explain why “p = 0.03, so there is a 97% chance our hypothesis is right” is wrong.

Solution

The p-value is computed assuming the null hypothesis; it is the probability of data this extreme if H0 holds. It is not the probability that H0 (or the alternative) is true, which requires a prior and Bayes' theorem [58].

Capstone exercise Design a one-page experimental plan to test whether the period of a pendulum depends on the bob's material (steel versus wood of the same size). State the hypothesis, the controlled variables, the expected effect size from air drag and buoyancy (qualitatively), the number of trials needed for 0.1% precision if a single 20-swing timing has 0.2% scatter, and what result would refute “no dependence”.

Solution outline

Hypothesis: T is the same for both bobs to within 0.1%. Controls: same length (measured to the centre of mass), same amplitude (< 5°), same string and support, same timing method, interleaved runs to average out drift. Expected effects: buoyancy and air drag matter more for the low-density wooden bob, lengthening its period slightly; this is a real physical difference, not a failure of the idealized model, and the experiment must be sensitive enough to see or bound it. Trials: to reach 0.1% on each mean from 0.2% scatter needs N = (0.2/0.1)2 = 4 timings; to resolve a difference at 0.1% with 2σ confidence, aim for about 0.035% per mean, i.e. roughly 30 timings each. Refutation: a difference in mean periods exceeding about 3 combined standard uncertainties.
