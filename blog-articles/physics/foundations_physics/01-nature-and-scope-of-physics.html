0How to use this article

This is the opening article of the Foundations of Physics strand. It is less about any one force or particle and more about the questions behind all of them: what counts as a physical explanation, how far it reaches, and how confident we can be in it.

Learning objectives

  • State what physics studies and what it aims to achieve.
  • Distinguish phenomena, laws, theories and models.
  • Explain why idealization is a strength, not a cheat.
  • Tell fundamental from emergent descriptions, with examples.
  • Weigh reductionism against emergence.
  • Separate determinism, predictability and probability.
  • Connect symmetries to conservation laws (Noether's theorem).
  • Use dimensional analysis and orders of magnitude.
  • Place classical and modern theories by their domains of validity.
  • Describe how physics feeds and is fed by other fields.

SECTION 1Definition and objectives of physics

The word physics comes from the Greek phúsis, "nature". Aristotle's treatise Physics was a study of change and motion in general1, and for two millennia "natural philosophy" covered what we now split into many sciences. Newton's masterwork was still titled Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy2, and the English word physicist was only coined by William Whewell in 18403.

Definition

Physics is the empirical science that seeks the most general laws governing matter, energy, space and time, and their interactions, expressed as far as possible in quantitative, mathematical form and tested against measurement.

Three features of this definition matter. It is empirical: nature, not authority, is the final judge. As Feynman put it in his introductory lectures, "the test of all knowledge is experiment"4. It aims at generality: the same law should apply to a falling apple and the orbiting Moon, as Newton showed2. And it is quantitative: a physical claim usually predicts a number with an uncertainty, which is what makes it possible to refute. Popper made falsifiability the dividing line between science and non-science5; physics is the field where that criterion is applied most sharply.

Objectives

  1. Describe phenomena precisely, through measurement in agreed units6.
  2. Explain them by a small number of principles, so that many facts follow from few assumptions.
  3. Predict new phenomena. The planet Neptune (found in 1846 where Le Verrier's calculations placed it7), radio waves (predicted by Maxwell's theory8), the Higgs boson9,10 and gravitational waves11 were all predicted before they were observed.
  4. Unify apparently separate domains: celestial and terrestrial motion (Newton), electricity, magnetism and light (Maxwell8), space and time (Einstein12).
  5. Enable control and technology, from engines to transistors13 to MRI scanners14.
Worked example 1.1 · Unification in one line

Newton's insight was that the force pulling an apple down also holds the Moon in orbit. Check it with numbers. At Earth's surface, g ≈ 9.81 m s−2 at a distance RE ≈ 6.37 × 106 m from Earth's centre16. If gravity weakens as 1/r2, then at the Moon's distance (r ≈ 60.3 RE ≈ 3.84 × 108 m) it should be g/60.32 ≈ 2.70 × 10−3 m s−2.

The Moon's actual centripetal acceleration, with orbital period T = 27.32 days = 2.36 × 106 s, is a = 4π2r/T2 ≈ 4π2(3.84 × 108)/(2.36 × 106)2 ≈ 2.72 × 10−3 m s−2. The two agree to within 1 %: one law, two very different phenomena2.

SECTION 2Physical phenomena and natural laws

A physical phenomenon is any observable, reproducible occurrence involving matter or energy: a rainbow, a falling stone, a current in a wire, the glow of a hot filament. A natural (physical) law is a concise, usually mathematical statement of a regularity that holds across a whole class of phenomena, within a stated domain and to a stated precision.

Properties of a good law

  • Universality within its domain: the same law here, on Mars and in a distant galaxy.
  • Precision and testability: it predicts numbers. The electron's magnetic moment is now measured to about 0.13 parts per trillion and agrees with quantum electrodynamics at that level17.
  • Economy: it compresses many observations into one statement.
  • Provisional status: every law is accepted "until further notice". Newton's gravity survived for two centuries until a 43 arc-second-per-century excess in Mercury's perihelion advance was explained by general relativity18.
Common misconception

"Laws are proven; theories are guesses." In physics, a law is usually a single relationship, and a theory is a larger explanatory framework from which many laws follow. Theories are not promoted to laws with more evidence; Maxwell's theory contains Coulomb's law, Faraday's law and the wave equation for light8.

Worked example 2.1 · Universality of free fall

Galileo argued that, without air resistance, all bodies fall with the same acceleration19. In 1971 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather on the Moon; they landed together20. The modern MICROSCOPE satellite compared titanium and platinum test masses in orbit and found their accelerations equal to about one part in 101521. One qualitative phenomenon, tested over four centuries with precision improving by many orders of magnitude, is now a cornerstone principle (the weak equivalence principle) of general relativity.

SECTION 3Physical theories, models, and idealizations

A physical theory is a mathematical framework with a small set of basic quantities, postulates and rules, from which laws and predictions can be derived; examples are classical mechanics, electromagnetism, thermodynamics, special and general relativity and quantum mechanics. A model is a simplified representation of a particular system within a theory: a "point-mass planet", an "ideal gas", a "Bohr atom", a "frictionless incline". An idealization is a deliberate simplification that drops or distorts features believed to be unimportant for the question at hand.

The statistician George Box summarized the attitude: since all models are wrong, the scientist must be alert to what is importantly wrong22. A good idealization is controlled: you can estimate the error it introduces and improve it if needed. The pendulum is the classic case.

Worked example 3.1 · How wrong is the small-angle pendulum?

For a simple pendulum of length L, the equation of motion is θ̈ = −(g/L) sin θ. Replacing sin θ by θ gives simple harmonic motion with period

T0 = 2π √(L/g)(3.1)

The exact period, valid for any amplitude θ0, involves a complete elliptic integral and can be computed very efficiently with the arithmetic–geometric mean (AGM)23:

T = T0AGM(1, cos(θ0/2)) ≈ T0 (1 + θ02/16 + …)(3.2)

At 10° the error of the idealization is 0.19 %, at 20° it is 0.77 %, and at 90° it is 18 %. So for a clock pendulum swinging a few degrees, the model is excellent; for a playground swing pushed high, it is not.

Theories have domains and limits

Theories are related by limits. Special relativity reduces to Newtonian mechanics when speeds are small compared with c; quantum mechanics reproduces classical behaviour when actions are large compared with Planck's constant ħ; Newtonian gravity is the weak-field, slow-motion limit of general relativity. Okun organized these relations as a cube whose axes are 1/c, ħ and G24 (Figure 2). A new theory must reproduce the successes of the old one in the old domain; this is often called the correspondence principle.

SECTION 4Fundamental versus emergent phenomena

A phenomenon or law is called fundamental if, as far as we know, it is not derived from anything deeper: the behaviour of the elementary particles and their interactions in the Standard Model25, and gravity as described by general relativity. A phenomenon is emergent if it arises from the collective behaviour of many constituents and is described by concepts that make no sense for a single constituent.

Emergent conceptArises fromWhy it is "new"
Temperature, pressure, entropyStatistics of ~1023 moleculesOne molecule has no temperature.
Rigidity, elasticityOrdered crystal latticesArises from broken translational symmetry.
SuperconductivityPaired electrons in a metal26Zero resistance and flux expulsion appear only collectively.
Phonons and other quasiparticlesCollective lattice vibrationsBehave like particles, but exist only inside matter.
Fractional charge in the quantum Hall effect2-D electron gases in strong fields27,28Excitations carry a fraction of the electron charge, though every electron carries e.
Arrow of timeLow-entropy initial conditions plus statisticsFundamental laws are (almost) time-symmetric.

Philip Anderson's 1972 essay "More is different" argued that each level of complexity has its own laws and that "the ability to reduce everything to simple fundamental laws does not imply the ability to start from those laws and reconstruct the universe"29. Laughlin and Pines went further, pointing out that the exact equations for electrons and nuclei are known, yet properties such as the precise quantized Hall resistance are measured far more accurately than they could ever be computed from those equations, because they are protected by collective principles30.

Worked example 4.1 · Temperature as an emergent quantity

In kinetic theory, the average translational kinetic energy of a molecule in an ideal gas is ⟨Ek⟩ = (3/2)kBT, where kB = 1.380 649 × 10−23 J K−1 is exact in the SI6. At room temperature (T = 300 K), ⟨Ek⟩ = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10−23 × 300 ≈ 6.2 × 10−21 J ≈ 0.039 eV. Temperature is a property of the distribution: a single molecule's speed changes at every collision, but the average of 1023 of them is stable and measurable with a thermometer.

SECTION 5Reductionism and emergence

Reductionism is the strategy, and for some the doctrine, that complex systems are best understood by analysing them into simpler parts and their interactions. It is spectacularly successful: chemical bonding was explained by quantum mechanics within two years of the theory's creation31, prompting Dirac to write in 1929 that "the underlying physical laws necessary for the mathematical theory of a large part of physics and the whole of chemistry are thus completely known"32. Weinberg's Dreams of a Final Theory defends the view that explanations ultimately point downward toward a few fundamental principles33.

Emergence is the counterweight: higher-level regularities can be largely independent of microscopic details. The clearest physical account comes from the renormalization group, developed by Kenneth Wilson, which shows how microscopic details "wash out" at large scales so that very different systems (a magnet and a liquid–gas mixture at their critical points) share identical critical exponents. This property is called universality34.

Reductionist viewEmergentist view
Direction of explanationDownward: parts explain wholesBoth ways: wholes have their own laws
Typical slogan"The whole is the sum of its parts""More is different"29
Strongest exampleChemistry from quantum mechanics31,32Universality at critical points34
Main limitationCalculation is often impossible in practiceRisks vagueness if not tied to mechanisms
 Table 4. Reductionism and emergence are best seen as complementary.

Most working physicists hold both views at once: ontological reductionism (everything is made of the same particles obeying the same laws) together with epistemological emergence (understanding a superconductor or a hurricane needs concepts of its own).

SECTION 6Determinism, probability, and causality

Classical determinism

In Newtonian mechanics, the present state (all positions and velocities) and the forces fix the entire future and past. Laplace imagined an "intellect" that, knowing all forces and positions at one instant, would see the future "just like the past"35.

Determinism is not predictability: chaos

In 1963 Edward Lorenz found that a three-variable model of convection, though fully deterministic, amplifies tiny differences in initial conditions exponentially36. This sensitive dependence means that a finite-precision measurement only allows prediction up to a finite horizon. The growth rate is measured by the largest Lyapunov exponent λ: errors grow roughly as δ(t) ≈ δ0 eλt37.

Quantum probability

Quantum mechanics introduced probability at a deeper level. Born's rule says that the squared magnitude of the wave function gives the probability of a measurement outcome38; Heisenberg's uncertainty relations limit how sharply position and momentum can be defined together39. Bell showed in 1964 that no theory of local "hidden variables" can reproduce all quantum predictions40, and a loophole-free experiment in 2015 confirmed the quantum violation of Bell's inequality41. Radioactive decay is the textbook example: we can predict the fraction of a sample that decays with great precision, but not which nucleus will decay next (see decay.py in Section 13).

Causality

In physics, "cause precedes effect" acquires a precise geometric meaning in relativity: an event can influence only events inside its future light cone, because no signal travels faster than c12. Quantum entanglement produces correlations between distant measurements, but these cannot be used to send signals, so relativistic causality is preserved.

SECTION 7Symmetry, invariance, and conservation laws

A symmetry of a physical system is a transformation that leaves its laws unchanged. Shifting an experiment to another lab, doing it tomorrow, or rotating the apparatus should not change the result. A quantity that keeps its value under a transformation is invariant; a quantity that keeps its value in time is conserved.

In 1918 Emmy Noether proved that every continuous symmetry of the action of a physical system corresponds to a conserved quantity42,43. This is one of the deepest results in physics: conservation laws are not separate facts but consequences of symmetry.

Worked example 7.1 · Momentum conservation from translation symmetry

Take two particles whose potential energy depends only on their separation: V = V(x1 − x2). Shifting both by the same amount a leaves V unchanged, so the system is translation-invariant. Then F1 = −∂V/∂x1 = −V′ and F2 = −∂V/∂x2 = +V′. So F1 + F2 = 0, and the total momentum p1 + p2 has zero time derivative. Newton's third law, here, is a consequence of the symmetry.

Discrete symmetries and their breaking

Not all symmetries are continuous. Parity (P, mirror reflection), charge conjugation (C, particle ↔ antiparticle) and time reversal (T) were long assumed to be exact. In 1956 Lee and Yang pointed out that parity conservation had never been tested in weak interactions44; within months, Wu and collaborators found that electrons from polarized cobalt-60 nuclei are emitted preferentially in one direction, so nature distinguishes left from right45. The combined CPT symmetry, however, is guaranteed by very general principles of relativistic quantum field theory46. Symmetry can also be spontaneously broken: the laws are symmetric but the ground state is not, as in a magnet choosing one direction, or the Higgs field in the Standard Model25.

SECTION 8Physical dimensions and scales

Dimensions and units

A physical quantity = numerical value × unit. Its dimension records how it depends on base quantities. The SI has seven base quantities: time (T), length (L), mass (M), electric current (I), thermodynamic temperature (Θ), amount of substance (N) and luminous intensity (J). Since 20 May 2019, all seven SI base units are defined by fixing the numerical values of seven defining constants, including the speed of light c, Planck's constant h, the elementary charge e and Boltzmann's constant kB6.

Dimensional analysis

Every valid physical equation must be dimensionally homogeneous: you cannot add metres to seconds. Buckingham's Π theorem states that a relation among n quantities involving k independent dimensions can be rewritten as a relation among n − k dimensionless groups47. This often gives the form of an answer before any detailed theory.

Worked example 8.1 · Pendulum period from dimensions alone

Suppose the period T of a pendulum depends on length L [L], mass m [M], gravitational acceleration g [L T−2] and amplitude θ0 [dimensionless]. Try T ∝ La mb gc. Matching dimensions: M: b = 0; L: a + c = 0; T: −2c = 1. So c = −½, a = ½ and

T = f(θ0) √(L/g)(8.1)

Here 5 quantities and 3 dimensions give 5 − 3 = 2 dimensionless groups, gT2/L and θ0, exactly as Buckingham's theorem predicts47. The period does not depend on the mass, and the full theory (Section 3) shows f → 2π for small amplitudes. The script buckingham_pi.py in Section 13 finds these groups automatically.

Scales: the range of physics

Physics spans an extraordinary range of scales. The Planck length, ℓP = √(ħG/c3) ≈ 1.616 × 10−35 m, is where quantum gravity is expected to matter48. The proton charge radius is about 0.84 × 10−15 m and the Bohr radius about 5.29 × 10−11 m48. At the other end, the observable universe has a radius of about 46 billion light-years (≈ 4.4 × 1026 m)49, and its age is 13.79 ± 0.02 billion years50. Planck length to the diameter of the observable universe is a factor of about 1062.

Worked example 8.2 · A Fermi estimate

How many atoms are in a 70 kg human? The body is mostly water (molar mass 18 g mol−1, 3 atoms per molecule). Moles of "water-like" matter ≈ 70 000 g / 18 g mol−1 ≈ 3.9 × 103 mol. Molecules ≈ 3.9 × 103 × 6.02 × 1023 ≈ 2.3 × 1027; atoms ≈ 3 × 2.3 × 1027 ≈ 7 × 1027. The exact composition barely changes the order of magnitude, which is the point of a Fermi estimate.

SECTION 9Classical versus modern physics

Classical physics means mechanics (Newton, Lagrange, Hamilton), thermodynamics and statistical mechanics, and electromagnetism (Maxwell), as they stood at the end of the 19th century. Modern physics begins around 1900 with quantum theory and relativity. In a lecture delivered in 1900, Lord Kelvin spoke of two "clouds" over the dynamical theory of heat and light51: the failure to detect motion through the ether, and the failure of equipartition of energy. They foreshadowed relativity and quantum theory respectively. Planck's quantum hypothesis for black-body radiation52, Einstein's light quanta53 and special relativity12, and Rutherford's nuclear atom54 followed within a dozen years.

Worked example 9.1 · Relativity in your pocket (GPS)

GPS satellites orbit at about 3.87 km s−1 at an altitude of about 20 200 km. Special relativity makes their clocks run slow by about 7 μs per day; the weaker gravitational potential at altitude makes them run fast by about 46 μs per day. The net effect, about +38 μs per day, would cause position errors growing by roughly 10 km per day if uncorrected, so the satellite clock frequencies are offset before launch55. Modern physics is everyday engineering.

Worked example 9.2 · Muons that live too long

At rest, a muon's mean lifetime is τ = 2.197 μs25. In the CERN muon storage ring, muons circulated with Lorentz factor γ ≈ 29.3. Special relativity predicts a laboratory lifetime of γτ ≈ 29.3 × 2.197 ≈ 64.4 μs, which is what was measured, confirming time dilation to about one part in a thousand56.

SECTION 10Relationships with mathematics, chemistry, biology, and engineering

Mathematics

Mathematics is the language of physics. Eugene Wigner called its effectiveness "unreasonable": structures developed for their own sake, such as complex numbers, group theory or Riemannian geometry, turn out to describe nature57. The traffic runs both ways. Calculus was created largely to do mechanics2; Hilbert's sixth problem (1900) asked for the axiomatization of physics58; Noether's theorem is both mathematics and physics42. The difference is in the final authority: a mathematical theorem is settled by proof, a physical law by experiment.

Chemistry

Chemistry is, in principle, applied quantum mechanics of electrons and nuclei32. The Heitler–London treatment of the hydrogen molecule explained the covalent bond31, and physical techniques such as X-ray diffraction, nuclear magnetic resonance and mass spectrometry are the daily tools of chemists. In practice chemistry keeps its own concepts (valence, electronegativity, reaction mechanism), a good example of epistemological emergence (Section 5).

Biology

Schrödinger's What Is Life? asked how physics could account for the stability of hereditary information59, a question that inspired several founders of molecular biology. The double-helix structure of DNA was deduced using X-ray diffraction, a physical method60,61. Hodgkin and Huxley's model of the nerve impulse treats the neuron membrane as an electrical circuit62. Physics provides imaging (MRI14, X-rays, electron microscopy) and modelling tools; biology provides physics with hard problems in non-equilibrium, self-organizing matter.

Engineering

Engineering applies physical principles to design systems that meet human needs under constraints of cost, safety and reliability. Carnot's analysis of steam engines founded thermodynamics63; the transistor came from solid-state physics13; GPS depends on relativity55. Engineers lean heavily on dimensionless numbers (Reynolds, Mach, Biot) from dimensional analysis47 to scale models to full size.

Exercise 11.1 ★ · Objectives of physics

For each of the following, say which objective of physics (describe, explain, predict, unify, enable) it illustrates best: (a) Maxwell showing that light is an electromagnetic wave; (b) measuring the speed of sound in air at 20 °C; (c) the 2016 detection of gravitational waves, a century after general relativity; (d) the invention of the transistor.

Show solution

(a) Unify: optics, electricity and magnetism become one theory8. (b) Describe: a precise measurement of a phenomenon. (c) Predict: the waves were a consequence of general relativity long before LIGO observed them11. (d) Enable: solid-state physics turned into technology13. Many examples fit more than one objective; (c) also tests and so strengthens the explanation.

Exercise 11.2 ★ · Law, theory or model?

Classify each: (a) PV = nRT; (b) quantum electrodynamics; (c) treating the Earth as a uniform sphere to find g; (d) "the entropy of an isolated system never decreases".

Show solution

(a) A law (the ideal gas law), which also rests on a model of point-like non-interacting molecules. (b) A theory: a full framework from which many laws follow. (c) A model or idealization of a particular system. (d) A law/principle: the second law of thermodynamics, which constrains all processes.

Exercise 11.3 ★★ · Dimensional analysis of a falling drop

A small sphere of radius r falls at constant terminal speed v through a viscous fluid of viscosity η (unit Pa s = kg m−1 s−1). Assume the drag force depends only on η, r and v. Use dimensional analysis to find how the drag force scales.

Show solution

Let F ∝ ηarbvc. Dimensions: [F] = M L T−2, [η] = M L−1 T−1, [r] = L, [v] = L T−1.

M: a = 1. T: −a − c = −2, so c = 1. L: −a + b + c = 1, so b = 1. Hence F = C η r v, with a dimensionless constant C. Solving the fluid equations gives C = 6π (Stokes' law). Dimensional analysis gives the form; a theory gives the number.

Exercise 11.4 ★★ · When is quantum mechanics needed?

Compute the de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p for (a) a 0.145 kg baseball at 40 m s−1, and (b) an electron with kinetic energy 100 eV (non-relativistic). Compare each with the size of the object or of an atom (≈ 10−10 m) and comment.

Show solution

(a) p = 0.145 × 40 = 5.8 kg m s−1; λ = 6.626 × 10−34 / 5.8 ≈ 1.1 × 10−34 m, some 33 orders of magnitude smaller than the ball. Wave effects are unobservable, so classical mechanics is perfect.

(b) p = √(2meE) = √(2 × 9.109 × 10−31 × 100 × 1.602 × 10−19) ≈ 5.40 × 10−24 kg m s−1; λ ≈ 1.23 × 10−10 m. This is the size of an atom, so electrons of this energy diffract from crystals and quantum mechanics is essential. Constants from the SI and CODATA6,48.

Exercise 11.5 ★★ · Emergent or fundamental?

Label each as fundamental (in the current Standard Model plus general relativity) or emergent, and justify: (a) the electron's charge; (b) the viscosity of honey; (c) the colour of gold; (d) the ferromagnetism of iron; (e) the speed of light in vacuum.

Show solution

(a) Fundamental: a property of an elementary particle25. (b) Emergent: a collective property of interacting molecules. (c) Emergent: it depends on electron band structure in the metal, with relativistic effects in the gold atom. (d) Emergent: aligned electron spins in a crystal, an example of spontaneous symmetry breaking. (e) Fundamental: a constant of spacetime, now fixed exactly by the SI definition of the metre6.

Exercise 11.6 ★★ · The prediction horizon

In a chaotic system, an initial error grows as δ(t) = δ0eλt. Suppose λ = 0.9 per unit time (close to the Lorenz system's largest Lyapunov exponent37) and a forecast is useless once δ reaches 1. (a) What is the horizon for δ0 = 10−3? (b) How much longer can we predict if we improve the initial measurement a million-fold, to δ0 = 10−9?

Show solution

Horizon t* = (1/λ) ln(1/δ0). (a) t* = ln(103)/0.9 = 6.91/0.9 ≈ 7.7 time units. (b) t* = ln(109)/0.9 = 20.7/0.9 ≈ 23.0 time units. A million-fold improvement in precision only triples the horizon. This logarithmic payoff is a fundamental reason why detailed weather forecasts have a limited range, however good the observations36.

Exercise 11.7 ★ · Probability in radioactive decay

Carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5730 years. (a) What is the probability that one given 14C nucleus survives 11 460 years? (b) Of 1012 nuclei, about how many remain after that time?

Show solution

(a) 11 460 years is two half-lives, so the survival probability is (½)2 = 0.25. (b) About 0.25 × 1012 = 2.5 × 1011, with a statistical spread of about √(N p(1 − p)) ≈ 4 × 105, tiny compared with N. The individual event is random; the ensemble is predictable.

Exercise 11.8 ★★★ · Noether in a central potential

A particle of mass m moves in a plane under a potential V(r) that depends only on the distance r from the origin. The Lagrangian in polar coordinates is L = ½m(ṙ2 + r2φ̇2) − V(r). (a) Which symmetry does L have? (b) Find the conserved quantity. (c) Which famous law of planetary motion follows?

Show solution

(a) L does not depend on φ, so it is invariant under rotations φ → φ + a.

(b) The Euler–Lagrange equation for φ reads d/dt(∂L/∂φ̇) = ∂L/∂φ = 0, so ℓ = m r2φ̇ is conserved: the angular momentum, as Noether's theorem predicts43,23.

(c) The area swept per unit time is dA/dt = ½r2φ̇ = ℓ/(2m), a constant. This is Kepler's second law (equal areas in equal times), valid for any central force, not only gravity.

Exercise 11.9 ★★ · Orders of magnitude

(a) How many proton radii (0.84 × 10−15 m) fit across a hydrogen atom (diameter ≈ 2 × the Bohr radius, 1.06 × 10−10 m)? (b) How long does light take to cross the observable universe's radius (4.4 × 1026 m)? Why is this longer than the age of the universe?

Show solution

(a) 1.06 × 10−10 / 0.84 × 10−15 ≈ 1.3 × 105. The atom is over 100 000 times larger than its nucleus; it is mostly empty space48,54.

(b) t = 4.4 × 1026 / 3.0 × 108 ≈ 1.5 × 1018 s ≈ 46 billion years. This exceeds the 13.8-billion-year age50 because space has expanded while the light was travelling: the 46-billion-light-year radius is the present distance of the most distant matter whose light reaches us49.

Exercise 11.10 ★★ · GPS special-relativistic drift

A GPS clock moves at v = 3.87 km s−1. Using the low-speed approximation Δt/t ≈ v2/(2c2), how many microseconds does it lose per day relative to a clock at rest? About how far does light travel in that time?

Show solution

v2/(2c2) = (3870)2/(2 × (2.998 × 108)2) ≈ 8.33 × 10−11. Times 86 400 s gives ≈ 7.2 × 10−6 s = 7.2 μs per day, matching Ashby's 7 μs55. Light travels 3 × 108 × 7.2 × 10−6 ≈ 2.2 km in that time, so ignoring even this smaller effect would ruin positioning within a day.

Exercise 11.11 ★★★ · Scaling a model in engineering

The Reynolds number Re = ρvL/η measures inertial versus viscous forces. (a) Show that Re is dimensionless. (b) A 1:10 scale model of a car is tested in the same air. At what speed must the model move to match a full car travelling at 30 m s−1? Is this practical, and what do engineers do instead?

Show solution

(a) [ρ][v][L]/[η] = (M L−3)(L T−1)(L)/(M L−1 T−1) = 1, dimensionless47.

(b) Matching Re with the same ρ and η needs vmodelLmodel = vcarLcar, so vmodel = 10 × 30 = 300 m s−1, close to the speed of sound. That would also introduce compressibility effects absent at full scale (a mismatched Mach number). Engineers instead use pressurized or cryogenic wind tunnels (raising ρ/η), larger models, or accept partial similarity and correct with theory and simulation.

buckingham_pi.py · dimensionless groups (Section 8)
"""Buckingham Pi: find dimensionless groups from a dimension matrix.
Pure standard library (exact rational arithmetic)."""
from fractions import Fraction as F

def nullspace(rows):
    """Return a basis of the null space of an integer matrix (list of rows)."""
    m = [[F(x) for x in r] for r in rows]
    nrow, ncol = len(m), len(m[0])
    pivots, r = [], 0
    for c in range(ncol):                      # Gauss-Jordan elimination
        p = next((i for i in range(r, nrow) if m[i][c] != 0), None)
        if p is None:
            continue
        m[r], m[p] = m[p], m[r]
        m[r] = [x / m[r][c] for x in m[r]]
        for i in range(nrow):
            if i != r and m[i][c] != 0:
                m[i] = [a - m[i][c] * b for a, b in zip(m[i], m[r])]
        pivots.append(c); r += 1
    free = [c for c in range(ncol) if c not in pivots]
    basis = []
    for f in free:
        v = [F(0)] * ncol; v[f] = F(1)
        for i, pc in enumerate(pivots):
            v[pc] = -m[i][f]
        basis.append(v)
    return basis

"""Sensitive dependence on initial conditions in the Lorenz (1963) system."""
import math

def lorenz(s, sigma=10.0, rho=28.0, beta=8.0/3.0):
    x, y, z = s
    return (sigma * (y - x), x * (rho - z) - y, x * y - beta * z)

def rk4(f, s, h):
    k1 = f(s)
    k2 = f(tuple(a + h/2*b for a, b in zip(s, k1)))
    k3 = f(tuple(a + h/2*b for a, b in zip(s, k2)))
    k4 = f(tuple(a + h*b for a, b in zip(s, k3)))
    return tuple(a + h/6*(b + 2*c + 2*d + e) for a, b, c, d, e in zip(s, k1, k2, k3, k4))

"""Deterministic law for the ensemble, probabilistic law for the individual:
Monte Carlo simulation of radioactive decay."""
import math, random

random.seed(1)
half_life = 1.0                      # in arbitrary time units
lam = math.log(2) / half_life        # decay constant
dt, N0 = 0.01, 100_000
p = 1 - math.exp(-lam * dt)          # probability that one nucleus decays in dt
alive, t = N0, 0.0
for step in range(1, 401):

SECTION 14Summary and key terms

  • Physics seeks general, quantitative, testable laws of matter, energy, space and time; experiment is the final judge.
  • Laws summarize regularities; theories are frameworks from which laws follow; models and idealizations make theories usable for particular systems, with controllable error.
  • Theories are linked by limits (the cube of c, ħ, G); a new theory must reproduce the old in the old domain.
  • Fundamental laws describe elementary constituents; emergent laws describe collective behaviour and are often insensitive to microscopic detail (universality).
  • Determinism does not imply predictability (chaos); quantum mechanics makes probability fundamental for outcomes; relativity defines causality by light cones.
  • Continuous symmetries imply conservation laws (Noether); discrete symmetries can be violated (parity) or spontaneously broken.
  • Dimensional analysis constrains equations; physics spans about 62 orders of magnitude in length.
  • Modern physics (quantum theory, relativity) contains classical physics as a limit and is part of everyday technology.
  • Physics exchanges methods and problems with mathematics, chemistry, biology and engineering in both directions.
