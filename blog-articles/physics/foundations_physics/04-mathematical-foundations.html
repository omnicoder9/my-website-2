Why physics speaks mathematics

In 1623 Galileo wrote that the "book" of nature "is written in the language of mathematics, and its characters are triangles, circles, and other geometric figures, without which it is humanly impossible to understand a single word of it" [1]. More than three centuries later Eugene Wigner called the success of mathematics in physics "unreasonable," a gift "which we neither understand nor deserve" [2]. In 1900 David Hilbert listed the axiomatic treatment of physics as the sixth of his famous problems for the coming century [3]. That problem is still only partly solved, but every working physicist relies on the same toolkit, and that toolkit is the subject of this article.

The toolkit is not a list of separate subjects. Each tool sits on the ones before it. Trigonometry feeds Fourier analysis. Linear algebra becomes tensor calculus, and tensor calculus becomes differential geometry. Calculus grows into differential equations, the calculus of variations and, in the end, numerical simulation. The map below shows how the twenty topics depend on one another and where each one shows up in physics. The standard references for the whole field are the textbooks by Arfken, Weber and Harris [4], Boas [5], and Riley, Hobson and Bence [6], and most of the results below can be found in all three.

1Algebra and trigonometry

Algebra is the grammar of physics. A physical law is a relation between quantities, and solving it means rearranging it. Every symbol carries a dimension, and dimensional consistency is the first check on any result. The SI defines seven base units, and every other unit, the radian included, is built from them. The radian is classed as a dimensionless derived unit, a ratio of two lengths [10].

Polynomials, exponentials and logarithms

The quadratic formula $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$ solves $ax^2+bx+c=0$. It reappears in the characteristic equation of the damped oscillator (Section 8), where the sign of $b^2-4ac$ decides whether the motion oscillates. Exponentials describe processes whose rate is proportional to their size, such as radioactive decay $N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$ and Boltzmann factors $e^{-E/k_BT}$. Logarithms turn products into sums, which is why entropy, decibels and magnitudes are logarithmic.

Trigonometry

The trigonometric functions are defined on the unit circle (Figure 1.1). Every periodic phenomenon in physics, from waves and alternating currents to orbits and quantum phases, is built from them. The identities follow most easily from Euler's formula, which Euler set out in his Introductio of 1748 [11]:

$$e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos(\alpha\pm\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta\mp\sin\alpha\sin\beta,\qquad \sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1.$$

Worked example: the small-angle approximation

For a pendulum, $\ddot\theta = -(g/\ell)\sin\theta$. Since $\sin\theta = \theta - \theta^3/6 + \dots$, the ratio $\sin\theta/\theta \approx 1-\theta^2/6$ differs from 1 by less than 1% as long as $\theta^2/6 \lt 0.01$, that is, $\theta \lesssim 0.245\ \text{rad} \approx 14^\circ$. In that range the equation becomes the linear oscillator $\ddot\theta=-\omega_0^2\theta$ with $\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}$ and period $T=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$, which does not depend on amplitude.

2Geometry and coordinate systems

Euclid's Elements (around 300 BCE) built geometry from axioms [14], and its fifth postulate, on parallel lines, was later the key to non-Euclidean geometry (Section 14). René Descartes's La Géométrie (1637) joined geometry to algebra by naming points with numbers [15]. That is the step that made geometry something you could calculate with.

Choosing coordinates to fit the symmetry

Physics is the same whatever coordinates you use, but the algebra is not. A problem with spherical symmetry, such as the Coulomb field or the hydrogen atom, becomes simple in spherical coordinates:

$$x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi,\quad y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi,\quad z=r\cos\theta,\qquad dV = r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi .$$

The factor $r^2\sin\theta$ is the Jacobian determinant of the change of variables (Section 6). Cylindrical coordinates $(s,\phi,z)$ have $dV=s\,ds\,d\phi\,dz$. All of these are orthogonal curvilinear coordinates, whose scale factors $h_i$ set the line element $ds^2=\sum_i h_i^2\,dq_i^2$ [4].

Worked example: volume of a sphere and moment of inertia

$V=\int_0^R\!\int_0^\pi\!\int_0^{2\pi} r^2\sin\theta\,d\phi\,d\theta\,dr = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3$. For a uniform solid sphere of mass $M$, the distance from the $z$ axis is $r\sin\theta$, so $I_z=\rho\int (r\sin\theta)^2\,dV = \tfrac{2}{5}MR^2$.

3Vectors and vector spaces

A vector has magnitude and direction and adds by the parallelogram rule. Force, velocity and electric field are vectors. The modern notation, with the dot and cross products, was worked out in the 1880s by J. Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside. They took it from Hamilton's quaternions by keeping the useful parts [16], and E. B. Wilson's 1901 book from Gibbs's lectures made it standard [17].

$$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = \sum_i a_ib_i = |\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta,\qquad \mathbf a\times\mathbf b = \begin{vmatrix}\hat{\mathbf x}&\hat{\mathbf y}&\hat{\mathbf z}\\ a_x&a_y&a_z\\ b_x&b_y&b_z\end{vmatrix},\quad |\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\sin\theta .$$

In physics, work is $W=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d$, torque is $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F$, and the magnetic force is $\mathbf F=q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$. With index notation, the Levi-Civita symbol $\varepsilon_{ijk}$ and the Kronecker delta $\delta_{ij}$, vector identities become short calculations: $(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)_i=\varepsilon_{ijk}a_jb_k$ and $\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon_{ilm}=\delta_{jl}\delta_{km}-\delta_{jm}\delta_{kl}$.

Abstract vector spaces

Giuseppe Peano gave the first axiomatic definition of a vector space in 1888 [18]. It requires a set closed under addition and scalar multiplication that satisfies eight axioms (associativity, commutativity, a zero vector, inverses, distributivity and so on) [19]. Arrows in space qualify, but so do polynomials, solutions of a linear ODE and square-integrable wavefunctions. That generality is why quantum mechanics is linear algebra on a Hilbert space, a complete inner-product space, as von Neumann made rigorous in 1932 [20]. Dirac's bra–ket notation $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ writes the inner product [21].

Key ideas: linear independence, basis and dimension; the inner product $\langle u,v\rangle$, which gives norms and angles; the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\langle u,v\rangle|\le\|u\|\|v\|$; and Gram–Schmidt orthonormalization, which turns any basis into an orthonormal one.

4Linear algebra

A linear map $T$ satisfies $T(\alpha u+\beta v)=\alpha Tu+\beta Tv$. Once a basis is chosen it becomes a matrix. Arthur Cayley set out matrix algebra in 1858 [23]. In 1925 Max Born saw that Heisenberg's new "quantum-theoretical" multiplication rule was matrix multiplication, and with Pascual Jordan he founded matrix mechanics [24].

Core results

  • Systems $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ have a unique solution when $\det A\ne0$. Gaussian elimination (LU factorization) costs $O(n^3)$ operations [25].
  • Eigenvalues: $A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$, found from $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$. Figure 4.1 shows that eigenvectors are the directions the map only stretches.
  • Spectral theorem: a real symmetric (or complex Hermitian) matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis, so $A=Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}$. Quantum observables are Hermitian operators for this reason: measured values are real [19,26].
  • Determinant and trace: $\det A=\prod\lambda_i$ and $\operatorname{tr}A=\sum\lambda_i$. The determinant is the factor by which the map scales volume.
  • Singular value decomposition $A=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$ exists for any matrix. It is the basis of least squares, principal component analysis and data compression [25].
  • Commutators $[A,B]=AB-BA$. In quantum mechanics $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$.

Worked example: normal modes of coupled oscillators

Two masses $m$ are joined to the walls and to each other by springs $k$. Newton's law reads $M\ddot{\mathbf x}=-K\mathbf x$ with $K=k\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Trying $\mathbf x=\mathbf v e^{i\omega t}$ gives the generalized eigenproblem $K\mathbf v=\omega^2M\mathbf v$. The modes are in-phase motion at $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$ and out-of-phase motion at $\omega=\sqrt{3k/m}$ [27].

Normal modes by eigen-decomposition (NumPy)linalg.py
import numpy as np

# Coupled oscillators: two equal masses m joined to walls and each other by springs k.
# Equation of motion  M x'' = -K x  ->  eigenproblem  K v = w^2 M v
m, k = 1.0, 1.0
K = np.array([[2*k, -k],
              [-k, 2*k]])
M = np.diag([m, m])
w2, modes = np.linalg.eigh(np.linalg.solve(M, K))
print("normal-mode frequencies w =", np.sqrt(w2))   # sqrt(k/m), sqrt(3k/m)
print("mode shapes (columns):\n", modes.round(4))
print("orthonormal? ", np.allclose(modes.T @ modes, np.eye(2)))
Output
normal-mode frequencies w = [1.         1.73205081]
mode shapes (columns):

 [-0.7071  0.7071]]

5Differential and integral calculus

Newton and Leibniz independently invented calculus in the late seventeenth century. Newton's Principia (1687) presented its results mostly in geometric form [13,28], while Leibniz's notation, $dy/dx$ and $\int$, is the one we still use. The rigorous foundation of limits came in the nineteenth century from Cauchy and Weierstrass [29].

The derivative and the integral

$$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h},\qquad \int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x,\qquad \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a).$$

The last identity, the fundamental theorem of calculus, links rates and accumulations. In kinematics $v=\dot x$ and $a=\ddot x$. Work is $W=\int F\,dx$. Charge is $Q=\int I\,dt$.

Taylor series

A smooth function is approximated near $a$ by $f(x)=\sum_{n}\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n$. Physics runs on truncated Taylor series: small oscillations (expand a potential to second order about a minimum), the binomial expansion $(1+x)^n\approx1+nx$ (used for example in $\gamma\approx1+v^2/2c^2$), and perturbation theory (Figure 5.1).

Techniques worth knowing

Substitution, integration by parts $\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$, partial fractions, differentiating under the integral sign (Feynman's favourite trick), and the gamma function $\Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty t^{z-1}e^{-t}dt$ with $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$ [22]. Stirling's approximation $\ln n!\approx n\ln n-n$ is used throughout statistical mechanics [30].

6Multivariable calculus

Physical fields depend on several variables, such as $T(x,y,z,t)$. The partial derivative $\partial f/\partial x$ holds the other variables fixed. The total differential $df=\sum_i(\partial f/\partial x_i)dx_i$ is central to thermodynamics, where you must always say what is held fixed: $(\partial U/\partial S)_V=T$ and $(\partial U/\partial V)_S=-p$ [30].

Key tools

  • Chain rule: $\dfrac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf x(t),t)=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\dot{\mathbf x}\cdot\nabla f$. This is the convective (material) derivative of fluid mechanics.
  • Equality of mixed partials (Schwarz–Clairaut): $\partial_x\partial_yf=\partial_y\partial_xf$ for smooth $f$. It gives the Maxwell relations, for example $(\partial T/\partial V)_S=-(\partial p/\partial S)_V$.
  • Jacobian: $dx\,dy=\left|\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|du\,dv$.
  • Constrained extrema by Lagrange multipliers: $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$. Maximizing entropy subject to fixed energy gives the Boltzmann distribution, and the multiplier turns out to be $\beta=1/k_BT$ [30].
  • Multivariable Taylor expansion with the Hessian $H_{ij}=\partial_i\partial_jf$. A minimum needs $H$ to be positive definite, which links back to the eigenvalues of Section 4.

7Vector calculus

Vector calculus is the language of classical field theory: electromagnetism, fluids, elasticity and gravitation. Everything is built from the del operator $\nabla=\hat{\mathbf x}\partial_x+\hat{\mathbf y}\partial_y+\hat{\mathbf z}\partial_z$ [31,32].

7.1 Gradient

For a scalar field $\phi$, the gradient $\nabla\phi$ points in the direction of steepest increase, and its size is the rate of increase. The directional derivative along a unit vector $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is $\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\phi$. The gradient is perpendicular to the level surfaces $\phi=$ const (Figure 7.1). Conservative forces are gradients, $\mathbf F=-\nabla V$, and the electrostatic field is $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$.

7.2 Divergence

$\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\partial_xF_x+\partial_yF_y+\partial_zF_z$ is the outward flux per unit volume, which measures how much a point acts as a source or a sink. Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\varepsilon_0$ says charges are the sources of the electric field. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ says there are no magnetic monopoles. In fluid flow, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0$ means the fluid is incompressible.

7.3 Curl

$\nabla\times\mathbf F$ is the circulation per unit area, the local rotation of the field. For a rigid rotation $\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r$, $\nabla\times\mathbf v=2\boldsymbol\omega$. Ampère–Maxwell: $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E$. Faraday: $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B$.

Two identities hold for every smooth field: $\nabla\times(\nabla\phi)=\mathbf 0$ and $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)=0$. They are why we can write $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$ in electrostatics and $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ in general. The Laplacian $\nabla^2=\nabla\cdot\nabla$ appears in nearly every PDE of Section 9.

import sympy as sp
from sympy.vector import CoordSys3D, gradient, divergence, curl

R = CoordSys3D('R')
x, y, z = R.x, R.y, R.z

phi = x**2*y + sp.sin(z)                 # scalar field
F   = -y*R.i + x*R.j + z*R.k             # vector field

print("grad phi      =", gradient(phi))
print("div F         =", divergence(F))
print("curl F        =", curl(F))
print("curl grad phi =", curl(gradient(phi)))         # always 0
print("div curl F    =", sp.simplify(divergence(curl(F))))  # always 0

7.4 Line and surface integrals and the integral theorems

A line integral $\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$ adds up the tangential component of a field along a curve, as in work or EMF. A surface integral $\iint_S\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf a$ measures flux through a surface. They are related by the integral theorems, all special cases of the generalized Stokes theorem $\int_{\partial\Omega}\omega=\int_\Omega d\omega$ (Section 14):

$$\underbrace{\int_a^b\nabla\phi\cdot d\mathbf r=\phi(b)-\phi(a)}_{\text{gradient theorem}},\qquad \underbrace{\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf a=\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV}_{\text{divergence (Gauss) theorem}},\qquad \underbrace{\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot d\mathbf a}_{\text{Stokes' theorem}}.$$

Worked example: from integral laws to Maxwell's equations

Gauss's law in integral form is $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a=Q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0=\frac1{\varepsilon_0}\int\rho\,dV$. The divergence theorem turns the left side into $\int\nabla\cdot\mathbf E\,dV$. Since this holds for every volume, the integrands must be equal: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\varepsilon_0$. Applying Stokes' theorem to Faraday's law in the same way gives $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B$. Taking the curl of Faraday's law in vacuum and using $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E$ gives the wave equation $\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t^2\mathbf E$, with wave speed $c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$. This is Maxwell's 1865 conclusion that light is an electromagnetic wave [32,33].

8Ordinary differential equations

Newton's second law $m\ddot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf F(\mathbf x,\dot{\mathbf x},t)$ is an ODE, so most of classical mechanics consists of solving ODEs. An $n$th-order ODE can always be rewritten as $n$ first-order equations $\dot{\mathbf y}=\mathbf f(\mathbf y,t)$. When $\mathbf f$ is Lipschitz, the Picard–Lindelöf theorem guarantees a unique solution for given initial data [36]. That is the mathematical form of classical determinism.

Linear ODEs with constant coefficients

The damped oscillator $\ddot x+2\zeta\omega_0\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0$ with the trial solution $x=e^{rt}$ gives $r=-\zeta\omega_0\pm\omega_0\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}$. The discriminant from Section 1 sets the regime (Figure 8.1): underdamped for $\zeta\lt1$, critically damped at $\zeta=1$, which returns to zero fastest without overshoot, and overdamped for $\zeta\gt1$. A periodic driving force adds a particular solution and a resonance near $\omega\approx\omega_0$ [5,27].

Series solutions and special functions

Variable-coefficient equations such as Legendre's $(1-x^2)y''-2xy'+\ell(\ell+1)y=0$ and Bessel's $x^2y''+xy'+(x^2-\nu^2)y=0$ are solved by power series (the method of Frobenius). Their solutions, Legendre polynomials, Bessel functions and Hermite polynomials, are the "special functions" of mathematical physics. The authoritative modern compendium is the NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions [22]. Many of them come from Sturm–Liouville problems, whose eigenfunctions form orthogonal bases, which is linear algebra again [4].

Nonlinear dynamics and the phase plane

The full pendulum $\ddot\theta=-\sin\theta$ cannot be solved in elementary functions, but its phase portrait (Figure 8.2) shows everything qualitatively. There are centres at $\theta=0$, saddles at $\theta=\pm\pi$, and a separatrix with energy $E=1$ that divides back-and-forth swinging from full rotation [36]. In three or more dimensions a deterministic system can be chaotic. Edward Lorenz's 1963 model of convection (Figure 8.3) showed sensitive dependence on initial conditions [37].

Separation of variables

Try $u(x,t)=X(x)T(t)$. For the heat equation on $[0,L]$ with $u=0$ at both ends, this gives $X''=-k^2X$ and $\dot T=-\alpha k^2T$, so $X=\sin(n\pi x/L)$ and $T=e^{-\alpha(n\pi/L)^2t}$. The initial condition is matched by a Fourier sine series (Section 11):

$$u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,e^{-\alpha n^2\pi^2t/L^2},\qquad b_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lu(x,0)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx .$$

High-$n$ modes decay much faster, so sharp features smooth out first (Figure 9.1). The same method on a string gives standing waves $\sin(n\pi x/L)\cos(n\pi ct/L)$ (Figure 9.2). In spherical coordinates, the angular part of Laplace's equation gives the spherical harmonics $Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi)$, which label the orbitals of the hydrogen atom [22,39].

Green's functions

For a linear operator $\mathcal L$, the solution of $\mathcal Lu=f$ is $u=\int G(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')f(\mathbf x')\,d\mathbf x'$, where $\mathcal LG=\delta(\mathbf x-\mathbf x')$. For the Laplacian in three dimensions, $G=-1/(4\pi|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|)$, which is Coulomb's law. The Dirac delta was introduced by Dirac [21], and Laurent Schwartz's theory of distributions later made it rigorous [38].

import numpy as np

# FTCS scheme for u_t = alpha u_xx on [0,1], u=0 at both ends, u(x,0)=sin(pi x)
alpha, nx = 1.0, 51
dx = 1/(nx - 1)
x = np.linspace(0, 1, nx)

def run(r, t_end=0.1):
    dt = r*dx**2/alpha                      # r = alpha dt / dx^2
    u = np.sin(np.pi*x)
    for _ in range(int(round(t_end/dt))):
        u[1:-1] += r*(u[2:] - 2*u[1:-1] + u[:-2])
    exact = np.exp(-np.pi**2*alpha*t_end)*np.sin(np.pi*x)
    return np.max(np.abs(u - exact))

for r in (0.25, 0.5, 0.52):
    print(f"r = {r}: max error = {run(r):.2e}")

10Complex numbers and complex analysis

Complex numbers first appeared in sixteenth-century work on cubic equations. Cardano's Ars Magna (1545) contains them, and Bombelli (1572) gave rules for calculating with them [13]. Euler introduced the symbol $i$ for $\sqrt{-1}$ [13]. A complex number $z=x+iy=re^{i\theta}$ is a point in the plane (Figure 10.1). Multiplying by $e^{i\alpha}$ rotates it by $\alpha$, and that is why phasors make AC circuits and waves easy to handle [42].

Analytic functions

A function $f=u+iv$ is analytic (holomorphic) where it has a complex derivative. That requires the Cauchy–Riemann equations $u_x=v_y$, $u_y=-v_x$, which imply $\nabla^2u=\nabla^2v=0$. Real and imaginary parts of analytic functions are therefore solutions of Laplace's equation, which makes them useful for two-dimensional electrostatics and ideal fluid flow by conformal mapping [42,43].

Cauchy's theorem and residues

$$\oint_C f(z)\,dz=0\ \ (f\text{ analytic inside }C),\qquad f(a)=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{z-a}\,dz,\qquad \oint_Cf\,dz=2\pi i\sum_k\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_k}f .$$

For a simple pole, $\operatorname{Res}_{z=a}f=\lim_{z\to a}(z-a)f(z)$. The residue theorem evaluates real integrals that are hard to do otherwise. On the contour in Figure 10.1 the arc contributes nothing as $R\to\infty$, so $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=2\pi i\cdot\frac1{2i}=\pi$ [43]. In physics, the analytic structure of response functions in the complex frequency plane is the content of causality and the Kramers–Kronig relations, and the "$i\varepsilon$" prescriptions of quantum field theory choose contours around poles [4].

11Fourier analysis and transforms

Joseph Fourier claimed, in a memoir submitted in 1807 and in his Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822), that "arbitrary" functions could be written as sums of sines and cosines, and he used the idea to solve the heat equation [44]. The idea is now everywhere: optics, acoustics, quantum mechanics, signal processing and crystallography all rely on it [45].

Series and transform

$$f(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_ne^{inx},\quad c_n=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)e^{-inx}dx;\qquad \tilde f(k)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)e^{-ikx}dx,\quad f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\tilde f(k)e^{ikx}dk .$$

Important properties:

  • Derivatives become multiplication: $\widetilde{f'}=ik\tilde f$. Linear constant-coefficient PDEs become algebraic equations.
  • Convolution theorem: $\widetilde{f*g}=\tilde f\,\tilde g$. Blurring by an instrument is a convolution, so it is undone in Fourier space.
  • Parseval/Plancherel: $\int|f|^2dx=\frac1{2\pi}\int|\tilde f|^2dk$. Energy is the same in both representations.
  • Uncertainty: $\Delta x\,\Delta k\ge\tfrac12$. With $p=\hbar k$ this is Heisenberg's $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ [26].

The discrete Fourier transform of $N$ samples takes $O(N^2)$ operations if done directly. Cooley and Tukey's 1965 algorithm does it in $O(N\log N)$ [46]. The same idea had already been used by Gauss around 1805, in unpublished notes on asteroid orbits [47]. The FFT made digital spectroscopy, MRI and modern telecommunications practical.

13Tensor algebra and calculus

A tensor is a multilinear object whose components change in a fixed way when coordinates change. Vectors and scalars are special cases. Tensors are needed whenever a physical relation between two vectors depends on direction. Stress relates a surface normal to the force on that surface, $t_i=\sigma_{ij}n_j$ (Figure 13.1). The inertia tensor relates angular velocity to angular momentum, $L_i=I_{ij}\omega_j$. Conductivity and permittivity tensors describe anisotropic crystals [4,6].

Transformation laws and index notation

Under a change of coordinates $x\to x'$, contravariant components transform as $V'^{\mu}=\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\nu}V^\nu$ and covariant ones as $W'_\mu=\frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial x'^\mu}W_\nu$. A rank-$(p,q)$ tensor carries $p$ upper and $q$ lower indices. Einstein summation means summing over any index that appears once up and once down. Einstein introduced the convention in his 1916 paper on general relativity [51]. The metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ raises and lowers indices and defines the interval $ds^2=g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu$. In special relativity $\eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$ (with $c=1$), and a quantity is Lorentz invariant exactly when all its indices are contracted [52].

Tensor calculus

In curvilinear coordinates or curved spacetime, the partial derivative of a vector is not a tensor, because the basis vectors change from point to point too. The covariant derivative corrects for this with Christoffel symbols, $\nabla_\mu V^\nu=\partial_\mu V^\nu+\Gamma^\nu_{\mu\lambda}V^\lambda$, with $\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}=\tfrac12g^{\lambda\sigma}(\partial_\mu g_{\sigma\nu}+\partial_\nu g_{\sigma\mu}-\partial_\sigma g_{\mu\nu})$. Ricci and Levi-Civita's "absolute differential calculus" of 1900 [53] gave Einstein the language he needed. He learned it with the help of his friend Marcel Grossmann [54].

The electromagnetic field tensor $F^{\mu\nu}$ combines $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$, and Maxwell's four equations become two: $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=\mu_0J^\nu$ and $\partial_{[\lambda}F_{\mu\nu]}=0$ [32,52].

14Differential geometry

Differential geometry studies curved spaces with calculus. Gauss showed in 1827 that the curvature of a surface can be measured from within the surface, without reference to the surrounding space. He called this his Theorema Egregium, the "remarkable theorem" [55]. In his 1854 habilitation lecture, Riemann generalized the idea to spaces of any dimension and suggested that the geometry of physical space was a question for experiment [56].

Manifolds, metrics and curvature

A manifold looks like $\mathbb R^n$ near each point. A metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ measures lengths and angles. A connection (the Christoffel symbols) defines parallel transport. Geodesics, the straightest possible curves, satisfy [52,57]

$$\frac{d^2x^\lambda}{d\tau^2}+\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}\frac{dx^\nu}{d\tau}=0 .$$

The Riemann tensor $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}$ measures the failure of parallel transport around a small loop to return a vector to itself (Figure 14.1). Contracting it gives the Ricci tensor and the scalar curvature. Einstein's field equations of November 1915 [51,54] state that spacetime curvature is set by energy and momentum:

$$R_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12Rg_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}.$$

For a closed surface the Gauss–Bonnet theorem gives $\int K\,dA=2\pi\chi$, so a local quantity, curvature, adds up to a topological invariant, the Euler characteristic $\chi$ [58]. In modern physics, differential forms, fibre bundles and connections are the setting for gauge theories and topological phases of matter [59]. The exterior derivative also unifies Section 7: gradient, curl and divergence are all $d$, and all the integral theorems are $\int_{\partial M}\omega=\int_Md\omega$.

15Probability and statistics

Probability enters physics in two ways. In statistical mechanics it describes our ignorance of the microscopic details of a system with around $10^{23}$ particles [30,62]. In quantum mechanics it seems to be built in: the Born rule says $|\psi|^2$ is a probability density [26]. Kolmogorov's 1933 axioms put probability on the footing of measure theory [63].

Essentials

  • Random variables: mean $\langle x\rangle=\int xp(x)\,dx$ and variance $\sigma^2=\langle x^2\rangle-\langle x\rangle^2$.
  • Key distributions: binomial (coin flips, random walks), Poisson $P(n)=\lambda^ne^{-\lambda}/n!$ (radioactive counts, photon arrivals), and Gaussian $\frac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}$.
  • Central limit theorem: a sum of many independent variables with finite variance tends to a Gaussian (Figure 15.1). This is why measurement errors are often normal, and why the mean of $N$ measurements has standard error $\sigma/\sqrt N$ [64].
  • Bayes' theorem $P(H|D)=P(D|H)P(H)/P(D)$ updates belief in a hypothesis as data arrive. It was published after Bayes's death, in 1763, by Richard Price [62,65].
  • Least squares and maximum likelihood: with Gaussian errors, maximizing the likelihood is the same as minimizing $\chi^2=\sum_i(y_i-f(x_i))^2/\sigma_i^2$. Legendre published least squares in 1805, and Gauss linked it to the normal distribution in 1809 [66].
  • Error propagation: $\sigma_f^2\approx\sum_i(\partial f/\partial x_i)^2\sigma_i^2$ for independent inputs. The international standard for reporting uncertainty is the GUM [67].

Worked example: diffusion as a random walk

A particle takes $N$ steps of $\pm\ell$. Its mean position stays at zero, but $\langle x^2\rangle=N\ell^2$, because the cross terms average to zero. With steps every $\tau$, $\langle x^2\rangle=2Dt$ with $D=\ell^2/2\tau$. This is Einstein's 1905 link between Brownian motion and diffusion, and the reason the heat equation of Section 9 has a statistical meaning [30].

16Calculus of variations

Ordinary calculus finds the points where a function is stationary. The calculus of variations finds the functions where a functional $S[y]=\int_a^bL(y,y',x)\,dx$ is stationary. Requiring $\delta S=0$ for all variations that vanish at the endpoints gives the Euler–Lagrange equation [69,70]:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}=0 .$$

In mechanics, with $L=T-V$, Hamilton's principle $\delta\int L\,dt=0$ gives Newton's laws in any coordinates [27,71]. Lagrange's Mécanique analytique (1788) built all of mechanics on this idea [72]. The principle reaches well beyond mechanics. Fermat's principle of least time gives Snell's law. Field theories are written as Lagrangian densities. In Feynman's path-integral formulation, each path contributes $e^{iS/\hbar}$, and the classical path is the one where the phases add up because $S$ is stationary [73].

Noether's theorem

Emmy Noether proved in 1918 that every continuous symmetry of the action gives a conserved quantity [74]. Invariance under time translation gives energy conservation, space translation gives momentum, rotation gives angular momentum, and the phase symmetry of $\psi$ gives conservation of electric charge. Few results in physics reach as widely.

17Group theory and representation theory

A group is a set with an associative operation, an identity and inverses. Groups are the mathematics of symmetry. Évariste Galois introduced the idea to study which polynomial equations can be solved by radicals, shortly before his death at age 20 in 1832 [13]. A century later Wigner [75] and Weyl made group theory a basic tool of quantum mechanics: physical symmetries act on Hilbert space as unitary representations, and the irreducible representations label the possible states.

Finite groups

The symmetries of an equilateral triangle form the dihedral group $D_3$ (Figure 17.1): the identity, two rotations and three reflections. The group is non-Abelian, because reflecting then rotating differs from rotating then reflecting. Crystallography classifies crystals into 32 point groups and 230 space groups. Molecular spectroscopy uses character tables to predict which vibrations are infrared- or Raman-active [76].

Lie groups and algebras

Continuous symmetries form Lie groups, generated by exponentiating a Lie algebra: $U=e^{-i\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf J}$. For rotations, the generators satisfy $[J_a,J_b]=i\varepsilon_{abc}J_c$ (with $\hbar=1$). The irreducible representations are labelled by $j=0,\tfrac12,1,\dots$ and have dimension $2j+1$, which is the theory of angular momentum and spin [26,77]. Spin $\tfrac12$ is a representation of SU(2), the double cover of SO(3). A $2\pi$ rotation multiplies a spinor by $-1$, which neutron interferometry confirmed in 1975 [78].

In particle physics, Gell-Mann's "eightfold way" grouped hadrons into SU(3) multiplets and predicted the $\Omega^-$ [79], which was found in 1964 [80]. Wigner classified elementary particles as irreducible representations of the Poincaré group, labelled by mass and spin [81]. The Standard Model is a gauge theory with group $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$ [82].

18Numerical methods

Most real problems, such as turbulence, many-body quantum systems, merging black holes and climate, have no closed-form solution. Numerical methods turn them into arithmetic a computer can do. To use them you need to understand three kinds of error: truncation error from the discretization, round-off error from finite precision, and instability, which amplifies both [83,84].

Floating point and finite differences

IEEE 754 double precision has a 53-bit significand, so machine epsilon is $2^{-52}\approx2.2\times10^{-16}$ [85]. The forward difference $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h$ has truncation error $O(h)$. The central difference $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}$ has $O(h^2)$. Making $h$ smaller does not always help, because round-off grows like $\epsilon/h$. The best step is about $\sqrt\epsilon\approx10^{-8}$ for forward differences and $\epsilon^{1/3}\approx10^{-5}$ for central ones (Figure 18.1) [83].

Integrating ODEs

  • Euler's method $y_{n+1}=y_n+hf(y_n)$ is first order and usually too crude.
  • Runge–Kutta methods (Runge 1895, Kutta 1901) evaluate $f$ several times per step [86,87]. Classical RK4 has global error $O(h^4)$: halving $h$ cuts the error about sixteen-fold [88].
  • Symplectic integrators such as velocity Verlet [89] preserve the geometric structure of Hamiltonian flow. Their energy error stays bounded for very long times instead of drifting, which is essential for planetary orbits and molecular dynamics [90] (Figure 18.2).
  • Stiff systems, which have widely separated time scales, need implicit methods.
A hand-written RK4 step and its fourth-order convergencerk4.py
import numpy as np

def rk4_step(f, t, y, h):
    """One classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta step for y' = f(t, y)."""
    k1 = f(t, y)
    k2 = f(t + h/2, y + h/2*k1)
    k3 = f(t + h/2, y + h/2*k2)
    k4 = f(t + h,   y + h*k3)
    return y + h/6*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

# Radioactive decay y' = -lambda*y, exact y = exp(-lambda t)
lam = 0.5
f = lambda t, y: -lam*y
for h in (0.5, 0.25, 0.125):
    t, y = 0.0, 1.0
    while t < 4 - 1e-12:
        y = rk4_step(f, t, y, h); t += h
    print(f"h = {h:<6} error at t=4: {abs(y - np.exp(-lam*4)):.3e}")

PDEs, stability and convergence

Finite-difference, finite-element and spectral methods turn PDEs into large linear systems. The Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition (1928) requires information in an explicit scheme not to travel more than one grid cell per step, $c\,\Delta t/\Delta x\le1$ for the wave equation [40]. The Lax equivalence theorem states that a consistent linear scheme converges if and only if it is stable [41]. The FTCS limit $r\le\tfrac12$ in Section 9 is an example.

Linear algebra, roots and Monte Carlo

Large sparse systems are solved iteratively, with conjugate gradient or GMRES. Eigenvalues come from QR or Lanczos iterations [83]. Newton's method $x_{n+1}=x_n-f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ converges quadratically near a simple root. For integrals in many dimensions, Monte Carlo sampling converges as $N^{-1/2}$ whatever the dimension. Markov-chain Monte Carlo, introduced by Metropolis and colleagues in 1953 on the MANIAC computer, samples Boltzmann distributions directly and is still the main tool of lattice QCD and statistical physics [91].

Synthesis: one problem, many tools

Take a single question, the hydrogen atom, and see how many of the tools above it needs. The Schrödinger equation is a PDE (Section 9) for a vector in Hilbert space (Sections 3–4). Separating it in spherical coordinates (Section 2) gives a radial ODE solved by series (Section 8), and spherical harmonics, which are the irreducible representations of SO(3) (Section 17). The extra symmetry of the $1/r$ potential, SO(4), explains why levels with different $\ell$ share the same energy [76]. Energies are eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator (Section 4). Transition probabilities use the Born rule (Section 15). Fine structure needs relativistic tensors (Section 13). Spectra are measured by Fourier transform spectroscopy (Section 11), and many-electron atoms are computed numerically (Section 18). Wigner's "unreasonable effectiveness" is the observation that one body of mathematics covers all of this [2].
