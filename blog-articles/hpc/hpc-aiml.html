Where do you cut?

A training step has four divisible axes and a fifth you can trade against memory. Every parallel strategy ever proposed is a cut along one of them — and every cut leaves a seam that has to be paid for in bytes, at a cadence, on a wire. This is an accounting of the seams.

01

The question

A training step is a four-dimensional object. Every parallel strategy is a choice of which dimension to slice.

A single training step of a modern neural network is a loop nest. It runs over examples in a batch, over positions in a sequence, over the width of each weight matrix, and over the depth of the network. Those four indices are the whole iteration space. Nothing else in the step is free to vary.

When the step no longer fits on one device — because the weights are too large, or the activations are too large, or because you want it to finish this month rather than next year — you have to divide that iteration space among many devices. And there are only four places to cut it, plus a fifth non-spatial axis, time, which you can trade against memory by throwing intermediate results away and recomputing them later.

Every technique in this article is one of those cuts. Data parallelism cuts the batch axis. Tensor parallelism cuts the width axis. Pipeline parallelism cuts the depth axis. Sequence and context parallelism cut the token axis. Expert parallelism cuts a fifth, learned axis that only exists in sparse models. Activation checkpointing cuts time. The names are a historical accident; the taxonomy is not.

What makes the choice non-trivial is that a cut is never free. Slicing the iteration space severs data dependencies that the mathematics still requires, and every severed dependency becomes an obligation to move bytes between devices before the step can complete. This article calls that obligation a seam. The seam is where the parallel program stops being a collection of independent local computations and has to become one global computation again.

So the organising question is: where do you cut, and what does the seam cost? Cost has three components that behave very differently. There is the volume of the seam, in bytes per device per unit of work. There is its cadence, meaning how often the bill falls due — once per step, once per micro-batch, once per transformer block, once per generated token. And there is whether the seam sits on the critical path, or whether some independent compute happens to be available to hide it behind.

It is worth saying plainly why this problem exists at all, because the answer is not that models became fashionably large. It is that measured loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data, and compute over many orders of magnitude88, that the compute-optimal allocation between parameters and tokens turned out to favour far more tokens than early practice assumed89, and that the capabilities which emerged at the top of those curves were qualitatively different from what sat below them87. Scaling is not an aesthetic preference; it is a response to an empirical regularity. The systems problem is a consequence.

Those three properties, not the name of the technique, determine what happens when you scale. A seam of thirty gigabytes paid once a step on a bandwidth-rich fabric is survivable. A seam of one megabyte paid twice per transformer block, on the critical path, with no independent work available to overlap it, will destroy a cluster. The rest of this article is an attempt to make that comparison quantitative rather than folkloric.

Two conventions throughout. First, the fifteen numbered plates inventory each cut with the same six fields and the same logarithmic gauge, so that seams which are usually discussed in incompatible units can be read against one another; they are collected into a single ledger at the end. Second, every quantitative claim in the prose and in the figures comes from the computational appendix at the foot of the article, which is a standard-library-only Python program that runs at build time. Where a number is a published measurement rather than something the appendix computes, it is cited.

One reference configuration is used for every plate, so that the gauges can be read against one another: a 70-billion-parameter dense model, 80 transformer blocks, hidden dimension 8192, sequence length 8192, micro-batch 1, 128 micro-batches per step, tensor-parallel degree 8, data-parallel degree 512, and grouped-query attention with 8 key-value heads. Where the appendix uses a different size — it works with a 7 B model in places, because the communication arithmetic is easier to follow — the text says so.

02

What a collective actually costs

Two prices, latency and bandwidth, that scale in opposite directions and want opposite algorithms.

Before any parallel strategy can be evaluated, the price list has to be established. The standard model is Hockney's: a point-to-point transfer of n bytes costs alpha + n beta, where alpha is a fixed latency and beta is the reciprocal of bandwidth1. LogP refines this by separating the sending overhead from the network delay, which matters when a processor is issuing many small messages2, but for reasoning about collective operations the two-parameter model is sufficient and its predictions are robust.

A collective is built from many such transfers, and the interesting fact is that the two terms in the cost pull toward different algorithms. Any all-reduce that is optimal in bandwidth must move at least 2(P-1)/P × n bytes per rank, because each rank must both contribute its data and receive the result6. Ring all-reduce achieves that bound exactly, by decomposing the operation into a reduce-scatter around the ring followed by an all-gather back around it, in 2(P-1) steps7. Rabenseifner's recursive halving-and-doubling achieves the same bandwidth bound in 2 log2P steps8. And for very small messages, plain recursive doubling — which sends the whole vector at every one of its log2P steps, and is therefore badly bandwidth-suboptimal — wins outright, because when n is tiny the bandwidth term is noise and only the step count matters. Production MPI implementations switch between these algorithms on message size, and have done for two decades5.

The appendix computes where that switch falls. On a 25 GB/s off-node fabric with a 5 microsecond latency, at P = 512, the crossover between recursive doubling and ring is at about 18 MB. Below that, the ring's 1022 latency-bound steps cost more than the extra bytes recursive doubling moves. This is the entire justification for the tensor fusion that every distributed training framework performs: an unfused parameter tensor of a few hundred kilobytes is on the wrong side of the crossover, and issuing one collective per tensor turns a bandwidth problem into a latency problem.

The second panel of that figure is, for the purposes of this article, the single most important fact about data parallelism. The bandwidth-optimal factor 2(P-1)/P is 1.75 at eight devices and 1.99994 at thirty-two thousand. It saturates at two. Per-rank traffic in a data-parallel gradient exchange is therefore essentially independent of the number of devices — it grew by 14.28 per cent across a 4096-fold increase in cluster size. What grows with P is only the latency term, 2(P-1) alpha, and that term is 0.07 ms at P = 8 and 327.67 ms at P = 32768.

Two consequences follow, and they are the reason the field looks the way it does. First, a data-parallel gradient exchange does not become more expensive as the cluster grows; it becomes more expensive as the model grows. The appendix times a 14 GB gradient all-reduce at 0.98 s on eight devices and 1.16 s on four thousand. Second, at extreme scale the latency floor becomes the dominant cost of small collectives, which is why hierarchical and in-network reduction exist. Aggregating inside the switch fabric rather than at the endpoints26, or inside a programmable dataplane 27, attacks the step count rather than the byte count.

Everything else in this article is downstream of those two prices. The strategies differ in which of the two they stress.

One framing to discard before going further. The instinct when analysing a parallel program is to reach for Amdahl's law and ask what fraction of the work is irreducibly serial3. That is the wrong instrument here, because the serial fraction in distributed training is not a fixed property of the algorithm — it is the seam, and the seam's relative size depends on the problem size you chose. Gustafson's reformulation is closer to the truth: practitioners do not hold the problem fixed and add processors, they enlarge the problem to fill the machine they have4. Every section below is implicitly a weak-scaling argument, and the quantity to watch is not speed-up but the ratio of seam to work at the size you actually intend to run.

03

The device, and the ecosystem around it

Why GPU acceleration made the seam matter in the first place.

The reason a seam exists at all is that a single accelerator became so fast that the wire around it became the interesting part. It is worth being precise about where that speed comes from, because the same structural facts reappear at cluster scale.

A GPU is a throughput machine: thousands of simple lanes, organised into warps that execute in lockstep, with latency hidden by keeping many warps resident rather than by speculating or caching aggressively. CUDA exposed this as a general-purpose programming model rather than a graphics abstraction13, and the productive consequence — established early and never really overturned — is that performance on these machines is governed by occupancy and memory traffic far more than by instruction count1514. The roofline model formalises the same point for any machine: a kernel is either bounded by arithmetic throughput or by memory bandwidth, and which one it is depends on its operational intensity, the ratio of FLOPs performed to bytes moved10. That distinction, applied at the level of an entire cluster rather than a single kernel, is what this article is about.

Deep learning arrived on this hardware in 2012 with a convolutional network trained on two consumer GPUs, split across them because the model did not fit in the memory of one17. It is worth noticing that the first famous result in modern deep learning was already a model-parallel program, and that it was model-parallel for the most mundane possible reason. The software stack that grew around it followed the same logic: cuDNN provided tuned primitives so that framework authors did not have to write convolutions16; CUTLASS provided composable templates so that people writing new operations did not have to start from registers18; and Triton eventually provided a tile-level language so that fusing an operation no longer required a CUDA expert19. Each layer raised the abstraction at which someone could be productive without giving up the performance model underneath.

The communication half of the stack evolved the same way. NCCL implements topology-aware collectives over NVLink, PCIe, and the network, and is the layer where the algorithmic choices of the previous section actually get made20. It is not a parallel programming framework; it is a library of collectives, deliberately narrow. As of August 2026 its release notes cover version 2.31.221, with 2.30.7 posted as the current download for CUDA 13.322. Recent releases have added one-sided host-side put and wait operations that consume no streaming multiprocessors, and Python bindings — both of which are best read as symptoms of the same pressure, namely that communication is now a first-class part of the program rather than something bolted to the end of it. Equivalent libraries exist for other vendors' hardware and for CPU fallback2324.

The important thing about this ecosystem, for our purposes, is what it does not abstract away. NCCL will not tell you that your tensor-parallel degree is too large for your fabric. CUTLASS will not tell you that your micro-batch count is too small for your pipeline. The stack gives you fast primitives and leaves the question of where to cut entirely to you.

04

The cluster beneath the collective

A GPU cluster is not a flat network, and no strategy survives pretending otherwise.

Collective cost models assume a single alpha and a single beta. Real machines have at least two of each, differing by more than an order of magnitude, and the boundary between them is a physical object: the chassis.

Inside a node, accelerators are connected by a switched, high-radix, memory-semantic fabric — NVLink and NVLink Switch on NVIDIA hardware, with UALink emerging as a multi-vendor alternative — delivering hundreds of gigabytes per second per device with latencies in the low microseconds2529. Between nodes, the same devices talk over InfiniBand or Ethernet at a fraction of that bandwidth and several times the latency, and the industry has been standardising a transport specifically for this traffic pattern28. The ratio between the two — call it the chassis ratio — is the most important number in a cluster design, because it decides which cuts can cross a node boundary and which cannot.

The memory hierarchy has the same shape. High-bandwidth memory delivers multiple terabytes per second to the device it is stacked beside30, which is one to two orders of magnitude more than the network delivers to that device. A byte that stays in HBM is nearly free; a byte that crosses the chassis boundary is expensive; a byte that crosses the datacentre is a design decision. Any parallel strategy that is indifferent to which of those three a given transfer is has already lost.

This is not new. It is the same hierarchy that has organised high-performance computing since the arrival of clustered SMP machines, and the scheduling, accounting, and topology-awareness machinery is largely inherited — Slurm still allocates most of the world's training jobs105, and the June 2026 TOP500 remains a reasonable census of what large machines look like, though the list's LINPACK metric captures little about the collective performance that matters here3111. What is new is the arrival of accelerators purpose-built for this workload with interconnect designed as a first-class feature rather than an afterthought, including systems that make the network itself reconfigurable3233.

The practical rule that falls out is simple and will recur in every section below: put the highest-cadence seam inside the chassis. A cut that requires communication once per transformer block belongs on the fast fabric. A cut that requires communication once per step can cross the network. A cut that requires communication once per micro-batch can go either way, depending on how much independent work is available to overlap it. Almost every real training configuration is an application of that rule, and Section 13 shows what happens when you apply it systematically.

05

Data parallelism, and the seam that saturates

Replicate the model, split the batch, and pay once per step for a gradient the whole cluster agrees on.

Data parallelism is the oldest cut and still the default. Each device holds a complete copy of the model, processes a different slice of the batch, and the gradients are averaged before the optimiser step so that every replica applies the same update. The mathematics is exactly synchronous SGD on the global batch; the parallelism is invisible to the optimiser.

The first thing to establish is that this cut is remarkably well-behaved at scale. As Section 2 showed, per-rank traffic saturates at twice the gradient size regardless of P. The seam is paid once per step, and the step gets longer as the model gets larger, so the ratio of communication to compute is roughly constant in model size and roughly constant in device count. That is a very unusual property, and it is why data parallelism scaled to thousands of devices years before any other cut did.

What it does not survive is the optimiser. Synchronous data parallelism enlarges the global batch in proportion to P, and beyond some critical batch size, additional examples per step stop buying additional progress per step4140. The empirical scaling of large-batch training was worked out in a sequence of results — linear learning-rate scaling with a warm-up period37, layerwise adaptive rate scaling3839, and the observation that increasing batch size and decaying the learning rate are, to first order, the same intervention43 — and the generalisation concerns raised early on 42 turned out to be largely a tuning artefact. But the ceiling is real, and it is a statistical ceiling, not a systems one. No amount of interconnect fixes it.

Hiding the seam

The seam's cadence is once per step, but its position within the step is a choice. Gradients become available in reverse layer order as backpropagation proceeds, so the gradient of the last block is ready long before the backward pass finishes. A framework that waits for the whole gradient before communicating has serialised communication after computation for no reason. One that issues a collective per parameter tensor has fallen off the small-message cliff. The resolution, in every production implementation, is bucketing: accumulate gradients into fixed-size buckets in reverse-ready order and launch a collective as each bucket fills48. Horovod called the same idea tensor fusion44.

The appendix simulates this and finds something more interesting than the usual advice. With an 80-block model, a 14 GB gradient, 12 ms of backward compute per block, and 512 devices on a 25 GB/s fabric, the best bucket count is 16, giving a 1259.6 ms step against 960 ms of compute — 23.8 per cent of the step still exposed. Unbucketed, the step is 2082.9 ms. So bucketing is worth 65.4 per cent, which sounds like a triumph until you notice why the residue will not go away: total communication at the optimum is 1199.6 ms, and total compute is 960 ms. The communication does not fit inside the computation. No schedule can hide something larger than the thing it is hiding behind.

Rerun the same experiment on a 100 GB/s fabric and the character of the problem changes completely. Now the optimum is 80 buckets — one per block — and 98.8 per cent of the seam hides, leaving 8.6 ms exposed, 0.89 per cent of the step. And the residue is now a genuinely different thing: it is the last bucket, which contains the first block's gradient, which is produced last and therefore has nothing left to overlap with. That tail is irreducible by scheduling. Solving the crossover exactly, the appendix finds that around 34 GB/s per device the data-parallel seam disappears into the backward pass entirely.

That number — the fabric bandwidth at which a cut stops mattering — is worth generalising. Every seam in this article has one. Below it, the strategy is bandwidth-bound and no amount of scheduling cleverness helps; above it, the strategy is free and the discussion moves on to something else. Reporting it is more useful than reporting a scaling efficiency, because it tells you what to buy.

Narrowing the seam instead of hiding it

If the seam will not fit inside the compute, the other option is to make it smaller. Gradient compression has a long literature and a consistent shape: quantise, sparsify, or factorise the gradient before sending it, and carry the residual forward so that nothing is discarded permanently. One-bit SGD with error feedback was demonstrated on speech models more than a decade ago50; QSGD gave the first clean analysis of the variance-versus-bits trade-off with convergence guarantees49; deep gradient compression pushed sparsification to sending well under one per cent of coordinates with momentum correction and warm-up51; and PowerSGD used a low-rank factorisation chosen specifically because, unlike sparsification, it is compatible with all-reduce rather than requiring an all-gather52.

That last detail is the one worth remembering, and it generalises. A compression scheme is only useful if the compressed representation is closed under the reduction operator — if you can add two compressed gradients and get a valid compressed gradient. Top-k sparsification is not, which is why it tends to degrade into an all-gather whose volume grows with P and undoes the saturation property of Section 2. Low-rank factorisation is. Compression that breaks the collective usually loses to no compression at all.

A related line relaxes the topology rather than the payload: decentralised SGD replaces the global collective with gossip among neighbours, which removes the global synchronisation point entirely and can outperform centralised training when the fabric is the bottleneck53. Like asynchrony, it trades exactness for tolerance, and like asynchrony it has found its home where the fabric is genuinely poor rather than in the datacentre.

Relaxing synchrony, and why it lost

There is an obvious alternative: do not synchronise. The parameter server architecture, in which workers push gradients to and pull parameters from a sharded store without a barrier3435, removes the collective entirely and tolerates stragglers. Its theoretical justification came from the observation that lock-free asynchronous updates converge under sparsity assumptions36.

It lost, and the reason is instructive. Asynchrony buys you tolerance to latency and to heterogeneity, which are real problems, at the cost of gradient staleness, which corrupts the thing you are actually optimising12. On a homogeneous cluster with a good fabric — which is what people building large models actually have — the collective is cheap and the staleness is not worth paying for. The parameter server survives where its assumptions hold: very large sparse embedding tables, heterogeneous fleets, and federated settings. TensorFlow still ships it as a strategy101. It is not how anyone trains a dense transformer.

The idea and its implementation

Horovod deserves a paragraph of its own, because its history is a clean illustration of how systems ideas propagate. In 2017, distributed TensorFlow meant parameter servers, and scaling past a few dozen GPUs was painful. Horovod's contribution was to import a piece of HPC practice wholesale: replace the parameter server with a ring all-reduce, adopt the MPI process model and its collective vocabulary9, and let a single-GPU script become a distributed one with about five lines of change44. The ring-allreduce implementation itself was derived from earlier work at Baidu47. It worked, it was adopted widely, it was contributed to the Linux Foundation in December 2018 and graduated in September 202046.

And then it was overtaken. Its last release, 0.28.1, was in June 2023; the GitHub repository was archived read-only on 29 July 2026 and its README now declares the project inactive45. This is not a failure. Horovod's argument — that synchronous ring all-reduce beats the parameter server for dense models — won so completely that the frameworks absorbed it. NCCL provides the ring; PyTorch DDP provides the bucketing and the overlap48; the five-lines-of-change ergonomics became torchrun. What was once a separate library became a default. The idea outlived its implementation, which is the best outcome an infrastructure project can have.

06

Sharded data parallelism

The same cut, with the replication removed — and almost none of it costs extra bandwidth.

Data parallelism replicates everything, and for a long time that was assumed to be the price of its simplicity. It is not. Most of what a replica holds is never needed by more than one device at a time, and the ZeRO family of techniques is an inventory of exactly which parts those are54.

Start with the ledger. Mixed-precision training with Adam holds, per parameter: two bytes of bf16 weights, two bytes of bf16 gradient, and twelve bytes of fp32 optimiser state — a master copy of the weights, a first moment, and a second moment90. Sixteen bytes per parameter, of which three-quarters is optimiser state that is touched exactly once per step, by one device, in a completely elementwise fashion. There is no reason for every device to hold all of it.

ZeRO stages the removal. Stage 1 partitions the optimiser state across the N data-parallel ranks; stage 2 additionally partitions the gradients; stage 3 additionally partitions the parameters themselves, gathering each layer's weights just before they are needed and discarding them immediately after54. PyTorch's FSDP is an independent implementation of the same idea with a different sharding unit and a native integration5859.

The appendix computes what this buys. For a 70 B-parameter model, model states alone are 1120 GB per device under plain data parallelism — fourteen times an 80 GB device, so the configuration is not merely inefficient but impossible. At N = 64, ZeRO-1 brings that to 293.1 GB, ZeRO-2 to 155.3 GB, and ZeRO-3 to 17.5 GB. Only the last one fits.

The asymmetry between the stages is worth dwelling on, because it is frequently misread as a matter of degree. Turning the ledger around and asking how large a model each stage can hold in 80 GB: DDP tops out at 5.0 B parameters and stays there forever. ZeRO-1 asymptotes at 20.0 B, ZeRO-2 at 40.0 B — however many devices you add. Only ZeRO-3 is linear in N, reaching 320 B at N = 64 and 2560 B at N = 512. Stages 1 and 2 are optimisations; stage 3 is a change of asymptotic class, because it is the only one that removes the last full-precision-independent replicated copy.

The remarkable part is the seam. A ring all-reduce is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather. ZeRO-1 and ZeRO-2 simply stop half-way through, do the optimiser update on the scattered shard, and then all-gather the result instead of the sum. The volume is identical to plain data parallelism. Stages 1 and 2 are, to a first approximation, free: a large memory saving for no additional bytes on the wire. That is rare enough in systems work to be worth naming.

Stage 3 is not free, and the difference shows up in cadence rather than volume. Because parameters are sharded, they must be gathered before each block's forward pass and again before its backward pass, which converts one per-step collective into roughly 3 / 2 collectives per block per step and raises total parameter traffic by about 50 per cent5458. That is an acceptable trade when the alternative is not fitting, and it is a bad trade when the model would have fitted anyway. The cadence shift also makes overlap essential rather than merely helpful: the all-gather for block i+1 must be in flight during the compute of block i, or the device stalls at every block boundary. PyTorch 2.13 added support for overlapping FSDP2's reduce-scatter and all-gather on separate process groups97, which is the same optimisation applied one level further in.

Two extensions are worth noting because they show where the idea ends. ZeRO-Offload moves optimiser state to host memory56 and ZeRO-Infinity extends this to NVMe57, trading progressively worse bandwidth for progressively larger capacity. Both are the correct answer when capacity is the binding constraint and the arithmetic is cheap. Neither helps when the fabric is already the bottleneck, which — as the previous section showed — is the more common failure at scale.

07

Tensor parallelism

Cut the width of the matrices and you cut the memory too — but the seam moves inside the layer, onto the critical path.

Sharding the batch does not help when a single example's activations exceed one device, and sharding the optimiser does not help when a single weight matrix does. For that you have to cut the matrices themselves.

The Megatron construction is elegant enough to state in a sentence. In a two-layer MLP with weights A and B, shard A by columns and B by rows; then each device computes a partial product of the full output, and a single all-reduce of the block's output reconstructs it. Attention shards the same way along the head dimension, since heads are independent by construction60. Two all-reduces per transformer block in the forward pass, two more in the backward pass, and the arithmetic is exactly equivalent.

It is a beautiful cut and it has a serious problem: the seam is squarely on the critical path. The output of the all-reduce is the input to the next matrix multiply. There is no independent work to overlap it with, unlike the data-parallel gradient exchange which had an entire backward pass to hide behind. Whatever the collective costs, you pay in full.

The appendix makes this concrete by deriving a critical bandwidth. Setting communication time equal to twenty per cent of compute time for a block sharded t ways yields, after the sequence length and micro-batch cancel out on both sides:

B_crit = (10/9) · (t − 1) · F / h

where F is the device's arithmetic throughput and h the hidden dimension. The cancellation is the interesting part: the tolerable tensor-parallel degree does not depend on how long your sequences are or how large your micro-batch is. It depends only on the ratio of arithmetic throughput to model width. With a device at 1000 TFLOP/s and h = 8192, sharding two ways demands 136 GB/s, four ways 407 GB/s, and eight ways 949 GB/s — more than the on-node fabric assumed here delivers.

Measured end to end, the same configuration gives 94.3 per cent block efficiency at t = 2 on the fast fabric and 69.5 per cent at t = 8. Across the chassis boundary it is 47.9 per cent and 11.6 per cent respectively. Tensor parallelism at t = 8 over a commodity network converts an expensive cluster into a slow one. This is the sharpest instance of the rule from Section 4, and it explains a structural fact about the industry: the reason vendors sell eight- and seventy-two-device NVLink domains, rather than selling more devices with less fabric between them, is that the largest affordable tensor-parallel degree is set by the size of the coherent domain.

The h-dependence points the other way and is under-appreciated. At t = 8, a model with h = 4096 needs 1899 GB/s, while one with h = 24576 needs only 316 GB/s. Wide models tolerate tensor parallelism; narrow ones do not. Compute per block grows as h2 while the seam grows only as h, so the ratio improves linearly with width. A model architect choosing depth over width is making a systems decision whether or not they realise it.

Two lines of work attack the unoverlappability directly. One decomposes the collective and the dependent matrix multiply into finer pieces that can be software-pipelined against each other, recovering overlap that the coarse-grained formulation destroyed84. The other moves the choice of sharding from the programmer to the compiler: Mesh-TensorFlow let a user name tensor dimensions and map them onto a named device mesh63, and GSPMD generalised this to whole-graph automatic partitioning from lightweight annotations64. Both are worth understanding as the same admission — that the sharding decision is too entangled with the fabric to be made by hand at every layer.

08

Sequence and context

The token axis is the newest cut, and the only one whose pressure comes from the activations rather than the weights.

Tensor parallelism shards the weights but replicates the sequence. That is fine when sequences are short and becomes untenable when they are not, because activation memory grows with sequence length and the attention term grows with its square.

The published formula for bf16 activation memory per transformer block, without recomputation, is s b h (34 + 5as/h)62. At s = 8192, h = 8192, 64 heads and a micro-batch of one, the appendix evaluates this at 1900.5 GB for an 80-block network — 23.8 times an 80 GB device, before a single parameter is stored. The attention term alone is 90 per cent of that total. Long-context training is not a variation on ordinary training; it is a different memory regime.

There are two distinct responses, often conflated. The first is sequence parallelism in the Megatron sense: within a tensor-parallel group, the operations that tensor parallelism leaves replicated — layer normalisation, dropout, the residual adds — are themselves split along the sequence axis, and the two all-reduces per block become a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather62. Volume is unchanged; what changes is that the replicated activations are now sharded too. The appendix measures the effect: at t = 8, activations fall from 284.5 GB with tensor parallelism alone to 237.6 GB with sequence parallelism added.

The second response addresses context length as such, by sharding the sequence across devices that each hold the full model. This requires attention itself to be distributed, since every query must eventually see every key. Ring attention circulates key-value blocks around a ring of devices while each device computes partial attention against whatever block it currently holds65, overlapping the transfer with the computation; the approach composes naturally with the blockwise, IO-aware attention kernels that made single-device attention memory-linear in the first place686970. DeepSpeed Ulysses takes a different route, using all-to-all to transpose between a sequence-sharded and a head-sharded layout at the attention boundary6667.

The token axis is the newest of the four and the one still moving fastest, for a straightforward reason: it is the only cut whose pressure comes from activations rather than parameters, and activation pressure is what long context creates. It is also the cut with the most favourable overlap properties, because attention is a lot of arithmetic per byte of key-value state, and there is therefore real work to hide the transfer behind. A ring of key-value blocks is a much friendlier seam than a blocking all-reduce.

09

Pipeline parallelism

Cut the depth and the seam becomes tiny — but the schedule grows a hole in it.

The depth axis is the cheapest to cut and the hardest to schedule. Assign consecutive blocks to consecutive devices, and the only thing that has to cross a device boundary is the activation tensor at the stage boundary — s b h elements, point-to-point, no collective at all. In the configuration used throughout this article that is 134 MB per micro-batch per boundary, against 939 MB per block for tensor parallelism. By volume, pipeline parallelism is nearly free.

The cost is structural instead. A device holding stage p cannot start until stage p−1 has produced something for it, and cannot finish its backward pass until stage p+1 has sent a gradient back. At the start and end of every step, some devices have nothing to do. That idle region is the bubble, and for a step split into M micro-batches over P stages it occupies (P−1) / (M+P−1) of the schedule71.

The appendix simulates the schedules cycle-accurately rather than assuming the formula, and reproduces it exactly. What the simulation also shows is the thing the formula hides. GPipe runs all forwards, then all backwards71; the 1F1B schedule from PipeDream interleaves them once the pipeline is full7273. Both have identical makespan and identical idle fraction at every configuration tested. 1F1B does not remove a single unit of bubble.

What it changes is memory. Under GPipe, stage 0 must retain the activations of all M micro-batches until their backward passes arrive; under 1F1B it retains at most P. At P = 8, M = 64 the appendix measures 64 live activation sets against 8, an eightfold difference for the same runtime. And since the only lever that shrinks the bubble is raising M, and raising M under GPipe raises memory proportionally, GPipe cannot use the lever. 1F1B can. The schedule that does not improve the bubble is the schedule that makes the bubble improvable — which is a nice illustration of why measuring a system on one axis tells you very little.

Raising M has diminishing returns of its own. At P = 32 the bubble is 49.2 per cent at M = 32, 19.5 per cent at M = 128, and 2.9 per cent at M = 1024 — but M micro-batches at fixed micro-batch size means a global batch of 1024 times the micro-batch, which runs straight into the critical batch size of Section 5. The pipeline's memory constraint and the optimiser's batch-size constraint are pushing on the same number from opposite sides.

The remaining schedule work attacks the bubble directly. Interleaving gives each device several non-contiguous virtual stages, cutting the bubble to (P−1) / (vM+P−1) at the cost of v times as many point-to-point sends — at P = 32, M = 128 the appendix puts that at 19.5 per cent falling to 5.7 per cent for v = 4, which is a good trade precisely because the pipeline seam was so small to begin with61. Chimera runs two pipelines in opposite directions so that each one's bubble is the other's work74. And zero-bubble schedules exploit the fact that the backward pass decomposes into an input gradient and a weight gradient with different urgency, deferring the latter into the gap75.

10

Expert parallelism

The fifth axis exists only in sparse models, and it is the only seam whose balance is decided at run time.

A mixture-of-experts layer replaces one feed-forward network with many, and a learned router sends each token to a small number of them76. Parameter count rises by the number of experts; arithmetic per token does not. This is the only technique in this article that changes the model rather than its execution, and it is included because it creates a genuinely new axis to cut along: the expert index.

The cut is natural. Place different experts on different devices, and the seam is an all-to-all: dispatch each token's hidden vector to the device holding its chosen expert, and combine the results back7778. Volume is modest — the appendix puts it at 0.27 GB per block per step for 8192 tokens at h = 8192, dispatch and combine together — which is comparable to the tensor-parallel seam and much smaller than the data-parallel one.

The problem is not volume. It is that this is the only seam in the article whose balance is decided by a learned function at run time.

The appendix routes 8192 tokens across 64 experts under three routers. A uniform router gives a max-to-mean load ratio of 1.188 — not 1.0, because throwing 8192 balls into 64 bins does not fill them evenly. A Zipf-0.5 popularity distribution gives 4.148, and Zipf-1.0 gives 13.930, with the busiest expert receiving 1783 of the 8192 tokens.

Read the drop table carefully, because it contains the whole argument for auxiliary load-balancing losses. At capacity factor c = 1.0 — the exact average load — even the perfectly uniform router drops 3.52 per cent of tokens, purely from multinomial fluctuation. Exact capacity is never enough, because "balanced on average" is not "balanced". It takes c = 1.25 to reach zero loss, and that 25 per cent of headroom is memory reserved on every device for the rest of training.

The skewed routers never recover. Zipf-1.0 still drops 20.68 per cent of tokens at c = 4.0 — four times the memory, and one token in five still falls on the floor. You cannot buy your way out of routing collapse with buffer space. Balancing has to happen in the loss, which is why every production MoE has carried an auxiliary balancing term since GShard7778, and why removing that term without replacing its function is a research problem rather than a simplification. DeepSeek-V3 is the notable attempt: it uses a per-expert bias adjusted during training to equalise load without an auxiliary gradient, alongside a compressed latent attention that shrinks the key-value state80.

The systems consequence of a data-dependent seam is that an all-to-all is a synchronising operation: the slowest destination sets the pace for every source. A single hot expert therefore stalls every device in the group, which means the tail of the load distribution, not its mean, sets the throughput. This is a qualitatively different failure mode from every other plate in this article, and it is why MoE training is disproportionately sensitive to fabric quality even though its seam volume is unremarkable.

That this matters at production scale is no longer arguable. MLPerf Training v6.0, published on 16 June 2026, added two mixture-of-experts benchmarks: DeepSeek-V3 at 671 billion total parameters with 37 billion activated per token — now the largest benchmark in the suite — and GPT-OSS 20B at 21 billion total and 3.6 billion activated, small enough to run on a single eight-device node. The round's own framing is that sparse computation has become the dominant architecture, and the DeepSeek-V3 benchmark was chosen specifically to exercise multi-head latent attention and auxiliary-loss-free load balancing108. The seam whose balance the data decides is now part of how the industry measures itself.

The fifth axis: trading time for memory

Recomputation is a cut with no wire, and precision is a multiplier on every other seam in the article.

Every cut so far has been spatial. There is one more axis, and it does not distribute anything: you can discard an intermediate result and recompute it when it is needed, spending arithmetic to buy memory.

The technique is older than deep learning. Reverse-mode automatic differentiation has always faced the choice between storing the forward trajectory and recomputing pieces of it, and Griewank and Walther's Revolve gives the provably optimal placement of checkpoints for a given memory budget93. Applied to neural networks, storing activations only at layer boundaries and recomputing within reduces memory from linear in depth to square-root in depth, for roughly one extra forward pass92.

Modern practice is more selective, because not all activations cost the same to keep or to recompute. Recomputing only the attention block — which is memory-hungry and arithmetically cheap to redo — while retaining everything else captures most of the saving for a fraction of the cost. In the appendix's configuration this drives the per-block formula from s b h (34+5as/h) down to 34 s b h/t, and combined with eight-way tensor and sequence parallelism it takes activations from 1900.5 GB to 22.8 GB — a factor of 83.362.

This plate is the only one whose gauge is empty, and that is the point of including it. A cut that costs arithmetic instead of bandwidth is categorically different from one that costs bandwidth, because arithmetic scales with the cluster and bandwidth does not. When a device is memory-bound — which, as Section 13 will show, is the normal condition during inference and a common one during training — recomputation is very nearly free.

Numerical precision deserves a note here rather than a plate, because it is not a cut at all: it is a multiplier on every cut simultaneously. Mixed precision, with reduced-precision compute and a full-precision master copy for the update, halved both memory and communication with no loss in final accuracy across a wide range of models90, and the subsequent standardisation of eight-bit floating-point formats halved them again for the operations that tolerate it91. In inference, post-training quantisation goes further still9495. MLPerf Training v6.0 recorded submitters using multiple distinct four-bit recipes, with the round's own commentary noting that the diversity of implementations is itself the finding108 — which is a polite way of saying that the numerics of very low precision are not yet settled. But the systems logic is unambiguous: a format change that halves the bytes halves every seam in this article at once, which no scheduling change can do.

Composing the cuts

Real configurations use four or five axes at once, mapped onto a device mesh whose shape is the whole design.

No large model is trained with one cut. The strategies compose, because they slice different axes of the same iteration space, and a production configuration is a mapping from a multi-dimensional device mesh onto that space.

The mapping is not arbitrary. Applying the rule from Section 4 — highest-cadence seam on the fastest wire — generates the standard configuration almost mechanically. Tensor and sequence parallelism have per-block, critical-path seams, so they go inside the chassis, at whatever degree the coherent domain supports. Pipeline parallelism has a small per-micro-batch point-to-point seam, so it spans nodes within a rack or island. Data parallelism, sharded or not, has a per-step seam that overlaps with the backward pass, so it spans the whole cluster and crosses whatever is slowest. Expert parallelism, when present, wants the same treatment as tensor parallelism but often cannot get it, because the expert count exceeds the domain size6185.

The results of doing this carefully are well documented. Megatron-LM's cluster-scale paper reported sustained throughput on thousands of GPUs for models up to a trillion parameters by combining tensor, pipeline, and data parallelism explicitly61. PaLM used a two-way pod configuration over TPU with a comparable decomposition86. Llama 3's report is unusually candid about the four-dimensional mapping and about what went wrong at scale85. MegaScale documents the engineering of a ten-thousand-GPU production system, and the substantial fraction of the effort that went into diagnosis rather than optimisation83.

What the frameworks decided

It is worth being blunt about how the two dominant frameworks landed, because the difference is not really about APIs.

Both began as answers to a narrower question. TensorFlow was built around a static dataflow graph that could be partitioned and placed across devices by the runtime100; PyTorch was built around eager execution and a tape-based autograd, on the argument that usability and debuggability were worth more than whole-graph optimisation96. That argument was substantially settled in PyTorch's favour for research, and the interesting consequence is that PyTorch then had to recover, incrementally and at some cost, the whole-graph information that a static graph gives you for free.

TensorFlow put distribution behind a strategy object: choose MirroredStrategy for one machine, MultiWorkerMirroredStrategy for many, TPUStrategy for TPU pods, ParameterServerStrategy for asynchronous training, and the training loop is meant to be unchanged underneath100101. The abstraction is clean, and it is a closed set. Each strategy is a policy someone implemented; if the cut you want is not among them, the abstraction does not help you. Keras 3 has since become multi-backend, running on JAX, TensorFlow, or PyTorch102, which is a reasonable response to a world where the framework and the distribution strategy are no longer the same choice.

PyTorch went the other way. DDP was a wrapper with bucketing and overlap48, FSDP was a second wrapper58, and the composition of the two was awkward. The eventual answer was to stop writing wrappers and to make sharding a property of the tensor: DTensor carries a placement — shard, replicate, or partial — against a named DeviceMesh, and the collectives required to reconcile placements are inserted by the runtime98. That is recognisably the Mesh-TensorFlow and GSPMD idea6364, arriving in PyTorch by a different road, and TorchTitan is the reference assembly of four-dimensional parallelism on top of it99. The 2.13 release in July 2026 continued in the same direction, adding a communications backend aimed at fault tolerance and debuggability on large clusters and improving FSDP2's overlap97.

The deeper shift is that both frameworks have converged on describing where tensors live rather than what strategy to run. Once the placement is data, the compiler can search for it. Alpa formulates the choice as a two-level optimisation over intra-operator and inter-operator parallelism81; FlexFlow searched a broader SOAP space earlier82. Neither has displaced hand-tuned configurations at the frontier, and the reason is exactly the subject of this article: the search space is defined by seam costs, and seam costs depend on a fabric topology that these systems model only approximately.

Two systems sit slightly outside this framing and are worth naming for contrast. JAX kept the functional, traced, whole-program model and built parallelism out of composable transformations over it, which makes it the closest thing to a direct descendant of the Mesh-TensorFlow lineage103. Ray occupies a different layer altogether — a general task-and-actor runtime rather than an array framework — and is used predominantly where a training job is one stage of a heterogeneous pipeline alongside data processing, simulation, or reinforcement-learning rollouts104. Neither competes with the frameworks above so much as it answers a different question about where the program's structure lives.

Finally, a note on how any of this gets compared. MLPerf established a closed-division methodology in which submitters must train a specified model to a specified quality target, which is the only way to make throughput numbers mean anything across systems that differ in precision, batch size, and optimiser106. The v6.0 training round in June 2026 drew submissions covering 95 unique systems across 13 accelerator types and 19 host processors, with roughly 60 per cent of results coming from multi-node configurations and cloud submissions more than doubling since the previous round108. Whatever one thinks of benchmark rounds, that distribution is a reasonable picture of where the cuts in this article are actually being made.

Inference at scale

The same taxonomy, one phase that is compute-bound, one that is not, and a seam that has moved into memory.

Inference is not training with the backward pass deleted. It has a different bottleneck, a different unit of work, and a seam that has migrated from the network into the memory system.

Autoregressive generation has two phases with opposite characters. Prefill processes the whole prompt at once: a large matrix multiply per block, plenty of arithmetic per byte, comfortably compute-bound. Decode produces one token per sequence per step: it reads every weight in the model to do a handful of arithmetic per weight, and is therefore memory-bound by construction111.

The roofline makes the second claim precise. In a decode step the device reads all parameter bytes once and performs 2 psi FLOPs per sequence in the batch, so arithmetic intensity from the weights alone is 2B / bytes-per-parameter, which for bf16 is simply B. The batch size is the operational intensity, in FLOPs per byte. With the appendix's device — 1000 TFLOP/s against 8.0 TB/s, a machine balance of 125 — you need at least 125 concurrent sequences before the arithmetic units are doing anything but waiting.

And you frequently cannot have them, because the key-value cache occupies the memory the batch needs. For a 70 B model at eight-way tensor parallelism, weights take 17.5 GB per rank, leaving 62.5 GB for cache. With eight grouped-query key-value heads over 80 blocks, each token costs 328 kB of cache, 41.0 kB of it per rank. At 2048 tokens of context that permits a batch of 745 and the device reaches the roofline comfortably. At 131072 tokens it permits a batch of 11.6, and the device is stranded in the memory-bound regime by a constraint that has nothing to do with its arithmetic.

This reframes several things usually presented as modelling choices. Multi-query and grouped-query attention share key-value projections across attention heads115116; the appendix measures what that does at 32768 tokens of context, where full multi-head attention permits a batch of 5.8, grouped-query 46.6, and multi-query 372.5 — only the last of which clears the roofline. Grouped-query attention is an eightfold reduction in the size of the seam between a decode step and its memory, and its quality cost is the price of that reduction.

Batching is the whole game

If intensity is batch size, then admission policy is a performance decision, and the appendix quantifies it starkly. Take 4000 requests with log-normal output lengths — median 146 tokens, p99 1161, a p99-to-median ratio of 8.0 — and 32 decode slots. A static batch of 32 holds every slot until the longest sequence in the group finishes, drains the queue at 21.1 per cent slot occupancy, and takes 129802 steps. Continuous batching, which retires a finished sequence immediately and admits a waiting one into its slot, holds 97.4 per cent occupancy and drains the same queue in 28173 steps: 4.61 times faster on identical hardware112.

The comparison holds at loads a static policy can carry comfortably. Offered 0.0216 requests per step — 70 per cent of the static policy's own capacity, so both queues are stable — static batching gives a mean latency of 1817 steps against 223, and a p99 of 3777 against 1146. Notice also that the continuous policy's p99-to-median latency ratio is 7.62, almost exactly the workload's own output-length ratio of 8.0. That is the correct answer: under continuous batching, latency spread is the workload's, not the scheduler's.

The remaining constraint is memory, and it turns out to be an allocator problem. A contiguous allocator must reserve the maximum sequence length for every admitted request; the appendix measures 78.13 per cent of that reservation as waste against actual usage. Paged allocation, handing out sixteen-token blocks on demand, cuts waste to 0.84 per cent and supports 1660 concurrent sequences against 366 in the same budget — a 4.5-fold increase in concurrency from bookkeeping alone113. Since concurrency sets batch size and batch size sets intensity, the memory allocator is a throughput decision in disguise. It is difficult to think of a cleaner example of an operating-systems technique — virtual memory, essentially — solving a machine-learning performance problem.

Three further techniques follow from the same analysis. Chunked prefill interleaves pieces of prompt processing with ongoing decode steps so that a long prompt does not stall every active generation, trading a little first-token latency for a lot of tail latency121. Speculative decoding runs a small draft model ahead and verifies several tokens in one pass of the large model, converting a sequence of memory-bound steps into one that is closer to compute-bound117118 — it is best understood not as a sampling trick but as a way of raising arithmetic intensity when batch size cannot be raised. And prefill-decode disaggregation runs the two phases on separate pools with independent parallel degrees, so that the compute-bound phase and the memory-bound phase are each sized for their own bottleneck119120.

Disaggregation has moved quickly from paper to product. NVIDIA's Dynamo is an orchestration layer built around exactly this split, with a key-value transfer path over RDMA and cache-aware request routing122123. It is worth noting, though, that vLLM's own documentation still marks disaggregated prefilling experimental114 — a reasonable reminder that a technique can be simultaneously well-understood and not yet routine. The benchmark suite has followed: MLPerf Inference v6.0, released on 1 April 2026, made five of its eleven datacentre tests new or updated, adding an open-weight 120-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts reasoning benchmark and — more to the point here — a latency-constrained interactive scenario for DeepSeek-R1 that is the suite's first standard for speculative decoding110109107. When a technique acquires a benchmark scenario of its own, it has stopped being an optimisation and started being part of the definition of the workload.

The seam you cannot schedule

Synchronous training turns every hardware fault into a global one.

Every cut in this article creates a dependency, and a dependency is a failure propagation path. A synchronous data-parallel step cannot complete until every replica has contributed; a pipeline stage cannot proceed until its neighbour has sent. The consequence is that in a strictly synchronous job, the mean time between failures of the cluster, not of a device, sets the achievable step count — and cluster MTBF falls linearly with device count while the value of a step does not.

The Llama 3 report is the most useful public account of this, and it is candid: over a 54-day pre-training snapshot on a 16384-GPU cluster the team recorded 466 job interruptions, of which 419 were unexpected, with roughly three-quarters traced to hardware faults — GPUs and their high-bandwidth memory accounting for the majority — and observed that environmental factors including a diurnal temperature cycle produced measurable throughput variation85. MegaScale's account of a comparable system devotes proportionally more space to diagnosis tooling than to optimisation83.

The classical response is checkpointing, and the classical analysis still applies: there is an optimal interval balancing the cost of writing against the expected work lost, and it scales as the square root of the product of write cost and mean time to failure124. What has changed is that checkpoint state for a large model is terabytes, so writing it to a shared filesystem at a useful cadence is itself a systems problem; in-memory and hierarchical checkpointing exist precisely to make the interval short enough for the failure rate125.

The more interesting responses change the dependency structure rather than the recovery time. Elastic and fault-tolerant training reconfigures the job around a failed worker instead of restarting it, which requires the parallelism plan to be re-derivable at run time126; Oobleck maintains pre-computed heterogeneous pipeline templates so that a stage loss can be absorbed by re-planning rather than re-launching127; Bamboo uses redundant computation across pipeline stages so that a preempted node's work can be reconstructed by its neighbours128. All three are, in the vocabulary of this article, attempts to make a seam survivable when one side of it disappears.

One last item belongs here because it is routinely underestimated: determinism. Collective reductions are not associative in floating point, and the order in which a ring or a tree combines contributions can vary with topology, message size, and library version. A cluster that reduces gradients in a different order after a reconfiguration is running a subtly different optimisation, which makes an unreproducible run hard to distinguish from a buggy one129. When a job that has been running for weeks starts diverging, the ability to rule out the seam is worth a great deal.

The ledger

Fifteen cuts, one scale.

Collecting the plates onto a single pair of axes — volume per device against cadence — makes the shape of the design space visible in a way that no individual comparison does.

The first thing the scatter shows is that seam volumes are not very different from one another. Excluding the two plates with no wire at all, every seam in this article falls between 134 MB and 280 GB — a range of about two thousand, most of it accounted for by one outlier. Cadence varies far more. That asymmetry is the reason a per-collective byte count is such a poor guide to whether a strategy will work.

Multiplying volume by cadence gives the per-step figure in the second panel, and it rearranges the ranking. The data-parallel all-reduce moves 279.4 GB per device per step, close to four times what the tensor and sequence all-reduces move at 75.2 GB. On volume alone, data parallelism looks like the expensive one and tensor parallelism looks cheap — which is the opposite of the operational reality, and the reason is the third column rather than the second.

Of the 382.5 GB that the fully composed configuration moves per step, only 85.9 GB — 22 per cent — sits on the critical path: the tensor and sequence all-reduces, whose outputs feed the very next matrix multiply, and the expert all-to-all, which synchronises on its slowest destination. The other 78 per cent — the data-parallel exchange and the pipeline sends — has independent work available to overlap it and, on an adequate fabric, costs nothing. The plate with the largest bar is not the plate that hurts.

The two plates with no bar at all — the single device, and recomputation — mark the boundary of the whole framing, because they are the configurations in which there is no wire to pay for. Recomputation earns its place in the ledger precisely by being the one entry that converts a bandwidth problem into an arithmetic one.

What the question is good for

The taxonomy is useful because it is finite. There are four spatial axes in a dense transformer's iteration space, a fifth learned axis in a sparse one, and time. Every technique in this article, and as far as I can tell every technique that has been proposed, is a cut along one of those, a schedule for a cut, a compression of a seam, or a compiler that chooses among them. That is a small enough space to hold in your head, and holding it in your head is what lets you evaluate a new proposal quickly: which axis does it cut, what does the seam cost, how often is it paid, and is there anything to hide it behind?

It also makes the open problems legible. The token axis is the least mature, and it is the one under the most pressure, because context length has been growing faster than either model width or device memory. Automatic partitioning has been technically possible for years8164 and is still not how frontier models are configured, because the search objective depends on a fabric model that nobody has made accurate enough. Expert parallelism has a seam whose balance is a learned quantity, which is a genuinely novel systems situation and not yet a solved one. And inference has inherited the whole taxonomy at a moment when serving cost, rather than training cost, has become the number most organisations actually care about.

What has not changed, and probably will not, is the shape of the problem. Arithmetic has been getting cheaper faster than bandwidth for as long as anyone has been measuring, and there is no indication of that reversing. Each new generation of accelerator makes the compute side of every ratio in this article larger, which makes every seam relatively more expensive, which pushes every cut toward the fastest wire available and every design toward larger coherent domains. The question stays the same and the answer keeps moving. It is worth knowing which one you are looking at.

E5  TENSOR PARALLELISM -- the seam is inside the layer, and it is on the critical path
==============================================================================

A Megatron-style transformer block sharded t ways needs 4 all-
reduces per block per step (2 forward, 2 backward), each of
s*b*h*2 bytes. Block compute (fwd+bwd) is about 72*s*b*h^2 FLOP.

Neither all-reduce can be overlapped: the next matrix multiply
consumes its output.

Setting comm time = 20% of compute time gives a critical fabric
bandwidth that does not depend on s or b at all:

    B_crit = (10/9) * (t-1) * F / h

With F = 1000 TFLOP/s and h = 8192:

   16         2035 GB/s     NO               NO
   32         4205 GB/s     NO               NO

E6  EXPERT PARALLELISM -- a data-dependent seam
==============================================================================

T = 8192 tokens, E = 64 experts, top-1 routing. Capacity per expert
is c * T * k / E; tokens beyond capacity are dropped.

Two routers, both seeded and reproducible:
  uniform  -- the idealisation every capacity calculation assumes
  skewed   -- Zipf(s) popularity, the failure mode routers fall into

   router          max/mean load   tokens to busiest expert
   --------------------------------------------------------------
   uniform            1.188        152
   zipf-0.5           4.148        531
   zipf-1.0          13.930        1783

Dropped tokens as a function of capacity factor c:

   c        uniform    zipf-0.5   zipf-1.0
   ----------------------------------------------
   1.00        3.52%    20.28%    46.69%
   1.10        0.66%    17.74%    44.97%
   1.25        0.00%    14.50%    42.59%
   1.50        0.00%    10.91%    38.95%
   2.00        0.00%     6.25%    33.29%
   4.00        0.00%     0.23%    20.68%

Read the first row carefully. Even a PERFECTLY uniform router drops
3.52% of tokens at c = 1.0, purely from multinomial fluctuation:
exact capacity is never enough, because 'on average balanced' is
not 'balanced'. It takes c = 1.25 to reach zero loss, and that 25%
of headroom is memory reserved on every device forever.

The skewed routers never recover. Zipf-1.0 still drops 20.68% of
tokens at c = 4.0 -- four times the memory, and two tokens in ten
still fall on the floor. Load balancing has to happen in the LOSS,
not in the buffer.

All-to-all volume per MoE block per step, bf16, h = 8192:
  dispatch + combine = 2 * T * k * h * 2 bytes per device
  = 0.27 GB per block per step.

   blocks   all-to-all per step   at 25 GB/s
   ----------------------------------------------------
   8              2.15 GB           0.086 s
   16             4.29 GB           0.172 s
   32             8.59 GB           0.344 s
   61            16.37 GB           0.655 s

This is the seam a compiler cannot size in advance. Its volume is
known, but its BALANCE is decided at run time by a learned router,
and one hot expert stalls every device waiting on the all-to-all.

E7  DECODE ROOFLINE -- why generation is a memory problem
==============================================================================

Machine balance = 1000 TFLOP/s / 8.0 TB/s = 125.0 FLOP per byte.

A decode step reads every weight once and does 2*psi FLOP per
sequence in the batch. Arithmetic intensity from weights alone is
therefore 2*B/bytes_per_param = B for bf16 -- the batch size, in
FLOP per byte. To saturate this device you need B >= 125.

Model: 70 B parameters, bf16, tensor-parallel 8.
  weights per rank         17.50 GB
  free for KV per rank     62.50 GB
  GQA 8 KV heads x 128 dim x 80 blocks x 2 (K,V) x 2 bytes
      = 328 kB per token, 41.0 kB per token per rank

   context   max batch (KV-bound)   intensity at that batch   verdict
   --------------------------------------------------------------------------
   2048               745.1                 163.8            compute-bound
   8192               186.3                  41.5            compute-bound
   32768               46.6                  11.0            MEMORY-BOUND
   131072              11.6                   3.3            MEMORY-BOUND
   1048576              1.5                   1.1            MEMORY-BOUND

At 131072 tokens of context the KV cache caps the batch at 11.6 -- far
below the 125 needed to reach the roofline. The device is stranded
in the memory-bound regime by a constraint that has nothing to do
with its arithmetic.

The KV cache is the whole story. Same model, three attention
variants, at 32768 tokens of context:

   variant        KV per token   max batch   reaches roofline?
   ----------------------------------------------------------------
   MHA (64 heads)    327.7 kB         5.8   no
   GQA (8 heads)      41.0 kB        46.6   no
   MQA (1 head)        5.1 kB       372.5   yes

Grouped-query attention is not only a modelling trick. It is an 8x
reduction in the size of the seam between a decode step and memory.

E8  SERVING -- what batching policy does to the same hardware
==============================================================================

Discrete-event simulation, 32 decode slots, 4000 requests, seeded.
A decode step costs one unit whatever the occupancy: the step is
memory-bound, so an idle slot is wasted work, not saved work.
Prompt and output lengths are log-normal, which is the shape that
makes this whole comparison matter.

   requests 4000, mean output 219.5 tokens, median 146, p99 1161,
   max 3756. The p99/median ratio is 8.0 -- that ratio is the entire
   argument for continuous batching.

(a) SATURATED. All 4000 requests are queued at step 0. Same work,
    same slots; the only difference is when a slot may be reused.

   policy            steps to drain   slot occupancy   speed-up
   ----------------------------------------------------------------
   static-8                309603             35.4%      0.42x
   static-16               204162             26.9%      0.64x
   static-32               129802             21.1%      1.00x
   continuous-32            28173             97.4%      4.61x

   Continuous batching drains the same queue 4.61x faster at the
   same slot count, and holds occupancy at 97.4% against 21.1%.
   Nothing about the hardware changed. The static batch simply
   holds every slot hostage to the longest sequence in its group.

(b) ONLINE. Static-32 sustains 0.0308 req/step, continuous-32 0.1420.
    Offering 0.0216 req/step -- 70% of the WEAKER policy's capacity,
    so both queues are stable and the latency comparison is fair.

   policy            mean latency   p50        p99        p99/p50
   ------------------------------------------------------------------
   static-32              1817           1772       3777     2.13
   continuous-32           223            150       1146     7.62

   At a load the static policy can comfortably carry, continuous
   batching still cuts mean latency 8.1x and p99 latency 3.3x.
   The residual p99/p50 spread of 7.62 under continuous batching is
   the workload's own length distribution, not a queueing artefact.

(c) KV ALLOCATION. A contiguous allocator must reserve max_len per
    admitted request; a paged allocator hands out 16-token blocks
    on demand. Same trace, 4000 requests.

   allocator                 reserved     used     waste    concurrency
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   contiguous (max_len)        16.38 M    3.58 M   78.13%       366.2
   paged (16-token blocks)      3.61 M    3.58 M    0.84%      1660.3

   With a 1.5 M-token KV budget the contiguous allocator supports
   366 concurrent sequences; the paged allocator supports 1660 --
   a 4.5x increase from bookkeeping alone. Since concurrency sets
   batch size, and batch size sets arithmetic intensity (E7), the
   memory allocator is a throughput decision in disguise.
