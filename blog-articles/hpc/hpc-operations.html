A supercomputer is not a thing, it is a stack of loops

A supercomputer, considered as an object, is a few thousand cabinets of silicon that arrived on trucks. Considered as an operation, it is something else entirely: a stack of feedback loops running at wildly different speeds, each one measuring something, comparing it to a set point, and moving something else in response. The power cap adjusts a thousand times a second. The chiller responds in a minute. The scheduler decides every thirty seconds. The fair-share controller forgets at a rate measured in weeks. The refresh plan turns over in five years, and the electrical service was sized against a demand curve someone drew a decade before the first rack was energised.

Every operational discipline in the standard list — monitoring, utilisation, capacity planning, maintenance, deployment, accounts, security, logging, metrics, energy, cooling — is one of these loops. Reading them as a catalogue of separate practices makes them look like eleven unrelated jobs that happen to share a building. Reading them as control loops makes something else visible: they are the same kind of object, they fail in the same characteristic way, and the failure has a name. A loop fails when its time constant is longer than the time constant of the thing it is supposed to control.

This is not a metaphor borrowed from control theory for decoration. It is the actual arithmetic. A sampled system cannot represent a disturbance faster than half its sampling rate, a result that predates the field entirely 1,2, and a controller whose integral term has a long memory will keep acting on conditions that no longer exist 3. Both of those statements have direct, measurable consequences on a production cluster, and this article measures them. The organising question throughout is the one an operator can ask of any practice, at any site, in any decade: how fast does this loop close, and is that fast enough for what it is controlling?

The figure above is the whole argument in one axis. Fifteen loops, spanning eleven orders of magnitude of time, from a package power controller acting in under a millisecond to a substation contract negotiated on a twenty-year horizon. Nothing in that range is optional. A site that closes the fast loops beautifully and the slow ones by accident ends up with an exquisitely monitored machine it cannot power in 2031; a site that plans capital brilliantly and samples at five-minute intervals ends up unable to explain why a job that should have taken six hours took eleven.

What the loops have in common

Each loop in this article is described by the same six properties, and each plate in the margin fills them in: what it senses, what set point it is holding, what it can actually move, who owns it, what happens when it is left open, and what evidence supports the claim. The uniformity is deliberate. It makes the comparison between a thermal control loop and a software deployment process a real comparison rather than an analogy, and it exposes the asymmetry that matters most in practice: the fast loops are automated and instrumented, and the slow loops are run by committees on spreadsheets. The consequences of the slow ones are larger.

The second common property is less comfortable. Every one of these loops has a tuning constant, and in almost every case the constant is more consequential than the policy it implements. The fair-share half-life decides what fairness means more decisively than the declared shares do. The log parser's similarity threshold decides which faults are detectable at all. The checkpoint interval decides what fraction of a machine is delivered to science. These constants are usually inherited, rarely revisited, and almost never justified in writing. Sections 3, 6 and 7 measure exactly how much they matter.

The lab

Everything quantitative below comes from a single deterministic Python program using nothing outside the standard library. It runs eight experiments: a space-sharing batch scheduler compared under first-come-first-served and EASY backfill; a load sweep across that scheduler; a fair-share controller with a usage half-life; the Daly checkpoint model across four machine scales; a telemetry compression and downsampling study; a log-template parser swept across its similarity threshold; a frequency–energy model of a compute node; and a first-principles heat-removal comparison with a synthetic weather year. Telemetry: the fastest loop you can afford

Cluster monitoring is usually specified as a coverage problem — every node, every metric, one pane of glass — when it is really a bandwidth problem with a sampling theorem attached. The question is not whether a node is watched. It is whether the watching happens faster than the thing being watched.

Start with cost, because cost is what sets the rate. Experiment 5 takes one node's power draw for one hour at one hertz and stores it two ways. Stored naively as a 64-bit value with a 32-bit timestamp, it is 96 bits a sample and 42.2 KiB an hour. Stored with delta-of-delta timestamp encoding and XOR-of-previous value compression — the scheme Facebook's Gorilla introduced for exactly this workload 5 — it is 15.0 bits a sample and 6.6 KiB, a factor of 6.4. That factor is what makes the fast loop affordable. Scaled to a real machine at ten thousand nodes and two hundred metrics a node, the stream is two million samples a second: 3.7 MB/s of sustained ingest and 118 TB a year at full resolution, against 24 MB/s and 757 TB a year for the naive encoding. The compression is not a storage optimisation. It is the difference between a site that can retain one-hertz data and a site that must throw away resolution to stay solvent.

Now the other half. Take the same hour and sample it more cheaply. The series contains an eight-second power excursion of the kind a collective communication phase or a synchronised solver iteration produces routinely. At one hertz, eight samples land inside it and the peak reads 517 W. At four seconds, two samples land inside it. At ten seconds, one does. At sixty seconds and at five minutes, none do: the peak reads 408.6 W, low by 21%, and the event does not exist in the record at all. Meanwhile the mean is nearly untouched — off by 1.93% at five-minute polling. This is the whole trap in one table. The statistic that survives coarse sampling is the one used for capacity planning and chargeback; the statistic that dies is the one used for protection, diagnosis and blame.

The consequence is that "how often do we sample" is not one decision. It is at least three, with different answers. Equipment protection needs a rate faster than the thermal or electrical time constant it protects — milliseconds for package power, seconds for rack-level breakers. Diagnosis needs a rate fast enough to resolve the application's own phase structure, which for a tightly coupled job means seconds or better; job-level power studies at production scale find phase behaviour that simply is not visible at coarse intervals 6. Accounting and planning need almost nothing: minute averages are ample and cheap. Sites that pick a single global interval are implicitly choosing which of those three to fail at, and because the failure is silent, they usually discover the choice during an incident review.

The architecture that resolves this is tiered rather than uniform: high-rate collection close to the node with short retention, aggregated to lower rates for long retention. The Lightweight Distributed Metric Service was built on precisely that principle for large-scale continuous monitoring, sampling on the node and transporting off the compute fabric so that the monitoring loop does not perturb the thing it measures 7. The general-purpose monitoring stacks most sites now run alongside HPC-specific collectors were designed around the same ingest economics — scraping into a compressed local time-series store 8 — and the discipline of deciding which signals justify waking someone, rather than collecting everything and alerting on all of it, is the one genuinely transferable import from web-scale operations practice 9. Out-of-band paths matter for the same reason: a baseboard controller exposing a standard telemetry model 10 keeps sampling when the operating system is the thing that has failed, which is exactly when the samples are most valuable.

Metrics are not the same object as telemetry

A distinction worth holding: a metric is a number with a name and a timestamp; a job record is a closed-out statement about a unit of work. The first is a stream, the second is a fact. Sites conflate them and then discover they cannot answer the question that actually gets asked — not "what was node 1174 doing at 03:12" but "what did this project consume last quarter, and was any of it wasted".

Answering the second question requires per-job resource use attributed to an account, which the resource manager only partly provides. This is why the community built job-level monitoring that joins hardware counters to scheduler records 11, and why the reporting layer above it became its own tool 12. The distinction has real operational teeth: aggregate node-hours say the machine was 90% busy, while per-job records reveal how much of that occupancy was memory-idle, accelerator-idle, or a rank waiting on a straggler. Studies of multi-tenant accelerator clusters routinely find allocated capacity substantially exceeding used capacity 13, and analyses of node-level resource use find memory capacity provisioned far above what most jobs touch 14. None of that is visible from a utilisation percentage, which is why the next three sections treat utilisation with suspicion.

Logs: the loop that has to be read

Logging is the one operational loop whose output is consumed by a human, and its time constant is therefore set by human attention rather than by hardware. A large machine emits log lines at a rate no one can read. The loop closes only if something reduces the stream to a reading list, and the quality of that reduction is an engineering property with a measurable failure mode.

The insight that makes reduction possible is old and simple: a log line is a template plus parameters. The template is a fixed string in some source file; the parameters are the node number, the job id, the byte count. Recover the templates and a stream of millions of lines becomes a small vocabulary with frequencies attached. The canonical study of supercomputer logs made the point early — that raw log volume vastly overstates the information content, and that the useful signal lives in structure and rarity rather than in text 15. Modern parsers formalise it, most influentially Drain, which keys a fixed-depth tree on token count and leading tokens and then merges lines by similarity 16, with an accompanying literature on how the parsers compare on real corpora 17,18.

Experiment 6 implements a stripped-down parser of exactly that family and runs it against 200,000 lines drawn from 22 routine templates, with three fault templates — a corrected machine-check exception, a filesystem eviction, and a critical processor temperature — injected 58 times in total, 0.029% of the stream. The parser has no domain knowledge. It masks bare integers, keys on token count and first token, and merges by similarity. The result is 25 templates recovered from 25, at 100.00% accuracy, and the encoded stream — one template id plus its parameters — is 4.1 times smaller than the text. Flagging every template seen fewer than 200 times reduces the operator's reading list from 200,000 lines to 58: a 3,448-fold reduction, with all three faults in it and nothing else.

That is the good result, and it is worth being precise about what produced it, because the same experiment produces a much worse one. The parser has a single tuning constant — the similarity threshold above which two lines are considered the same template — and the sweep across it is the actual finding.

At a threshold of 0.50, the parser recovers 23 templates rather than 25 and isolates only two of the three faults. The reason is specific and instructive: the critical-temperature line is textually close enough to the routine power-sensor line, once both have had their integers masked, that it is absorbed into it. The fault does not appear as a rare template requiring attention. It appears as nine more instances of a template that fires nine thousand times a day. It is not merely missed; it is hidden, and hidden by the very mechanism installed to reveal it. At 0.80 the failure inverts: the parser recovers 1,154 templates and the reading list swells to 1,183 lines. Every line is novel, so nothing is.

The usable band in this experiment is 0.60 to 0.70. Its location is a property of the log vocabulary — how many fixed tokens a typical message has, how many variable fields — and not of the machine's health, the operator's skill, or anything that will announce itself when it changes. Add a new firmware version with chattier messages and the band moves. This is the general shape of the problem the anomaly-detection literature keeps rediscovering: the detector's parameters, not the detector's design, determine what class of event is detectable 19, and closed-loop approaches that feed detection back into prediction inherit that sensitivity wholesale 20.

Scheduling: what backfill actually buys

The scheduler is the loop with the tightest coupling to user experience and the loosest coupling to anything physical. It runs every few seconds, holds a set point that nobody writes down honestly, and moves the only actuator that matters at the minute scale: which job starts next.

The mechanism at the centre of every production batch scheduler is backfill, and it is worth stating exactly, because it is routinely described as "letting small jobs jump the queue", which is both unflattering and wrong. Under first-come-first-served, a job that needs 256 nodes waits until 256 nodes are free, and every node that frees up in the meantime sits idle waiting with it. Backfill, introduced in production form on the ANL/IBM SP 22 and now standard, adds a single predicate: a job further down the queue may start immediately if it can be proven not to delay the reservation already granted to the job at the head. In the EASY variant, that proof uses the users' own requested walltimes to compute a shadow time — the moment the head job is promised its nodes — and admits any job that either finishes before the shadow time or fits in the nodes left spare at it 23.

Experiment 1 implements a space-sharing scheduler with that predicate and runs the identical 1,200-job workload through it twice. The job mix is the one every site recognises: power-of-two-heavy sizes from 1 to 256 nodes, heavy-tailed runtimes, and requested walltimes with a median 2.46 times the true runtime, which is the single most durable empirical fact about batch users 23,24. Under steady arrivals at 0.90 offered load, backfill leaves achieved utilisation essentially unchanged but cuts mean wait from 1.36 hours to 0.13 — a 90.2% reduction — and cuts the 95th percentile bounded slowdown from 22.50 to 3.15, with 716 of 1,200 jobs running out of order.

The unchanged utilisation is the part worth dwelling on. Backfill did not create work. At steady state the machine processes exactly the work that arrives and no more; what backfill removed was queueing delay from work that was already there. This is why utilisation is such a poor advertisement for a scheduling change and wait-time distributions are such a good one.

The picture is different when the machine is not in steady state. Run the same scheduler against 400 jobs all queued at once — a drained machine coming back from a maintenance window, which is a situation every site meets monthly — and there is no arrival stream to hide behind. Strict order clears the backlog in 13.56 hours at 63.8% occupancy. Backfill clears the same 400 jobs in 9.40 hours at 92.0%: 30.7% sooner, with 28.2 more points of the machine occupied while it works.

The figure shows why, and it shows it more honestly than the numbers do. The upper panel is not a badly configured machine. It is a correctly configured machine executing a policy that guarantees fairness in the strictest possible sense, and the white space is the price of that guarantee. The lower panel is the same jobs, the same order of arrival, the same guarantee for the job at the head of the queue, with one predicate added.

Where the predicate gets its data

Backfill's proof depends entirely on requested walltimes, and requested walltimes are wrong. The 2.46-times median over-request in the experiment is conservative against the field literature. This has a counter-intuitive consequence, first characterised carefully by Mu'alem and Feitelson: inaccurate estimates do not merely reduce backfill's effectiveness, they can increase it, because generously padded walltimes create the appearance of large holes that short jobs then fill 23. Systems that replace user estimates with system-generated predictions have to be designed with that effect in mind rather than treating prediction accuracy as an unalloyed good 25. The broader characterisation of backfilling strategies — how deep to look, how many reservations to hold, whether to sort the backfill queue — has been mapped in detail 26,27, and modern resource managers expose all of it as configuration 28,29.

Two operational points follow. The scheduler's dispatch cycle is itself a time constant: a controller that reconsiders the queue every thirty seconds cannot respond to a node failure in less than thirty seconds, and on a large machine the cycle is bounded by how long it takes to evaluate the backfill window at all, which is why production schedulers expose limits on backfill depth and duration 29,30. And the co-scheduling decision sits underneath everything in the next section: jobs placed adjacently on a shared network interfere with each other measurably, so the occupancy the scheduler reports is not the throughput the users get 31.

Capacity: utilisation is a position, not a score

Utilisation is the most reported number in high-performance computing operations and the least interpretable. It appears in annual reports, in funding renewals, in vendor comparisons, and in the sentence "we run at 95%", delivered with pride. It deserves considerably more suspicion than it gets, for a reason that has been understood in queueing theory since long before there were clusters to apply it to 32,33.

Experiment 2 holds the job mix and the scheduler fixed and sweeps only the arrival rate. Every row is the same 2,400 jobs; the only difference is how fast they are submitted. At 0.50 offered load the machine achieves 49.9% utilisation and the mean wait is 0.01 hours — jobs start when they arrive. At 0.75 offered, 73.7% achieved, the mean wait is 0.06 hours. At 0.95 offered, 91.1% achieved, the mean wait is 0.48 hours and the 95th percentile is 2.86. Those last two rows are the whole argument: 17.4 additional points of machine utilisation cost 8.1 times the mean wait and 7.2 times the p95.

There is nothing pathological in that trade. It is the shape of every queueing system ever measured, and the shape is not linear. Wait does not rise in proportion to load; it rises against the residual capacity, which is what makes the last few points so expensive. Little's law tells you the queue length that a given wait and arrival rate imply 34; the utilisation curve tells you what you paid for it.

Two further facts in that table are worth extracting. First, offering more than the machine can process does not raise utilisation. Offering 1.05 achieves 92.6% — barely above the 91.1% achieved at 0.95 offered — while mean wait climbs to 0.99 hours and the p95 to 5.59. Past saturation, the surplus does not become throughput. It becomes queue, and queue is a cost borne entirely by users while appearing on no operational dashboard.

Second, and more interesting: achieved utilisation never reaches 100%, and never can. The ceiling in this experiment is around 92%, and it is set by fragmentation — the arithmetic mismatch between the shapes of the jobs and the shape of the machine. A 512-node machine with 384 nodes free and only 256-node jobs eligible is 75% utilised and cannot do better at that instant. No amount of scheduler diligence recovers those nodes; only a different job mix does. Any target above the fragmentation ceiling is a target that can only be met by lengthening the queue.

What the number is actually for

The operational reform this suggests is small and unpopular: stop reporting utilisation alone and start reporting the pair. A site running at 73.7% with a four-minute median wait and a site running at 91.1% with a half-hour mean wait and a three-hour p95 are making different, defensible, opposite choices. Neither is better. Reported as single numbers, the second looks strictly superior, which is how sites end up chasing a statistic into a queue.

Capacity planning proper is the slowest loop in this article's first half. Its sensor is the demand that was not served — the allocation requests that were cut, the jobs that were never submitted because the queue made them pointless, the classes of science that moved elsewhere. That signal is structurally hard to collect, because it consists of things that did not happen, and the accounting database only records things that did. Sites that plan well supplement it deliberately: allocation request totals against awards, wait-time distributions broken out by job size class rather than averaged, and the observation that suppressed demand shows up as a change in the shape of the submitted mix rather than its volume 12,24.

Accounts: fair share is an integral controller

User and account management looks administrative. Creating users, mapping them to projects, attaching projects to allocations, expiring credentials: it reads like a database maintenance task. Underneath it is a controller, and one with a specific and consequential structure — it is an integrator, and integrators have memory.

The mechanism dates to a 1988 paper that named the problem exactly: how do you give users a defined share of a machine over time, when at any instant the machine can only run one thing 35? The answer, in the form every production scheduler now implements, is to compute a priority factor that falls as an account's recent usage rises relative to its declared share, and to let that factor order the queue. In Slurm's multifactor form the fair-share factor is two raised to the negative ratio of normalised effective usage to normalised share, and historical usage is decayed with a configurable half-life 36,29.

Experiment 3 runs that controller with four accounts holding declared shares of 50, 25, 15 and 10 per cent, all of them submitting more work than they can be given, every day, for 180 days. Each hour of the machine goes to the eligible account with the highest factor. The controller works: at a 28-day half-life the delivered shares are 50.0, 25.0, 15.0 and 10.0 per cent, a maximum error of 0.05%, reached within six days and held for the remaining 174. At a seven-day half-life the error is 0.19%; at one day, 1.02%. As a regulator holding a set point under constant load, this is a good controller.

Constant load is not the interesting case. The interesting case is the one every centre actually faces: an account goes quiet — the postdoc is writing up, the instrument is down, the grant is between phases — and then comes back. What should happen? The declared share says 10 per cent. The controller says something else entirely, and what it says depends not on the share but on the half-life.

Experiment 3 idles one account for thirty days and measures the return. At a one-day half-life, the returning account peaks at 20.8% of the machine on its first day back and banks 0.86 machine-days of above-share usage in total: the controller has forgotten the absence almost as fast as it happened, so almost no credit accrued. At a seven-day half-life, it takes 100% of the machine on the day it returns and banks 1.55 machine-days. At twenty-eight days, 100% again and 2.65 machine-days. The declared share is 10 per cent in every one of those rows. Three times the credit, from one constant, with no policy document changed and no committee consulted.

There is no correct answer here, which is the point. Both behaviours are defensible policies. "Use it or lose it" — a short half-life — favours accounts that submit steadily and penalises the observational-astronomy pattern of bursting hard around a data release. "Banked credit" — a long half-life — does the reverse, and hands a genuinely idle account the ability to disrupt everyone else's month at a time of its choosing. The failure is not choosing the wrong one. The failure is choosing it accidentally, by inheriting a configuration file, and then discovering during an angry meeting that the site's actual fairness policy has been a decay constant nobody read.

The rest of the account loop

Two adjacent controls belong with this one. The first is limits: fair share orders the queue, it does not cap anything, so a site that wants a hard bound needs per-account throughput and resource limits alongside it, which is a different control with a different time constant — instantaneous rather than integrated 29,30. The second is identity lifetime, which is where account management crosses into §9. An account is a credential with an expiry, and the interval between a researcher leaving and their access ending is one of the few operational numbers with a direct security interpretation 37.

Hardware: the loop that outlives its own components

Hardware maintenance is where the arithmetic of scale becomes brutal and unarguable. Take a component with an excellent mean time between failures — five years, 43,830 hours, better than most field studies report for real nodes — and assume failures are independent. The system's failure rate is then the sum of the nodes' rates, and the system MTBF is the node MTBF divided by the node count. At 1,000 nodes that is 43.8 hours: something breaks every couple of days. At 100,000 nodes it is 26.3 minutes. At a million, 2.6 minutes.

Nothing about the individual component got worse. This is the entire content of the exascale resilience problem, and it was seen coming a long way off 38,39. The empirical work underneath it is worth taking seriously rather than assuming: large-scale field studies of production systems find failure rates that vary by more than an order of magnitude between systems of similar size, strong correlation with node count and with system age, and distributions that are decidedly not the exponential the arithmetic above assumes 40,41,42. The point of the calculation is not that it is exact. It is that even under the most favourable possible assumption — independent failures, generous component lifetime — scale alone destroys availability.

The checkpoint interval is an operational parameter, not an application one

Applications survive this by checkpointing, and the interval is a genuine optimisation with a closed-form answer. Checkpoint too often and wall time disappears into writing state; too rarely and each failure costs more recomputation. Young gave the first-order optimum in 1974 — the square root of twice the checkpoint cost times the mean time between failures 43 — and Daly extended it to higher order and to the regime where the interval is not small compared with the MTBF 44.

Experiment 4 evaluates the Daly optimum and the resulting efficiency across four machine scales and two checkpoint costs. At 1,000 nodes with a 300-second checkpoint, the optimum interval is 158.9 minutes and the application spends 93.75% of its wall time on useful work. At 100,000 nodes it is 13.1 minutes and 41.60%. At a million nodes the optimum interval collapses to 2.3 minutes and efficiency to 0.87% — the machine spends essentially all of its time writing and re-reading state. Now change one thing. Reduce the checkpoint cost from 300 seconds to 30, and the million-node machine returns 41.63% instead of 0.87%: a factor of 48 in delivered capability.

The hardware in every bar of that figure is identical. The science is identical. The difference is entirely an operational property — how fast the site can get application state onto stable storage — which is why multi-level checkpointing, node-local NVMe, burst buffers and asynchronous writes are not storage-team luxuries but the dominant lever on delivered capability at scale 45,46. It is also why the checkpoint interval should be set from the observed system MTBF rather than inherited: the same application, unchanged, needs a different interval on a machine that is six months older.

Failures you can see, and failures you cannot

The maintenance loop has a fast half and a slow half. The fast half is health checking: a prologue that verifies mounts, memory size, accelerator presence, error counters and scratch writability before a job is placed, and a state machine that drains a node the moment it fails one 47,48. Its time constant is minutes, and it exists to solve one specific pathology — a sick node that accepts a job, kills it in thirty seconds, and becomes eligible again, consuming an entire queue one job at a time.

The slow half is component demography, and it runs on the timescale of procurement. The foundational field studies here are old enough to be uncomfortable and have never been contradicted: disk replacement rates in the field routinely exceed datasheet MTBF by an order of magnitude 49, failure is strongly age- and population-dependent rather than memoryless 50, and DRAM error rates in production are orders of magnitude higher than laboratory testing predicted, dominated by hard errors in a minority of modules 51,52. At the facility level the picture is consistent: industry outage surveys continue to find power and cooling infrastructure among the leading causes of significant outages, ahead of the compute hardware that gets the attention 53.

8SUSTAIN

Deployment: the loop with the longest memory

Software deployment on a cluster has a property that distinguishes it from almost every other kind: the artefacts have to still work in ten years, for people who are not there yet, on hardware that does not exist, and the results they produced have to remain defensible in the literature. The loop's nominal time constant is a release cadence measured in weeks. Its real time constant is the lifetime of the science.

The combinatorics are the first problem and they are worse than they look. A scientific application is not one package but a graph — a solver on an MPI implementation on a compiler on a BLAS on a set of accelerator runtimes — and each edge has version constraints, each node has build options, and the same package may need to exist several times over in mutually incompatible variants because two applications require different MPI implementations. Environment modules were the field's first answer, and the mechanism is still the right one: mutate the user's environment at load time rather than installing anything globally 56,48. What modules do not solve is how the installed tree got built.

That is a dependency-resolution problem, and treating it as one rather than as a scripting problem is what changed the field. Spack expresses the build as a specification — package, version, compiler, variants, dependencies — and concretises it into a fully determined graph before building anything 57; the concretiser was eventually reimplemented on an answer-set solver, because the constraint problem it embodies is genuinely combinatorial and ad hoc resolution was producing subtly wrong answers 58. EasyBuild approaches the same problem through parameterised build recipes and toolchain definitions 59, and OpenHPC assembles the whole into a validated distribution so that sites are not each solving it privately 60.

Containers occupy an awkward and useful position alongside this. The appeal is obvious: hand the user an image and the dependency graph becomes their problem, solved once, portable. The complication is that HPC containers must run unprivileged on a shared machine, must see the host's high-performance network and accelerator drivers, and must not become a way for a user to obtain privileges they were not granted — constraints that ordinary container runtimes do not satisfy, which is why the field built its own 61 62. A container also freezes exactly the thing that most needs to track the host: the interconnect stack. An image built against one MPI and moved to a machine with a different fabric will often run, slowly, in a way that looks like an application problem for weeks.

Provenance is the actual deliverable

The reason this loop is slow is not that building software is hard. It is that the output has to be re-derivable. A published result rests on a binary, which rests on a source tree, a compiler version, a set of flags, and a mathematics library whose vectorised reduction order is not the same as the scalar one. If none of that was recorded, the result is not reproducible even in principle, and nobody discovers this until someone tries — typically three years later, when the module has been deprecated and the compiler licence has lapsed.

The remedy is unglamorous and entirely procedural: build from a recorded specification, keep the specification in version control alongside the code that used it, and record the resolved graph — not the request — in the job's provenance. The reproducible-builds community has done the hard thinking on what it takes for a build to be verifiable rather than merely documented 63, and the argument transfers directly to a facility that will be asked to defend a number in a paper long after the machine has been decommissioned.

There is a security dimension here that §9 will pick up but that belongs to deployment first. The software a centre installs is overwhelmingly third-party source, built from upstream tarballs, by an automated pipeline, at scale. The XZ Utils compromise demonstrated that this supply chain is a live target and that a sufficiently patient attacker can place code inside a build artefact that is not visible in the repository the maintainers read 64. A package manager that pins and verifies content hashes is not bureaucratic overhead in that light. It is the only part of the deployment loop that has a sensor at all.

9DEFEND

Security: the loop that must not slow the others

Security in high-performance computing has a structural problem that most enterprise security does not: the environment is explicitly built to let large numbers of semi-trusted people run arbitrary code, at maximum speed, on shared hardware, with a filesystem designed for throughput rather than isolation. Most of the standard enterprise control set assumes it can insert itself in the data path. Here it cannot, because the data path is the product.

That tension went uncodified for a long time. The argument that HPC needed its own security treatment rather than a borrowed one was made carefully in the mid-2010s 65, and the formal answer arrived in 2024 with NIST SP 800-223, which does the single most useful thing a standard can do here: it stops treating the machine as one system 66. The reference architecture divides an HPC system into four functional zones — access, management, compute, and data storage — each with distinct exposure, distinct performance constraints, and therefore distinct controls. A login node facing the internet and a compute node on a private fabric are not the same asset and should not inherit the same control set. The companion overlay, SP 800-234, finalised in 2026, does the follow-through: it takes the SP 800-53 moderate baseline and tailors sixty controls specifically for HPC, with supplemental guidance where the generic control would be either meaningless or ruinous 67,68. A further draft extends the same zone-based reasoning to AI-oriented data centres, which increasingly share the same hardware and the same operators 69.

The zone model is worth taking seriously because it resolves the argument that otherwise recurs at every site: whether to instrument the compute nodes as heavily as the login nodes. Under the zone model, the answer is that they are different zones with different threat exposure, and the compute zone's defence is largely inherited from the access and management zones that gate it. That is not laxity; it is where the controls can be afforded.

Where the controls actually sit

Three loops carry most of the load. The first is identity, and its time constant is the credential lifetime. HPC's traditional posture — long-lived SSH keys, shared project accounts, hand-maintained authorised-key files — fails the basic requirement that a privileged action be attributable to a named human, and the modern guidance on authenticator lifecycle applies here as anywhere else 37. Inside the machine, node-to-node and daemon-to-daemon authentication is its own problem: a scheduler that trusts any host claiming to be a compute node has no security boundary at all, which is why credential services designed for exactly this pattern are standard infrastructure 70, and why current resource managers have moved toward encrypting internal traffic by default rather than assuming the management network is private 71,29.

The second is segmentation, which is the zone model made physical. The management network should not be reachable from the compute fabric; the storage network should not be reachable from a user shell; a login node should be assumed compromised in the threat model rather than assumed clean. This is a zero-trust posture applied to a specific architecture rather than a generic one 72, and it is the control most likely to bound the damage of the scenario that has actually happened repeatedly to academic computing: a stolen credential from one institution used to log into a collaborator's cluster, then a local privilege escalation, then cryptomining or lateral movement across an entire federation of sites that trusted each other's users.

The third is the supply chain, which §8 has already described from the other side. A centre that builds thousands of packages from upstream source is executing enormous quantities of third-party build logic as a privileged service. The XZ Utils backdoor is the canonical demonstration that this is a targeted path and not a theoretical one 64, and content-addressed, verified, reproducible builds are the mitigation that also happens to be good engineering practice for entirely unrelated reasons 63.

10POWER

Energy: the loop with a price attached

Energy efficiency became an operational discipline rather than a reporting obligation when power stopped being a line item and started being a constraint on what could be built. The macro numbers are now unavoidable: data centres consumed around 415 TWh in 2024, roughly 1.5% of global electricity, and the IEA's base case has that more than doubling to about 945 TWh by 2030 73. The careful bottom-up work that preceded the current surge is worth keeping in view precisely because it corrected an earlier round of alarmism — efficiency gains offset a decade of demand growth almost exactly 74 — and the national-scale accounting has been revised sharply upward since 75. For a facility operator none of this is atmosphere. It is the reason the next procurement has a power envelope in the requirements document.

Inside the machine, the fastest loop in this entire article is the package power controller: on-die energy counters read by firmware at kilohertz rates, adjusting voltage and frequency to hold a bound. Its time constant is under a millisecond, and the reason that matters is that a bound enforced in hardware is qualitatively different from a frequency setting chosen in advance. It holds under any instruction mix, including the ones nobody tested 76. The measurement side is mature enough to be trusted with caveats: the on-die interfaces are accurate for relative work and good enough for absolute work with known limitations, which have been characterised carefully 77,78.

What that enables is the interesting part. If power is enforced rather than estimated, a site can install more nodes than it can run at full power simultaneously and distribute a power budget across whatever is actually running — hardware overprovisioning, which turns a nameplate-limited machine into a workload-limited one and reliably delivers more science per watt of contracted supply 79,80. That in turn requires the budget to be allocated by something that knows what jobs exist, which is why power management has migrated from firmware settings toward runtimes and interfaces the scheduler can address 81,82.

One knob, two answers

Experiment 7 models a node whose runtime has a frequency-sensitive part and a frequency-invariant, memory-bound part, with power as a static term plus a cubic term in frequency. Both are normalised to a nominal 3.00 GHz. Sweeping frequency traces a curve in the energy–time plane, and the curve's shape depends entirely on how memory-bound the job is.

For a job that is 80% memory-bound, minimum energy is at 1.20 GHz: 30% slower for 55% less energy, which is close to free if the queue can absorb the time. For a job that is only 20% memory-bound, minimum energy is at 1.70 GHz and costs 61% more time for 31% less energy — a far worse trade, and one many sites would refuse. The minimum of the energy-delay product, the usual compromise metric, sits at 1.50 GHz and 2.45 GHz respectively for the same two jobs.

A single site-wide frequency cap therefore cannot be right. It will either leave most of the available saving on the table for the memory-bound jobs or impose an unacceptable slowdown on the compute-bound ones. This is exactly the conclusion the power-aware runtime literature reached experimentally two decades ago 83,84 and that production energy-aware scheduling has since operationalised, most visibly at LRZ, where per-job frequency selection was driven from application characterisation rather than from a global default 85. The scheduler knows which job is which. The BIOS does not, and never will.

11POWER

Cooling and power: the loop that closed before the machine arrived

Everything so far has had a loop that operations can close during the machine's lifetime. This one mostly cannot. The cooling plant and the electrical service are specified years before the hardware exists, on a horizon longer than the machine's operational life, and once poured in concrete they set the boundary conditions for every faster loop in the stack.

Begin with the physics, because it settles an argument that is often conducted as a matter of taste. Experiment 8 asks what it takes to remove 100 kW from a single rack — a density that is now unremarkable and that accelerated nodes exceed comfortably. Using air with a 12 K rise, it takes 8.29 kg/s, which is 6.91 m³/s, or 14,641 CFM through one cabinet. Using water with a 10 K rise, it takes 2.39 kg/s: 2.40 litres a second, about 38 GPM. The ratio of volumetric heat capacity between the two fluids is 3,456 to 1, and fan power rises with roughly the cube of flow while pump power for a couple of litres a second does not. The transition to direct liquid cooling in high-density computing was not a fashion; it was the point at which the air-side arithmetic stopped closing 91,92. The largest current systems are liquid cooled for precisely this reason 93,94.

The second question is what temperature the liquid should be, and that is where the money is. ASHRAE's thermal guidelines define a recommended envelope for air-cooled equipment and a set of liquid-cooling classes named for their maximum supply temperature — W17, W27, W32, W40, W45 and W+ 95. The class chosen determines whether the site needs mechanical refrigeration at all, because a dry cooler or cooling tower can reject heat to ambient whenever ambient plus the approach temperature is below the supply limit.

Experiment 8 generates a synthetic temperate year — mean 11.0 °C, peak 29.0 °C — and counts, with a 6 K dry-cooler approach, the hours in which the chiller can stay off. At W17 it is 4,394 hours, 50.2% of the year. At W27, 7,852 hours, 89.6%. At W32, 8,693 hours, 99.2%. At W40 and above, the whole year.

Half the year of chiller operation, or one per cent, from a number written in a procurement document. The silicon is indifferent — it runs at its junction temperature either way — and the difference is continuous, invisible and enormous. This is the case that warm-water cooling advocates have been making with production data for over a decade, from IBM's hot-water systems and the Aquasar demonstration onward 96, reinforced by the finding that raising data centre temperatures improves efficiency far more than the reliability folklore suggested it would cost 97. The systematic version of the argument treats the building, the plant, the machine and the workload as one optimisation rather than four 98.

The slowest loop of all

Underneath the coolant loop is the one that cannot be re-tuned: contracted electrical supply, distribution topology, floor loading and the physical space for plant. Its time constant is longer than the machine's life and, in many cases, longer than the career of the person who signed it. Its sensor is a demand projection for hardware that has not been designed, and the projection has been wrong in the same direction for three consecutive generations — rack densities have risen faster than nearly anyone planned for.

Facility standards give the vocabulary for stating capacity and availability requirements in a way that survives a change of personnel 99, and the efficiency indicators give the vocabulary for stating outcomes 86,87. What neither gives is the projection. The honest operational posture is to treat the plant as the binding constraint it is: to know the site's headroom in kilowatts and litres per second as precisely as its headroom in node-hours, and to publish both to the people writing the next procurement. The most consequential number in high-performance computing operations is often not on the TOP500 list 100; it is the size of the electrical service.

Three things fall out of reading it in this order.

The fast loops are automated and the slow loops are not. Everything above the one-minute line runs without a human: firmware, a scheduler, a control system, a parser. Everything below the one-day line runs on meetings, spreadsheets and institutional memory. That is not irrational — slow loops involve money and judgement — but it means the loops with the largest consequences have the weakest instrumentation, the least tested tuning, and no alarm when they drift. A site can tell you its scheduler's backfill depth to the integer and cannot tell you the assumption behind its cooling capacity. This is the configuration that the safety literature has spent forty years describing: tight coupling between fast components, loose and undocumented coupling between slow ones, and an operator expected to reason across both at the moment things go wrong 101,102.

Almost every failure in this article is a tuning constant, not a design flaw. The log parser's similarity threshold decides whether a critical-temperature message is visible or absorbed. The fair-share half-life decides whether an idle account banks 0.86 machine-days or 2.65. The checkpoint cost decides whether a million-node machine returns 0.87% or 41.63% of itself. The supply-water class decides whether the chiller runs half the year or one per cent of it. In each case the mechanism was correctly designed and correctly implemented, and the outcome was determined by a number that someone inherited. The single highest-value operational practice implied by all of this is embarrassingly cheap: write down each constant, the date it was chosen, the reasoning, and the observation that would justify changing it.

Utilisation is the wrong headline. It is the number every centre reports and the one that carries the least information, because it is a position on a curve whose other axis — wait — is not reported alongside it, and because its ceiling is set by fragmentation rather than by effort. The pair is informative and the single number is not. The same is true of most operational statistics in this article: availability without a definition of what counts as available, PUE without a load factor, mean power without a peak. Every one of them is a loop's output reported without the loop's set point.

What the frame predicts

The useful property of reading operations this way is that it generates a diagnostic rather than a checklist. When something goes wrong, the question is not which discipline failed but which loop was slower than the phenomenon it controlled — and the answer is usually available immediately. An unexplained performance regression that nobody can reconstruct: the telemetry loop is slower than the application's phase structure. A fairness dispute: the account loop's memory is longer than the users' patience. A machine that is busy and idle at once: the dispatch loop cannot see holes it could fill. A procurement that cannot be installed: the plant loop closed a decade ago and nobody has looked at it since.

None of this requires new technology, and very little of it requires more staff. It requires knowing, for each loop, what its time constant actually is — not what the documentation says, but what it measures out at — and comparing it honestly against the thing it exists to control. That comparison is cheap. It is also, on the evidence of this article's eight experiments, where nearly all of the available operational improvement is hiding.

EXPERIMENT 3 | ACCOUNTS -- FAIR SHARE IS AN INTEGRAL CONTROLLER
============================================================================
Slurm-style multifactor priority: F_a = 2^(-u_a/s_a), with u_a the
normalised decayed usage of account a and s_a its normalised share.
Each hour of the machine goes to the eligible account with the highest F.
Usage decays with a half-life. Nothing else remembers anything.

A. ALL FOUR ACCOUNTS OVERSUBSCRIBE EVERY DAY, FOR 180 DAYS
    half-life     astro   climate       bio      chem  max error   settled
          1d     49.0%     25.0%     15.6%     10.4%      1.02%        6d
          7d     49.8%     25.0%     15.1%     10.1%      0.19%        6d
         28d     50.0%     25.0%     15.0%     10.0%      0.05%        6d

B. ONE ACCOUNT GOES QUIET FOR 30 DAYS, THEN COMES BACK
    half-life    peak day share    machine-days banked   days to settle
          1d             20.8%                   0.86                3
          7d            100.0%                   1.55                3
         28d            100.0%                   2.65                4

The controller has no term for 'today'. It integrates history, so the
half-life -- not the share -- decides how much credit an absence banks.
At a one-day half-life the returning account peaks at 20.8% of the machine
and banks 0.86 machine-days; at twenty-eight days it takes 100% and banks
2.65. The declared share is 10% in every row. Fair share is a tuning
constant wearing the costume of a policy, and the constant is where the
arguments actually happen.

EXPERIMENT 4 | RELIABILITY -- SYSTEM MTBF AND THE CHECKPOINT INTERVAL
============================================================================
Per-node MTBF fixed at 43,830 h (5 years). Failures are
independent, so the system's failure rate is the sum of the nodes'.
tau is Daly's higher-order optimum interval; efficiency is the fraction
of wall time the application spends doing useful work.

     nodes     system MTBF    d=300s: tau     eff    d=30s: tau     eff
     1,000          43.8 h        158.9 m  93.78%        50.9 m  98.04%
    10,000           4.4 h         48.0 m  80.21%        15.9 m  93.78%
   100,000        26.3 min         13.1 m  41.59%         4.8 m  80.21%
 1,000,000         2.6 min          2.3 m   0.87%         1.3 m  41.59%

At 100,000 nodes something breaks every 26.3 minutes on average; at
1,000,000 nodes, every 2.6 minutes. With a 300 s checkpoint the million-node
machine returns 0.87% of itself to science; with a 30 s checkpoint, 41.6%.
Same hardware, same science, a factor of 48 in delivered capability, decided
entirely by how fast operations can get state onto stable storage.

EXPERIMENT 6 | LOGS -- STRUCTURE RECOVERY AND THE RARE-EVENT PROBLEM
============================================================================
200,000 lines drawn from 22 routine templates, with 3 fault templates
injected 58 times in all (0.029% of the stream). Ground truth: 25 templates, 10.03 MB of text.

Similarity threshold swept. 'faults isolated' counts how many of the three
fault templates end up in a cluster of their own rather than absorbed into
a routine one. 'reading list' is the lines under templates seen fewer than
200 times -- 0.1% of the stream -- which is all an operator ever sees.

 threshold  templates   accuracy   faults isolated   reading list   encoded MB
      0.40         23     95.48%            2 of 3           53         2.79
      0.50         23     95.48%            2 of 3           53         2.79
      0.60         25    100.00%            3 of 3           58         2.43
      0.70         25    100.00%            3 of 3           58         2.43
      0.80      1,154    100.00%            3 of 3        1,183         2.43

At 0.50 the parser is too generous: it recovers 23 templates and 2 of 3
faults, because the critical-temperature line is close enough to the routine
power-sensor line to be swallowed by it. The fault does not appear as a rare
template; it appears as nothing at all.
At 0.60 the same stream yields 25 templates, 100.00% accuracy, all 3 faults isolated,
a reading list of 58 lines out of 200,000 (3,448x), and 4.1x compression.
At 0.80 it shatters: 1,154 templates, a reading list of 1,183 lines --
every line is novel, so nothing is.

Nothing here understands a supercomputer. The parser knows only that a log
line is a template plus parameters, and that novelty is cheap to count. What
it can see at all is fixed by one constant chosen long before the fault.

EXPERIMENT 7 | ENERGY -- FREQUENCY, THE ENERGY-DELAY PRODUCT, AND THE BILL
============================================================================
Model: t(f) = a/f + b, with b the frequency-invariant (memory-bound)
share of the runtime, and P(f) = P_static + k f^3. Normalised so that
t = 1.00 and P = 300 W at the nominal 3.00 GHz.

 mem-bound     min-energy f    time   energy      min-EDP f    time   energy
       20%            1.70G   1.61x    0.69x          2.45G   1.18x    0.80x
       50%            1.50G   1.50x    0.58x          2.00G   1.25x    0.63x
       80%            1.20G   1.30x    0.45x          1.50G   1.20x    0.47x

The memory-bound job takes its minimum energy at 1.20 GHz: 30% slower for
55% less energy. The compute-bound job takes its minimum at 1.70 GHz:
61% slower for 31% less. Same knob, same silicon, two different answers,
which is why a site-wide frequency cap is a blunt instrument and per-job
power steering is not.

One 512-node job, 6 h at 500 W per node = 1,536 kWh of IT load:
        PUE    facility kWh    kg CO2e at 350 g/kWh
       1.05           1,613                     564
       1.20           1,843                     645
       1.40           2,150                     753
       1.60           2,458                     860

The IT load is the same 1,536 kWh in every row. The 845 kWh between the best
and the worst is bought with plant design and setpoints, and cannot be
recovered by a single line of application code.

EXPERIMENT 8 | HEAT -- WHY THE COOLANT CHANGED, AND WHAT WARM WATER BUYS
============================================================================
Removing 100 kW from a single rack:
   air,   dT 12 K     8.29 kg/s     6.91 m3/s     14,641 CFM
   water, dT 10 K     2.39 kg/s     2.40 L/s          38 GPM
   volumetric heat capacity, water : air = 3,456 : 1

Fan power rises with roughly the cube of air flow, so the last kilowatts of
an air-cooled cabinet are the most expensive in the building. Moving
2.4 litres of water a second does not work that way.

A synthetic temperate site: mean 11.0 degC, peak 29.0 degC, dry-cooler
approach 6 K. Hours a year the mechanical chiller can stay off:

   ASHRAE class   supply limit   chiller-free h   % of year
            W17           17 C            4,394       50.2%
            W27           27 C            7,852       89.6%
            W32           32 C            8,693       99.2%
            W40           40 C            8,760      100.0%
            W45           45 C            8,760      100.0%

Raising the supply-water class from W17 to W32 takes the chiller from 50.2% to
99.2% of the year idle at this site. Nothing about the computation changed.
The decision was taken years earlier, in a procurement document, by someone
choosing a water temperature class for a machine that did not yet exist.
