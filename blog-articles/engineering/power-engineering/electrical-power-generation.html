Electrical Power Generation

Conventional and renewable plants, electrical generators, and the economics of planning and operating a generation fleet — with worked examples, source code and exercises

Abstract. Nearly all of the world’s electricity is produced by converting a primary energy flow — chemical, nuclear, gravitational, solar, kinetic or geothermal — into rotating mechanical work or directly into direct current, and then into synchronised alternating current. This article surveys the main generation technologies, explains how the electrical machines at the heart of most plants work and are controlled, and introduces the optimisation problems that system operators solve every day to decide which generators run and how much each produces. Each section pairs the governing equations with a worked numerical example; Python implementations of economic dispatch, unit commitment, wind energy yield, maximum-power-point tracking, voltage regulation and load forecasting are included and were executed to produce the numbers quoted. Exercises with full solutions close the article. All figures are original diagrams drawn for this article.

1. Fundamentals: efficiency, heat rate and capacity factor

A generating plant is a chain of energy converters. In a thermal plant, fuel energy becomes heat in a boiler, reactor or combustor; heat becomes shaft work in a turbine or engine; and shaft work becomes electrical energy in a generator 1,2. The overall efficiency is the product of the stage efficiencies, and for any heat engine it is bounded above by the Carnot efficiency between the hot-source temperature TH and the cold-sink temperature TC (both absolute) 3:

ηCarnot = 1 − TC / TH(1)

Thermal-plant performance is commonly quoted as a heat rate: the fuel energy needed per kilowatt-hour of net electrical output. Because 1 kWh = 3 600 kJ = 3 412 Btu, efficiency and heat rate are reciprocals 2,4:

η = 3 600 / HR[kJ/kWh] = 3 412 / HR[Btu/kWh](2)

It matters whether the fuel’s higher (gross) or lower (net) heating value is used: the HHV includes the latent heat of the water vapour formed in combustion, so an efficiency on an LHV basis is several percentage points higher than the same plant’s HHV efficiency — roughly 10 % higher in relative terms for natural gas 5. Comparisons must always state the basis.

The capacity factor of a plant is its actual energy output over a period divided by the energy it would have produced at continuous nameplate output 6:

CF = Eactual / (Prated × T)(3)

Capacity factor reflects both the resource (wind, sun, water) and how the plant is used by the system: baseload nuclear units typically run at high capacity factors, while peaking gas turbines are designed to run only a few hundred hours a year 4,7.

Finally, every synchronous machine on an AC network turns at a speed locked to the system frequency f (50 Hz or 60 Hz). For a machine with p poles the synchronous speed in revolutions per minute is 8

ns = 120 f / p(4)

so a two-pole turbogenerator turns at 3 000 rpm on a 50 Hz system or 3 600 rpm on a 60 Hz system, whereas slow hydro turbines need many poles.

2. Conventional generation

2.1 Coal-fired power plants

A pulverised-coal plant burns finely ground coal in a water-tube boiler to raise high-pressure steam, which expands through a multi-stage steam turbine coupled to a generator, is condensed, and is pumped back to the boiler. This is the Rankine cycle, usually improved with reheat (steam returns to the boiler between the high- and intermediate-pressure turbines) and regenerative feedwater heating using steam bled from the turbine 2,3. Coal remained the single largest source of the world’s electricity in 2023, at about 35 % of global generation 9.

Plant efficiency rises with steam temperature and pressure. Subcritical units (steam below the critical pressure of water, 22.1 MPa) typically achieve about 33–37 % (HHV), supercritical units around 38–42 %, and ultra-supercritical units with steam near 600 °C or above reach roughly 42–47 % 10. Each percentage point of efficiency directly reduces fuel use and CO2 per kilowatt-hour. Modern plants clean the flue gas with electrostatic precipitators or fabric filters (particulates), flue-gas desulphurisation (SO2) and selective catalytic reduction (NOx) 2,10. Large coal units start slowly and are most economical at steady output, so historically they served base load 4.

Worked example 1 — coal burn and CO2 intensity.

A 500 MW (net) unit operates at 38 % efficiency on coal with a heating value of 25 MJ/kg and 65 % carbon by mass. Fuel energy input is 500/0.38 = 1 316 MW, so the coal feed is 1 316 MJ/s ÷ 25 MJ/kg = 52.6 kg/s (≈189 t/h). Complete combustion turns each kilogram of carbon into 44/12 kg of CO2, giving 52.6 × 0.65 × 3.667 = 125 kg/s of CO2. Dividing by 500 MJ/s of electricity gives 0.251 kg/MJ, or 0.90 kg CO2/kWh.

2.2 Natural gas power plants

Natural gas is burnt in three main kinds of plant: (i) conventional gas-fired steam boilers, which work like the Rankine plant above; (ii) gas turbines, which operate on the Brayton cycle; and (iii) reciprocating gas engines 2,11. In a simple-cycle gas turbine, a compressor raises the pressure of ambient air, fuel burns at nearly constant pressure in the combustor, and the hot gas expands through a turbine that drives both the compressor and the generator. For the ideal air-standard Brayton cycle with pressure ratio rp and ratio of specific heats γ, the thermal efficiency is 3,11

ηBrayton = 1 − rp−(γ−1)/γ(5)

With rp = 18 and γ = 1.4 the ideal efficiency is 56 %. Real machines, with component losses and finite turbine inlet temperature, achieve much less; heavy-duty simple-cycle units are typically in the mid-to-high 30s per cent (LHV), aeroderivatives somewhat higher 5,11. Their great virtues are fast start-up, quick ramping and low capital cost, which make them the classic peaking plant 4.

2.3 Nuclear power plants

A nuclear plant is a steam plant whose heat comes from fission. Each fission of a 235U nucleus releases about 200 MeV, most of it as kinetic energy of the fission fragments that is converted to heat in the fuel 12. A moderator (normally water) slows the fission neutrons so that a self-sustaining chain reaction is maintained in low-enriched uranium oxide fuel; control rods and dissolved neutron absorbers regulate the reactivity 12.

Light-water reactors dominate the world fleet, and pressurised water reactors (PWRs) are the most common type, followed by boiling water reactors (BWRs); pressurised heavy-water reactors, gas-cooled reactors and fast reactors make up the rest 13. In a PWR the primary coolant is kept at about 15.5 MPa so that it does not boil; it transfers heat to a secondary circuit in steam generators. In a BWR the coolant boils directly in the core at about 7 MPa and the steam goes straight to the turbine 12,13. Because reactor outlet temperatures are modest (about 300–330 °C), LWR steam is saturated or only slightly superheated and net efficiencies are typically about 33–34 % 12.

Worked example 2 — how much uranium does a reactor fission?

A reactor produces 3 400 MW of heat. With 200 MeV = 200 × 1.602 × 10−13 J = 3.20 × 10−11 J per fission, the fission rate is 3.4 × 109 / 3.20 × 10−11 = 1.06 × 1020 s−1. Over a day that is 9.17 × 1024 fissions, or 9.17 × 1024 / 6.022 × 1023 × 235 g = 3.6 kg of 235U fissioned per day — about 1.05 g per megawatt-day of heat, consistent with the textbook rule of thumb 12. At 33 % efficiency the plant delivers about 1 120 MW of electricity.

2.4 Hydroelectric power plants

Hydropower converts the potential energy of water falling through a head H into shaft power. With volumetric flow Q, water density ρ, gravitational acceleration g and overall efficiency η (turbine × generator), the electrical output is 6,14

P = η ρ g Q H(6)

Hydro is the world’s largest renewable source of electricity 15. Turbine choice depends chiefly on head: impulse Pelton wheels for high heads, Francis reaction turbines for medium heads, and propeller or Kaplan turbines (with adjustable blades) for low heads and large flows 14. Large modern turbines exceed 90 % efficiency 14. Storage hydro can be ramped very quickly, making it valuable for load following and frequency control, and pumped-storage plants pump water uphill at times of low prices and generate at peak, acting as large-scale energy storage 14,16.

Worked example 3 — hydro output.

A plant has 50 m net head, 100 m³/s flow and overall efficiency 90 %. By equation (6), P = 0.90 × 1 000 × 9.81 × 100 × 50 = 44.1 × 106 W = 44.1 MW.

2.5 Diesel and gas turbine generators

Packaged generating sets (“gensets”) pair a reciprocating engine or a small gas turbine with a synchronous generator on a common skid. They supply isolated grids, islands, mines and data centres, provide emergency standby power for hospitals and critical facilities, and offer black-start capability to restart a collapsed grid 16,17. Large medium-speed diesel and gas engines reach brake efficiencies in the 40–50 % range, higher than small simple-cycle gas turbines 18. ISO 8528-1 defines the ratings under which gensets are sold — continuous (COP), prime (PRP), limited-time running (LTP) and emergency standby (ESP) power — and specifies performance classes for transient voltage and frequency response to load steps 17. Because they are synchronous machines with their own speed governors and voltage regulators, the material in section 4 applies directly.

Worked example 4 — genset fuel consumption.

A 1 MW diesel genset at 40 % efficiency burns fuel with a lower heating value of 42.8 MJ/kg. Fuel power is 1/0.40 = 2.5 MW, so fuel flow is 2.5 / 42.8 = 0.0584 kg/s = 210 kg/h, or about 250 L/h at a density of 0.84 kg/L.

2.6 Combined-cycle power plants

The exhaust of a gas turbine leaves at roughly 450–650 °C and carries most of the fuel energy. A combined-cycle gas turbine (CCGT) plant passes this exhaust through a heat-recovery steam generator (HRSG) that raises steam for a bottoming Rankine cycle 5. If the gas turbine has efficiency ηGT and the steam cycle converts a fraction ηST of the exhaust heat into work, the combined efficiency is 3,5

ηCC = ηGT + ηST (1 − ηGT)(7)

State-of-the-art CCGTs exceed 60 % net efficiency (LHV), the highest of any large thermal plant 5,19. They emit roughly 0.35–0.4 kg CO2/kWh, less than half the intensity of coal, and can start in tens of minutes to a few hours depending on configuration 5.

Worked example 5 — combined-cycle efficiency.

A gas turbine with ηGT = 38 % feeds an HRSG/steam cycle that converts 33 % of the exhaust heat to work. By equation (7), ηCC = 0.38 + 0.33 × 0.62 = 58.5 %. The steam cycle adds about 54 % to the gas turbine’s output without burning any more fuel.

3. Renewable energy generation

3.1 Solar photovoltaic (PV)

A solar cell is a semiconductor p–n junction. Photons with energy above the band gap create electron–hole pairs that the junction’s built-in field separates, driving a direct current through an external load 6. The ideal cell behaves as a current source in parallel with a diode, giving the single-diode model

I = Iph − I0 [exp(V / (n Ns VT)) − 1](8)

where Iph is the photocurrent (proportional to irradiance), I0 the diode saturation current, n the ideality factor, Ns the number of series cells and VT = kT/q the thermal voltage (≈ 25.7 mV at 25 °C) 6. Shockley and Queisser showed that detailed balance limits a single-junction cell under unconcentrated sunlight to about 30 % for silicon-like band gaps 20; later tabulations using the standard AM1.5G spectrum put the maximum at about 33.7 % for a band gap near 1.34 eV 21.

Module ratings are quoted at Standard Test Conditions (STC): 1 000 W/m² irradiance, 25 °C cell temperature and the AM1.5 reference spectrum 22. Output falls as cells heat up — for crystalline silicon, by roughly 0.3–0.5 % per °C of cell temperature — and a common estimate of cell temperature uses the nominal operating cell temperature (NOCT) 6:

Tcell = Tamb + (NOCT − 20 °C) × G / 800 W m−2(9)

Because the maximum power point (MPP) moves with irradiance and temperature, PV inverters continuously adjust the operating voltage with a maximum-power-point tracking (MPPT) algorithm. The simplest and most widely used is perturb-and-observe (hill climbing): nudge the voltage, and if power falls, reverse direction 23. The listing below implements it on the model of equation (8).

import math

# Single-diode PV module model (series/shunt resistance neglected)
ISC, VOC, NS, N = 10.5, 49.0, 72, 1.2
VT = 1.381e-23 * 298.15 / 1.602e-19
I0 = ISC / (math.exp(VOC / (N * NS * VT)) - 1)

def module_power(v, g=1000.0):
    i = ISC * g / 1000 - I0 * (math.exp(v / (N * NS * VT)) - 1)
    return max(v * i, 0.0)

def perturb_and_observe(v=30.0, step=0.5, iters=60, g=1000.0):
    p_prev, direction = module_power(v, g), +1
    for _ in range(iters):
        v += direction * step
        p = module_power(v, g)
        if p < p_prev:            # power fell: reverse the perturbation
            direction = -direction
        p_prev = p
    return v, p_prev

v_mp, p_mp = perturb_and_observe()
best = max((module_power(x / 100), x / 100) for x in range(0, 5000))
print(f"P&O settles near V = {v_mp:.1f} V, P = {p_mp:.1f} W; "
      f"true MPP = {best[0]:.1f} W at {best[1]:.2f} V")

Output: P&O settles near V = 42.0 V, P = 422.1 W; true MPP = 422.4 W at 42.34 V. The tracker oscillates in a small band around the MPP, a known trade-off between step size, tracking speed and steady-state ripple 23.

Annual energy from a PV system is commonly estimated with the performance ratio PR, which lumps temperature, soiling, mismatch, wiring and inverter losses 24:

E = PSTC × (HPOA / GSTC) × PR(10)

where HPOA is the annual plane-of-array irradiation (kWh/m²) and GSTC = 1 kW/m².

Worked example 6 — utility PV yield and hot-day derating.

(a) A 100 MWp plant receives 1 800 kWh/m² per year in the array plane with PR = 0.80. Equation (10) gives E = 100 MW × 1 800 h × 0.80 = 144 GWh/yr, a capacity factor (DC basis) of 144 000 / (100 × 8 760) = 16.4 %.

(b) On a 30 °C day at 800 W/m², a module with NOCT = 45 °C runs at 30 + 25 × 800/800 = 55 °C. With a power coefficient of −0.35 %/°C its output is 30 × 0.35 = 10.5 % below what it would produce at 25 °C under the same irradiance.

3.2 Concentrated solar power (CSP)

CSP plants use mirrors to concentrate direct beam sunlight onto a receiver, heat a fluid to several hundred degrees Celsius, and run a conventional steam (or other heat-engine) cycle. Four configurations exist: parabolic troughs and linear Fresnel reflectors (line-focus), and central towers with heliostat fields and parabolic dishes (point-focus) 25,26. Because only the direct component of sunlight can be concentrated, CSP needs high direct normal irradiance (DNI) and clear skies 26.

The decisive advantage of CSP over PV is cheap thermal energy storage: molten nitrate salt, heated to about 565 °C in tower plants, can be stored in insulated tanks and used to raise steam hours after sunset, making the output dispatchable 26,27. The maximum efficiency of the heat engine follows the Carnot limit (1): with a 565 °C hot source and a 25 °C sink, ηCarnot = 1 − 298/838 = 64 %, whereas real annual solar-to-electric efficiencies, after optical, receiver, cycle and parasitic losses, are far lower 26.

3.3 Wind energy

The kinetic power in wind of speed v flowing through a rotor of swept area A is ½ρAv3. A turbine extracts a fraction Cp, the power coefficient 28:

P = ½ ρ A v3 Cp(λ, β)(11)

where Cp depends on the tip-speed ratio λ = ωR/v and the blade pitch β. Actuator-disc momentum theory shows that no turbine can extract more than 16/27 ≈ 59.3 % of the power in the wind — the Betz limit, reached when the wind is slowed to one third of its upstream speed behind the rotor 28,29. Modern three-blade turbines achieve peak Cp in the region of 0.45–0.5 30.

The cubic dependence on wind speed is the dominant fact of wind engineering: a 10 % increase in wind speed raises the available power by 33 %. A turbine therefore follows a power curve: no output below the cut-in speed; roughly cubic growth up to rated speed; constant rated output (held by pitching the blades) up to cut-out, when the turbine shuts down to protect itself 28,30.

Worked example 7 — turbine output.

For the turbine of Figure 6 at 10 m/s: A = π × 60² = 11 310 m², and P = 0.5 × 1.225 × 11 310 × 10³ × 0.45 = 3.12 MW.

Wind speeds at a site are usually described by a Weibull distribution with shape k (often near 2, the Rayleigh case) and scale c 28. Annual energy production (AEP) is the integral of the power curve weighted by the speed distribution, implemented here numerically:

import math

def power_curve(v, D=120.0, cp=0.45, p_rated=3.6e6, v_in=3.0, v_out=25.0, rho=1.225):
    """Idealised power curve (W): cubic region capped at rated power."""
    if v < v_in or v >= v_out:
        return 0.0
    area = math.pi * D**2 / 4
    return min(p_rated, 0.5 * rho * area * v**3 * cp)

def weibull_pdf(v, k, c):
    return (k / c) * (v / c) ** (k - 1) * math.exp(-((v / c) ** k))

def annual_energy(k=2.0, v_mean=7.5, dv=0.05):
    c = v_mean / math.gamma(1 + 1 / k)            # Weibull scale from mean speed
    e = sum(power_curve(v) * weibull_pdf(v, k, c) * dv
            for v in (i * dv for i in range(1, int(30 / dv))))
    return e * 8760 / 1e6                            # MWh per year

aep = annual_energy()
print(f"AEP = {aep:,.0f} MWh/yr, capacity factor = {aep / (3.6 * 8760):.1%}")

Output: AEP = 13,595 MWh/yr, capacity factor = 43.1%. This is a gross, idealised figure; wake, availability, electrical and air-density losses typically reduce net AEP noticeably 30.

Wind generator topologies. IEC 61400-27-1 classifies turbines electrically into four generic types: Type 1, a fixed-speed squirrel-cage induction generator connected directly to the grid; Type 2, a wound-rotor induction generator with variable rotor resistance; Type 3, the doubly-fed induction generator (DFIG) with a partial-rated rotor converter; and Type 4, a generator of any kind connected through a full-rated converter 31,32. Types 3 and 4 dominate new installations because variable speed lets the rotor run at the tip-speed ratio that maximises Cp, and the converter provides controllable active and reactive power 32. The machines themselves are discussed in section 4.2 and section 4.3.

3.4 Geothermal energy

Geothermal plants tap hot water and steam from wells drilled into permeable, heated rock. Three plant types are used 33:

  • Dry-steam plants pass steam from the reservoir directly through a turbine (for example The Geysers, California, and Larderello, Italy).
  • Flash-steam plants (single or double flash) bring pressurised hot water to the surface, drop its pressure in a separator so part of it flashes to steam, and send the steam to the turbine; these are the most common type.
  • Binary-cycle plants pass moderate-temperature brine through a heat exchanger to vaporise a low-boiling organic working fluid in an organic Rankine cycle (ORC), so the geofluid never touches the turbine.

Because resource temperatures are low compared with fossil boilers, Carnot limits are low and real conversion efficiencies are typically about 10–20 % 33. Geothermal output, however, is steady and largely independent of weather, so plants often run at high capacity factors 27,33.

Worked example 8 — binary plant.

Brine flows at 100 kg/s and is cooled from 150 °C to 70 °C (cp ≈ 4.2 kJ/kg·K). Heat input is 100 × 4.2 × 80 = 33.6 MWth. At a cycle efficiency of 12 % the plant produces about 4.0 MW. The Carnot limit between 150 °C and 27 °C, 1 − 300/423 = 29 %, shows how far a low-temperature source restricts conversion.

3.5 Biomass and biogas

Biomass — wood, agricultural residues, energy crops and wastes — can be converted to electricity by thermochemical or biochemical routes 34,35:

  • Direct combustion in stoker or fluidised-bed boilers driving a steam turbine, often in combined heat and power (CHP) schemes, or co-firing biomass with coal in existing boilers.
  • Gasification, partial oxidation at high temperature to a combustible producer gas (CO, H2, CH4) that can fire engines or gas turbines; and pyrolysis, thermal decomposition without oxygen to bio-oil, gas and char 34.
  • Anaerobic digestion of wet feedstocks (manure, sewage sludge, food waste) by microorganisms, producing biogas that is typically 50–75 % methane, the balance mostly CO2 36. Biogas fuels gas engines, often as CHP, or is upgraded to biomethane.

Biomass is dispatchable, but its sustainability depends on feedstock sourcing and land use; its carbon balance is only favourable when harvested biomass is regrown 35.

Worked example 9 — biogas engine.

A digester yields 500 m³/h of biogas with 60 % methane. Taking methane’s lower heating value as ≈ 35.8 MJ/m³, the biogas carries 0.60 × 35.8 = 21.5 MJ/m³, a fuel power of 500 × 21.5 / 3 600 = 2.98 MW. A gas engine at 38 % electrical efficiency delivers 1.13 MWe, with much of the remainder recoverable as heat in CHP.

3.6 Tidal and wave energy

Tidal range schemes impound water behind a barrage and generate as it flows through low-head bulb turbines on the ebb, the flood or both. The theoretical energy available from emptying a basin of area A through a tidal range R is 35

E = ½ ρ g A R2(12)

The 240 MW La Rance barrage in France (1966) and the 254 MW Sihwa Lake plant in South Korea (2011) are the largest examples 37. Tidal stream turbines, effectively underwater wind turbines, obey equation (11); seawater is about 840 times denser than air, so useful power comes from currents of only 2–3 m/s 35,37. Tides are driven by astronomical forces and are predictable years ahead 35.

Wave energy converters — point absorbers, attenuators, oscillating water columns and overtopping devices — extract energy from surface waves. In deep water the power per metre of wave crest is 38

Pw = ρ g2 Hs2 Te / (64π) ≈ 0.49 Hs2 Te kW/m(13)

with significant wave height Hs (m) and energy period Te (s). Wave energy remains largely pre-commercial because devices must survive extreme storms while efficiently capturing ordinary seas 35.

Worked example 10 — barrage and waves.

(a) A basin of 22 km² with an 8 m range stores ½ × 1 025 × 9.81 × 22 × 106 × 8² = 7.1 × 1012 J ≈ 2.0 GWh per tide (theoretical). (b) A sea with Hs = 2 m and Te = 8 s carries 0.49 × 4 × 8 ≈ 15.7 kW per metre of crest.

4. Electrical generators

4.1 Synchronous generators

Almost all large power stations use synchronous generators (alternators). A rotor carrying a DC field winding (or permanent magnets) produces a magnetic field that rotates with the shaft; the field sweeps past a three-phase armature winding in the stator, inducing balanced sinusoidal voltages 8,39. High-speed steam and gas turbines drive round-rotor (cylindrical) machines with two or four poles; slow hydro turbines drive salient-pole machines with many poles, according to equation (4) 8,40. The RMS induced EMF per phase is

EA = 4.44 f N Φ kw(14)

where N is the number of series turns per phase, Φ the flux per pole and kw the winding factor; for a given speed, EA is controlled by the field current 39. In steady state a round-rotor machine is represented per phase by EA behind the synchronous reactance Xs (armature resistance is small and often neglected) 8:

EA = Vt + RaIa + jXsIa(15)

The angle δ between EA and Vt is the load (power) angle. With Ra neglected, the three-phase real power delivered to an infinite bus is 1,8

P = 3 EAVt sin δ / Xs  (or E V sin δ / Xs in per unit)(16)

Real power is set by the prime mover (turbine governor) and appears as the load angle; reactive power is set by the excitation: an over-excited machine (large EA) exports reactive power, an under-excited one absorbs it 1,16. The steady-state stability limit is δ = 90°; in practice machines operate at much smaller angles so that they can ride through disturbances 16. A generator’s permissible operating region in the P–Q plane is its capability curve, bounded by armature heating, field heating, the prime mover limit and the under-excitation (stability and stator-end heating) limit 16,40.

Worked example 11 — excitation and load angle.

A 100 MVA, 13.8 kV round-rotor generator with Xs = 1.2 pu delivers rated current at 0.85 power factor lagging and rated voltage. In per unit, Ia = 1.0∠−31.79° = 0.850 − j0.527. Then EA = 1 + j1.2(0.850 − j0.527) = 1.632 + j1.020 = 1.925∠32.0° pu (26.6 kV line-to-line). The real power is 1.925 × 1 × sin 32.0° / 1.2 = 0.85 pu = 85 MW, as expected, and the maximum (pull-out) power at this excitation is 1.925/1.2 = 1.60 pu.

4.2 Induction generators

An induction (asynchronous) machine driven faster than synchronous speed operates as a generator. The slip

s = (ns − n) / ns(17)

becomes negative and the electromagnetic torque reverses, converting shaft power into electrical power 8,39. Induction generators are rugged and need no synchronising or excitation equipment, but they cannot produce their own magnetising current: they draw reactive power from the network, or, when isolated, from shunt capacitors that allow self-excitation 8. Their frequency is fixed by the system rather than by the shaft speed. These properties made the squirrel-cage machine the generator of early fixed-speed (Type 1) wind turbines and of many small hydro plants 28,32.

The doubly-fed induction generator (DFIG) has a wound rotor fed through slip rings by a back-to-back converter rated at roughly 30 % of turbine power. By injecting rotor currents at slip frequency it allows a speed range of roughly ±30 % around synchronous speed and independent control of active and reactive power 32.

Worked example 12 — slip of an induction generator.

A 4-pole, 60 Hz machine (ns = 1 800 rpm) is driven at 1 854 rpm. Its slip is (1 800 − 1 854)/1 800 = −0.03, i.e. −3 %. The rotor currents alternate at |s|f = 1.8 Hz.

4.3 Permanent magnet generators

A permanent magnet synchronous generator (PMSG) replaces the rotor field winding with high-energy magnets, usually neodymium–iron–boron. With no field copper losses, slip rings or brushes, PMSGs offer high efficiency and power density 32,41. Their drawbacks are that the excitation cannot be adjusted, so voltage and reactive power must be controlled by a power converter; magnet cost and supply risk; and the danger of irreversible demagnetisation at high temperature or under severe fault currents 41.

Large-diameter, many-pole PMSGs can be driven directly by a wind turbine rotor at 10–20 rpm, eliminating the gearbox. Because the generator frequency is then far from grid frequency, they are always connected through a full-rated converter (Type 4) 32,41. Small PM machines are also common in micro-turbines and small hydro.

Worked example 13 — direct-drive frequency.

A direct-drive PMSG with 80 poles turns at 15 rpm. Rearranging equation (4), f = pn/120 = 80 × 15/120 = 10 Hz. The converter rectifies this variable-frequency output and inverts it to 50 or 60 Hz.

4.4 Excitation systems

The excitation system supplies the DC field current of a synchronous machine and, through its regulator, controls terminal voltage and reactive power. It also contributes to system stability through fast field forcing during faults and through power system stabilisers 16,42. IEEE Std 421.5 groups excitation systems into three families 42:

  • DC types: a DC generator (commutator exciter) on the main shaft supplies the field through slip rings. These are older and largely superseded.
  • AC types: an AC alternator exciter whose output is rectified. In the common brushless arrangement, the exciter armature and a diode bridge rotate with the shaft, so no slip rings are needed.
  • ST (static) types: a thyristor bridge, fed by a transformer from the generator terminals or station bus, supplies the field through slip rings. Static exciters respond very quickly and can have high ceiling voltages.

Key performance figures are the ceiling voltage (the maximum field voltage available for forcing) and the response speed. The standard also provides the block-diagram models used in stability studies, together with models for power system stabilisers, over- and under-excitation limiters 16,42.

4.5 Automatic voltage regulators (AVR)

The AVR compares the measured terminal voltage with a set point and drives the exciter so as to remove the error. A classical linearised model treats each element as a first-order lag 16,43:

With loop gain K = KAKEKGKR, the final-value theorem gives the steady-state terminal voltage for a unit step in reference

Vt,ss = KAKEKG / (1 + K)(18)

so a higher amplifier gain reduces steady-state error but, because the loop contains four lags, also reduces damping and eventually causes instability 43. The simulation below integrates the loop for typical textbook time constants.

# Simplified AVR loop: amplifier -> exciter -> generator field, with voltage sensor feedback
KA, TA = 10.0, 0.1     # amplifier gain and time constant (s)
KE, TE = 1.0, 0.4      # exciter
KG, TG = 1.0, 1.0      # generator (open-circuit field time constant)
KR, TR = 1.0, 0.05     # voltage transducer

def simulate(v_ref=1.0, t_end=30.0, dt=1e-4):
    va = ve = vt = vs = 0.0          # states: amplifier, exciter, terminal, sensor outputs
    t, out = 0.0, []
    while t <= t_end:
        err = v_ref - vs
        va += dt * (KA * err - va) / TA
        ve += dt * (KE * va - ve) / TE
        vt += dt * (KG * ve - vt) / TG
        vs += dt * (KR * vt - vs) / TR
        out.append((t, vt))
        t += dt
    return out

res = simulate()
peak = max(v for _, v in res)
print(f"peak = {peak:.3f} pu, "
      f"settled value approaches {res[-1][1]:.3f}")
print(f"theory steady state = {KA*KE*KG/(1+KA*KE*KG*KR):.3f} pu")

With KA = 10 the output settles to 0.909 pu, matching equation (18), but with a peak of 1.66 pu and a 2 % settling time of about 19 s; rerunning with KA = 5 gives a peak of 1.22 pu and a settling time of about 5 s, at the cost of a larger steady-state error (0.833 pu). Routh–Hurwitz analysis of this loop shows instability for KA above about 12.2. Practical AVRs resolve the conflict with integral action (PI or PID control) and with rate (derivative) feedback or transient gain reduction, which together give zero steady-state error and good damping 16,43. Fast, high-gain AVRs can weaken the damping of electromechanical rotor oscillations, which is why a power system stabiliser (PSS) adds a supplementary signal, derived from speed, frequency or power, to the AVR summing junction 16,42.

4.6 Generator synchronisation

Before a generator’s breaker is closed onto a live network, the incoming machine must match the network in four respects 1,8:

  1. Phase sequence (checked once at commissioning);
  2. Voltage magnitude, set with the excitation;
  3. Frequency, set with the governor: the machine is normally run very slightly fast so that it picks up a little load when the breaker closes;
  4. Phase angle: the breaker is closed when the voltage phasors coincide.

Closing out of phase drives large circulating currents and torque transients through the machine and shaft, which can damage windings and couplings 16. Traditionally an operator watched a synchroscope or lamps; today automatic synchronisers and synchronism-check (25) relays issue or supervise the close command, compensating for breaker closing time 1. 4.7 Generator efficiency and losses

Generator losses fall into the following categories, determined by the test methods of IEC 60034-2-1 39,45:

  • Armature (stator) copper losses, 3Ia2Ra, varying with the square of load current.
  • Field (excitation) losses, If2Rf, plus exciter and brush losses; these rise with excitation and hence with reactive output.
  • Core (iron) losses: hysteresis and eddy-current losses in the stator laminations, approximately constant at fixed voltage and frequency.
  • Mechanical losses: friction in bearings and windage, including the power to circulate cooling air or hydrogen; constant at fixed speed.
  • Stray load losses: additional losses from leakage fluxes and harmonic currents, increasing with load.
η = Pout / (Pout + Σ losses)(19)

Large generators are very efficient, commonly above 98 % at rated load, and hydrogen cooling of large turbogenerators reduces windage losses because hydrogen is much less dense than air 39,40. Because some losses are fixed and others vary with the square of current, efficiency peaks at the load where the variable losses equal the fixed losses 8.

Worked example 14 — efficiency from a loss budget.

A 100 MVA generator delivers 85 MW at 0.85 power factor with stator copper 0.45 MW, field 0.25 MW, core 0.30 MW, friction and windage 0.35 MW and stray 0.15 MW of losses: 1.50 MW in total. Equation (19) gives η = 85 / 86.5 = 98.27 %.

5. Generation planning and economics

Power-system operation and planning are layered optimisation problems on different time scales 4,46. Decades ahead, capacity planning decides what to build. Days ahead, unit commitment decides which units will be on line in each hour. Minutes ahead, economic dispatch shares the load among the committed units. Seconds ahead, automatic generation control and governors keep frequency at its nominal value. Forecasts of load and renewable output feed every layer.

5.1 Economic dispatch

The fuel cost of a thermal unit is modelled from its heat-rate curve, usually as a quadratic in output 4:

Fi(Pi) = ai + biPi + ciPi2  [$/h](20)

The economic dispatch (ED) problem is to minimise total cost subject to meeting demand PD and respecting unit limits:

min Σi Fi(Pi)  s.t.  Σi Pi = PD,   Pimin ≤ Pi ≤ Pimax(21)

Forming the Lagrangian ℒ = ΣFi + λ(PD − ΣPi) and setting ∂ℒ/∂Pi = 0 yields the equal incremental cost criterion: every unit not at a limit runs where its incremental cost equals the same value λ 4,46:

dFi/dPi = bi + 2ciPi = λ(22)

Units pinned at a limit have incremental cost below λ (at maximum) or above λ (at minimum). λ is the system’s marginal cost of energy, the cost of one more megawatt-hour, and is the basis of locational marginal prices in electricity markets 7,46. When transmission losses PL are included, the condition becomes Li dFi/dPi = λ with penalty factor Li = 1/(1 − ∂PL/∂Pi), with losses often expressed through B-coefficients 4,43.

Worked example 15 — three-unit dispatch 4.

Three units have costs F1 = 561 + 7.92P1 + 0.001562P12 (150–600 MW), F2 = 310 + 7.85P2 + 0.00194P22 (100–400 MW) and F3 = 78 + 7.97P3 + 0.00482P32 (50–200 MW). The load is 850 MW. From (22), Pi = (λ − bi)/2ci; summing and setting equal to 850:

(λ − 7.92)/0.003124 + (λ − 7.85)/0.00388 + (λ − 7.97)/0.00964 = 850

gives λ = 9.148 $/MWh, P1 = 393.2 MW, P2 = 334.6 MW, P3 = 122.2 MW, all within limits, at a total cost of 8 194 $/h.

With unit limits the equations are no longer linear, so ED is solved iteratively. Because total output rises monotonically with λ, bisection on λ (“lambda iteration”) always converges:

"""Economic dispatch by lambda iteration (equal incremental cost), with unit limits.
Cost model per unit: F(P) = a + b*P + c*P^2  [$/h], P in MW."""

UNITS = [  # (a, b, c, Pmin, Pmax)
    (561.0, 7.92, 0.001562, 150.0, 600.0),
    (310.0, 7.85, 0.00194,  100.0, 400.0),
    ( 78.0, 7.97, 0.00482,   50.0, 200.0),
]

def outputs_at(lam, units):
    """Each unit runs where dF/dP = b + 2cP = lambda, clipped to its limits."""
    return [min(max((lam - b) / (2 * c), pmin), pmax) for a, b, c, pmin, pmax in units]

def economic_dispatch(demand, units, tol=1e-6):
    if not sum(u[3] for u in units) <= demand <= sum(u[4] for u in units):
        raise ValueError("demand outside committed capacity")
    lo = min(b + 2 * c * pmin for a, b, c, pmin, pmax in units)
    hi = max(b + 2 * c * pmax for a, b, c, pmin, pmax in units)
    while hi - lo > tol:                      # total output rises monotonically with lambda
        lam = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if sum(outputs_at(lam, units)) < demand:
            lo = lam
        else:
            hi = lam
    P = outputs_at(lam, units)
    cost = sum(a + b * p + c * p * p for (a, b, c, *_), p in zip(units, P))
    return lam, P, cost

if __name__ == "__main__":
    lam, P, cost = economic_dispatch(850.0, UNITS)
    print(f"lambda = {lam:.3f} $/MWh")
    for i, p in enumerate(P, 1):
        print(f"  P{i} = {p:7.2f} MW")
    print(f"total cost = {cost:,.2f} $/h")

5.2 Unit commitment

ED assumes the set of running units is known. Unit commitment (UC) chooses that set for each hour of the coming day or week, trading off running costs against start-up and no-load costs while respecting 4,47:

  • the power balance in every hour, and a spinning reserve margin to cover the loss of a unit or forecast error;
  • unit minimum up and down times and ramp-rate limits;
  • start-up costs, which depend on how long a unit has been cooling;
  • must-run units, fuel limits, crew constraints and network limits.

UC is a large mixed-integer non-linear problem. Historical methods include priority lists, dynamic programming and Lagrangian relaxation 4,47. Today system operators and market clearing engines solve it as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) with piecewise-linearised costs, using efficient formulations such as that of Carrión and Arroyo 46,48. A typical MILP keeps, for each unit i and hour t, a binary on/off variable uit and a start-up variable vit ≥ uit − ui,t−1, with uitPimin ≤ Pit ≤ uitPimax.

For a small system, forward dynamic programming over the 2N on/off states is exact and instructive. The listing below commits the three units of worked example 15 over eight hours with start-up costs and a 10 % reserve requirement, calling an ED for each candidate state:

import itertools

# Unit data: (name, a, b, c, Pmin, Pmax, startup_cost)
UNITS = [
    ("U1", 561.0, 7.92, 0.001562, 150, 600, 1200.0),
    ("U2", 310.0, 7.85, 0.00194, 100, 400,  800.0),
    ("U3",  78.0, 7.97, 0.00482,  50, 200,  150.0),
]

RESERVE = 0.10                                     # spinning reserve fraction

def dispatch(on, demand):
    """Lambda-iteration economic dispatch for the committed units.
    Returns (cost, outputs) or (inf, None) if infeasible."""
    units = [u for u, s in zip(UNITS, on) if s]
    if not units:
        return float("inf"), None
    if sum(u[4] for u in units) > demand or sum(u[5] for u in units) < demand * (1 + RESERVE):
        return float("inf"), None
    lo, hi = 0.0, 100.0
    for _ in range(100):                       # bisection on lambda ($/MWh)
        lam = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        P = [min(max((lam - b) / (2 * c), pmin), pmax) for _, a, b, c, pmin, pmax, _ in units]
        lo, hi = (lam, hi) if sum(P) < demand else (lo, lam)
    cost = sum(a + b * p + c * p * p for (_, a, b, c, *_), p in zip(units, P))
    return cost, P

def unit_commitment():
    states = list(itertools.product((0, 1), repeat=len(UNITS)))
    # best[t][state] = (cumulative cost, previous state)
    best = [{} for _ in LOAD]
    for t, demand in enumerate(LOAD):
        for s in states:
            op, _ = dispatch(s, demand)
            if op == float("inf"):
                continue
            if t == 0:
                prev_opts = [((0,) * len(UNITS), 0.0)]          # all units off before t=0
            else:
                prev_opts = [(p, c[0]) for p, c in best[t - 1].items()]
            cand = []
            for p, c in prev_opts:
                start = sum(u[6] for u, a, b in zip(UNITS, p, s) if b and not a)
                cand.append((c + start + op, p))
            best[t][s] = min(cand)
    # backtrack
    s = min(best[-1], key=lambda k: best[-1][k][0])
    total = best[-1][s][0]
    plan = []
    for t in range(len(LOAD) - 1, -1, -1):
        plan.append(s)
        s = best[t][s][1]
    return total, plan[::-1]

if __name__ == "__main__":
    total, plan = unit_commitment()
    for t, (d, s) in enumerate(zip(LOAD, plan)):
        cost, P = dispatch(s, d)
        names = [u[0] for u, x in zip(UNITS, s) if x]
        print(f"h{t+1}: {d:5.0f} MW  on={names}  P={[round(p,1) for p in P]}  ${cost:,.0f}")
    print(f"Total cost incl. start-ups: ${total:,.0f}")

5.3 Optimal power generation scheduling

Real scheduling couples hours together and includes non-thermal resources 4,46:

  • Multi-period (dynamic) dispatch adds ramp constraints |Pit − Pi,t−1| ≤ RiΔt, so the optimum in one hour depends on its neighbours.
  • Hydrothermal coordination allocates a limited volume of reservoir water across the horizon. At the optimum the marginal value of water, γ, times the marginal water use of the hydro plant equals the system λ, so water is used when it displaces the most expensive thermal energy 4.
  • Storage scheduling treats batteries and pumped storage similarly: charge when λ is low and discharge when it is high, subject to state-of-charge limits and round-trip efficiency.
  • Security-constrained UC and ED, and optimal power flow (OPF), add network flow limits and contingency (N−1) constraints, so that cheap generation can be curtailed when transmission is congested 4,46.

5.4 Fuel cost optimisation

Fuel dominates the variable cost of thermal generation, so operators optimise it on several levels 4:

  • Heat-rate improvement: keeping turbines, condensers and feedwater heaters in good condition and operating units near their best heat rate.
  • Fuel scheduling: fuel supply contracts with take-or-pay clauses, storage limits and delivery constraints are themselves optimisation problems coupled to UC, often solved by linear programming 4.
  • Fuel switching and blending: dual-fuel units and coal blending trade fuel price against heat rate, emissions and maintenance.
  • Emissions: carbon prices or emission caps add a cost term per tonne, changing the merit order between coal and gas 46.
Worked example 16 — value of a 1 % heat-rate improvement.

A 500 MW unit runs at a 70 % capacity factor with a heat rate of 9.5 MMBtu/MWh and fuel at 3 $/MMBtu. Annual output is 500 × 8 760 × 0.70 = 3.066 TWh, and annual fuel cost is 3.066 × 106 × 9.5 × 3 = 87.4 M$. A 1 % heat-rate improvement saves about 0.87 M$ per year, plus the corresponding emissions.

5.5 Capacity planning

Generation expansion planning decides what mix of plants to build, when and where, to meet forecast demand reliably at least cost 7,46. Reliability is measured with probabilistic indices such as loss-of-load expectation (LOLE). A widely used planning criterion is an LOLE of 0.1 days per year, i.e. one day in ten years 49. Variable renewables are credited by their effective load-carrying capability (ELCC), the additional load the system can carry at the same reliability after adding the resource 49.

The levelised cost of electricity (LCOE) spreads capital and lifetime costs over lifetime output. With discount rate r and life n years, the capital recovery factor is 50

CRF = r(1 + r)n / [(1 + r)n − 1](23) LCOE = (CRF × Ccap + CFOM) / (8 760 × CF) + CVOM + HR × pfuel(24)

LCOE is useful for comparing technologies but ignores when and where energy is produced; the system value of a megawatt-hour depends on its timing, so planners use full optimisation models or screening curves 7. A screening curve plots the annualised cost of 1 kW of capacity against the hours it runs: the intercept is the annualised fixed cost and the slope the variable cost. The lower envelope shows the cheapest technology for each duration on the load duration curve 7,46.

Worked example 17 — LCOE of a combined-cycle plant.

Capital 1 000 $/kW, fixed O&M 15 $/kW-yr, 7 % discount rate, 25-year life, 60 % capacity factor, heat rate 6.4 MMBtu/MWh, gas at 3.5 $/MMBtu and variable O&M 3 $/MWh. CRF = 0.07 × 1.0725/(1.0725 − 1) = 0.0858. The fixed part is (0.0858 × 1 000 + 15) × 1 000 / (8 760 × 0.6) = 19.2 $/MWh, and the variable part is 3 + 6.4 × 3.5 = 25.4 $/MWh, giving LCOE ≈ 44.6 $/MWh.

5.6 Renewable energy forecasting

Wind and solar output must be forecast so that operators can commit enough flexible capacity and reserves and so that plants can bid in markets 51,52. Methods depend on the horizon:

  • Seconds to minutes: persistence (the next value equals the present value) is hard to beat; for solar, sky cameras track approaching clouds 52.
  • Minutes to ~6 hours: statistical and machine-learning models on recent measurements; satellite-derived cloud motion vectors for solar 52.
  • Hours to days: numerical weather prediction (NWP) models, whose wind or irradiance forecasts are converted to power by physical models (power curves, PV models) or statistical post-processing, often using ensembles of several NWP runs 51.

Solar forecasting usually works with the clear-sky index (measured over modelled clear-sky irradiance) to remove the predictable diurnal and seasonal geometry 52. Accuracy is reported with normalised RMSE or MAE and with a skill score relative to a reference such as persistence, SS = 1 − RMSEmodel/RMSEref. Increasingly, forecasts are probabilistic, giving quantiles or prediction intervals that size reserves directly; the largest operational concern is ramp events, sudden large changes in aggregate output 51.

5.7 Load forecasting

Load forecasts drive every planning layer. They are usually grouped as very short-term (minutes, for dispatch and AGC), short-term (hours to two weeks, for UC and trading), medium-term (months, for maintenance and fuel) and long-term (years, for capacity planning) 53. The main drivers of short-term load are time of day, day of week and holidays, and weather, above all temperature, through heating and cooling demand; long-term forecasts depend on economic and demographic growth, electrification and efficiency 53. Methods range from multiple linear regression and time-series models (ARIMA, exponential smoothing) to artificial neural networks and gradient-boosted trees; well-specified regression models remain strong benchmarks 53,54. Accuracy is commonly reported as the mean absolute percentage error

MAPE = (100 % / N) Σ |yt − ŷt| / |yt|(25)

The following program builds a regression model with Fourier terms for the daily cycle, a weekend indicator and cooling-degree terms, trains it on seven weeks of synthetic hourly data and tests it on the eighth, comparing it with a seasonal-naive benchmark (same hour last week), as recommended practice suggests 54:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
hours = np.arange(24 * 7 * 8)                      # 8 weeks of hourly data
hod = hours % 24
dow = (hours // 24) % 7
temp = 22 + 8 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (hod - 9) / 24) + rng.normal(0, 1.5, hours.size)

# Synthetic "true" load: daily shape + weekend dip + cooling load + noise
load = (800 + 250 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (hod - 6) / 24)
        - 80 * (dow >= 5)
        + 12 * np.maximum(temp - 18, 0) ** 1.3
        + rng.normal(0, 20, hours.size))

def features(hod, dow, temp):
    X = [np.ones_like(temp)]
    for k in (1, 2, 3):                               # Fourier terms for daily cycle
        X += [np.sin(2*np.pi*k*hod/24), np.cos(2*np.pi*k*hod/24)]
    X += [(dow >= 5).astype(float)]                   # weekend indicator
    cdd = np.maximum(temp - 18, 0)                    # cooling degrees
    X += [cdd, cdd ** 2]
    return np.column_stack(X)

X = features(hod, dow, temp)
split = 24 * 7 * 7                                    # train on 7 weeks, test on week 8
beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X[:split], load[:split], rcond=None)
pred = X[split:] @ beta
actual = load[split:]

mape = np.mean(np.abs((actual - pred) / actual)) * 100
rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((actual - pred) ** 2))
naive = load[split - 168:-168]                        # seasonal-naive: same hour last week
mape_naive = np.mean(np.abs((actual - naive) / actual)) * 100
print(f"Regression  MAPE = {mape:.2f} %, RMSE = {rmse:.1f} MW")
print(f"Naive(168h) MAPE = {mape_naive:.2f} %")

Output: Regression MAPE = 2.06 %, RMSE = 20.6 MW; Naive(168h) MAPE = 4.67 %. The model halves the error of the naive benchmark. Because the data are synthetic, the regression structure matches the generating process; real loads need holiday effects, lagged temperatures and careful treatment of weather-forecast error 53.

Exercise 14 (phasors). A generator with Xs = 1.0 pu delivers 1.0 pu current at 0.8 power factor lagging and 1.0 pu terminal voltage. Find EA, δ and the pull-out power. To what value could the EMF be reduced, at the same real power, before reaching the steady-state stability limit?

Solution

I = 0.8 − j0.6; EA = 1 + j(0.8 − j0.6) = 1.6 + j0.8 = 1.789∠26.6° pu. Pmax = 1.789 × 1/1.0 = 1.79 pu. At the limit δ = 90°, P = E/Xs = 0.8, so Emin = 0.8 pu, although practical under-excitation limiters act well before this.

Solution

Exercise 16 (synchronisation). A 1 MVA DER generator is at 50.15 Hz, 4 % above network voltage, and its phase angle is passing through 12° as it approaches coincidence. Using Table 2, is closing permitted now? What is the slip period?

Solution

Exercise 17 (losses). A generator has 400 kW of fixed losses and 600 kW of load-dependent losses at rated output. At what fraction of rated load is its efficiency greatest?

Solution

Variable losses scale with load squared: 600x2 = 400 gives x = √(2/3) = 0.816, i.e. about 82 % of rated load.

Exercise 18 (economic dispatch). Two units have F1 = 500 + 8P1 + 0.004P12 and F2 = 400 + 7P2 + 0.006P22. Dispatch 600 MW, ignoring limits and losses. Check your answer with the calculator in section 5.1.

Exercise 19 (unit commitment). (a) Rank the three units of worked example 15 by full-load average cost to form a priority list. (b) Explain how you would add a minimum up time of 3 h to the dynamic-programming code.

Solution

(a) Average cost at Pmax: U1 = (561 + 4 752 + 562.3)/600 = 9.79 $/MWh; U2 = (310 + 3 140 + 310.4)/400 = 9.40 $/MWh; U3 = (78 + 1 594 + 192.8)/200 = 9.32 $/MWh. Priority order: U3, U2, U1. (b) Enlarge the DP state to record, for each unit, how many consecutive hours it has been on or off (or record the hour of the last switch), and forbid a transition that switches off a unit that has run for less than 3 h. The state space grows, which is one reason large systems use MILP formulations instead 48.

Exercise 20 (screening curve). Using the costs of Figure 13, find the break-even hours between the peaker and the combined cycle, and between the combined cycle and the baseload plant.

Exercise 21 (LCOE). A PV plant costs 900 $/kW with fixed O&M 15 $/kW-yr, a 30-year life, a 6 % discount rate and a 22 % capacity factor. It has no fuel or variable cost. Find its LCOE.

Solution

CRF = 0.06 × 1.0630/(1.0630 − 1) = 0.0726. LCOE = (0.0726 × 900 + 15) × 1 000/(8 760 × 0.22) = 80.4 × 1 000/1 927 = 41.7 $/MWh.

Solution

Absolute percentage errors: 5.00 %, 4.17 %, 3.33 %, 5.00 %; MAPE = 4.38 %. Squared errors: 2 500, 2 500, 900, 1 600; RMSE = √(7 500/4) = 43.3 MW.

Exercise 23 (renewable forecast skill). A wind-power forecast has RMSE = 8 % of capacity at a 6-hour horizon, while persistence has 13 %. Compute the skill score and interpret it.

Solution

SS = 1 − 8/13 = 0.385: the model removes about 38 % of the persistence error. Skill relative to persistence is usually small at very short horizons and grows with horizon as persistence degrades 51.
