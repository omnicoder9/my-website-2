Power Transmission Systems: Infrastructure, Line Engineering, Transformers and System Analysis

Abstract. Bulk electric power is moved from generating stations to load centres over a meshed high-voltage network of overhead lines, cables, transformers and substations. This article develops the engineering of that network from first principles. It surveys transmission infrastructure from high voltage (HV) to ultra-high voltage (UHV) and high-voltage direct current (HVDC); derives line parameters and the models used to predict voltage drop, losses and power transfer capability; treats corona, insulation coordination and thermal ratings; covers the construction, modelling, protection and testing of power transformers; and introduces the system-level analyses (power flow, voltage, transient and small-signal stability, reactive power compensation and congestion management) used to plan and operate a transmission grid. Each topic is supported by worked examples; key calculations are implemented in short, runnable Python programs whose printed output is reproduced in the text, and a set of exercises with full solutions closes the article. Generation is treated in a companion article and is outside the scope of this one.

1. Introduction: the role of transmission

An electric power system is conventionally divided into generation, transmission, sub-transmission and distribution1,2. Generator terminal voltages are limited by machine insulation to a few tens of kilovolts, so a step-up transformer at the power station raises the voltage to transmission level; the transmission network then carries power over long distances to bulk-supply substations, where step-down transformers feed sub-transmission and distribution systems1. The reason for transmitting at high voltage is elementary: for a given power $P$ at power factor $\cos\varphi$, the three-phase line current is $I = P/(\sqrt{3}\,V_{LL}\cos\varphi)$, so the resistive loss $3I^2R$ falls with the square of the voltage2. Doubling the voltage quarters the losses for the same conductor, or allows a much smaller conductor for the same loss.

Transmission networks are normally meshed rather than radial, so that the loss of any single element does not interrupt supply; this is the basis of the "N−1" security criterion used in planning and operation3. Because a mesh has many parallel paths, power divides among them according to their impedances rather than according to commercial contracts, which is the physical root of the congestion problem treated in Section 5.64.

2. Transmission infrastructure

2.1 Voltage classes: HV, EHV and UHV

Transmission voltages are standardised. IEC 60038 specifies, for systems above 245 kV, the highest voltage for equipment $U_m$ at preferred values of 300, 362, 420, 550, 800, 1100 and 1200 kV, with lower ranges for systems from 35 kV to 230 kV5. In engineering usage the levels are commonly grouped as follows; the boundaries are conventions rather than physical thresholds, and they differ slightly between authorities1,6,7.

2.2 Overhead transmission lines

Overhead lines carry the great majority of transmission circuit length because air is a free, self-restoring insulant and bare conductors are cheap to cool1,6. The principal components are:

  • Conductors. Stranded aluminium is used rather than copper because of its better conductivity-to-weight ratio and cost. The workhorse is ACSR (aluminium conductor steel-reinforced), in which a galvanised steel core carries mechanical tension and the outer aluminium layers carry current; other types include all-aluminium (AAC), all-aluminium-alloy (AAAC) and high-temperature low-sag conductors such as ACSS that permit higher operating temperatures1,8.
  • Bundles. At EHV and above each phase consists of two to eight sub-conductors held apart by spacers. Bundling increases the effective conductor radius, which lowers the surface electric field (and hence corona), reduces series reactance and raises capacitance and surge-impedance loading1,6.
  • Insulators. Strings of porcelain or toughened-glass discs, or composite (silicone rubber) long-rod insulators, sized for the required withstand voltages and pollution creepage distance (Section 3.6)6,9.
  • Structures. Lattice steel towers, tubular steel poles, or concrete and wood poles at lower voltages. Tower geometry fixes phase spacings and ground clearances, which in turn fix line inductance and capacitance (Section 3.1)2.
  • Shield (earth) wires. Grounded wires strung above the phases intercept lightning strokes and, often, carry optical fibre (OPGW) for protection and communications6.

2.3 Underground transmission cables

Cables are used where overhead lines are impractical or unacceptable: dense urban areas, river and sea crossings, and environmentally sensitive routes10. Modern AC transmission cables are predominantly extruded cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE), having largely superseded self-contained fluid-filled and high-pressure pipe-type designs; a cable comprises a copper or aluminium conductor, semiconducting screens, the main insulation, a metallic sheath or screen that carries charging and fault current, and an outer jacket10.

Two physical features dominate cable engineering. First, the conductor is separated from the earthed screen by only a few centimetres of insulation, so cable capacitance per kilometre is typically twenty or more times that of an overhead line; the resulting charging current limits the length of an AC cable that can be used without intermediate shunt reactors1,10. Second, heat must be conducted through the insulation and surrounding soil rather than convected into air, so ampacity is set by thermal resistance; the Neher–McGrath method and the IEC 60287 series are the standard calculation procedures11,12.

Worked Example A — Cable charging and critical length

A 400 kV, 50 Hz XLPE cable has capacitance $C = 0.20\ \mu\text{F/km}$ per phase. The three-phase charging reactive power per kilometre is

$$Q_c = V_{LL}^2\,\omega C = (400\times10^3)^2(2\pi\cdot 50)(0.20\times10^{-6}) \approx 10.1\ \text{Mvar/km},$$

and the charging current is $I_c = (V_{LL}/\sqrt3)\,\omega C \approx 14.5$ A/km. If the conductor's thermal rating is 1000 A, charging current alone would consume the entire rating after roughly $1000/14.5 \approx 69$ km of uncompensated cable (if all charging current were fed from one end). This "critical length" is why long AC cable links need shunt compensation, and why long submarine links use HVDC, which has no steady-state charging current10,13.

2.4 High-, extra-high- and ultra-high-voltage AC transmission

The natural loading level of an AC line rises with the square of its voltage. The surge-impedance loading (SIL), the power delivered to a resistive load equal to the line's characteristic impedance $Z_c$, is $\text{SIL} = V_{LL}^2/Z_c$1. With $Z_c$ roughly 250–400 Ω for overhead lines, SIL is of the order of 50 MW at 138 kV, 400–600 MW at 345–400 kV and 2000 MW or more at 765 kV1,14. Higher voltages therefore reduce the number of parallel circuits, rights-of-way and losses for a given transfer.

The step to each higher class is not free. At EHV, conductor surface gradients must be controlled by bundling to keep corona loss, radio interference and audible noise acceptable, and switching overvoltages (rather than lightning) begin to dictate air clearances and insulation6,9. At UHV the trends intensify: tower heights, clearances and bundle sizes (typically eight sub-conductors at 1000 kV) grow, and closely controlled switching overvoltages, using pre-insertion resistors and surge arresters, become essential7. China's 1000 kV Southeast Shanxi–Nanyang–Jingmen pilot project entered commercial operation in 2009 and was followed by a national UHV AC/DC programme7.

2.5 High-voltage direct current (HVDC)

An HVDC link converts AC to DC in a rectifier station, transmits DC over an overhead line or cable, and inverts back to AC at the far end. The first commercial scheme, the 20 MW, 100 kV Gotland link in Sweden, was commissioned in 1954 using mercury-arc valves13. Two converter technologies are now used:

  • Line-commutated converters (LCC) use thyristor valves in twelve-pulse bridges. They need a reasonably strong AC system for commutation, absorb reactive power (supplied by filters and capacitor banks at both stations) and reverse power by reversing DC voltage polarity. LCC remains the choice for the highest power ratings13,15.
  • Voltage-source converters (VSC) use self-commutated devices (IGBTs), today usually in modular multilevel converters (MMC). They control active and reactive power independently, can feed weak or passive networks and black-start a dead system, and reverse power flow by reversing current, which suits cross-linked polyethylene cables and multi-terminal grids16.

HVDC is preferred over AC (i) for long overhead lines, where the absence of reactive power and stability limits and the use of two conductors instead of three outweigh converter cost beyond a break-even distance, usually quoted as several hundred kilometres; (ii) for long cables, where AC charging current is prohibitive (Example A); and (iii) for asynchronous interconnections between systems of different frequency or unsynchronised control areas13,15. The present technological frontier is the ±1100 kV, 12 GW Changji–Guquan link in China, about 3300 km long, energised in 201917.

2.6 Substations and switching stations

Substations are the nodes of the transmission network. A transformer substation connects networks of different voltage; a switching station has no transformation and serves only to connect and sectionalise lines at one voltage; a converter station terminates an HVDC link18. Major equipment includes power transformers, circuit breakers, disconnectors and earthing switches, instrument transformers (current and voltage transformers) feeding protection and metering, surge arresters, shunt reactors and capacitors, and the protection, control and communication systems housed in a control building18.

The bus arrangement determines how reliably and flexibly circuits can be switched. In increasing order of cost and reliability, common schemes are the single bus, main-and-transfer bus, ring bus, breaker-and-a-half and double-bus double-breaker; the breaker-and-a-half scheme, in which three breakers serve two circuits, is widely used at EHV because any breaker can be maintained, and any single bus fault cleared, without losing a circuit1,18.

Two construction technologies are used. Air-insulated substations (AIS) place equipment outdoors with air clearances; they are cheaper and easy to inspect but need large areas. Gas-insulated substations (GIS) enclose busbars and switchgear in earthed metal enclosures filled with pressurised sulphur hexafluoride (SF6), reducing footprint by an order of magnitude, which is valuable in cities and in polluted or harsh environments18. Every substation also needs an earthing (grounding) grid designed so that step and touch voltages during earth faults remain below tolerable limits for people, following IEEE Std 8019.

3. Transmission line engineering

3.1 Transmission line parameters: resistance, inductance and capacitance

A transmission line is a distributed circuit characterised per unit length by series resistance $R$, series inductance $L$, shunt capacitance $C$ and shunt conductance $G$. For overhead lines $G$ represents insulator leakage and corona loss and is usually neglected in performance calculations1,2.

Resistance

The DC resistance of a conductor of resistivity $\rho$, length $l$ and cross-section $A$ is $R_{dc}=\rho l/A$. Three corrections apply: stranding adds 1–2% because the spiralled strands are longer than the conductor; resistance increases with temperature as $R_2 = R_1 (T + t_2)/(T + t_1)$, with $T \approx 228\,{}^\circ\text{C}$ for hard-drawn aluminium; and at AC, skin effect pushes current toward the conductor surface, raising the effective resistance by a few percent at power frequency for large conductors1,2. Manufacturers' tables quote AC resistance at specified temperatures, and these values should be used in practice.

Inductance

For a fully transposed three-phase line, each phase has the same inductance per unit length

$$L = 2\times10^{-7}\ln\frac{D_{eq}}{D_s}\ \ \text{H/m}, \qquad D_{eq}=\sqrt[3]{D_{ab}D_{bc}D_{ca}},$$

where $D_{eq}$ is the geometric mean distance (GMD) between phases and $D_s$ is the geometric mean radius (GMR) of the phase conductor. For a solid round conductor of radius $r$, $D_s = e^{-1/4}r = 0.7788\,r$; for stranded conductors $D_s$ is tabulated1,2. Transposition, exchanging phase positions at intervals along the route, balances the three phase inductances that would otherwise differ because of asymmetric spacing2.

Capacitance

The capacitance from each phase to neutral of a transposed line is

$$C = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(D_{eq}/r)}\ \ \text{F/m},\qquad \varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/m},$$

where $r$ is now the physical outer radius, because charge resides on the surface1. For a bundle of $n$ sub-conductors with spacing $d$, both $D_s$ and $r$ are replaced by equivalent bundle radii: $\sqrt{D_s d}$ for two, $\sqrt[3]{D_s d^2}$ for three and $1.091\sqrt[4]{D_s d^3}$ for four sub-conductors (with $r$ substituted for $D_s$ in the capacitance case)1. The effect of earth on capacitance can be included by the method of images; it is small for typical heights and is omitted here2.

3.2 Line impedance and line models

The series impedance per unit length is $z = R + j\omega L$ and the shunt admittance $y = G + j\omega C$. Solving the telegrapher's equations for sinusoidal steady state gives the propagation constant $\gamma = \sqrt{zy} = \alpha + j\beta$ and the characteristic (surge) impedance $Z_c = \sqrt{z/y}$1,2. Sending- and receiving-end quantities are related by the two-port (ABCD) equations

$$\begin{bmatrix}V_S\\ I_S\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A & B\\ C & D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V_R\\ I_R\end{bmatrix},$$

with constants depending on line length $l$1:

Model (typical length)$A = D$$B$$C$
Short line (< 80 km): series $Z$ only$1$$Z$$0$
Medium line (80–250 km): nominal π$1 + YZ/2$$Z$$Y(1+YZ/4)$
Long line (> 250 km): exact / equivalent π$\cosh\gamma l$$Z_c\sinh\gamma l$$\sinh(\gamma l)/Z_c$

Here $Z = zl$ and $Y = yl$ are the total series impedance and shunt admittance. The exact long-line constants can be represented by an equivalent π circuit whose elements are $Z' = Z\,\sinh(\gamma l)/(\gamma l)$ and $Y'/2 = (Y/2)\,\tanh(\gamma l/2)/(\gamma l/2)$1,2.

The following program computes the parameters of Worked Example 1 and then the full long-line performance used in Worked Example 2. It uses only the Python standard library.

"""Per-phase parameters and long-line performance of a transposed 3-phase line."""
import cmath, math

EPS0 = 8.854e-12                 # F/m

def bundle_radius(r, d, n):
    """Equivalent radius of an n-conductor symmetrical bundle with spacing d."""
    if n == 1: return r
    if n == 2: return math.sqrt(r * d)
    if n == 3: return (r * d * d) ** (1 / 3)
    if n == 4: return 1.0905 * (r * d ** 3) ** 0.25
    raise ValueError("n must be 1-4")

def line_parameters(gmr, radius, d12, d23, d13, n=1, d=0.0, f=50.0):
    gmd = (d12 * d23 * d13) ** (1 / 3)                 # geometric mean distance
    ds = bundle_radius(gmr, d, n)                      # GMR used for inductance
    dsc = bundle_radius(radius, d, n)                  # physical radius for capacitance
    L = 2e-7 * math.log(gmd / ds)                      # H/m per phase
    C = 2 * math.pi * EPS0 / math.log(gmd / dsc)       # F/m to neutral
    return gmd, L, C

f, Vll, length = 50.0, 400e3, 300.0                    # Hz, V, km
gmd, L, C = line_parameters(gmr=0.01143, radius=0.01407,
                            d12=11, d23=11, d13=22, n=4, d=0.45, f=f)
R = 0.0798 / 4                                         # ohm/km (AC, 50 degC, 4 sub-conductors)
z = complex(R, 2 * math.pi * f * L * 1e3)              # ohm/km
y = complex(0, 2 * math.pi * f * C * 1e3)              # S/km
Zc, gamma = cmath.sqrt(z / y), cmath.sqrt(z * y)
print(f"GMD = {gmd:.2f} m, L = {L*1e6:.3f} mH/km, C = {C*1e12:.2f} nF/km")
print(f"z = {z:.4f} ohm/km, y = j{y.imag*1e6:.3f} uS/km")
print(f"Zc = {abs(Zc):.1f} ohm, SIL = {Vll**2/abs(Zc)/1e6:.0f} MW")

# Exact long-line ABCD constants
A = cmath.cosh(gamma * length); B = Zc * cmath.sinh(gamma * length)
Cc = cmath.sinh(gamma * length) / Zc; D = A
print(f"A = {abs(A):.4f} at {math.degrees(cmath.phase(A)):.2f} deg, "
      f"B = {abs(B):.2f} ohm at {math.degrees(cmath.phase(B)):.2f} deg")

# Receiving end: 700 MW at 0.95 lagging, 400 kV
Vr = Vll / math.sqrt(3); P, pf = 700e6, 0.95
Ir = P / (math.sqrt(3) * Vll * pf) * cmath.exp(-1j * math.acos(pf))
Vs = A * Vr + B * Ir; Is = Cc * Vr + D * Ir
Ss = 3 * Vs * Is.conjugate()
print(f"Vs = {abs(Vs)*math.sqrt(3)/1e3:.1f} kV at {math.degrees(cmath.phase(Vs)):.2f} deg, "
      f"Is = {abs(Is):.0f} A")
print(f"Ps = {Ss.real/1e6:.1f} MW, Qs = {Ss.imag/1e6:.1f} Mvar, "
      f"losses = {Ss.real/1e6 - 700:.1f} MW, efficiency = {700e6/Ss.real*100:.2f} %")
print(f"voltage regulation = {(abs(Vs)/abs(A) - Vr)/Vr*100:.1f} %")

Worked Example 2 — Long-line performance and the Ferranti effect

The line of Example 1 delivers 700 MW at 0.95 power factor lagging and 400 kV at its receiving end. Using the exact long-line model, the program gives $A = 0.9504\angle0.22^\circ$, $B = 79.2\angle85.8^\circ$ Ω; the sending-end voltage must be $|V_S| = 456.5$ kV (1.14 pu) at a load angle of $17.4^\circ$; sending-end power is 718.5 MW, so line losses are 18.5 MW (efficiency 97.4%).

The receiving-end voltage at no load with the same sending voltage is $|V_S|/|A|$, giving a voltage regulation of $(|V_S|/|A| - |V_R|)/|V_R| = 20.1\%$. Conversely, if the sending end is held at 400 kV and the receiving end is opened, the receiving voltage rises to $400/0.9504 = 420.9$ kV: the Ferranti effect, caused by line charging current flowing through series inductance1,2. A sending voltage of 1.14 pu is unacceptable in practice (equipment is typically rated for no more than about 1.05 pu continuous at 400 kV, since $U_m = 420$ kV5), so this transfer requires reactive compensation; Section 5.5 sizes it.

3.3 Voltage drop and voltage regulation

For a short line with series impedance $Z = R + jX$ carrying current $I$ to a receiving voltage $V_R$, $V_S = V_R + ZI$. Taking $V_R$ as reference and writing the load as $P + jQ$, the in-phase and quadrature components of the drop are approximately2,20

$$\Delta V \approx \frac{RP + XQ}{V_R}, \qquad \delta V \approx \frac{XP - RQ}{V_R}.$$

Because $X \gg R$ on transmission lines, the magnitude of the voltage drop is governed mainly by reactive power flow ($XQ$), while the phase-angle difference is governed mainly by active power flow ($XP$). This "P–δ, Q–V" decoupling underlies voltage control by reactive power and the fast-decoupled power flow method (Section 5.1)20,21.

Worked Example 3 — Voltage drop on a 132 kV line

A 50 km, 132 kV line has $R = 0.10$ Ω/km and $X = 0.40$ Ω/km and supplies 60 MW + j30 Mvar at 132 kV. Then $R_{tot} = 5$ Ω, $X_{tot} = 20$ Ω, and the approximate drop is $\Delta V \approx (5\cdot60 + 20\cdot30)/132 = 6.82$ kV, or 5.2%. The exact phasor calculation gives $|V_S| = 139.0$ kV (5.3% above $V_R$), confirming the approximation. Note that the reactive term (600) is twice the resistive term (300) even though the reactive load is only half the active load. Line current is 293 A and the $3I^2R$ loss is 1.29 MW.

Voltage regulation is defined as the percentage rise in receiving-end voltage from full load to no load with the sending voltage held constant: $\text{VR} = (|V_{R,NL}| - |V_{R,FL}|)/|V_{R,FL}| \times 100\%$, where $|V_{R,NL}| = |V_S|/|A|$1.

3.4 Line losses

Active power losses in a line are dominated by series $I^2R$ loss: $P_{loss} = 3|I|^2R$ for a three-phase line, with $|I| = S/(\sqrt3 V_{LL})$1. Substituting, $P_{loss} = R\,S^2/V_{LL}^2$, showing the inverse-square dependence on voltage and the direct dependence on apparent (not just active) power: reactive power flow raises current and therefore loss without delivering energy22. Additional, generally smaller, components are corona loss (Section 3.5), insulator leakage and, for cables, dielectric loss and sheath circulating-current loss6,10. Losses also depend on conductor temperature through $R(T)$, so heavily loaded lines lose proportionally more. In long lines, the exact loss is obtained from the difference between sending- and receiving-end power, as in Example 2.

3.5 Corona discharge

When the electric field at the conductor surface exceeds the breakdown strength of the surrounding air, the air partially ionises in a glow discharge called corona. Corona dissipates energy, generates radio and television interference, produces audible noise (a crackle and a 100/120 Hz hum) and ozone, and can accelerate conductor ageing; it is a principal design constraint at EHV and UHV6,23. Peek's empirical work established the disruptive critical voltage (phase-to-neutral, rms) for a smooth round conductor23:

$$V_d = m_0\, g_0\, \delta\, r \ln\frac{D}{r}\ \ \text{kV}, \qquad \delta = \frac{3.92\,b}{273 + t},$$

where $g_0 \approx 21.1$ kVrms/cm (30 kV peak/cm) is the breakdown strength of air at standard conditions, $r$ and $D$ are in cm, $m_0 \le 1$ is a surface irregularity factor (about 0.8–0.9 for weathered stranded conductors), and $\delta$ is the relative air density for pressure $b$ in cmHg and temperature $t$ in °C23,24. Peek's corresponding loss formula in fair weather is $P = (241/\delta)(f+25)\sqrt{r/D}\,(V - V_d)^2\times10^{-5}$ kW/km/phase23,24. Rain, fog and snow lower the inception voltage sharply, so foul-weather losses can exceed fair-weather losses by an order of magnitude6.

Worked Example 4 — Why EHV lines use bundled conductors

Consider a single Drake conductor ($r = 1.407$ cm) with $D_{eq} = 1386$ cm at 25 °C and 76 cmHg ($\delta = 1.00$), $m_0 = 0.85$. Then $V_d = 0.85\times21.1\times1.00\times1.407\times\ln(1386/1.407) = 174$ kV phase, or 301 kV line-to-line. Since this is well below 400 kV, a single Drake conductor would be in heavy corona continuously. Bundling four conductors raises the effective radius and reduces the maximum surface gradient well below inception, which is why EHV lines use bundles1,6.

3.6 Insulation coordination

Insulation coordination is "the selection of the dielectric strength of equipment in relation to the voltages which can appear on the system for which the equipment is intended, taking into account the service environment and the characteristics of the available protective devices"9. IEC 60071-1 classifies stresses by shape and duration9,25:

  • Continuous power-frequency voltage, up to $U_m/\sqrt3$;
  • Temporary overvoltages (TOV) at or near power frequency, from earth faults, load rejection and resonance, lasting up to seconds;
  • Slow-front overvoltages, mainly from switching, represented by the standard 250/2500 μs switching impulse;
  • Fast-front overvoltages, mainly from lightning, represented by the standard 1.2/50 μs lightning impulse;
  • Very-fast-front overvoltages, notably from disconnector operation in GIS.

Equipment is assigned standard withstand voltages: the lightning impulse withstand level (LIWL, called BIL in IEEE practice) and, for $U_m > 245$ kV (IEC range II), the switching impulse withstand level (SIWL or BSL); in range I a short-duration power-frequency withstand replaces the switching impulse9,25. For $U_m = 420$ kV, for example, IEC 60071-1 lists standard switching impulse levels of 850 to 1050 kV phase-to-earth and lightning impulse levels from 1050 to 1425 kV9. Metal-oxide surge arresters limit overvoltages to a protective level, and IEEE Std C62.22 recommends protective ratios (withstand level divided by arrester protective level) of at least 1.2 for lightning and 1.15 for switching impulses26. At EHV and UHV, where switching overvoltages dominate, they are further controlled by circuit-breaker pre-insertion resistors and controlled (point-on-wave) switching6,7.

Worked Example 5 — Protective margin

A 420 kV transformer has LIWL 1425 kV and SIWL 1050 kV. The arrester at its terminals has a lightning impulse protective level of 820 kV and a switching impulse protective level of 700 kV (illustrative values). Protective ratios are $1425/820 = 1.74$ and $1050/700 = 1.50$, both above the C62.22 minima of 1.2 and 1.1526. In a real study the lightning margin would be reduced by the travelling-wave voltage rise over the lead length and separation distance between arrester and transformer, which is why arresters are mounted as close as possible to protected equipment9,26.

3.7 Thermal limits

The current an overhead conductor may carry is limited by its maximum allowable temperature, which is set by annealing (loss of tensile strength) of aluminium and, more often, by sag: a hotter conductor elongates and sags toward the ground, reducing statutory clearances8,27. IEEE Std 738 and CIGRE TB 601 compute ampacity from a steady-state heat balance8,27:

$$q_c + q_r = q_s + I^2 R(T_c) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I = \sqrt{\frac{q_c + q_r - q_s}{R(T_c)}},$$

where $q_c$ is convective cooling (strongly dependent on wind speed and direction), $q_r$ radiative cooling, $q_s$ solar heating, all in W/m, and $R(T_c)$ the AC resistance at the maximum conductor temperature $T_c$. Static ratings assume conservative weather (for example low wind speed and high ambient temperature); dynamic line rating uses measured or forecast weather to release extra capacity when conditions allow27. Short-time emergency ratings exploit the conductor's thermal time constant (typically several minutes)8. For cables and transformers the thermal limit is set instead by insulation ageing (Sections 2.3 and 4.4).

Worked Example 6 — Steady-state ampacity

3.8 Power transfer capability

For a lossless line with sending and receiving voltages $V_S\angle\delta$ and $V_R\angle0$ and reactance $X$, the active power transferred is2,20

$$P = \frac{|V_S||V_R|}{X}\sin\delta,\qquad P_{max} = \frac{|V_S||V_R|}{X}.$$

For a long line $X$ is replaced by $|B|$; the line of Example 2 has a theoretical steady-state limit of $400^2/79.2 \approx 2020$ MW. Operation near $\delta = 90^\circ$ would leave no margin for disturbances, so practical limits are much lower20. Transfer capability is the minimum of three limits: the thermal limit (dominant for short lines), the voltage-drop limit (typically a 5% drop for medium lines) and the steady-state stability limit (dominant for long lines, evaluated with a 30% stability margin, corresponding to an angle of about 44and the steady-state stability limit (dominant for long lines, evaluated with a 30% stability margin, corresponding to roughly 44° across the line and its terminal systems)deg; across the line and its terminal systems)14. Transfer capability can be raised by series capacitors (reducing effective $X$), by shunt compensation and static var systems that support voltage at intermediate points, by higher-temperature conductors that raise the thermal limit, and by HVDC22,29.

4. Power transformers

Transformers connect the voltage levels of the network: generator step-up (GSU) units, transmission interconnecting transformers and autotransformers, and step-down units at bulk-supply points. They are among the most expensive and longest-lead-time items in a substation, so their design, protection and condition monitoring receive particular attention30,31.

4.1 Transformer construction

  • Core. Laminated cold-rolled grain-oriented (CRGO) silicon steel, with laminations a few tenths of a millimetre thick and insulated from each other to limit eddy currents; step-lap mitred joints reduce no-load loss and noise. Core-type designs (windings around the limbs) dominate; shell-type designs (core surrounding the windings) are used by some manufacturers for large units because of their short-circuit strength30,31.
  • Windings. Copper conductors, often continuously transposed cable (CTC) in large units, arranged concentrically (low-voltage winding nearest the core) as disc, helical or layer windings. Windings must withstand the large radial and axial forces of through-faults31.
  • Insulation and cooling. Oil-impregnated cellulose (paper and pressboard) in mineral oil, or in natural or synthetic esters with higher fire point. Cooling modes are designated by four letters: ONAN (oil natural, air natural), ONAF (forced air via fans), OFAF and ODAF (pumped or directed oil flow)30,32.
  • Accessories. Bushings (often oil-impregnated or resin-impregnated paper, capacitance graded), the conservator tank with breather or bladder, the Buchholz relay, pressure-relief device, oil and winding temperature indicators, and the tap changer (Section 4.5)30.

4.2 Transformer equivalent circuits

A practical transformer is modelled as an ideal transformer plus series and shunt elements. Winding resistances $R_1, R_2$ and leakage reactances $X_1, X_2$ appear in series; the magnetising branch, with conductance $G_c$ representing core loss and susceptance $B_m$ representing magnetising current, appears in shunt1,2. Because magnetising current in a power transformer is typically well under 1% of rated current, the shunt branch is often moved to the terminals or omitted in system studies, leaving a series impedance $R_{eq} + jX_{eq}$ referred to one side1. In per unit on the transformer's own rating the ideal ratio disappears, which is the principal reason per-unit analysis is used1,2. Converting a per-unit impedance to a new base uses

$$Z_{pu,new} = Z_{pu,old}\left(\frac{V_{base,old}}{V_{base,new}}\right)^2\frac{S_{base,new}}{S_{base,old}}.$$

The parameters are found from two standard tests. The open-circuit (no-load) test, at rated voltage on one winding with the other open, measures core loss and magnetising current and so yields $G_c$ and $B_m$. The short-circuit (load-loss) test, with one winding shorted and rated current circulated at reduced voltage, measures copper loss and impedance voltage and so yields $R_{eq}$ and $X_{eq}$1,32.

4.3 Voltage transformation

For an ideal transformer $V_1/V_2 = N_1/N_2 = a$ and $I_1/I_2 = 1/a$, so apparent power is conserved and impedances transform by $a^2$1. Three-phase transformers (or banks of three single-phase units, used for the largest ratings to ease transport) are connected star (Y) or delta (D) on each side. IEC vector-group notation such as YNd11 or Dyn11 states the connections, the neutral, and the phase displacement in multiples of 30° (11 = 330°, i.e. the low-voltage side leads by 30°)32. A delta winding provides a path for triplen-harmonic magnetising currents and zero-sequence currents, which matters for earth-fault protection and system grounding2. Where the voltage ratio is small (for example 400/275 kV or 500/230 kV) autotransformers, which share a common winding, are used because only part of the power is transformed magnetically, giving a smaller, cheaper and more efficient unit at the cost of galvanic connection between systems1,30.

4.4 Transformer losses and efficiency

Losses divide into no-load loss $P_0$ (core hysteresis and eddy-current loss, practically independent of load and present whenever the transformer is energised) and load loss $P_{cu}$ (winding $I^2R$ plus stray and eddy losses in windings and structural parts, proportional to the square of load)31,32. At per-unit load $x$ and power factor $\cos\varphi$,

$$\eta = \frac{x S_r\cos\varphi}{x S_r\cos\varphi + P_0 + x^2 P_{cu}},$$

and efficiency is maximum when load loss equals no-load loss, at $x = \sqrt{P_0/P_{cu}}$1. Large power transformers routinely exceed 99% efficiency, but because they carry enormous energy throughput even 0.5% loss is economically significant, so losses are capitalised in purchase evaluations30. Losses also produce heat: the hot-spot temperature governs the ageing rate of cellulose insulation, which roughly doubles for every 6 K rise above the reference temperature in the IEC loading guide model30,31.

A 100 MVA, 230/66 kV transformer has no-load loss 60 kW, full-load load loss 400 kW and impedance 10% (with $R = 0.4\%$). Efficiency at full load, 0.85 pf: $\eta = 85/(85 + 0.06 + 0.40) = 99.46\%$. At half load, 0.85 pf: $\eta = 42.5/(42.5 + 0.06 + 0.10) = 99.62\%$. Maximum efficiency occurs at $x = \sqrt{60/400} = 0.387$ (38.7% load) and, at unity pf, is 99.69%. 4.5 Tap changers

Tap changers vary the turns ratio to regulate voltage and control reactive power flow between networks. A de-energised tap changer (DETC, or off-circuit tap changer) is set with the transformer out of service, typically ±2 × 2.5%, to suit long-term conditions. An on-load tap changer (OLTC) changes taps under load, typically over ±10% to ±15% in steps of about 0.6–1.5%; it is usually placed in the high-voltage winding (where current is lower) or at its neutral end30,33. Because the circuit cannot be interrupted, an OLTC uses a selector to preselect the next tap and a diverter switch that transfers load through transition impedances (resistors in the high-speed Jansen design, reactors in some North American designs) to limit circulating current between taps; modern diverters commonly switch in vacuum interrupters, reducing oil contamination and maintenance30,33. An automatic voltage regulator (AVR, ANSI device 90) commands the OLTC, using a deadband and time delay to avoid hunting and, often, line-drop compensation to regulate voltage at a remote point30. OLTC behaviour matters for voltage stability: after a disturbance, tap changers restore load-side voltage and hence voltage-dependent load, which can drive the transmission system further toward collapse (Section 5.2)34.

4.6 Transformer protection

IEEE Std C37.91 describes the protection of power transformers35. The principal functions are:

  • Differential protection (87T). Compares currents entering and leaving the transformer, after correction for ratio and vector-group phase shift. A percentage (biased) characteristic tolerates CT error and tap position. Inrush current on energisation flows in one winding only and would look like an internal fault; it is recognised by its high second-harmonic content (harmonic restraint or blocking), and overexcitation by its fifth harmonic35,36.
  • Restricted earth-fault (64REF) protection for faults near the neutral of an earthed star winding, which give too little differential current35.
  • Mechanical protections. The Buchholz relay (gas accumulation alarm and oil-surge trip), sudden-pressure relay (63) and pressure-relief device detect internal arcing faults rapidly and sensitively30,35.
  • Backup and system protections. Phase and earth overcurrent (50/51), overexcitation or volts-per-hertz (24) and thermal (49) protection using oil and winding temperature35.

4.7 Transformer testing and maintenance

IEC 60076-1 and IEEE C57.12.90 define factory tests32,37. Routine tests on every unit include winding resistance, voltage ratio and phase displacement, short-circuit impedance and load loss, no-load loss and current, dielectric tests (applied and induced voltage, lightning impulse for higher voltages) and on-load tap changer operation. Type tests on a representative unit include temperature-rise tests; special tests include short-circuit withstand, sound level and switching impulse32. Partial-discharge measurement during induced-voltage tests reveals insulation defects before they cause failure31.

In service, condition monitoring focuses on the insulation system30,31:

  • Dissolved gas analysis (DGA) of oil samples identifies fault gases (hydrogen, methane, ethane, ethylene, acetylene, CO, CO2). Gas ratios and the Duval triangle classify faults into partial discharge, low- and high-energy discharge, and thermal faults of increasing temperature; IEEE C57.104 gives gas-level interpretation guidance38,39.
  • Oil tests: moisture, dielectric breakdown strength, acidity, interfacial tension and furan compounds (an indicator of paper degradation)30.
  • Electrical diagnostics: power factor/dissipation factor and capacitance of windings and bushings, insulation resistance, winding resistance, turns ratio, and sweep frequency response analysis (SFRA) to detect winding movement after through-faults or transport31.
  • Tap-changer maintenance at intervals based on operation count or time, plus infrared thermography of connections and bushings30,33.

5. Transmission system analysis

Power system stability is defined as "the ability of an electric power system, for a given initial operating condition, to regain a state of operating equilibrium after being subjected to a physical disturbance, with most system variables bounded so that practically the entire system remains intact"40. The IEEE/CIGRE classification divides it into rotor-angle stability (small-disturbance and transient), voltage stability and frequency stability; a 2021 revision added resonance stability and converter-driven stability to account for power-electronic resources40,41. These dynamic analyses all start from a steady-state operating point supplied by a power flow.

5.1 Power flow analysis

The power flow (load flow) problem computes the voltage magnitude and angle at every bus for specified generation and load, from which line flows and losses follow1,2. The network is described by its bus admittance matrix $\mathbf{Y}_{bus}$, built from line and transformer π-models, so that $\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{Y}_{bus}\mathbf{V}$. The complex power injected at bus $i$ is $S_i = V_i\sum_k Y_{ik}^*V_k^*$, which in polar form gives the nonlinear power-balance equations

$$P_i = \sum_{k} |V_i||V_k|\left(G_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik} + B_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik}\right),\qquad Q_i = \sum_{k} |V_i||V_k|\left(G_{ik}\sin\theta_{ik} - B_{ik}\cos\theta_{ik}\right).$$

Each bus has two known and two unknown quantities: a slack bus has specified $|V|$ and $\theta$ (and absorbs the unknown losses); a PV (generator) bus has specified $P$ and $|V|$; a PQ (load) bus has specified $P$ and $Q$1.

The Newton–Raphson method42, made practical for large networks by Tinney and Hart through sparse-matrix techniques, linearises these equations around the current estimate and solves $\mathbf{J}\,[\Delta\boldsymbol\theta;\ \Delta|\mathbf V|] = [\Delta\mathbf P;\ \Delta\mathbf Q]$ for corrections, converging quadratically43. The fast decoupled method of Stott and Alsaç exploits the P–θ, Q–V decoupling of Section 3.3 to use two constant, sparse matrices, trading a few extra iterations for much cheaper ones21. The linear DC power flow ($|V| = 1$, $R = 0$, small angles, so $\mathbf P = \mathbf B'\boldsymbol\theta$) is widely used in market clearing and contingency screening; Stott et al. review its accuracy and limits44.

The program below is a compact but complete Newton–Raphson solver with an analytic Jacobian, applied to a three-bus network on a 100 MVA base.

"""Newton-Raphson power flow (polar form) for a small network.
Bus types: 0 = slack, 1 = PV, 2 = PQ. All quantities in per unit."""
import numpy as np

def build_ybus(n, lines):
    Y = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=complex)
    for i, j, r, x, b in lines:          # b = total line charging
        y = 1 / complex(r, x)
        Y[i, i] += y + 1j * b / 2
        Y[j, j] += y + 1j * b / 2
        Y[i, j] -= y
        Y[j, i] -= y
    return Y

def newton_raphson(Y, btype, V, th, P, Q, tol=1e-8, itmax=20):
    n = len(V)
    pv_pq = [i for i in range(n) if btype[i] != 0]
    pq = [i for i in range(n) if btype[i] == 2]
    G, B = Y.real, Y.imag
    for it in range(itmax):
        Vc = V * np.exp(1j * th)
        S = Vc * np.conj(Y @ Vc)                # calculated injections
        dP = P[pv_pq] - S.real[pv_pq]
        dQ = Q[pq] - S.imag[pq]
        mis = np.concatenate([dP, dQ])
        print(f"iter {it}: max mismatch = {np.max(np.abs(mis)):.2e}")
        if np.max(np.abs(mis)) < tol:
            return V, th, S, it
        # Jacobian via analytic partial derivatives
        Pc, Qc = S.real, S.imag
        J11 = np.zeros((len(pv_pq), len(pv_pq))); J12 = np.zeros((len(pv_pq), len(pq)))
        J21 = np.zeros((len(pq), len(pv_pq)));   J22 = np.zeros((len(pq), len(pq)))
        for a, i in enumerate(pv_pq):
            for b_, k in enumerate(pv_pq):
                if i == k:
                    J11[a, b_] = -Qc[i] - B[i, i] * V[i]**2
                else:
                    t = th[i] - th[k]
                    J11[a, b_] = V[i]*V[k]*(G[i,k]*np.sin(t) - B[i,k]*np.cos(t))
            for b_, k in enumerate(pq):
                if i == k:
                    J12[a, b_] = Pc[i]/V[i] + G[i, i]*V[i]
                else:
                    t = th[i] - th[k]
                    J12[a, b_] = V[i]*(G[i,k]*np.cos(t) + B[i,k]*np.sin(t))
        for a, i in enumerate(pq):
            for b_, k in enumerate(pv_pq):
                if i == k:
                    J21[a, b_] = Pc[i] - G[i, i]*V[i]**2
                else:
                    t = th[i] - th[k]
                    J21[a, b_] = -V[i]*V[k]*(G[i,k]*np.cos(t) + B[i,k]*np.sin(t))
            for b_, k in enumerate(pq):
                if i == k:
                    J22[a, b_] = Qc[i]/V[i] - B[i, i]*V[i]
                else:
                    t = th[i] - th[k]
                    J22[a, b_] = V[i]*(G[i,k]*np.sin(t) - B[i,k]*np.cos(t))
        J = np.block([[J11, J12], [J21, J22]])
        dx = np.linalg.solve(J, mis)
        th[pv_pq] += dx[:len(pv_pq)]
        V[pq] += dx[len(pv_pq):]
    raise RuntimeError("did not converge")

5.2 Voltage stability

Voltage stability is the ability of a system to maintain steady acceptable voltages at all buses after a disturbance; instability manifests as progressive, uncontrollable voltage decline, often ending in voltage collapse34,40. Its driving force is the load: loads restored by tap changers, thermostatic controls and motor dynamics try to consume more power than the transmission network can deliver34,45. For a load supplied from a stiff source $E$ through reactance $X$, the receiving voltage satisfies

$$V^4 + (2QX - E^2)V^2 + X^2(P^2 + Q^2) = 0,$$

which has real solutions only up to a maximum transfer: for a load of power factor $\cos\varphi$, $P_{max} = E^2\cos\varphi\,/\,[2X(1+\sin\varphi)]$34. The resulting P–V or nose curves (Figure 9) show that (i) near the nose, voltage falls steeply with load; (ii) operating points on the lower branch are unstable for most load characteristics; and (iii) the nose voltage can be close to normal operating voltage when loads are compensated, so voltage magnitude alone is a poor warning34,45.

"""P-V (nose) curve of a load fed from a stiff source through a reactance."""
import numpy as np
E, X = 1.0, 0.5                         # pu
for pf in (1.0, 0.95, 0.9):
    tanphi = np.tan(np.arccos(pf))
    P = np.linspace(0, 2, 20001)
    # receiving-end voltage: V^4 + (2QX - E^2)V^2 + X^2(P^2+Q^2) = 0, Q = P tan(phi)
    Q = P * tanphi

    Pn = P[ok][-1]; Vn = V_hi[-1]

5.3 Transient stability

Transient (large-disturbance rotor-angle) stability concerns whether synchronous machines remain in synchronism after a severe disturbance such as a short circuit on a transmission line40. The motion of a machine rotor is governed by the swing equation20,36

$$\frac{2H}{\omega_s}\frac{d^2\delta}{dt^2} = P_m - P_e(\delta) - D\frac{d\delta}{dt},\qquad P_e = P_{max}\sin\delta,$$

where $H$ is the inertia constant (s), $\omega_s$ synchronous speed (rad/s), $P_m$ mechanical input and $P_e$ electrical output (pu). For a single machine connected to an infinite bus (SMIB), the equal-area criterion gives stability without integrating: the kinetic energy gained while $P_m > P_e$ (area $A_1$) must be removed while $P_e > P_m$ (area $A_2$) before the angle reaches the post-fault unstable equilibrium $\delta_{max} = \pi - \sin^{-1}(P_m/P_{max,3})$1,20. Equating the areas for pre-, during- and post-fault amplitudes $P_{max,1}, P_{max,2}, P_{max,3}$ gives the critical clearing angle

$$\cos\delta_{cr} = \frac{P_m(\delta_{max} - \delta_0) + P_{max,3}\cos\delta_{max} - P_{max,2}\cos\delta_0}{P_{max,3} - P_{max,2}}.$$ 
"""Transient stability of a single-machine infinite-bus (SMIB) system:
equal-area criterion plus RK4 integration of the swing equation (NumPy only)."""
import numpy as np

f0, H, Pm = 50.0, 5.0, 0.8          # Hz, s, pu
ws = 2 * np.pi * f0
P1, P2, P3 = 1.8, 0.4, 1.3          # Pmax before / during / after the fault (pu)

d0 = np.arcsin(Pm / P1)             # pre-fault operating angle
dmax = np.pi - np.arcsin(Pm / P3)   # post-fault unstable equilibrium
cos_dc = (Pm*(dmax - d0) + P3*np.cos(dmax) - P2*np.cos(d0)) / (P3 - P2)
dc = np.arccos(cos_dc)              # critical clearing angle (equal-area criterion)
print(f"delta0 = {np.degrees(d0):.2f} deg, delta_max = {np.degrees(dmax):.2f} deg, "
      f"critical clearing angle = {np.degrees(dc):.2f} deg")

def f(x, Pmax):
    d, w = x                         # w = rotor speed deviation (rad/s)
    return np.array([w, ws / (2 * H) * (Pm - Pmax * np.sin(d))])

def simulate(tc, tend=2.0, h=1e-4):
    x, t, out = np.array([d0, 0.0]), 0.0, []
    while t < tend:
        Pmax = P2 if t < tc else P3
        k1 = f(x, Pmax); k2 = f(x + h/2*k1, Pmax)
        k3 = f(x + h/2*k2, Pmax); k4 = f(x + h*k3, Pmax)
        x = x + h/6 * (k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4); t += h
        out.append((t, x[0]))
    return np.array(out)

# critical clearing time = instant the sustained-fault trajectory reaches dc
traj = simulate(tc=10.0, tend=1.0)
tcc = traj[np.argmax(traj[:, 1] >= dc), 0]
print(f"critical clearing time = {tcc*1000:.0f} ms")
for tc in (0.20, 0.35):
    d = simulate(tc)[:, 1]
    verdict = "STABLE" if d.max() < dmax else "UNSTABLE"
    print(f"clear at {tc*1000:.0f} ms -> peak angle {np.degrees(d.max()):.1f} deg: {verdict}")

5.4 Small-signal stability

Small-signal (small-disturbance) rotor-angle stability is the ability to maintain synchronism under small perturbations, such as normal load changes; it is studied by linearising the system equations around an operating point, $\Delta\dot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf A\,\Delta\mathbf x$, and examining the eigenvalues $\lambda = \sigma \pm j\omega$ of the state matrix20,40. Each complex pair is an oscillatory mode of frequency $\omega/2\pi$ and damping ratio $\zeta = -\sigma/\sqrt{\sigma^2+\omega^2}$; any eigenvalue with positive real part signifies instability. Today, small-signal instability usually appears as insufficiently damped oscillations rather than as aperiodic drift: local modes of a plant swinging against the rest of the system (roughly 1–2 Hz), and inter-area modes of groups of machines swinging against each other over weak tie lines (roughly 0.1–0.7 Hz)20. Fast, high-gain excitation systems can reduce damping torque, which is the motivation for the power system stabiliser (PSS), a supplementary excitation control that adds damping torque in phase with speed deviation20,36.

For the classical SMIB model linearised about $\delta_0$, the synchronising power coefficient is $K_s = P_{max}\cos\delta_0$, the undamped natural frequency $\omega_n = \sqrt{\omega_s K_s/2H}$ and the damping ratio $\zeta = D/(2\sqrt{2H\omega_s K_s})$20.

"""Small-signal stability of the post-fault SMIB system (classical model)."""
import numpy as np
f0, H, Pm, Pmax, D = 50.0, 5.0, 0.8, 1.3, 1.0   # D in pu torque / pu speed
ws = 2 * np.pi * f0

Ks = 1.025 pu/rad, eigenvalues = [-0.05+5.674j -0.05-5.674j]

The 0.90 Hz mode (a local mode) has a damping ratio below 1%; utilities commonly seek at least a few percent damping for all modes, which here would require a PSS or other damping control20.

5.5 Reactive power compensation

Because voltage magnitude depends chiefly on reactive power flow and reactive power cannot be transmitted efficiently over long reactive lines, reactive power is best supplied locally20,22. A line operated below its SIL generates surplus reactive power (raising voltages, as in the Ferranti effect), and above SIL absorbs it1,22. Compensation devices include22,29:

Shunt reactorShuntAbsorbs line charging at light load; fixed or switched; standard on long EHV lines and cables
Shunt capacitor bankShuntCheap voltage support at heavy load; output falls with $V^2$, so support weakens as voltage falls

Synchronous condenserShuntRotating machine; continuous control, contributes inertia and short-circuit strength

Worked Example 8 — Compensation for the 400 kV line

In Example 2 the 700 MW load required a sending-end voltage of 456.5 kV. How much shunt capacitive compensation at the receiving end would bring $|V_S|$ down to 420 kV (1.05 pu) with 400 kV maintained at the load? With load reactive demand $700\tan(\cos^{-1}0.95) = 230$ Mvar, a bisection search on $Q_c$ using the ABCD equations gives $Q_c \approx 200$ Mvar. The compensation supplies most of the load's reactive demand locally, so the line carries mainly active power. At light load, the opposite problem arises (Ferranti rise to 421 kV at no load) and switched shunt reactors are needed instead1,22.

5.6 Congestion management

Congestion occurs when the economically preferred dispatch would violate a transmission limit (thermal, voltage or stability) under normal or N−1 contingency conditions3,4. Because power flows divide according to network impedances, a transaction between two points loads every parallel path, and limits cannot be managed by contract paths alone4. Approaches include4,47,48:

  • Nodal pricing (locational marginal pricing, LMP): a security-constrained economic dispatch prices energy at each bus at the marginal cost of serving one more megawatt there, accounting for losses and constraints48. Price differences between buses signal congestion and generate congestion rent.
  • Financial transmission rights (FTRs), which entitle holders to the congestion rent between two points and so hedge locational price risk47.
  • Zonal pricing and market splitting, with redispatch (counter-trading) of generation inside zones4.
  • Physical measures: phase-shifting transformers and FACTS devices to redirect flow, dynamic line rating, and transmission reinforcement27,29.

Worked Example 9 — Congestion and LMPs on a three-bus network

Three buses are connected in a triangle by identical lines. A cheap generator (marginal cost $20/MWh) at bus 1 and an expensive generator ($50/MWh) at bus 2 supply a 300 MW load at bus 3. Line 1–3 is limited to 150 MW. With identical reactances, the DC power flow distributes an injection at bus 1 withdrawn at bus 3 as 2/3 on the direct path and 1/3 via bus 2; similarly for bus 2. Hence $F_{13} = \tfrac23 G_1 + \tfrac13 G_2$3,44.

Unconstrained, $G_1 = 300$ MW gives $F_{13} = 200$ MW, violating the limit. Enforcing $F_{13} = 150$ with $G_1 + G_2 = 300$ gives $G_1 = G_2 = 150$ MW, at cost $10 500/h instead of $6000/h. LMPs: $\lambda_1 = 20$ and $\lambda_2 = 50$ $/MWh (the marginal units). To serve one more MW at bus 3 without increasing $F_{13}$ requires $\Delta G_1 = -1$, $\Delta G_2 = +2$, so $\lambda_3 = -20 + 100 = 80$ $/MWh, higher than either generator's cost, a well-known feature of meshed networks3,48. Loads pay 80 × 300 = $24 000/h and generators receive $10 500/h, leaving a congestion rent of $13 500/h, equal to $\sum F_{ij}(\lambda_j - \lambda_i) = 150(80-20) + 150(80-50)$, which funds FTRs47.

Exercise 1 — Why transmit at high voltage?

500 MW at unity power factor is to be transmitted over a three-phase line with resistance 10 Ω per phase. Compare $I^2R$ losses at 230 kV and at 500 kV.

Show solution

At 230 kV: $I = 500\times10^6/(\sqrt3\times230\times10^3) = 1255$ A; $P_{loss} = 3(1255)^2(10) = 47.3$ MW (9.5%). At 500 kV: $I = 577$ A; $P_{loss} = 10.0$ MW (2.0%). The ratio is $(500/230)^2 = 4.73$, as predicted by $P_{loss} = RS^2/V^2$ (Section 3.4).

Exercise 2 — Line parameters

A 60 Hz, 138 kV transposed line uses one Drake conductor per phase (GMR 1.143 cm, radius 1.407 cm) in a flat arrangement with 4 m between adjacent phases. Find $L$, $X$, $C$ and $B$ per km.

Show solution

$D_{eq} = \sqrt[3]{4\cdot4\cdot8} = 5.04$ m. $L = 2\times10^{-7}\ln(5.04/0.01143) = 1.218$ mH/km, $X = 2\pi(60)(1.218\times10^{-3}) = 0.459$ Ω/km. $C = 2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(5.04/0.01407) = 9.46$ nF/km, $B = 3.57\ \mu$S/km. Compare these with the bundled 400 kV line of Example 1 (0.268 Ω/km and 4.15 μS/km at 50 Hz): bundling and wider spacing lower $X$ and raise $B$.

Exercise 3 — Surge-impedance loading

A 765 kV line has characteristic impedance 260 Ω. Find its SIL, and use the St. Clair rule of thumb to estimate its loadability at 480 km.

Show solution

SIL $= 765^2/260 = 2251$ MW. At about 480 km loadability is roughly 1 SIL, so about 2.25 GW without compensation (Section 3.8).

Exercise 4 — Per-unit base change

A transformer nameplate gives 8% impedance on 50 MVA, 138 kV. Express this on a 100 MVA, 132 kV system base.

Show solution

$Z_{new} = 0.08\,(138/132)^2(100/50) = 0.175$ pu (Section 4.2).

Exercise 5 — Tap setting

A 400/132 kV transformer has an OLTC on its HV winding with steps of 1.25%. The HV voltage sags to 380 kV. Neglecting the transformer's internal drop, how many steps, and in which direction, restore 132 kV on the LV side?

Show solution

The required ratio is $380/132$, i.e. the HV tap must be reduced to $380/400 = 0.95$ of nominal turns, a change of −5%. That is $5/1.25 = 4$ steps toward fewer HV turns (Section 4.5).

Exercise 6 — Equal-area criterion with a bolted fault

A generator with $H = 5$ s, 50 Hz, delivers $P_m = 0.8$ pu with $P_{max} = 1.8$ pu before and after a three-phase fault at its terminals, during which $P_e = 0$. Find the critical clearing angle and time.

Show solution

$\delta_0 = \sin^{-1}(0.8/1.8) = 26.4^\circ$, $\delta_{max} = 180^\circ - 26.4^\circ = 153.6^\circ$. With $P_{max,2} = 0$ the formula gives $\cos\delta_{cr} = [P_m(\delta_{max}-\delta_0)]/P_{max} + \cos\delta_{max}$, so $\delta_{cr} = 84.8^\circ$. With $P_e = 0$ the swing equation integrates in closed form, $\delta = \delta_0 + (\omega_s P_m/4H)t^2$, so $t_{cr} = \sqrt{4H(\delta_{cr}-\delta_0)/(\omega_s P_m)} = \sqrt{4(5)(1.019)/(314.16\times0.8)} = 0.285$ s1,20.

Exercise 7 — Damping requirement

For the small-signal example of Section 5.4 ($K_s = 1.025$ pu/rad, $H = 5$ s, 50 Hz), what damping coefficient $D$ gives a damping ratio of 5%?

Show solution

$D = 2\zeta\sqrt{2H\omega_s K_s} = 2(0.05)\sqrt{10\times314.16\times1.025} = 5.67$ pu. Natural damping is far smaller, so a PSS would be used to provide it.

Exercise 8 — Reactive support in the power flow

Modify the Newton–Raphson program to add a shunt capacitor at bus 3 and find the smallest susceptance (in steps of 0.01 pu) that raises $|V_3|$ to at least 0.95 pu. What happens to losses?

Show solution

A shunt susceptance $b$ is added to the diagonal: Y[2, 2] += 1j * b. Looping $b$ from 0 in 0.01 pu steps, the first value meeting the limit is $b = 0.12$ pu, giving $|V_3| = 0.951$ pu and about 10.8 Mvar delivered ($bV^2$). Losses fall from 6.69 to 6.17 MW because less reactive power flows through the lines.

Exercise 9 — HVDC line losses

A 2000 MW bipolar HVDC line is 1000 km long with 0.01 Ω/km per pole. Compute line losses at ±500 kV and at ±800 kV.

Show solution

At ±500 kV the pole current is $I_d = 2000/(2\times500) = 2$ kA and losses are $2I_d^2R = 2(2000)^2(10) = 80$ MW (4.0%). At ±800 kV, $I_d = 1.25$ kA and losses are 31.3 MW (1.6%). Converter losses (typically under 1% per station for LCC) must be added for a full comparison13.

Exercise 10 — Interpreting DGA

An oil sample from a 400 kV transformer shows a sudden rise in acetylene (C2H2) together with hydrogen, and the Buchholz relay has alarmed. What type of fault is indicated and what action follows?

Show solution

Significant acetylene is the signature of high-energy electrical discharge (arcing), Duval zone D2, since acetylene forms only at very high temperatures38. Together with gas accumulation, this indicates an active internal fault; the unit should be removed from service for investigation (electrical diagnostics such as winding resistance, ratio and SFRA, then internal inspection) rather than left in service on alarm35,39.

Exercise 11 — Congestion

In Worked Example 9, the limit on line 1–3 is raised to 180 MW. Find the new dispatch and the LMP at bus 3.

Show solution

$\tfrac13 G_1 + 100 = 180$ gives $G_1 = 240$ MW and $G_2 = 60$ MW. The line is still binding, so the marginal adjustment is unchanged ($\Delta G_1 = -1$, $\Delta G_2 = +2$) and $\lambda_3$ remains $80/MWh; the dispatch cost falls to $7800/h. Only when the limit reaches 200 MW does congestion disappear and all three LMPs equal $20/MWh.
