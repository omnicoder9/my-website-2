SECTION 01The question this asks

Technology and engineering are not the same thing, and the difference is why this subject exists. Technology is everything humans make and use, and philosophers have been writing about it, under that description, since 1877.1 Engineering is a narrower thing: a body of trained practice in which particular people are answerable for particular artefacts, on a schedule, against a specification someone else wrote. The philosophy of technology asks what our making does to us. The philosophy of engineering asks what the makers are doing, and how they can possibly know they have done it right.

That second question turns out to have a shape. Take any matter genuinely in dispute inside an engineering project — whether the margin is enough, whether the model applies, whether the thing is finished, whether the trade-off is acceptable — and ask not who is correct but something blunter: what would have to happen for this to stop being a dispute? In some cases a derivation ends it. In some cases a test ends it. In a great many cases what ends it is a clause in a standard, or a signature, or the client saying which of two acceptable things they want. And in a residue of cases nothing ends it in advance at all: the matter stays open until the artefact meets the world and the world returns a verdict.

Call the kind of thing that ends a dispute its closure. This article is organised around a single claim: that the recognised problems of the philosophy of engineering — the demarcation from science, the nature of engineering knowledge, the status of design, the meaning of function, reasoning under uncertainty, the criterion of success, the entry of values, the obligation to the public — are not a list of separate topics. They are all disputes about closure. Ask of each what settles it, and the field reorganises itself.

The rung rule

The ladder is only useful if it can be applied against the person applying it, so the rule needs stating precisely. For each claim two sets are declared: the closures that operate on it in mainstream practice, and the subset of those without which it cannot be closed at all. The claim’s rung is the weakest closure in the second set.

The rule is deliberately unflattering. It records where a claim actually ends, not the most impressive kind of evidence that appears along the way. A finite-element result surrounded by mesh convergence studies, benchmark comparisons and a validated material model still ends at a signature, because somebody has to decide that the idealisation was the right one and no amount of further computation discharges that decision. On the ladder that claim sits at S2, not S5, and the mathematics on display is not evidence against the placement. It is the phenomenon being described.

What follows if the claim is right

Three things follow, and they are worth stating in advance so that the rest of the article can be checked against them.

First, the applied-science thesis becomes answerable rather than rhetorical. If engineering were applied science, its characteristic claims would close where scientific claims close: on demonstration and measurement. Whether they do is a question about practice, and it can be counted. Of the twenty-four claim-types coded here, two do.

Second, several long-running disagreements turn out to be disagreements about the same thing seen from two ends. Whether engineering is “really” optimisation or problem-solving or construction or creation is, on this reading, a question about which closure you have in mind. Optimisation is a claim that closes on demonstration once the objective is fixed; fixing the objective closes on the principal’s choice. Both descriptions are accurate, of different claims, and the argument between them has been an argument about emphasis.

Third, engineering ethics stops looking like a bolt-on. The reason that professional obligation became a distinct research area so much later than professional engineering itself — roughly the 1970s, against a discipline already a century into its institutional life2,3 — is not that engineers were previously careless. It is that the claims which carry moral weight are exactly the claims that close low on the ladder, at choice and at event, where the discipline’s prestigious instruments do not reach. A profession organised around demonstration will notice those claims last, because they do not look like engineering problems. They look like somebody else’s problem, until the somebody else turns out not to exist.

A note on what this article is not. It is not an argument that engineering is unrigorous. Nearly everything interesting here follows from premises engineers grant without hesitation: that specifications are finite, that test programmes are finite, that objectives conflict, that decisions are distributed across many people. The computational appendix in section 9 exists because the consequences of those concessions are considerably larger and sharper than the concessions sound. A discipline can be entirely rigorous and still have most of its questions end somewhere other than where its self-image says they do.

SECTION 02How engineering became a philosophical subject

Engineering is roughly five thousand years old. Sustained philosophical attention to it is about one hundred and fifty. Attention that took engineering, rather than technology, as its object is about twenty-five.

The ancient inheritance is a distinction and a slight. Aristotle separates techne, the productive knowledge of the craftsman, from episteme, knowledge of what cannot be otherwise, and separates poiesis, making, from praxis, doing.4 Techne is genuine knowledge — it has an account, it can be taught, it is not mere knack — but its objects are contingent and its end lies outside itself, in the product. That structure survived almost unexamined for two millennia, and it is still the structure of the intuition that engineering is where science gets used. Vitruvius supplies the other durable inheritance, a criterion rather than a category: a building must have firmitas, utilitas and venustas, strength, utility and delight, a triple standard that already concedes the point that success is plural and that not all of it is technical.5

The word itself arrives through the medieval Latin ingeniator, the contriver of engines, and stays military for centuries; the civil branch is named by contrast with the military one. John Smeaton is generally credited as the first person to call himself a civil engineer6, and the Institution of Civil Engineers, founded in a coffee house in 1818, acquired a royal charter in 1828.7,8 Its Council had resolved in December 1827 to ask Thomas Tredgold for a definition to put in the petition, and his answer is the first serious attempt to say what the thing is: the art of directing the great sources of power in nature for the use and convenience of man.7 Two features of that sentence repay attention. It calls engineering an art, not a science. And it makes the criterion of success external — use and convenience — which is to say that it locates closure outside the discipline in the very act of founding it.

The science-based century, and the reaction to it

The institutional model that produced the modern engineer was French and it was mathematical: the École des Ponts et Chaussées from 1747, the École Polytechnique from 1794, and the idea that practice should be deduced from mechanics.9 It worked well enough to make the applied-science picture look obvious, and badly enough to make its failures instructive. Thermodynamics runs the other way: Sadi Carnot's 1824 analysis of the motive power of heat was written about steam engines that already worked and generalised a practice that had no theory.10 Historians of technology have made this the standard counter-example. Science is at least as often the residue of engineering as its source.

Philosophical writing under the name arrives with Ernst Kapp’s Grundlinien einer Philosophie der Technik in 1877 and his thesis that tools are projections of human organs.1 Franz Reuleaux had already given the kinematics of machines a systematic treatment two years earlier.11 Pyotr Engelmeyer in Russia and Friedrich Dessauer in Germany continued the line, Dessauer arguing in 1927 that invention is a kind of contact with a realm of pre-existing possible solutions — a strong metaphysical claim that has aged badly but that at least took designing seriously as an epistemic act.12,13 Then the tradition turns, and for half a century the dominant philosophical writing about technology is critical and social rather than practical: Ortega, Heidegger, Ellul, Marcuse.14–17 Carl Mitcham’s Thinking Through Technology (1994) is the book that named the split, between an engineering philosophy of technology that starts inside the practice and a humanities philosophy of technology that starts outside it and asks what the practice is doing to us.2 Mitcham’s point was that the second had almost entirely displaced the first.

Engineering studies, and the recovery of the practice

The recovery came first from historians. Edwin Layton’s The Revolt of the Engineers (1971) documented the American profession’s attempt to constitute itself as a moral community, and in the same year his essay “Mirror-Image Twins” argued that the communities of science and technology are parallel and epistemologically equivalent rather than one being the application of the other.18,19 Three years later he pressed the stronger claim that technology is knowledge, not merely knowledge-applying.20 Mario Bunge had already tried the opposite tack, defending a sophisticated version of the applied-science thesis in which technology has its own substantive and operative theories and its own technological rules, which are neither true nor false but effective or not.21 Bunge’s rules are, in the vocabulary used here, an early recognition that some engineering claims close nowhere near demonstration.

Herbert Simon’s The Sciences of the Artificial (1969) supplied the other pillar: the artificial as an interface between an inner and an outer environment, design as the distinctive activity of the professional schools, and satisficing as what bounded agents actually do instead of optimising.22 Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber added, in 1973, the observation that planning problems are “wicked” — they have no stopping rule, no exhaustive list of admissible operations, and no test of correctness independent of the parties.23 That is a closure claim in all but name.

The engineering-authored literature of the 1970s and 1980s is where the subject acquires its readership. Samuel Florman’s The Existential Pleasures of Engineering (1976) is a defence of engineering as a source of meaning, written explicitly against the antitechnological criticism of the period, and it is still the best account of why people do this work.24 Henry Petroski’s To Engineer Is Human argues that failure is not the opposite of successful design but its epistemic engine: the discipline learns almost exclusively from things that broke, and its codes are sedimented accident reports.25 The book is frequently dated to 1982 in secondary sources and in at least one contemporary journal review; the first edition is St Martin’s Press, New York, 1985.

Walter Vincenti’s What Engineers Know and How They Know It (1990) is the pivot on which the modern field turns.26 Through five aeronautical case studies — airfoil design, flying-quality specifications, control-volume analysis, propeller testing, flush riveting — Vincenti argues that engineering knowledge is an autonomous body, identifiably different from the scientific knowledge with which it interacts, and he gives it a taxonomy: fundamental design concepts, criteria and specifications, theoretical tools, quantitative data, practical considerations, and design instrumentalities. He also imports Edward Constant’s distinction between normal and radical design, and Constant’s notion of a presumptive anomaly: the case in which theory says the established technology will fail in some future condition, before it has failed in any.27 Louis Bucciarelli’s Designing Engineers (1994) and Engineering Philosophy (2003) then did the ethnography, showing design as a social process in which participants inhabit distinct “object worlds” and the design is what their negotiation produces.28,29 Billy Vaughn Koen’s Discussion of the Method defines the engineering method as the use of heuristics, and defines a heuristic as something that provides plausible aid but is in the final analysis unjustified and potentially fallible.30 Eugene Ferguson’s Engineering and the Mind’s Eye makes the parallel case for nonverbal, visual thought.31

Ethics arrives late, and separately

Professional engineering ethics has its own chronology, and it is worth keeping distinct. Codes of ethics are old — the American societies were writing them from 1912 onward, and the paramountcy of public safety enters the American codes in that period18 — but ethics as a research field is a product of the 1970s. The National Science Foundation created its Ethics and Values in Science and Technology programme in 1972; Robert Baum and Albert Flores published the field’s first substantial anthology in 1978; Stephen Unger, who had led the IEEE’s investigation of the BART whistleblowers, published his own text and model code.32–34 Mike Martin and Roland Schinzinger’s Ethics in Engineering (1983) supplied the framing that has proved most durable: engineering as social experimentation, with the public as the subject population and informed consent as the missing ingredient.35,36 Michael Davis grounded professional obligation in the code as a kind of convention among practitioners, and Caroline Whitbeck argued that moral problems in engineering have the structure of design problems, with multiple acceptable resolutions rather than a solution.3,37,38 The Science and Engineering Ethics journal began in 1995. In the United States, accreditation only began requiring an understanding of professional and ethical responsibility in 2000.39

The empirical turn, and a field with its own name

The final movement is Dutch. The 2000 collection The Empirical Turn in the Philosophy of Technology argued that the field had to stop theorising about Technology with a capital T and start looking at what engineers do.40 At Delft this produced the dual-nature research programme: technical artefacts have both a physical structure and an intentional function, and neither description reduces to the other.41–43 From a small meeting at MIT in 2006 came the first Workshop on Philosophy and Engineering at Delft in October 2007, a second at the Royal Academy of Engineering in London in November 2008, and from 2010 the biennial Forum on Philosophy, Engineering and Technology, which met at the Colorado School of Mines, then Beijing, Virginia Tech, Erlangen-Nuremberg, Maryland, Chile, Delft and Karlsruhe; the 2026 meeting returned to the University of Maryland in June.44,45 Two handbooks now define the territory: volume 9 of the Elsevier Handbook of the Philosophy of Science, edited by Anthonie Meijers in 2009, and the Routledge Handbook of the Philosophy of Engineering, edited by Diane Michelfelder and Neelke Doorn in 2021, with fifty-five chapters across eight parts.46,47 Meijers opened his with a count from the Philosopher’s Index for 1940–2008: about 46,250 entries for “science”, about 1,250 for “technology”, about 450 for “engineering”, and sixteen for artifact and ontology together.46 The asymmetry has narrowed since, but it has not closed, and it is the best single measure of how recent the field is.

The field is not only Western. Li Bocong published an introduction to the philosophy of engineering in Chinese in 2002, and the Chinese Academy of Engineering has supported a research programme in which engineering, science and technology are treated as three distinct kinds of activity rather than stages of one.48 The Society for Philosophy and Technology, founded in 1976 and publisher of Techné since 1995, holds its 2027 conference in Beijing.49

SECTION 03The claims that face science

What distinguishes engineering from science, and is engineering primarily applied science? Both questions become tractable when you stop comparing the subject matters and start comparing what ends an argument.

The applied-science thesis has a respectable version and a lazy one. The lazy version says that engineers take results from science and use them. It is refuted by almost any episode in the history of technology, including the one that produced thermodynamics. The respectable version, argued most carefully by Bunge, concedes that technology has its own theories and its own rules but maintains that these are grounded in and controlled by scientific knowledge.21 Even the respectable version has a problem that shows up immediately in practice: the claims an engineer must actually close are not mostly of the form that scientific method closes.

Q03 is the claim on which every use of a model in design actually depends, and it does not close on demonstration or measurement. Whether a two-dimensional idealisation is adequate, whether the boundary condition is representative, whether the constitutive model may be used at this strain rate — these are settled by a qualified person deciding that they are, and the deciding is not a residual bit of hand-waving to be eliminated by better computation. It is the load-bearing step. Vincenti’s “design instrumentalities” and Ferguson’s nonverbal thought are both attempts to name the faculty that does it.26,31

Q04 is more radical and easier to overlook. The choice of problem is the largest single determinant of what gets built, and it closes on the principal’s choice and nothing else. No amount of engineering can establish that a problem was worth solving; engineering inherits the problem and cannot revisit it. Langdon Winner’s argument that artefacts have politics is often read as a claim about hidden design intentions, but its sharpest form is about this: the political content of a technology enters at the moment the problem is framed, upstream of every claim an engineer is professionally competent to close.56,57

The demarcation, stated as a closure claim. Science and engineering are not distinguished by subject matter, by method, by the use of mathematics, or by whether the object is natural or artificial. They are distinguished by the fact that a scientific claim is closed when the community accepts it as true, and an engineering claim is closed when a responsible party accepts it as adequate — and adequacy is a three-place relation involving a purpose that engineering did not choose. That is why the applied-science thesis is not so much false as beside the point. Where engineering does close on demonstration it is doing something science-like; those claims exist; they are two of the twenty-four here.

SECTION 04The claims that carry knowledge

What is engineering knowledge? The question has a good answer, which is Vincenti’s; what the closure ladder adds is an account of why so much of it is unwritable.

Vincenti’s taxonomy divides engineering design knowledge into six categories: fundamental design concepts, criteria and specifications, theoretical tools, quantitative data, practical considerations, and design instrumentalities.26 The taxonomy has held up for thirty-five years, and a recent collaboration between philosophers, engineers and design researchers has extended it by arguing that methods are themselves a form of engineering knowledge, carrying commitments about what counts as a well-formed problem.58 What the categories do not by themselves explain is why engineering knowledge is so persistently difficult to write down — a fact every practitioner reports and every knowledge management initiative rediscovers.

The closure reading gives the explanation. Knowledge that closes on codified rule is writable by construction: that is what a handbook allowable, a material standard or a design code is, and Davis Baird has argued that instruments and standards should be treated as bearers of knowledge in their own right rather than as mere conveniences.59,60 Knowledge that closes on accredited judgement is not writable, because what is being certified is a person’s capacity to judge in cases the writing did not anticipate. Michael Polanyi’s tacit knowing is the classical statement.61–63 Bucciarelli’s object worlds are the engineering-specific one: the structural engineer and the thermal engineer do not merely have different information, they have different objects, and the translation between them is performed by people rather than by documents.28

Q07 deserves separate emphasis because it is where the profession has changed most in fifty years. A modern design leans on simulation to a degree that would have been unrecognisable to Vincenti’s aeronautical engineers, and the verification-and-validation literature exists precisely because the tool closes nothing by itself. Verification asks whether the equations were solved correctly; validation asks whether they were the right equations; and only the second bears on the design, and only a person can perform it.64,65 The characteristic modern failure is not a bad mesh. It is a good mesh treated as a closure.

SECTION 05The claims that constitute a design

What is a design? What is a function? What counts as a successful design, and is engineering fundamentally optimisation? These are the questions on which the closure reading pays the largest dividend, because all four turn out to bottom out in the same place.

Start with the ontology, since the rest depends on it. A design is not an artefact and not a description of one; it is closer to a set of constraints that some artefacts satisfy. Peter Kroes and Anthonie Meijers’ dual-nature thesis holds that a technical artefact is individuated by both a physical structure and an intentional function, that neither is reducible to the other, and that design is precisely the activity that binds them.41,42 Michael Jackson and Pamela Zave gave the crispest formal statement of what a design has to do, in requirements engineering: the specification S together with the domain assumptions D must entail the requirements R.68–70 The formula is exact and its interest lies in what it exposes. D is where the world enters, and D is never complete.

The completeness of a specification is not a provable property, and the consequences are measurable. In a space of 4,096 candidate designs, imposing requirements of the commonest realistic shape prunes hard: twelve requirements cut the space to a hundred candidates, twenty-five to ten. But the space empties after about twenty-four and a half requirements on average, with roughly nine designs still standing on the step before, and the window in which exactly one design is admissible is 0.64 requirements wide — under three per cent of the specification’s life.

A specification is therefore almost never read at the moment it determines a design, because for nearly all of its existence it is satisfied by many designs and then by none. Which of the many gets built is settled somewhere the specification cannot see. This is what Bucciarelli found ethnographically and what Rittel and Webber asserted about planning: the design is the output of a negotiation among people, and the requirements document is one of the things they negotiate with, not the thing that decides.23,28

Function is the hardest concept in the field and it has the largest literature. In philosophy of biology the two established accounts are Larry Wright’s etiological analysis, on which a function is an effect that explains why the item is there, and Robert Cummins’ causal-role analysis, on which a function is a contribution to a containing system’s capacity.71–73 Neither transfers cleanly to artefacts. The etiological account struggles with the first specimen of a new design, which has a function and no history; the causal-role account struggles with malfunction, since a broken part contributes nothing and would therefore have no function. Wybo Houkes and Pieter Vermaas’ ICE account resolves this by grounding artefact function in use plans: a function is ascribed within a plan of action that an agent has, and the ascription is justified by a combination of intentional, causal-role and evolutionist considerations.74,75 Beth Preston has argued for pluralism, on the ground that artefacts have both proper functions grounded in reproduction and system functions grounded in current use, and that neither can be eliminated.76–78

What the closure ladder adds is small but clarifying. Whichever analysis is right, the function of a particular component in a particular design is fixed by somebody’s plan for it, and the plan belongs to the principal. This is why reuse, misuse and accidental function are not marginal cases: they are what happens when the plan changes hands.

Q14 deserves a note of its own, because the claim that a design is finished is the one the discipline has most recently begun to doubt. Mark Thomas Young has argued that the philosophical literature’s focus on design has obscured maintenance, which is where most of an artefact’s existence is actually spent and where most of the decisions that determine its behaviour are actually made.79 On the closure reading the point is sharp: handover closes the contract, not the design, and every claim in group C is reopened by the first modification.

Which brings us to the question of whether engineering is fundamentally about optimisation. Maarten Franssen has shown that the design-as-optimisation picture runs into a formal wall: aggregating multiple criteria into a single ranking imports all the difficulties of social choice theory, and there is no value-neutral way to do it.80–82 The wall is easy to see by counting.

Optimisation is a filter, and a weak one once the objectives are realistic. It removes designs that are worse on everything, which is worth doing, and then it stops. Everything remaining is a matter of what you weight, and the weights are not engineering facts. Simon’s satisficing is the honest description of what practitioners do instead: search until something clears the aspiration level, then stop.22 The stopping is closure at the principal’s choice wearing a technical costume.

SECTION 06The claims that manage uncertainty

How do engineers reason under uncertainty, and what counts as a successful design? The first question has a well-developed literature. The second has an answer most of the profession would rather not give.

Engineering has two families of technique for uncertainty and a long argument about their relation. The older family is the safety factor: multiply the expected load, or divide the expected strength, by a number written into a code. Numerical safety factors are a nineteenth-century invention, though the practice of adding strength is ancient.83 The younger family is probabilistic risk assessment, which calibrates margins to a target failure probability. Neelke Doorn and Sven Ove Hansson examined whether the second should replace the first and concluded that it should not: the approaches aim at different things, and safety factors remain indispensable for hazards that cannot be assigned meaningful probabilities at all.83,84 Hansson’s account of safe design names four strategies — inherently safe design, safety factors, negative feedback and multiple barriers — of which only the second is quantitative in the way the debate assumes.85,86

The limits on testing are equally arithmetic and less widely internalised. If a programme runs n independent trials and none fails, the exact ninety-five per cent upper bound on the per-trial failure rate is 1 − 0.051/n, approximately 3/n — the rule of three, given its canonical statement by Hanley and Lippman-Hand under the title “If nothing goes wrong, is everything all right?”89

No programme runs three billion trials. The rare-event claim is therefore never closed by measurement, whatever its presentation suggests. It is closed by a code that declares an analytical route acceptable, and by a signature that accepts the analysis. This is not a scandal — there is no alternative — but it does mean that a number of the form “probability of catastrophic failure less than 10−9 per flight hour” is a statement about a procedure having been followed, not a measurement. Confusing the two is the characteristic pathology, and the Boeing 737 MAX gives the textbook instance: the House Committee’s 2020 report found that the manoeuvring characteristics augmentation system was vulnerable to the failure of a single angle-of-attack sensor, that it did not meet its own design requirements, and that an internal document had already classified a slow crew response — defined as longer than ten seconds — as catastrophic.90 The hazard analysis had been performed. It closed at a signature, and the signature was inside the organisation whose schedule the analysis was constraining.91 Charles Perrow’s normal-accident theory and Diane Vaughan’s reconstruction of the Challenger launch decision describe the organisational mechanism by which such a signature comes to be given, and Nancy Leveson’s systems-theoretic accident model is the engineering response to it.92–94

SECTION 07The claims that carry values

How do values enter engineering decisions? Not alongside the technical work, and not as a constraint applied afterwards. They enter through the specification, and they enter as numbers whose derivation is not a derivation.

The design-for-values research programme is the most developed answer in the field, and it has a genuinely useful analytical device. Ibo van de Poel’s value hierarchy has three layers: values at the top, norms in the middle, and design requirements at the bottom. Values are too abstract to apply; norms are properties or capabilities the artefact should have; requirements are specific and checkable. Van de Poel is explicit that the relation between the layers is not deductive — it is a translation, performed top-down by specification or reconstructed bottom-up by asking what a requirement was for.97 The wider programme runs from Batya Friedman’s value sensitive design through Helen Nissenbaum’s work on values in design to the Handbook of Ethics, Values, and Technological Design.98–101

Both are social, and neither is the designer’s to declare.

SECTION 08The claims that carry obligation

What obligations does an engineer have to society? The codes give a clear answer. The interesting question is what the codes can actually reach, and the answer is: not the claims that matter most.

The first fundamental canon of the National Society of Professional Engineers code requires engineers to hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public.107 The paramountcy language dates from the reform period that Layton documented, when the American societies were attempting to constitute engineering as a profession with obligations running past the employer;18 the code in its current architecture was adopted in 1964, and its Board of Ethical Review has published more than six hundred opinions since the late 1950s.107,108 The American Society of Civil Engineers and the IEEE maintain parallel instruments.109,110 Michael Davis has argued that a code of this kind is best understood not as a summary of independent moral truths but as a convention that solves a coordination problem among practitioners: it makes it rational for each engineer to bear the cost of doing the right thing, because the others are bound too.3,111,112

The last claim is the one on which the philosophical literature has done the most careful recent work. The problem of many hands, analysed at length by Ibo van de Poel, Lambèr Royakkers and Sjoerd Zwart, is the situation in which a collective causes a harm and no individual within the collective satisfies the ordinary conditions for being responsible for it.116–118 It is not a problem of concealment. It arises when every contribution is individually defensible.

The shape of the result matters more than its magnitude. As the threshold rises — that is, as the system is given more margin — harm becomes rarer but the harms that do occur are more likely to have an identifiable pivotal actor. Where margin is thin, failures are common and unattributable. A profession whose accountability machinery is built around identifying the responsible individual will therefore perform worst precisely where it is needed most, and will tend to locate responsibility after the event, by whichever mechanism is available. In practice that mechanism is litigation, which is closure at S0 with extra steps.

Caroline Whitbeck’s response to this remains the most constructive in the literature: treat ethical problems in engineering as design problems. They have multiple acceptable resolutions rather than a solution, they are constrained rather than determined, and the skill they call for is the skill of generating options that satisfy more of the constraints — not the skill of selecting between two prefabricated horns.38 That is also the only approach that engages the closure structure honestly, because it does not pretend that a claim ending at S0 can be made to end at S5 by thinking harder about it.

SECTION 09What the coded matrix implies

Twenty-four claim-types, six closure kinds, and eight deterministic experiments. Every number quoted anywhere in this article comes from the run reproduced at the end of this section.

The matrix is a declaration and it can be disputed. Each claim carries two sets: the closures that operate on it, and the subset without which it cannot be closed at all. The rung is the weakest member of the second set. Reasonable people will move individual claims, and the ledger in section 11 is laid out so that they can.

Two of the twenty-four claims end at demonstration or measurement: 8.3 per cent. Twenty end at accredited judgement or below: 83.3 per cent. Five — whether experience transfers, whether the design succeeded, whether the artefact is politically neutral, whether the public interest was served, and where responsibility lies — cannot be closed in advance at all.

The more informative statistic is the gap between the strongest closure that participates in a claim and the closure that actually ends it.

That gap is the article’s central empirical observation about its own subject. It is not that engineering lacks rigorous instruments; the instruments are everywhere, and the hatched cells in Figure 11 record them. It is that on ten of twenty-four claims the instruments on display are three or more rungs stronger than the thing that ends the argument. A reader of an engineering document who takes the visible evidence as the closure will be systematically wrong about what the document has established, and wrong in a consistent direction.

Two further results from the first experiment are worth recording. Only 18 of the 64 possible operating profiles are occupied, and no closure kind is redundant: with all six kinds, ten of the 276 pairs of claims are indistinguishable, and dropping any single kind raises that to at least thirteen, so each kind does separating work the others cannot do. And the correlation between the number of closure kinds that operate on a claim and the rung at which it ends is −0.192 — close to nothing, and slightly negative. Weight of evidence and finality are nearly unrelated.

SECTION 10The neighbouring disciplines

The philosophy of engineering has porous borders with at least seven adjacent fields. Locating them relative to closure is more useful than listing them.

Philosophy of technology is the parent, and the relation is the one Mitcham described: the humanities tradition asks what technology does to human life, and the engineering tradition asks what the practice is.2 Don Ihde’s postphenomenology, which analyses the ways artefacts mediate perception and action, and Peter-Paul Verbeek’s development of it into an account of technological mediation as moral, are the most active strands of the humanities line today.119–122 Joseph Pitt has argued from the other side that philosophy of technology should be an epistemology of “humanity at work”, which is close to the position taken here.123 The parent field’s journals — Techné from 1995, Philosophy & Technology from 2011 — carry a good deal of engineering-specific work.

Science and technology studies supplies the empirical discipline the philosophical field would otherwise lack. The social construction of technology, Thomas Hughes’ large technical systems with their reverse salients and technological momentum, and Latour’s actor-network account are the three canonical programmes.95,104,106,124,125 The relationship has been uneasy, because STS is often read as denying that technical constraints do any work. On the closure reading the two are compatible and complementary: STS is largely the study of what happens at S1 and S0, which is where most engineering claims end, and it has therefore been describing the majority of the phenomenon while philosophers were describing the minority.

Design research is the closest cousin and the most under-read by philosophers. Nigel Cross’ account of “designerly ways of knowing” argues for a third culture alongside the sciences and the humanities, with its own objects and its own validity criteria;126 Donald Schön’s reflective practitioner describes design as a reflective conversation with the materials of a situation;127 the German systematic-design tradition of Pahl and Beitz supplies the prescriptive counterweight.128–130 The journal Design Science now carries philosophically serious work on engineering knowledge.58

Engineering education research matters more than its reputation suggests, because accreditation criteria are where philosophical positions become binding on practice. When ABET began requiring an understanding of professional and ethical responsibility in 2000, a claim about what an engineer must be able to do acquired an S3 closure.39 John Heywood has argued at length for a philosophy of engineering education as a distinct enterprise.131,132

Systems engineering has become, in effect, a body of applied philosophy of engineering conducted by people who do not call it that: its central preoccupations are requirements, verification, validation, emergence and the management of interfaces, which map almost exactly onto Q10, Q11 and Q07 here. Peter Simons has written on emergence in engineering for the Routledge handbook, and the field’s own literature on the distinction between verification and validation is philosophically more careful than most philosophical literature on the same topic.47,64

Philosophy of computer science is the fastest-growing neighbour and the one where the closure question is most acute. A program is an artefact with a formal specification, so Q11 — does the design satisfy the specification — genuinely can close on demonstration, which is what program verification is. Raymond Turner’s work on computational artefacts develops the point that computer science has both a mathematical and an engineering face, and that the specification is the hinge between them.133–135 The interesting cases are the ones where the hinge fails: machine-learned components have no specification of the kind verification needs, which pushes claims that used to close at S5 back down to S4 and S2, and the current regulatory difficulty around them is a direct consequence.

Applied ethics and risk analysis complete the circle. Hansson’s work on the epistemology of technological risk, Shrader-Frechette on risk assessment methodology, and the design-for-values programme are all, in the vocabulary used here, attempts to give better instruments to claims that close at S1 and S0.51,100,102

Where the field is now. The infrastructure exists: two handbooks, a biennial forum that met at Maryland in June 2026, a Springer book series, three journals that regularly publish the work, and an active Chinese tradition.44,47,48 The unfinished business is mostly empirical. The claims coded here were coded from the literature and from practice as reported in it; nobody has yet done the study that codes them from observation, across disciplines and jurisdictions, in the way Bucciarelli observed design. The prediction the closure reading makes is that the distribution differs sharply between industries with artefact-level regulatory approval and industries without — that aerospace and medical devices have more claims resting on S3 and fewer on S2 than software or consumer products — and that is a checkable claim rather than a philosophical one.

The canonical questions, relocated

The questions this field is usually introduced through are not abandoned by the closure reading; they are given addresses.

Canonical questionWhere it lands
What distinguishes engineering from science? Not subject matter or method. A scientific claim closes when the community accepts it as true; an engineering claim closes when a responsible party accepts it as adequate to a purpose engineering did not set. Q01–Q04
Is engineering primarily applied science? Only for the claims that close at S5 and S4, which are two of twenty-four. The thesis is not false so much as true of a small minority. Q01 Q02
What is engineering knowledge? Vincenti’s six categories, sorted by closure: the writable parts are the ones that close at S3, the unwritable parts are the ones that close at S2. Q05–Q09
What is a design? A binding of physical structure to intended function that some artefacts satisfy. Not entailed by the specification: the uniqueness window is 2.6 per cent of a specification’s life. Q10, Q14
What is a function? Ascribed within a use plan and therefore owned by the principal, whichever of the etiological, causal-role or ICE analyses is adopted. Q12
How do engineers reason under uncertainty? With two families of instrument that close differently: safety factors at codified rule, probabilistic assessment at measurement plus signature. Q15 Q16–Q17
What counts as a successful design? Service life, and nothing available at handover. This is the one claim about which Petroski and the closure ladder agree completely. Q18
How do values enter engineering decisions? Through specification, as numbers whose derivation is a translation rather than a deduction, and through budgets that select conceptions without anyone choosing one. Q19–Q21
What obligations does an engineer have to society? Those the codes state; the difficulty is that the claims carrying the obligation close at S1 and S0, where the profession’s machinery does not reach. Q22–Q24
Is engineering optimisation, construction, problem-solving or creation? All four, of different claims. Optimisation closes at S5 once an objective is fixed; fixing the objective closes at S1; construction closes at S4 and S3; creation is the part that closes nowhere until it has been used. Q04, Q13

What would falsify this

A framework that cannot be wrong is not worth the trouble of building, so the conditions are worth stating.

The coding is wrong if the rung rule cannot be applied consistently by someone else. The rule is that the rung is the weakest closure the claim cannot be closed without, judged for an artefact actually entering service under current mainstream practice. If two competent readers applying that rule to the same twenty-four claims disagree on more than a handful, the instrument is too soft to carry the conclusion.

The thesis is wrong if the distribution is an artefact of which claims were chosen. Twenty-four claim-types is a sample, and it was assembled to span the field’s recognised problems rather than to span engineering practice. A sample drawn instead from, say, the verification activities in a single avionics programme would sit much higher on the ladder. The defence is that the claims here are the ones the philosophical literature actually argues about; the concession is that this is a claim about the literature’s subject matter as much as about engineering.

The thesis is wrong, more interestingly, if closure at S1 and S0 turns out to be a feature of any complex professional practice rather than of engineering specifically. Medicine, law and architecture would all show low rungs on their characteristic claims. The engineering-specific part of the argument is therefore not that the rungs are low, but that engineering is the profession whose self-image is furthest from its rungs — the one whose documents display S5 evidence on S1 claims most consistently, because its prestige is staked on demonstration. The mean gap of 2.08 rungs is the quantity that carries this, and it is the quantity a critic should attack.

The point of asking

An engineer who has read this should not conclude that the instruments are worthless. They should conclude something narrower and more useful: that on any given claim it is worth knowing which rung is bearing the weight, because the characteristic engineering failure is not a bad calculation. It is a good calculation asked to close a claim it cannot reach — a factor of safety read as a probability, a verified program taken as a fit one, a test programme reporting a rate it never measured, a code compliance reported as a public interest.

And a philosopher who has read this should notice that the field’s distinctive problems are not distributed randomly across its topics. They cluster at the bottom of the ladder, in the claims about problems worth solving, specifications worth writing, trade-offs worth accepting and responsibility worth assigning. Those are the claims that engineering must close and has the fewest instruments for. They are also, not coincidentally, the ones where a philosopher can still be of use.
