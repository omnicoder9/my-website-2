The central question

How can something made entirely of ordinary physical or informational constituents possess properties such as being correct, defective, compliant, redundant, obsolete, misused, or fit for purpose — properties that cannot be read off its material composition?

Physics has no predicate for defective. Engineering cannot do without one. What has to be true of the world for both of those facts to hold at once?

A metallurgist can give a complete account of a cracked turbine blade. Composition, grain structure, residual stress, the crack's origin at a subsurface inclusion, the propagation history written in the striations, the moment of separation. Nowhere in that account will the word defective appear, and nothing in the account entails it. Yet the blade is defective, and the fact that it is defective is not a mood anyone is in. It grounds a recall, an airworthiness directive, a redesign, a liability, and a change to a specification that will shape blades not yet forged. Engineering is full of predicates like this — correct, malfunctioning, compliant, redundant, obsolete, misused, fit for purpose — that are not physical predicates and are not merely subjective either. They are answerable to physical facts without being reducible to them.

This article proposes foundations for a metaphysics of engineering: an account of what engineered things are, what makes them the kinds of things they are, how their parts and functions relate, and how intentional and normative concepts connect to physical reality. The proposal takes the form of thirty provisional axioms across eight strata, each tested against a case that engineering practice has already decided; five cross-cutting meta-principles; a relational model; and a machine-checked conformance suite that runs five candidate ontologies against twelve verdicts that competent practice renders without hesitation. The suite is reproduced in full, and every figure quoted below comes from its output.

The thesis defended is this. Engineered entities cannot be adequately understood in terms of matter and causation alone, because their identities are constituted partly through organisation, modal capacities, functional roles, intentional histories, requirements, and normative standards. That is not a mystical claim, and it does not require anything over and above matter to exist. It is a claim about what an adequate individuation of engineered things has to quantify over, and the argument for it is that every candidate that quantifies over less gets the easy cases wrong.

MOE-100The predicate problem

Begin with a contrast that is easy to state and hard to dissolve. Consider two descriptions of the same brake caliper. The first is exhaustively physical: an alloy of specified composition, machined to specified tolerances, subject at each instant to a determinate stress tensor, converting kinetic energy into heat at a rate fixed by friction coefficients and contact pressure. Every fact in that description is a fact physics recognises. The second description is the one the engineer actually uses: this caliper ought to arrest the vehicle within a stated distance under stated conditions; it is within or outside its service limits; its pad wear sensor is redundant with the hydraulic pressure monitor; this variant is obsolete; that one is non-compliant with a regulation that took effect last year.

The second description is not a gloss on the first. It cannot be derived from it, because nothing in the stress tensor says what the caliper is for. It is not independent of it either, because a caliper that cannot generate the torque is not merely disapproved of — it fails. Peter Kroes and Anthonie Meijers named this the dual nature of technical artefacts and made it the organising problem of a decade of work: artefacts have a physical nature and an intentional nature, the two descriptions are not translatable into each other, and no account that keeps only one of them is recognisable as an account of engineering.1 Maarten Franssen sharpened the point by asking what kind of fact a malfunction is, and concluding that judgements of the form this is a good X and this X is broken carry a normative force that no purely descriptive rendering preserves.2

The predicate problem is not a curiosity at the edge of the discipline. It sits at the centre of the activities that consume most engineering effort. Verification asks whether an artefact conforms to a specification. Certification asks whether an organisation may attest that it does. Failure analysis asks which of a system's many behaviours was the one it was not supposed to have. Configuration management asks which artefact, among several physically similar ones, is the artefact whose history the records describe. Every one of these is a question about a property that is not in the stress tensor.

Engineering's characteristic predicates are neither physical nor optional. An ontology that cannot state them cannot state what engineers spend their days doing.

Note also that the problem is not confined to hardware. A distributed service that returns correct answers slowly may be in violation of a service-level objective and therefore failing, though every instruction it executes is the instruction its source code specifies. A cryptographic library that implements its algorithm perfectly may be deprecated, a status that describes nothing about its execution and everything about the standing of the algorithm in a community of practice. Raymond Turner's study of computational artefacts turns on exactly this: a specification does not describe a program, it governs one, so that a mismatch between them is always a fault in something, and deciding which thing is faulty is not a computational question.3

MOE-110What the question is, and what it is not

Three neighbouring inquiries should be set aside at the start, not because they are unimportant but because conflating them with this one guarantees confusion.

The first is the ethics of technology. Whether a technology ought to exist, who bears its risks, how values enter design — these are live and urgent questions, and Langdon Winner's argument that artefacts can embody political arrangements has organised much of the field since 1980.4 But the question of whether a bridge ought to have been built is downstream of the question of what a bridge is, and the second question turns out to be the harder one.

The second is the epistemology of engineering. Walter Vincenti's studies of aeronautical practice, and Billy Vaughn Koen's account of engineering as the use of heuristics under uncertainty, are about how engineers know what they know.5 That is a different question from what there is for them to know about. The two intersect — some ontological commitments are visible only in how engineers reason — but the questions come apart cleanly enough to be pursued separately.

The third is engineering methodology. Systems engineering has a large and mature literature on how to decompose systems, elicit requirements, and manage interfaces, codified in standards such as ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 and the INCOSE handbook.6 That literature is indispensable evidence for the present inquiry, but it is evidence rather than answer: it tells us what practitioners do, and one of the things it shows is that practitioners take positions on ontological questions without noticing that they are doing so. When a standard defines a system of interest relative to a stated purpose, it has committed itself on the question of whether system boundaries are observer-relative. When a configuration management process treats a serial-numbered assembly as the same item after overhaul, it has committed itself on persistence. The methodology contains an implicit metaphysics, and making it explicit is the work.

What remains is the ontological question proper, and it can be put in one sentence: what makes a collection of matter, information, relations, behaviours, intentions, and norms constitute one engineered thing of a particular kind? An adequate answer must simultaneously explain what the thing is made of, how it is organised, what it does, what it can do, what it is supposed to do, why it was created, what standards govern it, and what makes it remain the same thing through change. No existing account does all eight.

The exposition below proceeds through eight strata, from purpose down to realisation. The order is expository rather than foundational. Design reads the chain downward, from a purpose through requirements to a structure that might satisfy them; diagnosis reads it upward, from a behaviour observed to the component that produced it and the requirement it violated. Neither direction is a reduction, and, as §16 argues, the chain is better drawn as a network with no source.

MOE-200The neighbourhood: a survey

Nothing in what follows is unprecedented. Every stratum of the problem has been worked on, often with great care, by a field that treats it as central and the adjacent strata as someone else's business. The purpose of this survey is to show where the coverage is dense, where it is thin, and where the seams fail to meet.

Two philosophies of technology

Carl Mitcham's history of the field distinguishes two traditions that barely spoke to one another for most of a century. Engineering philosophy of technology, running from Ernst Kapp through Friedrich Dessauer, took technology as its subject from the inside and asked what technical making is. Humanities philosophy of technology, running through Lewis Mumford, José Ortega y Gasset, Martin Heidegger, and Jacques Ellul, asked what technology means for human life, and generally answered at the level of technology-as-such.7 Heidegger's account of modern technology as a mode of disclosure that reveals nature as a stock of resources on call is the most influential single text in that second line.8 It is also, for present purposes, unusable: it says nothing about why the third flight computer is a component and the dust grain beside it is not.

Two developments changed this. The first was Gilbert Simondon's study of the mode of existence of technical objects, which took the individual technical object as a serious ontological subject and described its development as a process of concretisation, in which a design's separately functioning parts converge on parts that discharge several functions at once.9 That is a genuinely engineering-facing thesis about structure and function, and it long predates the analytic literature that rediscovered the territory. The second was the empirical turn of the 1990s, which redirected attention from technology in general to particular artefacts, practices, and design processes.10 Postphenomenology, in Don Ihde's and Peter-Paul Verbeek's development, took up the question of how particular artefacts mediate perception and action, which is an ontology of the human–artefact relation rather than of the artefact.11 The handbook volumes edited by Anthonie Meijers and, more recently, by Diane Michelfelder and Neelke Doorn, map the resulting field.12

Science and technology studies contributed a further pressure. The social construction of technological systems programme, and Bruno Latour's insistence that artefacts are participants in action rather than passive means, both argue that the boundary between the technical and the social is drawn rather than found.13 That claim is compatible with the present programme and will be revisited under the objections, but it does not by itself individuate anything.

The analytic metaphysics of artefacts

Analytic metaphysics arrived at artefacts by way of a puzzle about kinds. Randall Dipert's book set out a hierarchy in which an instrument becomes an artefact when an agent intends that its intentional modification be recognised as such.14 Risto Hilpinen developed the idea that artefacts are author-dependent in a way that natural objects are not: an object is an artefact of kind K in virtue of somebody having made it with the intention that it be a K, so that the maker's intention is partly constitutive rather than merely causal.15 Amie Thomasson gave the strongest version, arguing that artefactual kinds are mind-dependent in a substantive sense: for an object to be a table, its maker must have had a substantially correct concept of what a table is and have intended to realise it.16 Lynne Rudder Baker approached the same terrain through her constitution view, on which an artefact is constituted by, but not identical with, an aggregate of matter, and possesses intention-dependent properties that the aggregate lacks.17

Against all of this stands Peter van Inwagen's eliminativism. On his view the only composite objects are living things; there are no chairs, only simples arranged chairwise.18 Crawford Elder's realism about familiar objects is the principal reply, arguing that ordinary artefacts have real natures fixed by copied kinds and historically proper placements.19 The volume edited by Eric Margolis and Stephen Laurence collects the state of that debate.20

This literature is indispensable and consistently under-scaled. Its paradigm cases are chairs, hammers, and statues: middle-sized, unitary, materially continuous, made by an individual, functioning or not. It has almost nothing to say about a system whose components are distributed across three continents, about a requirement that constrains an artefact not yet built, about an interface violation, or about a certificate.

Theories of function

The philosophy of function is the most developed sub-literature and the one from which engineering can take the most. Larry Wright's etiological account made a thing's function a matter of why it is there: an item has the function of doing F if its doing F explains its presence.21 Robert Cummins offered the rival systemic account, on which a function is a capacity that contributes to a containing system's capacity, relative to an analysis someone gives.22 Ruth Millikan's proper functions grounded normativity in reproductive history.23 Beth Preston argued that no single account covers the phenomena and defended a pluralism combining proper and system functions, which handles the standing engineering fact that things get used for what they were not made for.24

Pieter Vermaas and Wybo Houkes then showed that etiological accounts transfer badly to technical artefacts, since a novel prototype has a function on its first day, before any reproductive history exists.25 Their positive proposal, the ICE theory, grounds function ascription jointly in the intentions of designers and users, the causal capacities of the object, and the way both are transmitted through use plans and evolutionary selection.26 Houkes and Meijers set out what they called the hard problem for artefact ontology: how the intentional and structural descriptions can be jointly true of one object without one reducing to the other.27 John Searle's account of status functions, imposed on objects by collective acceptance, supplies the institutional layer that the engineering literature needs and rarely names.28

Mechanism, level, and decomposition

The philosophy of biology and neuroscience solved several problems that engineering shares. Herbert Simon's argument that complex systems are nearly decomposable — that interactions cluster into subsystems with weak coupling between them — explains why hierarchical decomposition works at all, and why it is not arbitrary even though several decompositions are available.29 William Bechtel and Robert Richardson analysed decomposition and localisation as research strategies with characteristic failure modes.30 Peter Machamer, Lindley Darden, and Carl Craver's account of mechanisms as entities and activities organised so as to produce regular changes gives an ontology in which organisation is a first-class constituent rather than an afterthought.31 Craver's mutual manipulability criterion — roughly, that x is a component of the mechanism for Φ when interventions on x change Φ and interventions on Φ change x — is the closest thing in the literature to a workable test for componenthood, and it will be adapted below.32

Formal mereology supplies the other half. Peter Simons's study and Achille Varzi's survey set out the classical theory of parts, and also make plain its limitation for present purposes: classical extensional mereology is insensitive to arrangement, so a disassembled engine and an assembled one have the same parts.33 Kit Fine's theory of rigid and variable embodiments, which treats an object as a pair of a matter and a principle by which the matter is arranged, is the natural repair and maps unusually well onto engineering structure.34

Design theory and applied formal ontology

Design research produced its own ontology under its own name. John Gero's function–behaviour–structure schema, and the later situated version developed with Udo Kannengiesser, model designing as a set of transformations among expected behaviour, structure, and the behaviour derived from structure, with function ascribed to expected behaviour.35 Balakrishnan Chandrasekaran and John Josephson analysed the several senses in which devices are said to have functions and how each supports a different kind of reasoning.36 Yoshinobu Kitamura and Riichiro Mizoguchi built a functional ontology intended to make design knowledge systematically reusable across domains.37

Applied formal ontology brought analytic rigour to the same material. Stefano Borgo, Massimiliano Carrara, Paweł Garbacz, and Pieter Vermaas produced a formal treatment of artefact behaviour and function within a foundational ontology, and Borgo and Vieu surveyed how artefacts fare in formal ontologies generally.38 The wider tradition, from Nicola Guarino's programme for formal ontology in information systems to the Basic Formal Ontology of Barry Smith and colleagues, supplies the machinery of continuants, occurrents, qualities, and roles that any formalisation of the present axioms would use.39 Meanwhile the architecture description standard ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010 codifies, as an international standard, the thesis that a system admits multiple valid descriptions relative to stakeholder concerns — perspective-dependence installed in normative practice.40

Requirements and computational artefacts

The single most precise result in this entire neighbourhood comes from requirements engineering. Pamela Zave and Michael Jackson showed that requirements are statements about the environment, that specifications are statements about phenomena shared between the environment and the machine, and that the two are connected only by domain knowledge, so that correctness is the entailment of the requirement by the specification together with the domain assumptions.41 That is an ontological claim of unusual sharpness, and it is testable, as the lab below demonstrates. Jackson's problem frames and Axel van Lamsweerde's goal-oriented method develop the same separation into method.42

David Parnas's criterion for decomposing a system into modules is another ontological claim wearing methodological clothes: a module is not a step in the processing but a design decision hidden behind an interface, which makes componenthood a matter of what is concealed rather than what is contained.43 And the philosophy of computer science has taken up the dual-nature problem directly. Turner's treatment of specification as normative, Nurbay Irmak's argument that software is an abstract artefact created at a time and capable of being destroyed, and Timothy Colburn and Gary Shute's analysis of abstraction as information neglect rather than information loss, together supply what a metaphysics of engineering needs for the informational half of its subject matter.44

The lacuna

Figure 2 makes the shape of the problem visible. Function theory owns function and rarely touches requirements. Mereology owns parthood and is deliberately blind to role. Mechanistic philosophy owns decomposition but is oriented to discovered rather than designed systems, so it has no account of what happens when the decomposition is stipulated in advance by a specification. Applied ontology owns formal rigour but generally adopts its metaphysics off the shelf and then engineers around the mismatches. Requirements engineering owns the normative layer with unusual precision and treats the ontology instrumentally. Science and technology studies owns the institutional layer and declines, as a matter of method, to individuate anything.

What is missing is an account that runs the whole length of the chain, is engineering-facing at every point, and is answerable to the verdicts that practice already renders. Constructing one is the business of the rest of this article.

MOE-300Method: axioms and pathologies

Two methodological commitments shape what follows, and both are borrowed from the subject matter.

The first concerns the word axiom. In what follows an axiom is a provisional starting principle rather than an unquestionable truth: a posit adopted because its consequences can then be tested, and abandoned if they fail. This is closer to the engineer's use of a design assumption than to the logician's use of an axiom. Each is stated in prose, given a formal rendering where formalisation adds precision rather than the appearance of it, and immediately confronted with a case.

The second concerns testing. An ontology of engineering cannot be tested against experiment, because it makes no predictions about matter. But it can be tested against something almost as good: the verdicts that competent practice renders without hesitation and would defend under challenge. A stopped clock is a clock. A dormant backup channel is a component. A counterfeit bolt with correct metallurgy is not an airworthy part. These are not intuitions in the philosopher's sense, to be weighed against each other in reflective equilibrium. They are load-bearing commitments of an industrial practice, embedded in certification regimes, maintenance manuals, and liability law, and a theory that contradicts them is not offering a revisionary insight but simply failing to describe its subject.

So the method is this: assemble a regression suite of pathological cases, state each candidate ontology as a predicate over declared facts, and run the suite. This is the engineering approach to validation applied reflexively to engineering's own ontology, and it has the virtue of making disagreement productive: a rival who rejects the axioms below can propose a different predicate and run the same suite, and the disagreement will then be about the twelve cases rather than about first principles.

Adequacy conditions

Five conditions determine whether a proposed ontology is adequate. It must be descriptively adequate to competent practice, in the sense just described. It must be discriminative, drawing the distinctions that practice actually enforces — between a designed and an assigned function, between a part and a component — rather than collapsing them. It must be modally adequate, because engineering claims are overwhelmingly about what would happen rather than what does. It must be normatively adequate, supporting the correctness predicates without either eliminating them or treating them as ineffable. And it must be portable across mechanical, civil, electrical, chemical, software, and sociotechnical domains, since an ontology that works only for gearboxes is a theory of gearboxes.

Notation

Formal renderings use standard first-order notation with the following conventions. ⇒ is material implication in the object language; ⇏ marks the denial of an implication, which is how most of the axioms below do their work — the interesting content is usually what does not follow. ⟨ ⟩ denotes an ordered tuple. ⊨ is semantic entailment. Predicates are written in full rather than abbreviated, on the grounds that the appearance of formality is worth nothing and its substance is worth having only where the informal statement is genuinely ambiguous.

The gauge

Each axiom below carries a five-cell gauge recording which ontological commitments it loads. M material and causal; O organisational; K modal; I intentional and historical; N normative. The distribution across all thirty-five plates is collected in the ledger, and it is itself an argument: if the axioms were dispensable ornament on a physical core, the M column would dominate. It does not.

MOE-400Stratum I — artefact

The chain begins with the crudest question: what makes something an engineered thing at all? Four axioms are needed, and each is confronted by a case that a simpler answer gets wrong.

A1 The artefact principle MOKIN
Asserts
An engineered artefact is an entity whose existence, organisation, modification, or selection is substantially explained by intentional technical activity.
Formally
Artefact(x) ⇒ ∃a [ Agent(a) ∧ IntentionalTechnicalActivity(a, x) ]
Pathology
A person finds a naturally shaped stone and uses it every day as a hammer. Is it a natural object used as a hammer, a hammer, an artefact, or all three under different descriptions?
Denies
That artefacthood requires manufacture. Deliberate selection for a technical role is sufficient, which makes artefacthood relational rather than intrinsic.

The disjunction in A1 does real work. If manufacture were required, the hafted stone and the found stone would fall on opposite sides of a line no archaeologist draws, and the whole category of appropriated artefacts — the shipping container turned dwelling, the general-purpose processor turned cryptographic accelerator — would have to be reclassified as something other than what it is. Beth Preston's material-culture pluralism is the fullest defence of the position that appropriation is a mode of artefact production, not a deviant use of one.45 The consequence is that whether an object is an artefact is not settled by examining the object.

A2 The artefact-kind principle MOKIN
Asserts
Membership in an artefact kind depends partly on intentional, functional, or historical facts, and cannot generally be determined from physical composition alone.
Formally
SameMaterial(x, y) ⇏ SameArtefactKind(x, y)
Pathology
A geological process produces an object molecule for molecule identical to a screwdriver. Is it a screwdriver?
Denies
That artefact identity supervenes on physical structure. Two indiscernible objects can differ in kind because one has a maker and the other does not.

The case is a technological cousin of Donald Davidson's Swampman, and it produces the same split verdict for the same reason.46 Somebody who picks the object up and drives screws with it is using a screwdriver in the loosest sense; the customs officer classifying it, the certification authority accepting it into a maintenance programme, and the historian cataloguing it will all say it is not one. Practice sides decisively with the second group. This is why the counterfeit fastener case in the conformance suite is so instructive: a bolt with correct metallurgy, correct dimensions, and forged paperwork is not an airworthy part, and the difference between it and the genuine article is invisible to every instrument that measures the object rather than its history.

A3 The failed-artefact principle MOKIN
Asserts
An artefact need not perform its intended function in order to belong to its artefact kind.
Formally
ArtefactKind(x, K) ⇏ ActuallyPerforms(x, FK)
Pathology
A stopped clock. A crashed operating system. An airframe with its engines removed. A decommissioned reactor.
Denies
That kind membership reduces to current successful behaviour. It also secures malfunction as a coherent verdict: only something that is a clock can be a broken one.

A3 is the axiom that makes engineering's central normative predicate available. If a thing ceased to be a pump the moment it stopped pumping, there would be no broken pumps, only former pumps, and the entire apparatus of fault diagnosis would be describing a change of kind rather than a defect. Kroes makes this point the hinge of his account: malfunction requires a standard the object continues to be held to after it stops meeting it.47 Note the modal cell on the gauge. To say that a stopped clock is a clock is to say something about what it was made to do and what it would do if repaired, not about what it does.

A4 The impossible-artefact principle MOKIN
Asserts
Intended function and physically realisable function must be distinguished. An entity may belong to a kind defined by an impossible function.
Formally
IntendedFunction(x, F) ⇏ Possible(F)
Pathology
Somebody designs and builds a machine intended to deliver unlimited work without input. It cannot do what it was made to do, and never could. Yet this is a failed perpetual-motion machine is a perfectly intelligible sentence, and museums curate such objects under that description.
Denies
That the space of artefact kinds is bounded by the space of physical possibility. Engineering ontology must have room for failed intentions and impossible designs.

A4 has more purchase than its whimsical case suggests. Every specification written before the feasibility study is complete is a candidate impossible design, and a large fraction of engineering effort consists in discovering that a proposed artefact kind is empty. If ontology admitted only realisable kinds, it could not describe the object of that effort, and the discovery that a design is impossible would have to be reported as the discovery that one had never been talking about anything.

MOE-410Stratum II — system

A5 The system principle MOKIN
Asserts
A system is a plurality of entities whose relations are relevant to some organised behaviour, function, model, or explanatory purpose.
Formally
System(S) ⇒ ∃x₁…xₙ, R such that the organisation expressed by R matters to the characterisation of S
Pathology
Is a charging station part of an electric-vehicle system? What about the grid, the navigation servers, the driver's phone, the road?
Denies
That systemhood is a wholly observer-independent feature of the world, and equally that it is a free stipulation.
A6 The boundary-relativity principle MOKIN
Asserts
System boundaries are relative to the question being asked, and different questions yield different, equally correct boundaries.
Formally
Boundary(S, C₁) ≠ Boundary(S, C₂) for distinct contexts C₁, C₂
Pathology
For aerodynamic analysis the pilot is outside the system. For flight-control analysis the pilot is inside the loop. For airline operations the maintenance organisation is inside. The same aircraft belongs to several legitimate decompositions.
Denies
That there is a uniquely correct decomposition. It does not follow that decomposition is arbitrary: perspectival is not the same as optional.

The distinction A6 turns on is worth dwelling upon, because it is where most objections to this programme begin. The claim is that boundary choice is perspectival without being arbitrary. Two things secure the second half. First, Simon's near-decomposability: real systems have interaction structures in which coupling is dense within clusters and sparse between them, so some boundaries cut few edges and others cut many, and that difference is a fact about the system rather than about the analyst.48 Second, the standards have already institutionalised the position. ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 defines a system of interest as the system whose life cycle is under consideration, which is an explicit relativisation to purpose written into a normative document, and 42010 requires that architecture descriptions be framed by stakeholder concerns.49 Practice has conceded the metaphysical point and has not thereby become relativist about bridges.

A7 The distributed-system principle MOKIN
Asserts
Spatial containment is neither necessary nor sufficient for system membership.
Formally
ComponentOf(x, S) ⇏ SpatiallyInside(x, S)
Pathology
A service whose processes execute in Virginia, Ireland, Singapore, a browser, and a third party's infrastructure. There is no physical container corresponding to the system.
Denies
That engineered wholes are individuated by physical envelopes. The unifier must be sought among causal interaction, protocol relation, shared function, administrative control, information flow, and common design history.

The experimental result in §17 is blunt on this point: of ten candidate entities assessed against a certified aircraft configuration, two are inside the physical envelope and are not components, and two are components and are nowhere near it. Spatial containment scores six out of ten, which is barely better than a coin, and it fails in both directions at once.

MOE-420Stratum III — component and part

A8 The part–component distinction MOKIN
Asserts
Not every part of an engineered object is a component of it. Componenthood is a narrower relation than parthood.
Formally
ComponentOf(x, S) ⇒ PartOf(x, S)   often holds
PartOf(x, S) ⇏ ComponentOf(x, S)   always
Pathology
A scratch is part of the present physical condition of a wing. A dust grain inside an avionics bay is part of the aggregate. Neither is a component, and no parts list will ever contain them.
Denies
That mereology alone can supply the engineering notion of a component.
A9 The functional-component principle MOKIN
Asserts
Componenthood is role-relative rather than merely spatial: a thing is a component partly because it occupies a position within an organised functional or causal decomposition.
Formally
ComponentOf(x, S) ⇒ ∃D [ Decomposition(D, S) ∧ Role(x, D) ]
Pathology
A mobile application depends on an authentication server three thousand kilometres away. The server is a component of the application system and shares no enclosure with it.
Denies
That componenthood can be read off a physical survey of the object.
A10 The hierarchical-decomposition principle MOKIN
Asserts
Engineered systems admit multiple levels of decomposition, and the categories in the chain have no universal boundaries.
Formally
System ⊃ Subsystem ⊃ Assembly ⊃ Component ⊃ Part   (indexed to a decomposition, not absolute)
Pathology
A transistor is a component of a circuit; it is also a semiconductor device whose junctions are its components; its lattice can be analysed structurally in turn. Whether it is a system or a component depends on who is asking.
Denies
That there is a metaphysically privileged lowest engineering level.
A11 The redundancy principle MOKIN
Asserts
A component can belong to a system even when the system's current operation does not depend on it.
Formally
ComponentOf(x, S) ⇏ NecessaryForCurrentOperation(x, S)
Pathology
An aircraft carries three flight computers and flies on one. The two idle channels are components in every sense that matters: they appear on the parts list, they are maintained, their absence grounds the aircraft.
Denies
Any criterion of componenthood framed in terms of actual causal contribution. What makes a dormant channel a component is designed participation under counterfactual conditions.

A11 forces the criterion that the rest of this stratum needs. Craver's mutual manipulability, framed for discovered mechanisms, says roughly that a part is a component when interventions on it change the phenomenon and interventions on the phenomenon change it.50 Applied to a triple-redundant architecture that gives the wrong answer, because intervening on the dormant channel changes nothing at all in normal operation — that is precisely the point of the design. The engineering version has to quantify over the specified operating scenarios rather than the actual one:

ComponentOf(x, S) ≡df ∃D ∃σ [ Decomposition(D, S) ∧ Scenario(σ, S) ∧ Role(x, D) ∧ MakesADifference(x, ΦS, σ) ] where σ ranges over the designed operating scenarios of S, including failure and degraded modes, and ΦS is S's function-relevant behaviour.

This is what the fourth column of Figure 6 tests. It is the only one of the four criteria that returns the certified answer in all ten cases, and it earns that result by quantifying over scenarios that never occur. The dormant channel makes a difference in the two-failure scenario; the emergency beacon makes a difference in the post-impact scenario; the runway makes a difference in every scenario but occupies no role in any decomposition of the aircraft, and so is excluded. Note what this criterion costs: it presupposes that the set of designed scenarios is already fixed, which means componenthood inherits whatever indeterminacy attaches to the specification. That is a real limitation, and it is the right one to have, because disputes about whether something is a component in engineering practice are almost always disputes about scope of specification.

MOE-430Stratum IV — structure

A12 The structure principle MOKIN
Asserts
Engineering structure consists not merely of constituent entities but of organised relations among them.
Formally
Structure(S) ≠ {x₁, x₂, …, xₙ}
Structure(S) = ⟨ X, R ⟩, where X is a set of entities and R a set of relations that matter
Pathology
Place every part of an engine in a box. The same pieces exist; the engine does not.
Denies
That a material inventory suffices for engineered organisation. Classical extensional mereology, which is insensitive to arrangement, cannot distinguish the box from the engine.

Kit Fine's rigid embodiments are the natural formal home for A12: an object is a pair of a matter and a principle by which that matter is arranged, and two objects with identical matter and different principles are different objects.51 Engineering needs the variable version as well, since the arrangement persists while the matter is exchanged — which is the topic of §13.

A13 The organisation-sensitivity principle MOKIN
Asserts
Two systems may contain the same kinds and quantities of parts while differing radically because their relations differ.
Formally
SameParts(S₁, S₂) ∧ DifferentRelations(S₁, S₂) ⇒ possibly SameKind(S₁, S₂) is false
Pathology
Identical electronic components connected according to two different diagrams. One is an amplifier. Reverse the phase of one feedback path and it is an oscillator.
Denies
Any account on which kind membership is fixed by the inventory. Structure has explanatory priority over membership.
A14 The interface principle MOKIN
Asserts
Interfaces are structurally significant relations or boundary conditions that regulate the possible interactions between components.
Formally
Interface(I, x, y) = ⟨ Σ, Π ⟩, where Σ is a set of shared phenomena and
Π ⊆ the interactions over Σ that the parties are permitted
Pathology
A web API has no material boundary. Yet violating it causes failure, and engineering treats the violation as a fault in the violator rather than an accident.
Denies
That an ontology limited to material objects can represent engineering structure. An interface is neither an object nor a mere boundary; it is a permission set.

The question what kind of thing is an interface? has a longer list of candidate answers than it should: an object, a boundary, a specification document, a relation, a protocol, a family of permitted interactions. The last is closest, and Parnas's criterion explains why. A module, on his account, is not a stage in the processing but a design decision concealed behind an interface, so that what the interface delimits is not a region of space but a region of ignorance — what the other party is entitled to rely upon and, equally, what it is forbidden to rely upon.52 This is why an interface can be violated by a caller that reaches around it to something true but unpublished, even though nothing physically prevented the reach. The formal descendant of this idea is the theory of assume–guarantee contracts, in which a component is specified by a pair of an assumption on its environment and a guarantee on its behaviour, and composition is defined on the pairs.53 That is an engineering formalism with an ontology inside it: what a component is, for purposes of composition, is its contract.

MOE-440Stratum V — behaviour

A15 The behaviour principle MOKIN
Asserts
Behaviour concerns what an entity actually does or would do under specified conditions, so behaviour is dispositional rather than merely actual.
Formally
Behaviour(x) = { ⟨ c, o ⟩ : c ∈ Cx ∧ would-produce(x, c, o) }
where Cx is x's specified operating envelope
Pathology
An airbag that never deploys. Engineers nevertheless make meaningful, testable, litigable claims about how it behaves in a frontal impact at 30 mph with an unbelted occupant.
Denies
Any actualist reading of behaviour. The claims that matter quantify over conditions the individual device never meets.

The quantitative form of this point is stark. A modest certification envelope — thirteen closure speeds, five principal directions, four occupancy states, two restraint states — contains 520 condition–outcome pairs that the device is required to satisfy. A device deploys at most once. Its maximum possible coverage of its own specification is therefore 1/520, or 0.19 per cent; 519 of the pairs, 99.81 per cent, are never actualised by that device and never could be. The engineering claim that this airbag is sound quantifies over the 519, not the one. Any ontology that admits only actual behaviour cannot state the specification, let alone the verdict.

This puts engineering squarely inside the metaphysics of dispositions, with all its difficulties. The naive conditional analysis — x is fragile iff x would break if struck — famously fails to finks and masks, and both have exact engineering counterparts.54 A fuse in a circuit with a parallel bypass is masked: the disposition is present and its manifestation is prevented by something extrinsic. A protection system that reconfigures the plant on detecting the very condition it is meant to protect against is finkish: the stimulus removes the disposition. Engineers handle both by explicitly indexing behaviour to a configuration and an operating mode, which is exactly the repair the metaphysical literature converged on, arrived at independently and a good deal earlier.

A16 The behaviour–function distinction MOKIN
Asserts
Behaviour is descriptive; function is role-laden and normative. The two must not be identified.
Formally
Behaviour(x) = B ⇏ Function(x) = B
Pathology
A processor produces heat; that is behaviour. Heating the room is not its function. A brake rotor produces noise; noise production is not its function, which is why the noise is a defect and the heat is a design constraint.
Denies
Any behavioural reduction of function. The gap between the two is where the whole normative vocabulary lives.

The design-theory literature encodes this distinction structurally. Gero's schema separates the behaviour expected of a structure from the behaviour derived from it, and locates function in the ascription of purpose to the former; designing is then the repeated comparison of the two.55 That a mature design methodology needs three concepts where a physicalist ontology offers one is evidence about the subject matter, not about the methodology.

MOE-450Stratum VI — function

A17 The function principle MOKIN
Asserts
A technical function is a privileged contribution that an entity is supposed, intended, selected, assigned, or organised to make within some practical context.
Formally
Function(x, F) ⇒ ∃G [ Grounds(G, F, x) ] where G ranges over designer intention,
user intention, convention, design history, system contribution, and specification
Pathology
Most of what an artefact does is not its function. The difficult word in the axiom is privileged, and the axiom is empty until the grounds of privilege are named.
Denies
That there is a single ground. The plural in the formal statement is the substantive claim.

The literature's long argument about whether function is grounded in etiology, in systemic contribution, or in intention is best read as an argument about which of several genuinely distinct concepts deserves the word. The engineering evidence favours accepting all of them and keeping them apart. Four concepts are needed, and the case for four rather than one is that they come apart in every combination:

designed function what x was produced under the concept of doing [etiological, after Wright and Millikan] assigned function what some agent now takes x to be for [intentional, after Houkes and Vermaas] system function what x contributes to a containing capacity here [systemic, after Cummins] normative function what a standard or certificate requires x to do [institutional, after Searle]

A new screwdriver in its packaging has all four. A screwdriver kept in the paint cupboard has the designed and normative functions of a screwdriver and the assigned and system functions of a lever. A shipping container converted into a dwelling has acquired assigned and system functions it was never designed for and holds no certificate for. A rifle in a museum vitrine retains its designed function and has lost the other three. Waste heat has a system function — it is what the heatsink is sized for — and none of the others.56

A18 The function–capability distinction MOKIN
Asserts
Capability concerns what an entity can do; function concerns what it is assigned or understood to do. Neither entails the other.
Formally
Function(x, F) ⇒ Capability(x, F)   is defeasible, since broken artefacts falsify it
Better: distinguish DesignedCapability(x, F) from PresentCapability(x, F)
Pathology
A screwdriver can lever the lid off a paint can. That is a capability, and it is not its function. Conversely a seized valve has the function and lacks the capability.
Denies
The dispositional reduction of function, in both directions.
A19 The repurposing principle MOKIN
Asserts
An artefact may acquire new functions without any corresponding physical alteration.
Formally
ΔFunction(x) ⇏ ΔPhysicalState(x)
Pathology
A shipping container becomes a house, a data centre, a shop, a swimming pool. Nothing about the steel changed.
Denies
That function supervenes on physical state at a time. The supervenience base must include intentional and institutional facts.

The obvious follow-up question — did the artefact change kind, or did only its use change? — is not well posed until the four function concepts are separated. Once they are, the answer is that the assigned and system functions changed, the designed function did not, and whether the kind changed depends on which function the kind term is keyed to. Container is keyed to designed function and so persists; dwelling is keyed to assigned function and so is acquired; certified dwelling is keyed to normative function and so requires an inspection.

A20 The multi-function principle MOKIN
Asserts
Engineered entities may legitimately possess several functions at once, with no privileged primary among them.
Formally
∃x ∃F₁…Fₙ [ ∧i Function(x, Fi) ∧ ¬∃Fj Primary(Fj, x) ]
Pathology
A telephone that is also a camera, a computer, a navigation device, an authentication token, and a payment instrument. Which is the essence?
Denies
Definitions of artefact kind that presuppose a single essence-like function.

The experimental result in §17 shows how ordinary this is even for elementary components: across a table of fourteen structures and six functions, nine of the fourteen carry more than one function, the mean is 1.714 functions per structure, and not one of the six functions has a unique realisation. Simondon anticipated the pattern and gave it a name: a design matures by converging on parts that discharge several functions at once, so multifunctionality is a mark of good engineering rather than an inconvenience for the theorist.57

A21 The emergent-function principle MOKIN
Asserts
A system can possess functions that no individual component possesses.
Formally
Function(S, F) ⇏ ∃c [ ComponentOf(c, S) ∧ Function(c, F) ]
Pathology
No component of an aircraft has the function of carrying two hundred people across an ocean. The function belongs to an organised whole.
Denies
Compositional reduction of function to component functions taken severally.

A21 should be stated carefully, because the word emergent invites more than is intended. Nothing here requires that system-level function fail to be determined by components plus organisation plus environment. The claim is the weaker and more defensible one that the function is not possessed by any part, so that the ascription is irreducibly to the whole under a decomposition. That is emergence in the ascriptive sense that Cummins's systemic analysis already provides, not in any sense that would embarrass a physicalist.

MOE-460Stratum VII — requirement

A22 The requirement principle MOKIN
Asserts
A requirement specifies a condition whose satisfaction is treated as normatively binding on an engineered entity or design.
Formally
Requirement(R, S) ⇒ ∃σ [ SatisfactionCondition(σ, R, S) ] ∧ ∃a [ Imposes(a, R) ]
Pathology
A requirement is not a component, not a force, not a behaviour, and not a property. Yet requirements shape physical reality more reliably than most physical causes do.
Denies
That the inventory of an engineered system can be given in physical terms. Requirements are among the things that have to be quantified over, and they have authors.
A23 The requirement–property distinction MOKIN
Asserts
A requirement is not identical to the property that satisfies it.
Formally
R = “the bridge shall support 20,000 kg”
P = the bridge's actual load-bearing capacity
R ≠ P
Pathology
A bridge may satisfy a requirement accidentally, without its designers knowing that it does. A requirement may exist that nothing satisfies.
Denies
Identification of the normative with the natural. Satisfaction is a relation between them, and relations are not their relata.
A24 The unrealised-requirement principle MOKIN
Asserts
Requirements are meaningful, and causally influential through agents, before any satisfying artefact exists.
Formally
Requirement → DesignDecision → ArtefactStructure
Pathology
Engineers write the spacecraft shall survive reentry at temperature T years before any spacecraft exists. The requirement is ontologically prior to its realisation and does causal work in the interval.
Denies
That the direction of explanation in engineering runs only from structure to behaviour to function. It also runs the other way, through agents.

A24 identifies an unusual direction of dependence, and it is worth being precise about what is and is not being claimed. The requirement does not act on the spacecraft; nothing abstract pushes anything. What the requirement does is constrain the deliberations of agents who then act, so the causal chain runs through minds and documents and organisations, and the requirement's role in it is the role of a content rather than a force. This is exactly the structure John Searle described for institutional facts and Ibo van de Poel traced in the translation of values into design requirements: the abstract item is real, is efficacious, and is efficacious only through acceptance.58

Correctness is a three-place relation

The sharpest available result about requirements is Zave and Jackson's, and it deserves restating precisely because it settles a question that looks metaphysical and turns out to be formal. Let R be a requirement, stated wholly in terms of phenomena in the environment. Let S be a specification, stated wholly in terms of phenomena shared between the environment and the machine. Let D be the domain knowledge that describes the environment's own regularities. Then correctness is not a property of the machine at all. It is the entailment

S, D ⊨ R

and the machine's contribution is only the S.59 Experiment 3 in §17 checks this exhaustively over a five-atom propositional model of a flow-isolation function. The specification alone has twenty models and does not entail the requirement; ten of those models have flow and a counterexample is immediate. Adding the single domain assumption that the sensor reads high exactly when there is flow reduces the models to ten and secures the entailment. Withdrawing either of two other domain assumptions breaks it again, in each case producing a machine that satisfies its specification perfectly inside a system that violates its requirement, with no defect anywhere in the machine.

That result is a metaphysical claim in formal clothing. It says that being correct is never a monadic property of an engineered thing. It is a three-place relation among a specification, a set of assumptions about a world, and a demand — and the most common engineering catastrophes are failures of the middle term, which is the one nobody owns.

A25 The requirement-conflict principle MOKIN
Asserts
Requirements need not be jointly satisfiable, and engineered correctness is therefore often graded rather than binary.
Formally
∃R₁, R₂ [ Requirement(R₁, S) ∧ Requirement(R₂, S) ∧ ¬◊(Sat(R₁, S) ∧ Sat(R₂, S)) ]
Pathology
Maximum strength and minimum mass. Across thirty-five candidate beam sections, eight satisfy the stiffness requirement, fifteen the mass requirement, eleven the cost requirement, and none satisfies stiffness and mass together.
Denies
That satisfaction partitions designs into the acceptable and the unacceptable. It locates a threshold, and somebody sets the threshold.

Experiment 7 makes the consequence concrete. In the design space examined, the intersection of the three requirements is empty, twelve of the thirty-five designs are Pareto-optimal on the two conflicting criteria, and relaxing the stiffness threshold by fifteen per cent admits exactly one design, while forty per cent admits two. Nothing about the steel changed at any point in that table. What changed was a number in a document, and with it the extension of the predicate acceptable. Simon's satisficing is the standard description of what engineers do in this situation; the ontological observation is that the objects being sorted do not have the property being ascribed to them independently of the sorting criterion, and that this is not a defect in the practice but a structural feature of it.60

MOE-470Stratum VIII — purpose

A26 The purpose principle MOKIN
Asserts
Purposes belong to agents, organisations, and practices. Artefacts participate in purposes through their relations to those agents and practices; they do not have purposes of their own.
Formally
Purpose(a, P) with a an agent  ≠  Function(x, F) with x an artefact
ParticipatesIn(x, P) ⇒ ∃a [ Purpose(a, P) ∧ UsesFor(a, x, P) ]
Pathology
A rifle in a museum vitrine. It was designed to injure; its present institutional purpose is education. Historical function, present function, and the purposes of its custodians all diverge.
Denies
Intrinsic teleology in artefacts. It does not deny that artefacts carry purposes forward across time, which is what makes them politically consequential.

A26 is where this account must engage its most serious rival. Bruno Latour's programme insists on symmetry between human and non-human actors, and reads a speed bump or a door closer as an artefact into which a programme of action has been delegated, so that the artefact does something that would otherwise require a person.61 The description is accurate and the metaphysics is optional. Everything Latour's analysis captures can be stated within A26 by taking seriously the H slot of the model below: an artefact bears an intentional provenance, and that provenance persists in it, doing work long after its authors are gone. Winner's argument that some artefacts embody political arrangements is the same observation at civic scale.62 None of this requires granting the object a purpose of its own; it requires denying that provenance is metaphysically inert, which this account already denies.

MOE-480Identity, persistence, and copies

A27 The engineering ship-of-Theseus principle MOKIN
Asserts
The persistence of an engineered artefact does not reduce to the persistence of its original material parts.
Formally
IdentityThroughTime(x) ≠ IdentityOfMatter(x)
Pathology
An airframe in service for forty years. Every replaceable assembly has been replaced; a supplemental type certificate has modified the type. It retains its registration, its maintenance history, its operational identity, and its institutional role.
Denies
Material continuity as a necessary condition. Candidate replacements include causal, structural, functional, legal, design, maintenance, and social continuity — and they do not agree.

Experiment 5 tracks four criteria over the forty years. The structural criterion fails first, at year 22, when the type is modified. The material criterion fails at year 26, when the last long-lived assembly is replaced. The causal and institutional criteria never fail. All four agree for 22 of the 40 years — 55 per cent of the service life — and diverge thereafter, which means that for nearly half the artefact's existence the question is this the same aircraft? has a different answer depending on which criterion is consulted, and practice consults the institutional one without hesitation.

The philosophical literature on persistence and material constitution supplies the vocabulary for this — David Wiggins on sortal-relative identity, Michael Rea's survey of the constitution problem — but engineering supplies something the philosophical cases lack, which is an operative answer.63 Configuration management is applied metaphysics. A configuration baseline is a stipulated identity criterion, maintained by an authority, with a documented change history and a defined procedure for deciding whether a modification produces the same item in a new state or a different item. Engineers did not solve the ship of Theseus; they noticed that the question has no answer independent of a practice and then built the practice. The recent turn in the history of technology toward maintenance rather than innovation makes the same point historically: what persists is what is kept, and keeping is an activity with institutions attached.64

A28 The branching-identity principle MOKIN
Asserts
Identity becomes acutely problematic when an artefact's informational structure can be copied without loss.
Formally
S → S₁, S₂ at time t, with S₁ and S₂ sharing the whole history and structure of S
Which is the original? Both; neither; the one in the privileged repository; the one with institutional continuity.
Pathology
A software fork. Five criteria give two distinct answers: matter, organisation, and causal ancestry each return both; registry and maintenance authority each return exactly one.
Denies
That persistence criteria derived from ordinary material objects generalise to informational artefacts.

The fork is the cleanest experiment in the whole set, because it holds everything physical fixed and varies only the institutional facts. Nothing in the matter, the organisation, or the causal history distinguishes the branches; the criteria that answer the question at all are precisely the ones a physicalist ontology has no room for. This is not a reason to conclude that identity here is a legal fiction laid over a metaphysical fact. It is a reason to conclude that the institutional facts are the metaphysical facts — that for artefacts of this kind, being the original just is standing in the right relation to a maintaining authority. Irmak's argument that software is an abstract artefact, created at a time by an author and capable of ceasing to exist, is the account that makes room for this.65

MOE-490Design, description, and realisation

A29 The design–artefact distinction MOKIN
Asserts
A design is not identical to any realisation of it, and one design may have many instances.
Formally
Design(D) ≠ Artefact(A)    D → A₁, A₂, …, Aₙ
Pathology
A CAD file specifying a gear is not the gear; is itself an engineered artefact; is a representation of a possible artefact; and can produce indefinitely many instances. Which of those is it?
Denies
That the type–token distinction is dispensable in engineering ontology, and that a design belongs to exactly one ontological category. It has features of an abstract object, an information structure, a representation, a prescription, and an artefact simultaneously.

What makes a design more than a description is its direction of fit. A drawing that misrepresents the part it depicts is a bad drawing; a part that departs from the drawing that governs it is a bad part. The same marks on paper can do either job, and which job they are doing is not a fact about the marks. Turner's treatment of specification turns on precisely this: a specification is a prescription whose violation impugns the artefact rather than the document, which is why the same artefact can be simultaneously a correct implementation of one specification and an incorrect implementation of another.66

A30 The realisation principle MOKIN
Asserts
The same function, requirement, or design objective may be realised by radically different physical structures.
Formally
F → { S₁, S₂, …, Sₙ } with the Sₐ sharing no relevant physical kind
Pathology
Timekeeping realised by pendulums, quartz crystals, caesium transitions, electronic counters, and software. What makes these instances of the same engineering solution class?
Denies
Type identity between function and structure. This is the engineering form of multiple realisability.

Multiple realisability arrived in philosophy as an argument against type-identity theories of mind and became the standard rationale for the autonomy of the special sciences.67 Engineering exhibits it in a purer form, because here the realisations are not merely possible but catalogued, costed, and interchangeable by design intent. It also exhibits the converse, which the philosophical debate underweights: one structure serving many functions. The relation is many-to-many in both directions, and Experiment 4 measures it — twenty-four incidences across fourteen structures and six functions, nine structures multiply functional, no function uniquely realised, means of 1.714 and 4.000 in the two directions. Neither reading of the table licenses substitution, so no identification of function with structure survives it.

MOE-500Five cross-cutting meta-principles

The thirty axioms are not thirty independent discoveries. Five commitments recur across the strata, and stating them separately makes the structure of the position visible — and, more usefully, makes it falsifiable in a way that a list of thirty cannot be. Each is stated as a plate on the same pattern, and each is paired with the literature that has already established it in some narrower domain.

M‑A Dual description MOKIN
Asserts
Every engineered entity admits at least two irreducible descriptions: a physical or structural one and an intentional or functional one. Neither is translatable into the other, and both are true of the same object.
Formally
∀x [ Engineered(x) ⇒ ∃Dphys, Dint ( Describes(Dphys, x) ∧ Describes(Dint, x)
    ∧ ¬Reducible(Dint, Dphys) ) ]
Pathology
The stress tensor and the service limit, applied to one caliper.
Denies
Both eliminativism about the intentional description and dualism about its subject. There is one object and two descriptions.

This is Kroes and Meijers's thesis, and Houkes and Meijers's hard problem is the demand that it be more than a restatement of the difficulty.68 The present programme's answer to the hard problem is deflationary in form and substantive in content: the two descriptions are jointly true because the intentional description quantifies over relations — to makers, to standards, to decompositions, to scenarios — that the physical description of the object at a time simply does not range over. Nothing extra exists. Something extra is being talked about.

M‑B Many-to-many realisation MOKIN
Asserts
The relation between function and structure is many-to-many. One function admits many structures; one structure carries many functions.
Formally
Realises ⊆ Structure × Function is neither single-valued nor injective
Pathology
Fourteen structures, six functions, twenty-four incidences; no function uniquely realised; nine structures multiply functional.
Denies
Every type-identity claim between the functional and structural vocabularies, in both directions.
M‑C Counterfactual constitution MOKIN
Asserts
Engineered properties are constituted partly by what would happen under conditions that do not obtain. Modality is not an overlay on the ontology; it is inside it.
Formally
Properties(x) ⊇ { φ : φ is fixed by would-counterfactuals over the designed scenarios of x }
Pathology
Redundancy, safety margin, failure mode, fault tolerance, service life, and the entire discipline of reliability engineering. 519 of 520 specified behaviours never occur.
Denies
Actualism about engineered properties. An inventory of what happens is not an inventory of what there is.

M‑C is the meta-principle with the greatest distance between how obvious it is to practitioners and how awkward it is for received ontology. A safety factor of 1.5 is a claim about loads that will never be applied. A fail-safe design is characterised by its behaviour in a state the designer intends never to obtain. Reliability figures quantify over a population of counterfactual histories. The dispositional literature supplies the apparatus, and the finks and masks that complicate it there have exact engineering analogues here, handled in practice by indexing every behavioural claim to a configuration and a mode.69

M‑D Ineliminable normativity MOKIN
Asserts
Engineering vocabulary is irreducibly normative. Correct, defective, compliant, and fit for purpose cannot be paraphrased away without loss of the distinctions practice enforces.
Formally
Defective(x) ≡ ∃R [ Governs(R, x) ∧ ¬Satisfies(x, R) ]  — and R has an author
Pathology
Two physically identical bolts, one certified and one not. Every measurement agrees; the verdicts differ.
Denies
That the normative predicates are either reducible to physical ones or merely expressive of attitudes. They are relations to standards, and standards are real institutional objects.

Franssen's analysis of artefact normativity, Searle's account of status functions, and Turner's treatment of specification converge here from three directions.70 The convergence matters because each arrives at the same structure from a different starting point: a judgement of the form this is defective is elliptical for a three-place relation between an object, a standard, and an authority that maintains the standard. Fill in the second and third places and the predicate becomes as objective as anything in the manual. Leave them out and it looks like a preference.

M‑E Perspective dependence without arbitrariness MOKIN
Asserts
Boundaries, decompositions, and function ascriptions are relative to a question. Relativity to a question is not arbitrariness, because questions have answers and decompositions can be wrong.
Formally
Correct(D, S) is a two-place relation between a decomposition and a concern,
not a one-place property of decompositions
Pathology
Three analyses of one vehicle, three boundaries, all correct. And a fourth boundary, cutting through the battery pack mid-module, that is simply bad engineering.
Denies
Both the existence of the uniquely correct decomposition and the claim that any decomposition will do.

The constraint that keeps M‑E from collapsing into conventionalism is empirical: near-decomposability is a fact about the interaction structure of real systems, so decompositions that cut dense regions of coupling are worse than those that cut sparse ones, and this is measurable independently of anyone's preferences. ISO 42010's viewpoint machinery and Craver's account of levels as relative to a mechanism both institutionalise the same combination — relativity to a concern, discipline within it.71

MOE-600A general ontological model

Collecting the axioms yields a model. An engineered entity is characterised not by a substance but by a tuple of related aspects:

E = ⟨ C, Rs, B, F, Req, P, H ⟩ C components, and the parts that are not components Rs structural, causal, informational, and institutional relations B behaviours, actual and dispositional, over an operating envelope F functions, in the four senses of §10 Req requirements and the standards that impose them P purposes, held by the agents and institutions the entity serves H history: design, manufacture, modification, maintenance, provenance

Presented as a list this is unremarkable, and on its own it would be an ontology in the trade sense — a schema for a database rather than a metaphysical thesis. What gives it content is the set of constraints among the slots, because those are what an alternative would have to deny. Five are load-bearing.

The realisation constraint. Functions are ascribed to an entity only by way of its behaviours, and behaviours only by way of its components under a structure. There is no direct route from C to F. This is what makes functional analysis a distinct activity from physical analysis rather than an abbreviation of it, and what makes A21's emergence claim harmless: the function is determined by ⟨C, Rs⟩ plus environment without being possessed by any member of C.

The satisfaction constraint. An entity satisfies a requirement only relative to domain assumptions: S, D ⊨ R. The D is not a member of any of the seven slots, because it is not a fact about the entity at all. That is the deepest point in the model and the one most often missed in practice, since organisations assign owners to S and to R and to no one at all to D.

The provenance constraint. H fixes which of the four function concepts apply. Designed function is a fact about H; assigned function is a fact about present agents; normative function is a fact about Req and the authority behind it; system function is a fact about ⟨C, Rs, B⟩ under a chosen analysis. Cases where these diverge are not anomalies to be legislated away but the ordinary condition of artefacts in the world.

The modal constraint. B is a set of condition–outcome pairs over a specified envelope, not a trajectory. Every predicate defined over B — reliability, margin, fault tolerance, fitness for purpose — therefore inherits a modal character, and any attempt to evaluate one against an actual history alone commits a category error that the airbag numbers make quantitative.

The normative constraint. Correctness predicates are relations between B and Req under a perspective, with Req carrying an authority. Change the authority and the extension of compliant changes without any change to C, Rs, or B. Anyone who finds this implausible is invited to consider what happens to a fleet of aircraft on the day a new airworthiness directive is issued.

Two features of the model deserve emphasis. The first is that the relations are not hierarchical. It is tempting to read the strata as a layer cake with matter at the bottom, and design does traverse them roughly downward while diagnosis traverses them roughly upward. But requirements constrain structure through agents, purposes constrain requirements, function constrains decomposition, and history constrains function; the dependency graph has cycles and no source. An ontology that insisted on a foundation would have to nominate one, and every nomination fails a case in the suite.

The second is that nothing in the model requires anything non-physical to exist. Every slot is occupied by something a physicalist can accept: objects, relations, dispositions, mental states of agents, institutional facts of the sort Searle analysed, documents, and histories. The claim is not that engineered entities have ghostly parts. It is that the individuation conditions for engineered entities quantify over relations, modalities, and institutions, so that an inventory restricted to intrinsic physical properties at a time will misclassify cases that practice classifies without difficulty. That claim is testable, and the next section tests it.72

MOE-700The conformance suite

The axioms above make a claim that can be checked: that ontologies quantifying over less than the model does will misclassify cases practice classifies easily. To check it, five candidate ontologies were stated as predicates over declared facts and run against twelve verdict cases. No predicate is permitted to consult the name of a case; each sees only the declared facts, so the suite cannot be gamed by special pleading.73 The candidates are:

O1 physicalist kind and identity supervene on matter and arrangement O2 behaviourist to be a K is to do what Ks do, now O3 intentionalist the maker's concept alone fixes the kind O4 etiological made under the concept, with a realisable function O5 relational origin in technical activity, plus a role under some concept, plus institutional standing where the kind at issue is institutionally constituted

The results are in Figure 16 and in the run below. O2, the behaviourist, scores three out of twelve — worse than answering yes to everything — because it cannot tolerate a stopped clock. O1, the physicalist, manages six, and fails in a diagnostic pattern: it accepts the geological duplicate and the counterfeit bolt, and rejects the overhauled airframe. O3 reaches eight and O4 seven. O5 returns the practice verdict in all twelve cases.

That last figure should be read with care, because it is bought rather than won. O5 consults six declared facts where O1 consults three and O2 and O3 consult one apiece. In particular it consults whether the kind at issue is institutionally constituted — whether being an airworthy part or being the original of a lineage is the sort of status that standing confers. That is a substantive theoretical commitment and not a free parameter, and it is precisely the commitment that the four leaner candidates decline to make. The honest summary of the experiment is therefore not that O5 wins, but that the four cases it wins by are exactly the four that require the commitment: the found stone, the software fork, the counterfeit bolt, and the shipping-container dwelling. A reader who thinks the price too high owes an account of those four.

The remaining experiments test individual axioms rather than whole ontologies. Their headline results have been quoted at the relevant points above: componenthood criteria at 6, 6, 7, and 10 out of 10; the Zave–Jackson entailment holding and failing exactly as the theory predicts; a function–structure incidence density of 2/7 with no unique realisations; persistence criteria diverging at years 22 and 26 and a fork that only institutional criteria can adjudicate; 519 of 520 specified behaviours never actualised; and an empty intersection of three requirements over thirty-five candidate designs.

MOE-800Objections and replies

The eliminativist objection

Van Inwagen holds that there are no artefacts — that talk of chairs is loose talk about simples arranged chairwise, and that a rigorous inventory of the world would omit them.74 If that is right, the present programme is an elaborate taxonomy of a fiction.

Two replies. The first is concessive: nothing here depends on artefacts being fundamental entities. Read every axiom as a claim about the individuation conditions of engineering descriptions, and the content survives intact, because the explanandum is not the existence of the caliper but the truth-conditions of this caliper is defective. The eliminativist owes a paraphrase of that sentence, and the paraphrase will have to quantify over makers, standards, scenarios, and authorities exactly as the original did. Eliminating the object does not eliminate the relations, and it is the relations that carry the difficulty.

The second reply is less accommodating. The eliminativist's motivation is parsimony, but the parsimony is local. Deleting artefacts from the inventory does not shorten the list of things engineering must quantify over; it relocates the length into the paraphrase, where it is harder to inspect. Elder's realism about familiar objects is the standing counter-argument, and the engineering case strengthens it: the objects whose existence is being denied are the ones that carry serial numbers, appear in registries, and are the subjects of legal obligations. That is a strange population to describe as a manner of speaking.

The deflationary objection: methodology in fancy dress

A practitioner may grant every claim above and deny that any of it is metaphysics. Of course components are role-relative — that is what a work breakdown structure is for. Of course requirements are normative — that is what a specification is. The axioms restate the profession's working assumptions in a heavier vocabulary and add nothing.

The reply is that the working assumptions are not consistent with one another, and the inconsistencies have costs. Consider a cloud-hosted dependency. Is it a component of the safety-critical system it serves? A physical-containment view says no; the counterfactual-role view says yes; and which view an organisation implicitly holds determines the scope of its hazard analysis, the boundary of its safety case, the content of its supplier obligations, and the extent of its liability. Practitioners disagree about such cases, and they disagree because they hold incompatible implicit ontologies, not because they lack data. Making the commitments explicit is what lets the disagreement be adjudicated. Kroes's argument that design methodology presupposes and cannot supply an account of the nature of technical artefacts is the general form of this reply.75

The social-construction objection

From the other direction: the constructivist holds that the boundaries, functions, and even the working/not-working status of artefacts are negotiated among relevant social groups, so that treating them as ontological facts mistakes a settlement for a discovery.76

Much of this is accepted here and is doing useful work in M‑D and M‑E. Interpretive flexibility is real; the normative layer genuinely is instituted; the counterfeit bolt case is decided by standing rather than by metallurgy. But the flexibility is bounded, and the bound is not negotiable. A bridge that fails under design load has not been reinterpreted into failure. The airbag that does not deploy at 30 mph is not the subject of a settlement among relevant groups. The present account distinguishes the strata where construction is constitutive (Req, P, part of F) from those where it is not (C, Rs, B), and the conformance suite shows that both are needed: O2, which is purely behavioural, scores three; O3, which is purely intentional, scores eight; neither reaches the verdicts alone.

The conventionalist objection about boundaries

If boundaries are relative to questions, and questions are chosen, are boundaries not simply chosen? This is the objection M‑E was framed to resist, and the resistance depends on a factual claim: real systems exhibit near-decomposability, so the interaction structure makes some cuts cheap and others expensive, and that is a property of the system.77 A decomposition that severs a tightly coupled thermal path and calls the halves independent is not a different perspective; it is wrong, and it will be shown to be wrong by a failure that no one predicted. Relativity to a question is compatible with there being right and wrong answers to that question.

The over-population objection

The model quantifies over components, relations, behaviours, functions, requirements, purposes, and histories. Are all of these entities? An ontologist of parsimonious temperament will find the list extravagant.

The list is a list of what must be quantified over, not a list of substances, and several slots can be discharged without new entities. Behaviours are sets of condition–outcome pairs; relations are relations; requirements are contents of institutional acts; purposes are states of agents. Baker's constitution view offers one way to avoid double-counting the object and its matter, and the foundational-ontology tradition offers several vocabularies — continuants and occurrents, qualities, roles — in which most of these slots have well-behaved homes.78 What cannot be avoided is quantification over relations to things outside the object, and that is not extravagance but the content of the thesis.

Why not simply adopt Searle's account?

Searle's apparatus of status functions imposed by collective acceptance handles the counterfeit bolt, the registry, the certificate, and the fork with elegance.79 Why erect a further framework?

Because collective acceptance does not confer lift. Searle's account was built for institutional facts whose entire content is instituted — money, marriage, borders — and its central mechanism is that a status is imposed on an object that could not otherwise perform the function. Engineering has such facts and is not exhausted by them. A wing that is accepted by everyone as airworthy and cannot generate lift is not airworthy, and no amount of acceptance changes that. The distinctive feature of engineered entities is that they carry an instituted layer and a causal layer, and that verdicts require both. This is the same point as M‑A, arrived at from the institutional side rather than the physical one, and it is why neither O1 nor an unsupplemented Searlean account reaches twelve.

MOE-900A research programme

Foundations are worth laying only if something can be built on them. Five directions look tractable.

Formalisation. The axioms above are stated in a notation that is suggestive rather than rigorous. A serious formalisation would need a mereology sensitive to arrangement — Fine's embodiments are the obvious starting point — combined with a modal apparatus for the scenario quantification in the componenthood criterion, roles in the sense of applied ontology, and a treatment of requirement satisfaction along assume–guarantee lines.80 The contract formalism is particularly promising because it already composes: contracts have a defined conjunction, refinement order, and composition operator, which is more than any philosophical account of function can currently claim.

Learned components. A trained model strains every axiom in this article. Its designed function is fixed by an objective and a dataset rather than by a structure anyone specified; the relation between its requirements and its structure is not one any person traversed; and there is frequently no specification against which correctness could be assessed in the sense of A29 and the Zave–Jackson entailment. Turner's analysis of the specification–implementation relation applies but produces an uncomfortable result: where no specification exists, the predicate incorrect has no anchor, and what practice substitutes is a distribution of test outcomes.81 The metaphysics of an artefact whose function is statistical is genuinely open, and the engineering stakes are not small.

Certification and assurance. A safety case is an argument that an artefact is acceptably safe, offered to an authority. It is therefore an artefact whose function is to establish a normative fact about another artefact, which puts it in an ontological category the present model does not yet have a slot for. Van de Poel's work on the translation of values into requirements is the nearest existing treatment, and the reverse direction — what an assurance argument commits its author to — is largely unexamined.82

Digital twins. A model maintained in synchrony with a physical artefact, updated from its telemetry and used to decide its maintenance, is a second entity whose identity conditions are parasitic on the first. When the artefact is repaired and the twin is not, which one is out of date? The question sounds trivial and turns out to require the whole apparatus of §13.

Ceasing to exist. The axioms above say a good deal about persistence and almost nothing about termination. When does an artefact stop existing — at decommissioning, at deregistration, at disassembly, at recycling? The historiographical turn toward maintenance suggests that the answer is bound up with the institutions of keeping rather than with the matter, which would make destruction as institutional as construction.83 Given that a large fraction of engineering effort now concerns end-of-life, this is not an academic question.

MOE-950Conclusion

The question this article began with was how something made entirely of ordinary constituents can be defective. The answer defended here is that it cannot — not if made entirely of is read as exhaustively individuated by. An engineered entity is individuated by a tuple whose slots include components and relations, but also behaviours over an envelope of conditions that never obtain, functions in four distinct senses, requirements that have authors, purposes that belong to agents, and a history that no examination of the present object could recover. Defectiveness is a relation between two of those slots under a perspective, which is why no instrument detects it and why nothing about it is subjective.

The claim is not that engineered things have non-physical parts. Every slot in the model is occupied by something an ordinary naturalist can accept. The claim is that the individuation conditions quantify over relations, modalities, and institutions, and that an ontology restricted to intrinsic physical properties at a time will get the easy cases wrong. That is not a philosophical intuition. It is a result: six out of twelve, and it fails on the geological duplicate, the counterfeit bolt, and the overhauled airframe — three cases on which no competent engineer hesitates.

Thirty axioms are offered here, and they are offered as provisional in the engineer's sense rather than the diplomat's. Each is stated so that it can be contradicted, each is attached to a case that would embarrass its denial, and the whole set is attached to a suite that a rival can run. The productive form of disagreement with this article is not a competing intuition about what artefacts really are. It is a different predicate, run against the same twelve cases, with its declared facts on the table.

Engineering has been doing metaphysics all along — in configuration baselines, in hazard analyses, in the scoping of a safety case, in the question of whether the cloud dependency is part of the system. It has simply not been doing it explicitly, which means it has not been doing it consistently.
