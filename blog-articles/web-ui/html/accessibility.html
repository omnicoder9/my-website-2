HTML Accessibility

How to build pages that work for everyone, including people who use screen readers, keyboards, switches, magnification or voice control: the WCAG principles, semantic markup, keyboard and focus behaviour, text alternatives and accessible forms; WAI-ARIA roles, states and properties, and when not to use them; and how to test with a keyboard, a screen reader, the browser's accessibility tree, automated checkers and a contrast meter. Grounded in WCAG 2.2, WAI-ARIA 1.2, the HTML Standard and the W3C accessibility mapping specifications.

Updated 15 November 2026136 references (Vancouver style)Includes exercises, trivia, live widgets, a contrast checker and an accessibility-tree playground

Every earlier article in this series has touched accessibility in passing: alt text for images, landmarks and headings, header cells, labels for form controls and, most recently, keyboard alternatives to drag and drop. This article brings those threads together. Accessibility means that people with disabilities can perceive, understand, navigate and interact with the Web, and contribute to it.1 An estimated 1.3 billion people, about one in six, experience significant disability,2 yet the 2026 WebAIM Million survey found detectable WCAG failures on 95.9% of the top million home pages.3 Most of those failures are cheap to avoid, and almost all of them start in the HTML.

Section 1 explains how assistive technology reads a page. Section 2 covers the fundamentals: the WCAG principles and the techniques that satisfy them with plain HTML. Section 3 covers WAI-ARIA, the attribute vocabulary for describing custom widgets, and section 4 covers testing. Section 5 rebuilds an inaccessible component step by step, and sections 6 to 9 hold exercises, trivia, a playground that shows the accessibility tree of any markup you type, and a quick-reference table. The widgets in this article are live: try them with Tab, Enter, Space, the arrow keys and, if you have one, a screen reader.

1Introduction

1.1 Who accessibility is for

The W3C Web Accessibility Initiative (WAI) groups the disabilities that affect Web use as auditory, cognitive, neurological, physical, speech and visual.1 People in each group use the Web in different ways. A blind user may listen to a screen reader or read a refreshable braille display; a person with low vision may magnify the screen to 400% or switch to a high-contrast colour scheme; someone with a motor impairment may use only a keyboard, a switch, an eye tracker or voice commands; a Deaf user needs captions; and a person with a cognitive or learning disability benefits from clear language, consistent navigation and forgiving forms.4

Accessibility also helps people without a permanent disability. WAI lists people with "temporary disabilities" such as a broken arm, "situational limitations" such as bright sunlight or a noisy room, older people with changing abilities, and users on slow connections or small screens.1 Captions are used in quiet carriages, keyboard shortcuts by power users, and good contrast by anyone outdoors. Tim Berners-Lee put the principle in a sentence that WAI still quotes: "The power of the Web is in its universality. Access by everyone regardless of disability is an essential aspect."1

1.2 The standards

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the reference standard. WCAG 2.2 became a W3C Recommendation on 5 October 2023 and was updated on 12 December 2024; it is also published as the international standard ISO/IEC 40500:2025.5,6 It is technology-neutral: it says what must be true ("non-text content has a text alternative") and leaves the how to supporting documents. Several companion specifications cover the how for HTML.

Laws turn the standard into an obligation. In the United States, the Section 508 standards for federal ICT incorporate WCAG 2.0 Level AA by reference,13 and a 2024 Department of Justice rule under Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act adopted WCAG 2.1 Level AA for state and local government websites.14 In the European Union, the harmonised standard EN 301 549 incorporates WCAG for public-sector sites, and the European Accessibility Act extended accessibility requirements to many private-sector products and services from 28 June 2025.15,16 Underneath them all, Article 9 of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities commits states to ensure access to information and communications technologies, "including the Internet".17

1.3 A short history

The W3C launched the Web Accessibility Initiative on 7 April 1997.18 WCAG 1.0 followed as a Recommendation on 5 May 1999, organised as 14 guidelines with checkpoints of priority 1 to 3.19 WCAG 2.0, published on 11 December 2008, replaced HTML-specific checkpoints with testable, technology-neutral success criteria grouped under the four principles described in section 2.6,20 WCAG 2.1 (5 June 2018) added 17 criteria, many for mobile, low vision and cognitive accessibility, and WCAG 2.2 added nine more, including Focus Not Obscured and Target Size (Minimum), while removing 4.1.1 Parsing as obsolete.5,6 A successor, WCAG 3, is still a Working Draft and is not expected to become a standard for several years.21

ARIA grew out of the problem of making JavaScript widgets accessible. WAI-ARIA 1.0 became a Recommendation on 20 March 2014, 1.1 on 14 December 2017 and 1.2 on 6 June 2023; version 1.3 is a Working Draft.7,22–24 Assistive technology is older than the Web. JAWS was first released for DOS in 1989 by Ted Henter's company Henter-Joyce, and for Windows in 1995.25 Apple shipped VoiceOver with Mac OS X 10.4 Tiger and later added it to the iPhone 3GS.26 The free, open-source NVDA was started by Michael Curran in 2006; he and James Teh founded NV Access in 2007.27

1.4 How assistive technology reads a page

A screen reader does not read your HTML, and it usually does not read the DOM either. The browser builds a second tree from the DOM and the computed styles, the accessibility tree, and exposes it to assistive technology through the operating system's accessibility API.28,29 Each node in that tree is an accessible object with a role (what it is: button, link, heading), a name (what it is called), optionally a description, a value, and states such as checked, expanded or disabled.7,29 HTML-AAM specifies how each HTML element maps to these, and AccName specifies how names are computed.9,10

Three consequences follow. First, what is not in the accessibility tree does not exist for a screen reader user: an element hidden with display: none, the hidden attribute or aria-hidden="true" is dropped.30,31 Second, the role and name come from the markup, so a <div onclick> is announced as plain text, or not at all, while a <button> is announced as a button.8,9 Third, ARIA attributes change only this tree. They change what is announced, never how the element behaves; a div with role="button" still needs script to be focusable and to respond to Enter and Space.7,32

2Accessibility fundamentals

Most accessibility comes free with HTML used as designed. Native elements already have roles, accessible names, keyboard behaviour and states that browsers map to the accessibility API; the work is mostly to choose the right element, give it a name, and not break what it does.8,33 This section starts with the WCAG framework that defines "accessible", then covers the HTML techniques that meet it.

2.1 WCAG principles and structure

WCAG 2.2 has four layers. At the top are four principles. Under them sit 13 guidelines, which are goals and are not testable. Each guideline has testable success criteria, 86 in all once the obsolete 4.1.1 Parsing is set aside, and each criterion has a conformance level: A (the minimum), AA or AAA.5,6 Around the normative text are non-normative Understanding documents, which explain the intent of each criterion, and Techniques, which describe ways to meet it and common failures.34,35

A page conforms at a level only if it meets every criterion at that level and below; WCAG has no partial credit. Conformance applies to whole pages, and to every page in a process such as checkout, so one inaccessible step breaks conformance for the whole process.5,37 Level AA is the target in nearly every law and policy mentioned in section 1.2. WAI itself does not recommend requiring AAA for entire sites, because some AAA criteria cannot be met for all content.37

2.2 POUR: Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust

The four principles are the foundation of WCAG. The specification states each one as a requirement on content, and the Understanding documents add that if any one of them is not true, users with disabilities will not be able to use the Web.5,34

Perceivable
"Information and user interface components must be presentable to users in ways they can perceive."5 Content that exists only as an image, a sound or a colour excludes someone. Text alternatives (1.1), captions and audio description (1.2), structure that survives a change of presentation (1.3) and sufficient contrast and resizing (1.4) cover this principle.5
Operable
"User interface components and navigation must be operable."5 The guidelines are keyboard access (2.1), enough time (2.2), no content that causes seizures (2.3), navigability (2.4) and input modalities beyond the keyboard, such as pointer gestures and target size (2.5).5
Understandable
"Information and the operation of user interface must be understandable."5 Readable text with a declared language (3.1), predictable behaviour (3.2) and help with input, such as labels and error suggestions (3.3).5
Robust
"Content must be robust enough that it can be interpreted by a wide variety of user agents, including assistive technologies."5 In practice, guideline 4.1 means that every component exposes a correct name, role and value (4.1.2) and that status messages can be announced (4.1.3).54,55

2.3 Semantic markup

Semantic elements carry meaning that browsers expose as roles. A button element has the implicit role button, nav has navigation, h2 has heading with level 2, ul has list, and so on; ARIA in HTML lists the implicit role of every element.8 Screen reader users navigate by these roles. In WebAIM's 2026 screen reader survey, 67.8% of respondents said their first move on a long page is to navigate through its headings, and 88.3% found heading levels very or somewhat useful.56

Other semantic choices matter just as much. Use one h1 for the page's topic and do not skip levels for visual size, because the levels form the outline in Figure 4.39,62 Use ul, ol and dl for lists so screen readers can announce "list, 5 items".8 Use a href for navigation and button for actions: links are activated by Enter, buttons by Enter and Space, and users expect a link to change the URL.57,63 Mark up data tables with th and caption, as covered in part 6.39 Set the page language with <html lang="en"> so speech synthesisers pronounce the text correctly, and give the page a descriptive title, the first thing a screen reader announces.51,64,65

2.4 Keyboard navigation

WCAG 2.1.1 requires every function to be operable through a keyboard interface, and 2.1.2 requires that focus never gets stuck.43,44 Keyboard access matters to screen reader users, to people who cannot use a mouse, and to switch and voice-control users, whose devices often emulate a keyboard.4,43 The HTML Standard defines which elements are focusable and how Tab moves between them, which it calls sequential focus navigation.66,67 Links with href, buttons, form controls, summary and elements with a tabindex are focusable by default.66,68

Table 5. Standard keyboard interactions
KeyExpected behaviour

Arrow keysMove within a composite widget: radio group, tabs, menu, listbox, grid, slider

EscClose a dialog, menu or popup and return focus to what opened it

Based on the ARIA Authoring Practices Guide, "Developing a keyboard interface", and the HTML Standard's activation behaviour.57,63

The tabindex attribute has three useful kinds of value.68,69

  • tabindex="0" makes an element focusable and puts it in the tab order where it appears in the document. Use it for custom widgets built from non-interactive elements, and for scrollable regions that keyboard users need to scroll.
  • tabindex="-1" makes an element focusable by script (el.focus()) but leaves it out of the tab order. Use it for focus targets such as a dialog's heading, an error summary or the inactive tabs of a tab list.
  • A positive value such as tabindex="3" puts the element ahead of all tabindex="0" elements in a separate ordering. The HTML Standard tells authors to use caution with values other than 0 and −1; in practice they almost always produce a focus order that no longer matches the reading order, failing 2.4.3.46,69

Keyboard users must be able to see where focus is (2.4.7 Focus Visible), and WCAG 2.2 adds that the focused element must not be completely hidden behind author content such as a sticky header or cookie banner (2.4.11).47,48 Never remove the focus outline without a replacement. The :focus-visible pseudo-class matches when the browser decides a focus indicator should be shown, typically for keyboard focus but not for a mouse click on a button, which lets you style keyboard focus strongly without annoying mouse users.70,71 scroll-padding-top keeps focused elements clear of a sticky header.48

2.5 Screen reader compatibility

A screen reader turns the accessibility tree into speech or braille. In WebAIM's 2026 survey of 1,780 screen reader users, the primary desktop screen readers were JAWS (55.0%), NVDA (32.9%) and VoiceOver (6.5%); on mobile, 72.2% used VoiceOver and 29.5% TalkBack.56 Narrator is built into Windows and Orca into GNOME on Linux.56,73 Table 6 shows where each runs.

Screen readers on the desktop have two modes. In browse mode (NVDA) or the virtual cursor (JAWS), the screen reader intercepts keystrokes: arrow keys read line by line, and single letters jump by element type, such as H for the next heading, D (NVDA) or R (JAWS) for the next landmark, K for links and F for form fields. In focus mode or forms mode, keystrokes go to the page so the user can type in a field or operate a widget.60,74 The screen reader switches modes automatically when focus lands on a text field or on an element with a widget role, which is one reason correct roles matter: a custom widget without the right role may never receive its arrow keys.60,63 VoiceOver on macOS uses the VO keys (Control+Option) and the rotor instead.61

Being compatible with screen readers therefore comes down to a short list: correct roles from semantic elements, accessible names on every control and image, states such as expanded, checked and current exposed as attributes, a logical heading structure, a lang attribute, and dynamic changes announced through live regions (section 3.9) or focus management (section 2.8).39,54,55 Different screen readers announce the same tree slightly differently. That is expected; the goal is the same information, not the same words.74

2.6 Alternative text

WCAG 1.1.1 requires a text alternative for non-text content that serves the equivalent purpose.38 For img, that alternative is the alt attribute. The HTML Standard's section on providing alternative text (§4.8.4.4) describes the right value for more than a dozen situations, and WAI's alt decision tree condenses them into five questions.76,77 Images were covered in depth in part 4; Figure 6 summarises the decisions.

A few rules cover most cases.76,78,79

  • Write for the context, not the pixels. The same photo of a mug may need alt="Blue mug, 350 ml, with a white handle" on a product page and alt="" in a decorative banner.
  • Do not start with "image of" or "picture of"; the screen reader already says "graphic" or "image".
  • Keep it short, about a sentence. Put long descriptions, such as the data in a chart, in visible text or a linked table and refer to it from a figcaption.
  • An empty alt="" marks an image as decorative, and browsers then drop it from the accessibility tree.8,76 An absent alt is different: it is an error, and screen readers may announce the file name instead.78
  • title is not a substitute. It is shown only on hover, is unavailable to touch and keyboard users, and is used for the name only as a last resort.10,80

2.7 Accessible forms

The 2026 WebAIM Million found missing form labels on 51% of home pages, the third most common failure after low-contrast text and missing alternative text.3 Labelling is the core of form accessibility, covered by 1.3.1, 3.3.2 and 4.1.2: every control needs an accessible name, and the visible label should be that name.39,53,54 The forms article covers form elements in detail; this section collects the accessibility rules.

  1. Label every control with label. Either associate it with for and id, or wrap the control. Clicking the label then focuses or toggles the control, which also enlarges the target.82,83
  2. Group related controls with fieldset and legend. Radio buttons and checkboxes need a group label ("Delivery speed") as well as their own; screen readers announce the legend when focus enters the group.83,84
  3. Attach hints and errors with aria-describedby. The description is read after the name and role, so the user hears "Password, edit, protected, At least 12 characters".10,85
  4. Do not rely on placeholder. It disappears when the user types, often has low contrast, and is only a fallback for the accessible name.10,83
  5. Mark required fields in text and code. Use the required attribute, which exposes the required state, and say "required" in the label rather than relying on a red asterisk.40,53
  6. Identify errors in text (3.3.1). Set aria-invalid="true" on the field, describe the error next to it, and either move focus to an error summary or announce it.52,86
  7. Use autocomplete for personal data. WCAG 1.3.5 (AA) requires the purpose of fields about the user to be identifiable in code; HTML's autofill tokens such as email, given-name and postal-code do this and let browsers fill the fields in.87,88

2.8 Focus management

When a script changes what is on screen, it often has to move keyboard focus too. If a dialog opens and focus stays on the button behind it, a keyboard user is still "behind" the dialog; if the focused element is deleted, focus falls back to the body and the user is sent back to the top of the page.46,89 The rules are simple to state:

  • Opening a dialog or panel: move focus into it, to the first control or to its heading (with tabindex="-1").89
  • While open, a modal dialog keeps focus inside and makes the rest of the page inert.90,91
  • Closing: return focus to the element that opened it, normally the trigger button.89
  • Deleting the focused item: move focus to a sensible neighbour, such as the next item or the list's heading.63
  • Navigating in a single-page app: after a route change, move focus to the new page's h1 or main region and update document.title, since no page load tells the screen reader that anything happened.46,65

HTML now does most of the dialog work. dialog.showModal() opens a modal dialog in the top layer, makes everything outside it inert, runs the dialog focusing steps to focus the first focusable element (or one marked autofocus), closes on Esc, and, when the dialog closes, returns focus to the element that was focused before it opened.90,92 The inert attribute provides the same blocking for any subtree: inert content cannot be focused or clicked, and per the HTML Standard browsers "do not expose the inert nodes to accessibility APIs or assistive technologies".91,93

3ARIA: Accessible Rich Internet Applications

3.1 What ARIA is, and what it is not

WAI-ARIA is a vocabulary of attributes, one role attribute and several dozen aria-* attributes, that authors add to markup to describe user-interface semantics that the host language cannot express on its own.7,94 It was created for the era of JavaScript widgets: a tree view, a tab list or a combobox built from div elements has no native semantics, and ARIA lets the author say "this is a tab, it is selected, and it controls that panel".7,12

ARIA affects only the accessibility tree. The specification is explicit that it does not change an element's behaviour, appearance or focusability; those still come from HTML, CSS and script.7,32 The ARIA Authoring Practices Guide puts it as its first principle, "A role is a promise": adding role="button" promises screen reader users that the element will act like a button, and the author must then keep that promise with tabindex="0" and handlers for Enter and Space.32 The same guide's warning, "No ARIA is better than Bad ARIA", reflects a measurable pattern: in the 2026 WebAIM Million, home pages using ARIA averaged 59.1 detected errors, against 42 on pages without it.3,32 ARIA is not the cause of the errors so much as a marker of complex, custom interfaces, but misused ARIA makes those interfaces worse.3

Every ARIA attribute is one of three kinds:7

  • Roles say what an element is: role="tab", role="dialog".
  • Properties describe characteristics that rarely change after the page loads: aria-labelledby, aria-controls, aria-haspopup.
  • States describe conditions that change with interaction: aria-expanded, aria-checked, aria-selected, aria-disabled.

The specification notes that the distinction between states and properties is fuzzy and mostly matters to authors: both are written as aria-* attributes and treated alike by browsers.7

3.2 Roles

WAI-ARIA 1.2 sorts roles into six categories: abstract roles, widget roles, document structure roles, landmark roles, live region roles and window roles.7 Abstract roles, such as widget or landmark, exist only to build the taxonomy and must never be used in content.7 Figure 8 shows the categories with common examples and the HTML element that already provides each role.

Every HTML element already has an implicit role, so adding the same role explicitly, as in <button role="button">, is redundant, and ARIA in HTML says authors should not do it.8 An explicit role replaces the implicit one: <h2 role="tab"> is a tab and no longer a heading.7,8 ARIA in HTML also lists which roles are allowed on each element; <a href role="button"> is permitted, for example, but <button role="heading"> is not.8 Two roles deserve special mention. role="none" (or its older synonym presentation) removes an element's semantics but not its content, which is useful for a layout table; and generic is the role of a div or span, a nameless container that screen readers skip over.7,8

4Accessibility testing



4.1 A testing strategy


No single method finds every problem. Automated checkers are fast and repeatable but can verify only what is machine-decidable, such as whether an image has an alt attribute, not whether the text is right. In a 2017 test by the UK Government Digital Service, a deliberately inaccessible page with 143 barriers was run through ten free tools: together they found 71%, the best single tool 41%, and the worst 17%.110 Deque, the maker of axe-core, reports that across more than 13,000 audited pages its automated rules found 57.38% of all issues by count, because the most common issues, such as contrast, are also the most automatable.111 Both numbers lead to the same strategy: automate what can be automated, and test the rest by hand and with real assistive technology.110,112





WAI's Easy Checks is a good first pass for anyone: page title, image text alternatives, headings, contrast, text resize, keyboard access and forms, each checkable in minutes without special tools.113 For a full audit, the WCAG-EM methodology describes how to define scope, sample pages and report results.114



4.2 Keyboard-only testing


Keyboard testing needs no software and finds many serious problems. Put the mouse away, click into the browser's address bar, and press Tab into the page.72,113 Then work through this checklist.


    

  1. Can you see focus at every step? If focus disappears, an element has outline: none, is off-screen, or is behind a sticky header (2.4.7, 2.4.11).47,48
    2. 

  2. Is the order logical? Focus should follow the visual reading order (2.4.3). Jumps usually come from positive tabindex or CSS that reorders content (order, flex-direction: row-reverse, absolute positioning).46,68
    3. 

  3. Is everything reachable and operable? Every link, button, field, menu and custom widget must work with Tab, Enter, Space, arrows and Esc as in Table 5 (2.1.1). Click handlers on divs are the usual culprits.43,63
    4. 

  4. Can you always get out? Embedded media players, maps, rich-text editors and modal dialogs must not trap focus (2.1.2). A modal dialog should keep focus inside while open, but Esc must close it.44,89
    5. 

  5. Is there a skip link, and does it work? It should be the first thing Tab reaches and should move focus, not just scroll (2.4.1).45
    6. 

  6. Do hidden things stay hidden? Focus should not land on invisible elements, such as links in a collapsed menu or an off-screen drawer. Use hidden or inert on closed panels.46,91
    7. 

  7. Does focus go somewhere sensible after changes? Open and close dialogs, delete items, submit forms with errors and change routes, and check that focus follows the rules in section 2.8.46,89
    8. 

  8. Does focus cause surprises? Moving focus onto a field must not submit a form or open a new window (3.2.1 On Focus).115
    9. 



On macOS, Safari and Firefox do not move focus to links with Tab by default; enable "Keyboard navigation" in System Settings and, in Safari, "Press Tab to highlight each item on a webpage" before testing.72 Firefox's Accessibility Inspector can overlay numbered markers on every focusable element to show the tabbing order at a glance.116

4.5 Automated accessibility testing


The most widely used rules engine is Deque's open-source axe-core. It runs in the page, checks the DOM against rules mapped to WCAG success criteria, and reports violations together with "incomplete" results that need a human decision.111,122 Google's Lighthouse, built into Chrome DevTools, bases its accessibility audits on axe-core and reduces them to a weighted score.123 Other well-known tools include WebAIM's WAVE browser extension, the W3C Nu HTML Checker for markup validity, and Firefox's and Chrome's built-in issue checkers.116,124,125

    .analyze();

Automated tools are good at missing names (images, buttons, links, form fields), insufficient contrast for plain text on solid backgrounds, missing lang, invalid or misplaced ARIA, duplicate IDs referenced by ARIA, empty headings and skipped table headers.3,122 The WebAIM Million's six most common failures, all detected automatically, show where the effort pays off.3





What tools cannot judge is just as important: whether alt text is accurate, whether a heading describes its section, whether the focus order makes sense, whether a custom widget responds to the right keys, whether a live region announces at the right time, whether captions are correct, and whether content is understandable.110,112 A clean automated report is the beginning of testing, not the end. Treat accessibility rules like any other test suite: run linters such as eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y in the editor, axe in component and end-to-end tests, and fail the build on new violations so regressions are caught before release.112,122



4.6 Contrast and visual considerations


Low-contrast text is the most common failure on the Web, found on 83.9% of the home pages in the 2026 WebAIM Million.3 WCAG measures contrast with a ratio computed from the relative luminance of the two colours, a measure of brightness normalised to 0 for black and 1 for white.36



The contrast formulaFor each sRGB channel c = value/255: if c ≤ 0.04045 then c′ = c/12.92, else c′ = ((c + 0.055)/1.055)2.4. Then L = 0.2126 R′ + 0.7152 G′ + 0.0722 B′, and the contrast ratio is (L1 + 0.05)/(L2 + 0.05), where L1 is the lighter colour. Ratios range from 1:1 to 21:1. Before May 2021 WCAG printed the threshold as 0.03928, which has no practical effect on results.5,36

Contrast is one of several visual requirements that come from HTML and CSS decisions:


    

  • Colour alone (1.4.1). Links in body text need an underline or another non-colour cue; error fields need an icon or text as well as red; charts need labels or patterns. Roughly one in twelve men has a colour vision deficiency, and forced-colours modes remove author colours altogether.40,127
    • 

  • Resize and reflow (1.4.4, 1.4.10). Text must scale to 200% without loss, and at 400% zoom on a 1280-pixel window, which equals a 320-pixel viewport, content must reflow into one column without horizontal scrolling. Use relative units, responsive layout and no fixed heights on text containers.41,128
    • 

  • Text spacing (1.4.12). Content must survive users overriding line height to 1.5, paragraph spacing to 2 times, and letter and word spacing to 0.12 and 0.16 times the font size.129
    • 

  • Target size (2.5.8). Pointer targets should be at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, or spaced so that a 24-pixel circle on each does not overlap another target; inline links in text are exempt.50
    • 

  • Motion (2.2.2, 2.3.3). Anything that moves for more than five seconds needs a pause control, and non-essential animation triggered by interaction should be removable. The prefers-reduced-motion media feature reports that the user has asked the system to minimise motion.130–132
    • 

  • Forced colours. Windows high-contrast themes replace author colours with a small user-chosen palette. The forced-colors: active media query lets you restore meaning lost with backgrounds and box shadows, for example by adding a transparent outline that becomes visible.132,133
    • 

  • Flashing (2.3.1). Nothing should flash more than three times in any one-second period unless it is below the flash thresholds.134
    •

Tabs are a good test of everything in this article, because HTML has no tab element. The version below is typical of what audits find: it works with a mouse and looks right, but it fails 2.1.1, 1.3.1 and 4.1.2.39,43,54



Before: a mouse-only tab widgetHTML
<div class="tabs">
  <div class="tab active" onclick="show(0)">Description</div>

<div class="panel">…</div>

<div class="panel" style="display:none">…</div>


A screen reader reads this as three lines of text followed by the visible panel. Nothing says they are tabs, which one is selected, or that clicking does anything, and keyboard users cannot reach them at all.9,43 The repair follows the APG tabs pattern step by step.107


    

  1. Roles. The container gets role="tablist" with a name; each tab is a button with role="tab" (a button keeps native focusability and activation); each panel gets role="tabpanel".8,107
    2. 

  2. Relationships. Each tab has aria-controls pointing to its panel; each panel has aria-labelledby pointing back to its tab, so it is named "Reviews".106,107
    3. 

  3. State. The active tab has aria-selected="true", the others "false". Inactive panels get hidden.30,107
    4. 

  4. Keyboard. The tab list is one Tab stop. Only the selected tab has tabindex="0" and the others -1 (a roving tabindex); ←/→ move between tabs, Home/End go to the first and last, and the next Tab goes into the panel.63,107
    5. 

  5. Focusable panel. Each panel has tabindex="0" when it contains no focusable content, so keyboard users can reach and scroll it.107
    6. 

  6. Visuals from state. The selected style comes from [aria-selected="true"], there is a :focus-visible style, and the selected tab differs by more than colour (a thicker border).40,47
    7.

<script>
  const tabs = [...document.querySelectorAll("[role=tab]")];
  function select(tab, focus = true) {
    for (const t of tabs) {
      const on = t === tab;
      t.setAttribute("aria-selected", String(on));
      t.tabIndex = on ? 0 : -1;                         // roving tabindex
      document.getElementById(t.getAttribute("aria-controls")).hidden = !on;
    }
    if (focus) tab.focus();
    document.getElementById("log").textContent =
      "Selected: " + tab.textContent + " (tab " + (tabs.indexOf(tab) + 1) + " of " + tabs.length + ")";
  }
  tabs.forEach((tab, i) => {
    tab.addEventListener("click", () => select(tab));
    tab.addEventListener("keydown", (e) => {
      const n = tabs.length;
      const next = { ArrowRight: (i + 1) % n, ArrowLeft: (i - 1 + n) % n, Home: 0, End: n - 1 }[e.key];
      if (next !== undefined) { e.preventDefault(); select(tabs[next]); }
    });
  });
</script>

Exercise 3: Calculate a contrast ratio


Is white text (#FFFFFF) on the brand orange #FF6600 acceptable for 16-pixel body text? For a 24-pixel heading? Work it out from the formula in section 4.6, then check with the contrast checker.


Show solution
