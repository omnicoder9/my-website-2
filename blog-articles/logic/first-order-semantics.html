1. Introduction

A first-order formula such as ∀x∃y Rxy is, by itself, only a string of symbols. It becomes true or false only when we say what the quantifiers range over and what R stands for. Semantics is the part of logic that makes this precise: it pairs a formal language with a class of mathematical objects, called structures, and defines when a formula is true in one of them[1,2]. The study of how formal languages are interpreted in structures is model theory[3,4].

The companion article First-Order Logic (section 5) stated the definitions of structure and satisfaction in compact form so that it could use them to check quantifier laws. This article goes the other way: it takes semantics itself as the subject, unpacks each component of a structure, works through the satisfaction relation clause by clause, and then develops the semantic notions of model, countermodel, validity, consequence, satisfiability, equivalence and consistency, together with the main theorems that connect them. The syntax (terms, formulas, free and bound variables, sentences) is assumed from that article, sections 2 to 4.

The method used throughout is due to Alfred Tarski. In his 1933 monograph on truth (Polish original; English translation 1956) Tarski showed how to define truth for a formal language by first defining a more general relation, satisfaction of a formula by an assignment of objects to its variables, by recursion on the structure of formulas[5,6]. In 1936 he used the same apparatus to define logical consequence: a conclusion follows from premises when every model of the premises is a model of the conclusion[7,8]. The explicitly relativised notion of truth in a structure, which is the form used in modern textbooks, was published by Tarski and Robert Vaught in 1957[9,6], and Tarski proposed the name “theory of models” for the emerging field in 1954[10,3].

Example 1.1 (one sentence, two verdicts)

Read R as “is less than”. The sentence ∀x∃y Rxy (“everything has something greater than it”) is true when the quantifiers range over the natural numbers ℕ = {0, 1, 2, …}, since every n has n + 1 above it. It is false when they range over {0, 1, 2}, since nothing is greater than 2 (checked). Nothing in the sentence changed; only the structure did. Semantics is the theory of exactly this dependence[1,11].

2. Structures

2.1 Signatures and structures

A first-order language is fixed by its signature (also called a vocabulary or similarity type): a set of non-logical symbols, each classified as a constant symbol, a predicate (relation) symbol of some arity n ≥ 1, or a function symbol of some arity n ≥ 1[4,12,13]. The logical symbols (connectives, quantifiers, variables, parentheses and, in languages with identity, the symbol =) are common to all first-order languages and are not part of any signature[1,12]. The distinction matters semantically: non-logical symbols get their meaning from a structure, while logical symbols mean the same thing in every structure[1,8].

Definition 2.1 (structure)

Let σ be a signature. A σ-structure 𝔄 consists of

  1. a non-empty set A = |𝔄|, the domain (or universe) of 𝔄, and
  2. an interpretation function that assigns
    • to each constant symbol c an element c𝔄 ∈ A;
    • to each n-place predicate symbol P a relation P𝔄 ⊆ Aⁿ;
    • to each n-place function symbol f a function f𝔄 : Aⁿ → A.

This is the standard definition in Enderton, Chang and Keisler, Hodges and van Dalen[1,13,12,14]. Structures are also called interpretations, models or relational systems; Tarski and Vaught used the last term[9,2].

A structure is often written as a tuple listing its domain followed by the interpretations of the symbols, for example 𝔑 = (ℕ, 0, S, +, ·, <) for the natural numbers with zero, successor, addition, multiplication and the usual order[1,13]. The signature of 𝔑 then has one constant symbol, one 1-place and two 2-place function symbols and one 2-place predicate symbol. Two quite different objects can be structures for the same signature: a 2-place predicate symbol can be interpreted as “less than” on numbers, as “is a parent of” on people, or as an arbitrary set of ordered pairs of letters. Semantics deliberately ignores what the elements are and records only how the symbols are interpreted on them[12,4].

Two structural notions recur below. A structure 𝔄′ for a larger signature σ′ ⊇ σ is an expansion of a σ-structure 𝔄 (and 𝔄 is the reduct of 𝔄′ to σ) if the two have the same domain and agree on every symbol of σ[13,12]. An isomorphism between σ-structures 𝔄 and 𝔅 is a bijection h : A → B that preserves the interpretation of every symbol: h(c𝔄) = c𝔅, (a₁,…,aₙ) ∈ P𝔄 iff (h(a₁),…,h(aₙ)) ∈ P𝔅, and h(f𝔄(a₁,…,aₙ)) = f𝔅(h(a₁),…,h(aₙ))[1,13,12].

2.2 Domain of discourse

The domain is the set of objects the quantifiers range over: ∀x means “for every element of the domain” and ∃x means “for some element of the domain”[1,2]. The domain is the only place where the quantifiers get their range, which is why the same sentence can change truth value when only the domain changes, as in Example 1.1. In 1933 Tarski spoke of truth only for the case in which the domain is the class of all individuals, and of “correctness in the domain A” for other domains; making the domain a parameter of the structure belongs to the later, model-theoretic notion of truth[6].

Non-emptiness. Classical first-order logic requires the domain to be non-empty[2,1,4]. The requirement is reflected in valid formulas: ∀x Px → ∃x Px and ∃x(x = x) are valid only because every structure has at least one element (the first was checked over sizes 1 to 3; in an empty domain its antecedent would be vacuously true and its consequent false)[1,15]. Logics that admit the empty domain, sometimes called inclusive logics, are studied alongside free logic, and they must adjust the quantifier rules accordingly[15].

Size. First-order sentences with identity can constrain the size of the domain. Writing ∃≥n for the sentence

∃x₁…∃xₙ ⋀i<j ¬(xi = xj)

(“there are at least n things”), the sentence ∃≥n is true in a structure exactly when its domain has at least n elements, and ∃≥n ∧ ¬∃≥n+1 is true exactly when it has exactly n elements[1,13]. The infinite set {∃≥1, ∃≥2, …} is true precisely in the infinite structures. The converse task is impossible: by the compactness theorem (section 5.3), no set of first-order sentences is true in exactly the finite structures[1].

Example 2.1 (the domain decides)

The sentence ∃x∃y ¬(x = y) contains no non-logical symbols at all, so it is true or false in a structure depending only on the domain: true if the domain has two or more elements, false if it has one. Likewise ∀x∀y(x = y) is true exactly in the one-element structures. Neither sentence is valid and neither is unsatisfiable (both checked)[1].

2.3 Interpretation functions

The interpretation function is the dictionary of a structure: it sends each non-logical symbol to a semantic value of the right kind, an element for a constant, a relation for a predicate, a function for a function symbol[1,2,12]. The superscript notation c𝔄, P𝔄, f𝔄 names the value of the symbol under the interpretation of 𝔄[1,13]; other books write I(c), I(P), I(f) for an interpretation ⟨d, I⟩[2].

Three features of interpretation functions are worth making explicit.

  • Extensionality. A predicate is interpreted by a set of tuples, its extension, and nothing else. Two predicates with the same extension in a structure are indistinguishable by any first-order formula evaluated there, whatever they “mean” informally[1,2]. This is one reason why first-order logic is called an extensional logic.
  • Type matching. The value of a symbol must match its syntactic category and arity, so that every atomic formula receives a definite truth value. An n-place predicate gets a subset of Aⁿ; it cannot be given a set of pairs if n = 3[1,12].
  • Logical symbols are not interpreted. The connectives and quantifiers are handled by fixed clauses of the satisfaction definition (section 3.2), and identity is always interpreted as genuine sameness of elements. Only the non-logical vocabulary varies from structure to structure[1,8].

Which symbols count as logical is a choice built into the definition of consequence. Tarski noted in 1936 that his definition depends on how the vocabulary is divided into logical and non-logical terms, and he did not claim to have found a principled criterion for the division[7,8].

2.4 Constants

A constant symbol c is interpreted as a single element c𝔄 of the domain, its denotation or referent[1,2]. Constants behave like proper names, and classical semantics imposes two conditions on them that natural-language names need not satisfy:

  • Every constant denotes. The interpretation function is total, so c𝔄 always exists and lies in the domain. That is why Pc ⊨ ∃x Px and ∀x Px ⊨ Pc are valid (both checked). Logics that drop this requirement, so that a name like “Pegasus” can fail to refer, are free logics[2,15].
  • Names need not be unique or exhaustive. Two constants can denote the same element (then c = d is true), and many elements may have no name at all[1,12]. For instance, in (ℝ, 0, 1, +, ·) only countably many reals are denoted by closed terms, since there are only countably many such terms[1].

Because elements can lack names, the quantifier clause cannot be stated as “∀x φ is true iff every instance φ(c) is true”: an unnamed element could be a counterexample that no instance mentions. Tarski's use of assignments to variables, rather than substitution of names, is what lets the quantifiers reach every element[6,1]. When a structure is expanded with a new constant for each element (the language LA of the diagram method), the substitutional reading becomes correct for that expanded language[13,12].

Example 2.2 (co-denoting constants)

Let the signature contain constants h and p and let 𝔄 have the solar-system bodies as its domain, with h𝔄 = p𝔄 = Venus (for “Hesperus” and “Phosphorus”). Then h = p is true in 𝔄. In a different structure 𝔅 with h𝔅 ≠ p𝔅 it is false, so h = p is a contingent identity, true in some structures and false in others, never a logical truth[1].

2.5 Predicates

An n-place predicate symbol P is interpreted as an n-ary relation P𝔄 ⊆ Aⁿ, the set of n-tuples of which the predicate is true[1,13,2]. A 1-place predicate receives a subset of the domain, a 2-place predicate a set of ordered pairs, and so on. Any subset is allowed, including the empty set and the whole of Aⁿ; which subsets are allowed is what makes first-order semantics “full” in its treatment of non-logical symbols, as opposed to the treatment of predicate variables in second-order logic[1].

  • Atomic truth. The atomic formula P t₁ … tₙ is satisfied exactly when the tuple of values of t₁, …, tₙ belongs to P𝔄[1,2].
  • Identity. In first-order logic with identity, = is a logical predicate whose interpretation is fixed in every structure as the diagonal {(a, a) : a ∈ A}. Structures in which = is interpreted as an arbitrary congruence relation are called non-normal, and the restriction to normal structures is part of the definition of classical first-order logic with identity[16,1].
  • Sentence letters. Some presentations allow 0-place predicate letters, which behave like the sentence letters of propositional logic: such a letter is interpreted directly as a truth value[2]. Propositional logic thereby sits inside first-order logic as the fragment with only 0-place predicates.

Example 2.3 (one predicate, two structures)

Let D = {0, 1, 2, 3}. In 𝔄₁ interpret the 2-place predicate R as the order {(a, b) : a < b}; in 𝔄₂, as divisibility {(a, b) : a ≠ 0 and a divides b}. The sentence ∀x∀y∀z(Rxy ∧ Ryz → Rxz) (transitivity) is true in both. The sentence ∀x ¬Rxx (irreflexivity) is true in 𝔄₁ and false in 𝔄₂, because (1, 1) ∈ R𝔄₂. (Both checked.)

2.6 Functions

An n-place function symbol f is interpreted as a function f𝔄 : Aⁿ → A that is total (defined on every n-tuple) and closed in the domain (its values lie in A)[1,13,12]. Function symbols let terms be built up, as in f(g(x), c), and every term then denotes an element of the domain (section 3.1).

Totality is a real constraint. Division on the real numbers is undefined at 0, and subtraction on ℕ is not closed (2 − 5 ∉ ℕ), so neither can be the interpretation of a function symbol on those domains as it stands. Standard responses are to replace the function by its graph, a 3-place predicate D(x, y, z) meaning x / y = z, or to extend it to a total function by an arbitrary convention (for example, setting x / 0 = 0, or using truncated subtraction on ℕ)[1,14]. Logics with genuinely partial functions, in which a term may fail to denote, belong to the family of free logics[15].

Function symbols are not strictly necessary: any n-place function symbol can be eliminated in favour of an (n+1)-place predicate F together with the axiom ∀x₁…∀xₙ∃!y F x₁…xₙ y stating that F is the graph of a total function[1,14]. Similarly, a constant symbol behaves exactly like a 0-place function symbol, and some texts treat it that way[12,13].

Example 2.4 (arithmetic)

In 𝔑 = (ℕ, 0, S, +, ·, <) the closed term S(S(0)) · S(S(0)) denotes 4 and S(0) + S(S(0)) denotes 3, so the atomic sentence S(0) + S(S(0)) < S(S(0)) · S(S(0)) is true, and so is ∀x ¬(S(x) = 0), since 0 is not a successor[1]. In the structure ℤ₃ = ({0,1,2}, 0, S, +, ·) of arithmetic modulo 3, the same symbols are interpreted cyclically, S(2) = 0, and ∀x ¬(S(x) = 0) is false (checked).

3. Variable assignments

3.1 Assignments and the values of terms

A structure fixes the meaning of constants, predicates and function symbols, but not of variables. A formula with free variables, such as Rxy, is neither true nor false in 𝔄 until its variables are given values. Tarski's solution is to supply these values separately by an assignment[5,6].

Definition 3.1 (assignment, x-variant)

A variable assignment into a structure 𝔄 is a function s from the set of all variables into the domain A[1,2]. (Tarski worked with infinite sequences of objects, the i-th term being the value of the i-th variable; Mendelson's textbook keeps that formulation[5,16].)

For a variable x and element a ∈ A, the x-variant s(x↦a) is the assignment that sends x to a and agrees with s on every other variable[1,2].

Definition 3.2 (value of a term)

Given 𝔄 and s, the value t𝔄[s] of a term t is defined by recursion on t[1,14,12]:

  • x𝔄[s] = s(x) for a variable x;
  • c𝔄[s] = c𝔄 for a constant c;
  • f(t₁,…,tₙ)𝔄[s] = f𝔄(t₁𝔄[s], …, tₙ𝔄[s]).

Because function symbols denote total functions and constants always denote, every term has exactly one value under every assignment. A closed term (one without variables) has the same value under every assignment[1]. In 𝔄, with s(x) = 1, the term f(f(x)) has value f𝔄(f𝔄(1)) = f𝔄(2) = 0; with s(x) = 2 it has value 1.

3.2 Satisfaction

Satisfaction is a three-place relation between a structure, a formula and an assignment, written 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s] and read “𝔄 satisfies φ under s” or “s satisfies φ in 𝔄”[1,13,6]. It is defined by recursion on the construction of φ, so the satisfaction of a compound formula depends only on the satisfaction of its immediate subformulas[6].

Definition 3.3 (Tarskian satisfaction)

  1. 𝔄 ⊨ P t₁…tₙ [s] iff (t₁𝔄[s], …, tₙ𝔄[s]) ∈ P𝔄.
  2. 𝔄 ⊨ t₁ = t₂ [s] iff t₁𝔄[s] = t₂𝔄[s].
  3. 𝔄 ⊨ ¬φ [s] iff 𝔄 ⊭ φ [s].
  4. 𝔄 ⊨ (φ ∧ ψ) [s] iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ [s] and 𝔄 ⊨ ψ [s].
  5. 𝔄 ⊨ (φ ∨ ψ) [s] iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ [s] or 𝔄 ⊨ ψ [s] (or both).
  6. 𝔄 ⊨ (φ → ψ) [s] iff 𝔄 ⊭ φ [s] or 𝔄 ⊨ ψ [s].
  7. 𝔄 ⊨ (φ ↔ ψ) [s] iff either both or neither of 𝔄 ⊨ φ [s], 𝔄 ⊨ ψ [s].
  8. 𝔄 ⊨ ∀x φ [s] iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ [s(x↦a)] for every a ∈ A.
  9. 𝔄 ⊨ ∃x φ [s] iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ [s(x↦a)] for at least one a ∈ A.

Clauses 1 to 9 follow Enderton and the SEP entry on classical logic[1,2]; texts that take fewer primitives (for example only ¬, →, ∀) define the rest as abbreviations, and the clauses above then become derived facts[1,16].

The quantifier clauses explain why satisfaction, not truth, must be the recursively defined notion. The formula Rxy under the quantifier in ∀y Rxy has free variables and is neither true nor false on its own, so the truth of ∀y Rxy cannot be computed from the truth of its part. It can be computed from which assignments satisfy the part[6,5].

Example 3.1 (satisfaction step by step)

In the running structure 𝔄, evaluate φ = ∃y(Rxy ∧ Py), “x is below some element of P”, under an assignment with s(x) = 0.

  1. By clause 9, 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s] iff for some b ∈ {0,1,2}, 𝔄 ⊨ (Rxy ∧ Py)[s(y↦b)].
  2. b = 0: (0,0) ∉ R𝔄, so the conjunction fails (clause 4).
  3. b = 1: (0,1) ∈ R𝔄 but 1 ∉ P𝔄, so it fails.
  4. b = 2: (0,2) ∈ R𝔄 and 2 ∈ P𝔄, so it holds.witness found
  5. Therefore 𝔄 ⊨ ∃y(Rxy ∧ Py)[s].

The same reasoning gives satisfaction when s(x) = 1 (witness 2) and failure when s(x) = 2, since nothing is above 2. So the formula is satisfied by exactly the assignments that send x to 0 or 1 (checked).

The coincidence lemma. Whether 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s] depends only on the values s gives to the variables free in φ: if s and s′ agree on all free variables of φ, then 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s] iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s′][1,2]. This justifies the common notation 𝔄 ⊨ φ[a₁, …, aₙ] for a formula φ(x₁, …, xₙ) whose free variables are among x₁, …, xₙ, meaning that any assignment sending each xᵢ to aᵢ satisfies φ[1,12,4].

The substitution lemma. Substituting a term for a variable has the same effect as reassigning the variable to the term's value: if t is substitutable for x in φ, then 𝔄 ⊨ φ(t/x)[s] iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s(x ↦ t𝔄[s])][1,14]. This lemma is what makes rules like universal instantiation sound, and the substitutability condition is exactly what blocks variable capture (see First-Order Logic, section 8.3).

Definable sets. A formula φ(x₁,…,xₙ) defines in 𝔄 the relation {(a₁,…,aₙ) ∈ Aⁿ : 𝔄 ⊨ φ[a₁,…,aₙ]}[1,12,4]. In 𝔄, Example 3.1 shows that ∃y(Rxy ∧ Py) defines {0, 1}, and Px ∧ ∃y Ryx defines {2} (checked). Definability is one of the central topics of model theory: in (ℕ, +), for instance, the order is definable, since x ≤ y iff ∃z(x + z = y)[1].

3.3 Truth in a structure

By the coincidence lemma, a sentence (a formula with no free variables) is satisfied in 𝔄 either by every assignment or by none[1,6]. This yields the definition of truth.

Definition 3.4 (truth in a structure)

A sentence σ is true in 𝔄, written 𝔄 ⊨ σ, iff 𝔄 ⊨ σ[s] for every assignment s (equivalently, for some assignment s). Otherwise σ is false in 𝔄, written 𝔄 ⊭ σ[1,2,6].

Truth in a structure is bivalent for sentences: for every sentence σ, exactly one of 𝔄 ⊨ σ and 𝔄 ⊨ ¬σ holds[1,2]. Truth is also relative to a structure: there is no single notion of “true” for a formal sentence, only “true in 𝔄” for each 𝔄. The SEP entry on Tarski's truth definitions describes the model-theoretic setting as one in which the non-logical constants and the range of the quantifiers depend on the context of use, the structure playing the role of the context[6].

Example 3.2 (truth values of sentences in 𝔄)

  • ∃x(Px ∧ Rcx): true (take x = 2).
  • ∀x∃y Rxy: false (no y with R2y); equivalently ∃x∀y ¬Rxy is true.
  • ∀x ¬(f(x) = x): true (f𝔄 has no fixed point).
  • ∀x(¬Px → P f(x)): true (the only non-P element is 1, and f𝔄(1) = 2 ∈ P𝔄).
  • ∀x(Px → P f(f(x))): false (at x = 2, f(f(2)) = 1 ∉ P𝔄).
  • ∃x∀y(x = y ∨ Rxy): true (0 is a least element).

All six values were checked.

Formulas with free variables. Bivalence fails for formulas that are not sentences, in the following sense. Many texts extend the notation by saying that an arbitrary formula is true in 𝔄 if it is satisfied by every assignment, which amounts to evaluating its universal closure; Mendelson adopts this convention[16]. With that convention, in 𝔄 neither Px nor ¬Px is true, because some assignments send x into P𝔄 and some do not, although Px ∨ ¬Px is true (all checked). So 𝔄 ⊭ φ and 𝔄 ⊨ ¬φ come apart for open formulas, while they coincide for sentences.

The theory of a structure. The set Th(𝔄) = {σ : σ is a sentence and 𝔄 ⊨ σ} is the complete theory of 𝔄[1,12,13]. Two structures are elementarily equivalent, 𝔄 ≡ 𝔅, if they have the same theory, that is, if exactly the same sentences are true in both[13,12,1]. Isomorphic structures are always elementarily equivalent: an isomorphism carries satisfying assignments to satisfying assignments, so isomorphic structures cannot be told apart by any first-order sentence[1,13,12]. The converse fails for infinite structures. For example (ℚ, <) and (ℝ, <) are elementarily equivalent, since both are dense linear orders without endpoints and that theory is complete, but they are not isomorphic, since one is countable and the other is not[13,12,1].

4. Models

4.1 Models of formulas

Definition 4.1 (model)

A structure 𝔄 is a model of a sentence σ if 𝔄 ⊨ σ, and a model of a set of sentences Σ if it is a model of every member of Σ, written 𝔄 ⊨ Σ[1,12,4,2]. The class of all models of Σ is written Mod(Σ)[1,12].

For a formula with free variables there are two natural readings. Under the convention of section 3.3, 𝔄 is a model of φ when every assignment satisfies φ, so that a model of φ is a model of its universal closure[16]. Alternatively one speaks of a pair (𝔄, s) satisfying φ, which corresponds to its existential closure being true. The pair reading is the one used in Enderton's definitions of satisfiability and logical implication (section 5)[1].

The word reflects mathematical usage. Nineteenth-century geometers, with contributions from Lobachevski and Klein, showed that the other axioms of geometry have models in which the parallel postulate fails, and Hilbert in 1899 constructed such models systematically by reinterpreting the geometric vocabulary; the models of model theory first appeared as abstract versions of models of this kind[3].

Example 4.1 (models of single sentences)

  • Every structure with at least two elements is a model of ∃x∃y ¬(x = y); no one-element structure is (Example 2.1).
  • The running structure 𝔄 is a model of ∀x∀y(Rxy → ¬Ryx) (asymmetry) and of ∀x ¬(f(x) = x), but not of ∀x∃y Rxy (checked).
  • (ℕ, <) is a model of ∀x∃y Rxy and of ∃x∀y ¬Ryx (0 has nothing below it); (ℤ, <) is a model of the first but not the second[1].

4.2 Models of theories

Usage of the word theory varies. Hodges calls any set of sentences a theory[12], whereas Enderton reserves the word for sets of sentences closed under logical consequence, so that T is a theory when every sentence implied by T belongs to T[1]. The two usages are compatible in practice, because a set of sentences Σ and its deductive closure Cn(Σ) = {σ : Σ ⊨ σ} have exactly the same models[1]. A set Σ with Cn(Σ) = T is a set of axioms for T, and T is finitely axiomatizable if some finite Σ works[1,12].

There is a two-way traffic between theories and classes of structures[1,12,13]:

Σ ↦ Mod(Σ)       𝒦 ↦ Th(𝒦) = {σ : 𝔄 ⊨ σ for all 𝔄 ∈ 𝒦}

The more sentences a theory contains, the fewer models it has: Σ ⊆ Σ′ implies Mod(Σ′) ⊆ Mod(Σ). A class of structures of the form Mod(Σ) is called elementary (in the wider sense, if Σ may be infinite), and a theory T is complete if for every sentence σ of its language, T ⊨ σ or T ⊨ ¬σ[1,13]. The theory Th(𝔄) of a single structure is always complete, by bivalence[1].

Example 4.2 (strict linear orders)

Let ΣLO consist of the three sentences

  1. ∀x ¬Rxxirreflexive
  2. ∀x∀y∀z(Rxy ∧ Ryz → Rxz)transitive
  3. ∀x∀y(Rxy ∨ x = y ∨ Ryx)total

Its models are exactly the strict linear orders. (ℕ, <), (ℚ, <) and the reduct of the running structure 𝔄 to {R} are models; 𝔄₂ of Example 2.3 (divisibility) is not, since it fails axiom 1 (checked for the finite cases). The theory Cn(ΣLO) is not complete: the sentence ∃x∀y ¬Ryx (“there is a least element”) is true in (ℕ, <) and false in (ℤ, <), so neither it nor its negation follows from the axioms[1,12].

Example 4.3 (a theory with only infinite models)

Add to axioms 1 and 2 above the sentence ∀x∃y Rxy (“no maximum”). The resulting set has the infinite model (ℕ, <) but no finite model. Proof: in a non-empty finite structure satisfying 1 and 2, start anywhere and keep choosing a larger element; by transitivity and irreflexivity no element can repeat, so the chain must stop at an element with nothing above it, contradicting “no maximum”. (A search of all structures of size 1 to 4 found no model, as expected.) This shows that a satisfiable set of sentences need not have any finite model, which matters for the search for countermodels in section 4.3[1,17].

The limits of characterization. First-order theories cannot, in general, pin down a single infinite structure. By the upward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, a set of sentences in a countable language that has an infinite model has models of every infinite cardinality[2,13,1], and isomorphic structures have the same cardinality, so no such theory with an infinite model has, up to isomorphism, only one model. In particular Th(𝔑), the set of all sentences true in the standard natural numbers, has non-standard models that are not isomorphic to 𝔑[1,17]. A theory is called κ-categorical if all its models of cardinality κ are isomorphic; the theory of dense linear orders without endpoints is ℵ₀-categorical, which is the result of Cantor behind the example of section 3.3[13,12].

4.3 Countermodels

Semantic claims of the form “φ is valid” or “Γ ⊨ φ” are universal: they speak about all structures. Their negations are therefore existential, and are established by exhibiting a single structure. A countermodel to the claim Γ ⊨ φ is a structure (with an assignment, if free variables occur) in which every member of Γ is satisfied and φ is not[1,11,17]. A countermodel to the validity of φ is a structure in which φ is false.

Constructing a countermodel is the standard semantic method for proving invalidity. Textbooks teach it as the general way to show that a conclusion is not a first-order consequence of premises: describe a structure, often a very small one, and verify that the premises are true and the conclusion false in it[11,1]. Each item below was found by an exhaustive search over small structures.

Invalid claimCountermodelWhy it works
∀x∃y Rxy ⊨ ∃y∀x RxyA = {0,1}, R = {(0,1),(1,0)}each element has an R-successor, but no single element is R-related to by both
∀x(Px ∨ Qx) ⊨ ∀x Px ∨ ∀x QxA = {0,1}, P = {1}, Q = {0}everything is P or Q, but neither covers everything
∃x Px ∧ ∃x Qx ⊨ ∃x(Px ∧ Qx)A = {0,1}, P = {1}, Q = {0}something is P and something is Q, but nothing is both
∀x(Px → Qx), Qa ⊨ PaA = {0}, a = 0, P = ∅, Q = {0}affirming the consequent: a is Q without being P
∃x Px ⊨ PaA = {0,1}, a = 0, P = {1}the witness need not be the named element
∀x(Px → Qx) ⊨ ∃x(Px ∧ Qx)A = {0}, P = Q = ∅universal statements lack existential import
∀x Px → Q ⊨ ∀x(Px → Q)A = {0,1}, P = {1}, Q falsethe premise is true because ∀x Px is false; the conclusion fails at x = 1

In the last row Q is a 0-place predicate (a sentence letter), so the scope of ∀x matters even though x does not occur in Q. The correct law is ∀x(Px → Q) ≡ ∃x Px → Q, with x not free in Q; it and its dual ∃x(Px → Q) ≡ ∀x Px → Q were confirmed by the same search.

Where countermodels must be infinite. Small finite countermodels are common, but not guaranteed. Let ψ be the conjunction of the three sentences of Example 4.3. Then ¬ψ is true in every finite structure, yet ¬ψ is not valid: its only countermodels are infinite ones such as (ℕ, <). So no search through finite structures, however long, will ever refute the validity of ¬ψ[1,17].

No algorithm in general. This is a symptom of a deeper fact. By the theorem of Church (and, independently, Turing) of 1936, there is no algorithm that decides of an arbitrary first-order sentence whether it is valid[18,17]. Valid sentences can be effectively enumerated, since they are exactly the provable ones, but the invalid ones cannot; hence there is also no general effective method for finding countermodels[17]. In practice, countermodels are found by insight, by systematic search over small domains (as here), or by automated model finders, all of which can fail to terminate on some inputs.

5. Semantic concepts

With truth in a structure defined, the core notions of logic can be stated as quantifications over structures. The definitions below are for sets of formulas Γ and formulas φ, ψ, quantifying over pairs (𝔄, s) of a structure and an assignment, following Enderton[1]. For sentences, the assignment plays no role and each definition reduces to the familiar version stated with truth in a structure[1,2].

5.1 Logical validity

Definition 5.1 (validity)

A formula φ is (logically) valid, written ⊨ φ, iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s] for every structure 𝔄 for its language and every assignment s into 𝔄[1]. For a sentence: ⊨ σ iff σ is true in every structure[2]. Valid sentences are also called logical truths[2,19].

Validity is truth in virtue of form alone: a valid sentence comes out true however its non-logical symbols are interpreted and whatever non-empty domain is chosen[19,8]. The idea of truth under every reinterpretation of the non-logical vocabulary was anticipated by Bolzano in 1837, and Tarski in 1936 was the first to state explicitly how it could be characterized in standard mathematics, which is the basis of the model-theoretic notion used today[19]. Gómez-Torrente notes that the model-theoretic notion is usually accepted as a reasonably good delineation of the logical truths of first-order languages, and that for first-order languages without identity a refinement of the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem supports this, whereas no similar results hold for higher-order languages[19].

Example 5.1 (valid and invalid sentences)

  • Valid: ∀x Px → Pa;   ∀x(x = x);   ∃x∀y Rxy → ∀y∃x Rxy;   ∃x(Px → ∀y Py);   ¬∃y∀x(Rxy ↔ ¬Rxx).
  • Not valid: ∀x Px ∨ ∀x ¬Px (countermodel: A = {0,1}, P = {1});   Px → ∀x Px (same countermodel, with s(x) = 1).

The second-to-last valid sentence says that in any non-empty domain there is something such that, if it is P, everything is P: if everything is P, any element works, and otherwise a non-P element makes the conditional vacuously true. The last says that no element bears R to exactly those things that do not bear R to themselves: such a y would satisfy Ryy ↔ ¬Ryy. A search of all structures up to size 3 found no counterexample to any of the five, and found the stated countermodels for the two invalid ones. A finite search cannot by itself establish validity; the short proofs just given do.

Validity is not truth in a structure. Every valid sentence is true in 𝔄, but a sentence true in 𝔄 (or even in every structure one cares about) need not be valid. ∀x ¬(f(x) = x) is true in the running structure but false in any structure where f has a fixed point[1].

Validity and provability. Gödel proved in 1930 that a standard axiomatic calculus for first-order logic proves every valid formula, so validity coincides with provability; together with the soundness of the calculus this gives ⊢ φ iff ⊨ φ[20,2,1]. Validity is nevertheless undecidable, as section 4.3 explained[18,17].

5.2 Logical consequence

Definition 5.2 (logical consequence)

Γ logically implies φ, or φ is a logical consequence of Γ, written Γ ⊨ φ, iff for every structure 𝔄 and every assignment s such that 𝔄 satisfies every member of Γ under s, 𝔄 also satisfies φ under s[1]. For sentences: Γ ⊨ σ iff every model of Γ is a model of σ[2,8].

This is the model-theoretic reconstruction of the informal idea that an argument is valid when the truth of the premises guarantees the truth of the conclusion, whatever the subject matter (see Arguments and Inference). Tarski's 1936 paper defined it by saying that a sentence X follows logically from a class of sentences K if and only if every model of K is also a model of X[7], and the contemporary model-theoretic definition of logical consequence traces back to that paper[8]. Validity is the special case of consequence from no premises: ⊨ φ iff ∅ ⊨ φ[2,1].

Example 5.2 (consequence and non-consequence)

  • ∀x(Px → Qx), Pa ⊨ Qa. Proof: let 𝔄 be any model of the premises. Then a𝔄 ∈ P𝔄, and the first premise, instantiated at the element a𝔄, gives a𝔄 ∈ Q𝔄. (A search up to size 3 found no countermodel.)
  • ∀x(Px → Qx), Qa ⊭ Pa: countermodel in the table of section 4.3.
  • ∃y∀x Rxy ⊨ ∀x∃y Rxy, but not conversely (section 4.3)[1,11].

Structural properties. The following hold for every Γ, Δ, φ, ψ, directly from the definition[1]. The first and third correspond to conditions Tarski himself placed on a consequence operation, X ⊆ Cn(X) and Cn(Cn(X)) = Cn(X)[8].

  • Reflexivity: if φ ∈ Γ then Γ ⊨ φ.
  • Monotonicity: if Γ ⊨ φ and Γ ⊆ Δ then Δ ⊨ φ. Adding premises never destroys a consequence.
  • Cut (transitivity): if Γ ⊨ ψ for every ψ ∈ Δ and Γ ∪ Δ ⊨ φ, then Γ ⊨ φ.
  • Semantic deduction theorem: Γ ∪ {φ} ⊨ ψ iff Γ ⊨ φ → ψ. In particular φ ⊨ ψ iff ⊨ φ → ψ[1].
  • Compactness: if Γ ⊨ φ, then Γ₀ ⊨ φ for some finite Γ₀ ⊆ Γ[1,2].

Two readings for open formulas. Definition 5.2 is local: premises and conclusion must be satisfied by the same assignment. A global alternative would say that φ follows from Γ when every structure in which all members of Γ are true (satisfied by all assignments, as in Mendelson's convention[16]) is one in which φ is true. The two agree on sentences but not on open formulas. Under the local reading Px ⊭ ∀x Px (countermodel A = {0,1}, P = {1}, s(x) = 1); under the global reading Px does imply ∀x Px, because a structure in which every assignment satisfies Px has P𝔄 = A (both checked). The semantic deduction theorem holds for the local reading but fails for the global one, since Px → ∀x Px is not valid. This is the semantic counterpart of the restriction on the deduction theorem in Hilbert-style calculi with a generalization rule[16,1].

Philosophical questions. The model-theoretic definition is a mathematical analysis of an informal notion, and its adequacy has been debated. Etchemendy argued in 1990 that it cannot capture the necessity and formality of consequence on either of the two readings of what a model represents: if models represent possible worlds, logical consequence collapses into metaphysical consequence; if models are reinterpretations of the non-logical vocabulary, the definition fails to capture necessity and does not explain why domains of different sizes are considered[21,8]. One response blends the two perspectives[8]. Another, Kreisel's “squeezing” argument, appeals to the fact that for first-order logic a sound and complete proof system and the model-theoretic semantics agree on which arguments are valid, so the informal notion, squeezed between them, must coincide with both in extension[8].

5.3 Satisfiability

Definition 5.3 (satisfiability)

A set of formulas Γ is satisfiable iff there is a structure 𝔄 and an assignment s such that 𝔄 satisfies every member of Γ under s[1]. A set of sentences is satisfiable iff it has a model[2]. A formula is satisfiable iff {φ} is; a set that is not satisfiable is unsatisfiable.

Satisfiability is the existential dual of validity, and the two are interdefinable through negation[1,2]:

  • φ is valid iff ¬φ is unsatisfiable;
  • Γ ⊨ φ iff Γ ∪ {¬φ} is unsatisfiable.

The second equivalence turns every consequence question into a satisfiability question, which is how refutation-based theorem provers (resolution, tableaux) operate: to show Γ ⊨ φ, they show that Γ ∪ {¬φ} has no model[17,1]. It also explains countermodels: a countermodel to Γ ⊨ φ is exactly a model of Γ ∪ {¬φ}.

Example 5.3 (satisfiable and unsatisfiable sets)

  • {∃x Px, ∃x ¬Px} is satisfiable, but only in structures with at least two elements: no one-element structure satisfies it, while A = {0,1}, P = {1} does (checked).
  • {∀x(Px → Qx), Pa, ¬Qa} is unsatisfiable: a model would have to put a𝔄 in P𝔄, hence in Q𝔄, and also outside Q𝔄. Equivalently, ∀x(Px → Qx), Pa ⊨ Qa.
  • The set in Example 4.3 is satisfiable but has no finite model.

Compactness. A set of sentences is satisfiable if and only if every finite subset of it is satisfiable[2,1,13]. The theorem follows from the completeness theorem, because a derivation of a contradiction uses only finitely many premises, and it has striking consequences[1,2]. For example, if a set of sentences Σ has arbitrarily large finite models, then it has an infinite model: every finite subset of Σ ∪ {∃≥1, ∃≥2, …} is satisfiable, so the whole set is. It follows that finiteness cannot be expressed by any set of first-order sentences[1].

Löwenheim–Skolem. A satisfiable set of sentences that is finite or countably infinite has a model whose domain is finite or countably infinite[2,1]. Together with the upward theorem of section 4.2, this shows that first-order sentences constrain the cardinality of infinite models only very weakly[13,2].

5.4 Equivalence

Definition 5.4 (logical equivalence)

Formulas φ and ψ are logically equivalent, written φ ≡ ψ (Enderton writes φ ⊨⫤ ψ), iff φ ⊨ ψ and ψ ⊨ φ; equivalently, iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s] ⇔ 𝔄 ⊨ ψ[s] for every structure 𝔄 and assignment s; equivalently, iff ⊨ φ ↔ ψ[1,14].

Logical equivalence is an equivalence relation on formulas, and it is a congruence: by the replacement theorem, if φ ≡ ψ and χ′ results from χ by replacing an occurrence of φ with ψ, then χ ≡ χ′[14,1]. This licenses the step-by-step rewriting used to put formulas into prenex normal form (see First-Order Logic, section 7.2).

Example 5.4 (equivalences, all confirmed by the model checker)

  • ¬∀x Px ≡ ∃x ¬Px (quantifier negation).
  • ∀x(Px ∧ Qx) ≡ ∀x Px ∧ ∀x Qx (distribution of ∀ over ∧).
  • ∀x Px ≡ ∀y Py (renaming a bound variable, or alphabetic variance)[1,14].
  • ∀x(Px → Q) ≡ ∃x Px → Q, with x not free in Q.

Non-equivalences need only one countermodel to one direction: ∀x(Px ∨ Qx) ≢ ∀x Px ∨ ∀x Qx (section 4.3), and Px ≢ Py, since an assignment with s(x) = 1, s(y) = 0 in A = {0,1}, P = {1} satisfies one and not the other (checked). Formulas with different free variables are rarely equivalent, which is why equivalence is defined assignment by assignment.

Equivalence relative to a theory. Two formulas are equivalent modulo a set of sentences T if T ⊨ ∀x̄(φ ↔ ψ), that is, if they agree in every model of T[12,13]. For instance, modulo the linear-order axioms of Example 4.2, ¬Rxy ≡T (x = y ∨ Ryx), although the two formulas are not logically equivalent. Many results of model theory, such as quantifier elimination, are statements that every formula is equivalent modulo some theory to one of a simpler form[12,4].

A related notion applies to structures rather than formulas: elementary equivalence 𝔄 ≡ 𝔅 (section 3.3). The two uses of ≡ are dual. Formulas are logically equivalent when no structure separates them, and structures are elementarily equivalent when no sentence separates them[13,12].

5.5 Consistency

“Consistency” has two standard meanings, one semantic and one deductive.

  • Semantic consistency. A set of sentences is semantically consistent if it is satisfiable, that is, if all its members can be true together in some structure[11,1].
  • Deductive consistency. Relative to a proof system, Γ is consistent if there is no sentence θ with both Γ ⊢ θ and Γ ⊢ ¬θ; otherwise it is inconsistent[2,1,16]. In classical logic an inconsistent set proves every sentence, so consistency is equivalent to the existence of some sentence the set does not prove[1,2].

Theorem 5.5 (completeness theorem, consistency form)

For any set Γ of first-order sentences: Γ is deductively consistent if and only if Γ is satisfiable[2,1,22].

The direction “satisfiable ⇒ consistent” is soundness: a model of Γ would make both θ and ¬θ true, which bivalence forbids. The direction “consistent ⇒ satisfiable” is Gödel's completeness theorem of 1930 in its strong form[20,2].

The now-standard proof of the hard direction is due to Leon Henkin (1949). It extends a consistent set to a maximal consistent set in an enlarged language with new constants serving as witnesses for existential sentences (∃x φ → φ(c)), and then builds a model whose domain consists of the closed terms themselves, taken modulo provable identity[22,1,14]. The construction makes vivid that consistency is a syntactic surrogate for having a model: a consistent theory literally describes a structure built out of its own vocabulary. Theorem 5.5 is equivalent to the other form of completeness, Γ ⊨ φ ⇒ Γ ⊢ φ, because Γ ⊬ φ holds iff Γ ∪ {¬φ} is consistent, and Γ ⊭ φ holds iff Γ ∪ {¬φ} is satisfiable[1,14].

Example 5.5 (proving consistency by building a model)

Is Σ = {∀x∃y Rxy, ∀x ¬Rxx, ∃x∀y ¬Ryx} consistent? One could search for a derivation of a contradiction and never find one, but a model settles the matter at once: (ℕ, <) satisfies all three sentences (every number has a larger one; no number is less than itself; nothing is less than 0), so Σ is satisfiable and, by soundness, deductively consistent[1,11]. Hilbert's 1899 constructions of models for systems of geometric axioms, mentioned in section 4.1, use the same method of interpretation[3].
