1 Arguments and inferences

In logic, an argument is a group of propositions in which some, the premises, are offered as support for another, the conclusion.[1–3] An argument is therefore not a quarrel but a structure of claimed support. What makes a passage an argument is that it purports to give reasons: a list of statements, a report, or an explanation is not an argument unless some statements are put forward as grounds for accepting another.[2,3]

An inference is the passage of thought from premises to conclusion, the act of drawing a conclusion on the basis of reasons; the argument is the publicly expressible product that records that passage.[2] The two notions come apart in instructive ways. Harman stressed that logic, the theory of what follows from what, is not by itself a theory of reasoning, the activity of changing one's beliefs: when one discovers that one's beliefs jointly imply something absurd, logic says that something must go, but not which belief to give up.[4] Logic evaluates the relation between premises and conclusion; a theory of inference also has to say what a reasoner ought to do with that relation.

Arguments are standardly classified by the kind of support the premises are claimed to provide. In a deductive argument the premises are meant to guarantee the conclusion; in inductive and abductive arguments they are meant to make it probable or plausible, and the conclusion is ampliative: it goes beyond what is logically contained in the premises.[1] Induction typically generalizes or projects observed patterns, whereas abduction reasons to an explanation of what has been observed.[1,5] Sections 3 to 5 treat each mode in turn; Section 2 first examines the structure that all arguments share.

2 The structure of arguments

2.1 Premise–conclusion structure

Every argument has at least one premise and exactly one (final) conclusion. Premises and conclusions are propositions, things that can be true or false, so questions, commands and exclamations are not premises or conclusions unless they can be read as making a claim.[2,3] Natural-language arguments signal their structure with indicator words. Conclusion indicators include therefore, thus, hence, so, consequently and it follows that; premise indicators include since, because, for, given that and as shown by.[2,3] Indicators are fallible guides: since can be temporal (since Tuesday) and because often introduces an explanation rather than a premise.[3]

The distinction between argument and explanation is important. In an argument the premises are offered to show that the conclusion is true; in an explanation the fact to be explained is already accepted and the explanans says why it is so.[2,3] The pavement is wet because it rained is ordinarily an explanation; It must have rained, because the pavement is wet is an argument. The same sentence form can serve either purpose, and the context of utterance decides.

Logicians write arguments in standard form: premises listed and numbered, a line drawn, and the conclusion written below, often preceded by ∴.[3,6]

Standard form

  1. All mammals breathe air.
  2. Whales are mammals.

∴ Whales breathe air.

2.2 Simple and complex arguments

A simple argument consists of one or more premises supporting a single conclusion in a single inferential step, as in the whale example. A complex (or extended) argument contains more than one inferential step: the conclusion of one sub-argument serves as a premise of another, or several lines of support converge on the final conclusion.[6,7] Most arguments in essays, legal judgments and scientific papers are complex, and much of the work of argument analysis consists in exposing the separate steps so that each can be assessed on its own terms.[6,8]

2.3 Linked and convergent premises

When an argument has several premises, they may relate to the conclusion in different ways. Beardsley's pioneering treatment of argument diagrams distinguished serial, convergent and divergent structures; Thomas later added the category of linked support, and the linked/convergent distinction has since been a central topic in the theory of argument structure.[7,9–11]

  • Linked premises work together: each premise needs the other(s) to support the conclusion, and on its own gives little or no support. Removing one premise leaves the rest unable to support the conclusion.[6,8,10]
  • Convergent premises provide independent lines of support: each would give some reason for the conclusion even if the others were false or withdrawn, and together they give cumulative support.[6,8,10]

Example 2.1

Linked: (1) Every member of the committee must sign the report. (2) Dr Okafor is a member of the committee. So (C) Dr Okafor must sign the report. Neither premise alone gives any reason for C.

Convergent: (1) The train is cheaper than flying. (2) The train is less polluting than flying. So (C) we should take the train. Each premise would be some reason for C even without the other.

Several tests for telling the two apart have been proposed, for example asking whether the falsity of one premise would leave the other still giving some support (if so, convergent), or whether the premises would give as much support separately as together.[7,8,11] The pragma-dialectical tradition draws a related distinction between coordinative argumentation (premises that must be taken together) and multiple argumentation (alternative, independent defences of the standpoint), and calls the serial case subordinative.[11] Freeman argues that the linked/convergent distinction concerns how premises are relevant to a conclusion and gives a dialectical account of it.[7]

The distinction matters for evaluation. In a linked argument the failure of any one premise undermines the whole inferential step; in a convergent argument the failure of one premise weakens, but need not destroy, the case for the conclusion.[6,8]

2.4 Serial arguments and intermediate conclusions

In a serial (or chain) argument, one statement supports a second, which in turn supports a third, and so on.[9,10] The statements in the middle of the chain are intermediate conclusions (also called sub-conclusions): they are conclusions of one inferential step and premises of the next.[6,7]

Example 2.2

(1) The reservoir is below 20% capacity. So (2) the town will face a water shortage this summer. So (C) the council should introduce a hosepipe ban.

Statement (2) is an intermediate conclusion. An evaluator must ask two separate questions: does (1) support (2), and does (2) support (C)? A serial argument is only as strong as its weakest link.

Because each step is evaluated separately, identifying intermediate conclusions is one of the main payoffs of careful reconstruction: a critic can grant one step while rejecting another.[6,8]

2.5 Argument diagrams

An argument diagram represents the support structure of an argument graphically. In the standard convention, introduced in recognizably modern form by Beardsley and developed by Thomas, each proposition is numbered, numbers are placed in nodes, and arrows run from supporting propositions to the propositions they support.[7,9,10] Linked premises are joined (for instance by a bracket or plus sign) and share a single arrow; convergent premises each have their own arrow.[7,8,10] Diagramming has also been implemented in software for teaching and research, of which Araucaria is an early and widely used example.[12] Figure 1 shows the three basic patterns.

2.6 Argument reconstruction

Argument reconstruction is the process of extracting an argument from a natural-language text and setting it out explicitly so that it can be evaluated. Textbook procedures broadly agree on the steps:[3,6,8]

  1. Identify the main conclusion (what the author is ultimately trying to establish).
  2. Identify the stated premises, using indicator words and context, and discard material that plays no supporting role (background, repetition, rhetorical flourishes).
  3. Paraphrase each premise and conclusion as a clear, self-contained proposition, keeping terms consistent.
  4. Identify intermediate conclusions and determine which premises are linked and which convergent.
  5. Supply any unstated premises needed to make the reasoning explicit (§2.7).
  6. Set out the result in standard form and as a diagram.

Reconstruction is governed by the principle of charity: where a text admits more than one reasonable reading, the analyst should prefer the interpretation that makes the argument stronger and the author's claims more plausible, while remaining faithful to what was actually said.[6,8] Charity guards against attacking a straw man, an argument weaker than the one the arguer actually gave.[8]

Example 2.3 (worked reconstruction)

Text: The town should close the old bridge. The engineers' inspection found severe corrosion in the main supports, and any bridge with severely corroded supports is unsafe for traffic. Moreover, the new bridge two kilometres downstream already carries most of the traffic, so closing the old one would cause little disruption.

  1. The inspection found severe corrosion in the old bridge's main supports.
  2. Any bridge with severely corroded main supports is unsafe for traffic.
  3. [IC1] The old bridge is unsafe for traffic. (from 1 and 2, linked)
  4. The new bridge already carries most of the traffic.
  5. [IC2] Closing the old bridge would cause little disruption. (from 4)
  6. [Unstated] If a bridge is unsafe and closing it would cause little disruption, the town should close it.

∴ The town should close the old bridge. (from IC1, IC2 and the unstated premise, linked)

The diagram exposes where the argument can be challenged: one could deny premise 2 (perhaps some corrosion is repairable without closure), deny that 4 supports IC2 (the new bridge may already be near capacity), or reject the unstated premise (perhaps repair rather than closure is the right response).

2.7 Suppressed premises

Arguments in ordinary discourse frequently leave premises unstated. A suppressed (unstated, missing, or implicit) premise is a proposition that the arguer does not state but on which the argument depends, typically because it is assumed to be obvious or shared with the audience.[2,6,8] Supplying such premises is part of reconstruction, but it raises a methodological problem: which premise should be supplied?

Two strategies are commonly distinguished.[6,8] The logical minimum strategy adds the weakest premise that would make the argument deductively valid, usually a conditional of the form if [stated premises], then [conclusion]. This always succeeds technically but often attributes to the arguer a claim that is trivially question-begging or implausibly specific. The pragmatic strategy adds the premise that best fits the context and what the arguer can reasonably be taken to believe, often a more general principle. In Example 2.3 the logical-minimum premise would be If the old bridge is unsafe and closing it would cause little disruption, the town should close the old bridge; the pragmatic reconstruction supplies the more general principle shown. Charity counsels supplying a premise the arguer is plausibly committed to and that makes the argument as strong as the text allows, but no stronger.[6,8]

2.8 Enthymemes

An argument with an unstated premise or conclusion is traditionally called an enthymeme.[2,3] The term comes from Aristotle, for whom the enthymeme was the rhetorical counterpart of the demonstrative syllogism, a rhetorical demonstration drawing on what is probable or on signs, suited to an audience that cannot follow long chains of reasoning; Aristotle notes that if one of the premises is well known it need not be stated, since the hearer supplies it.[13] The modern textbook definition, an argument with a missing part, thus picks out only one feature of Aristotle's notion.[14]

Copi and colleagues classify enthymematic categorical syllogisms by what is missing: a first-order enthymeme omits the major premise, a second-order enthymeme omits the minor premise, and a third-order enthymeme omits the conclusion.[2]

Example 2.4

  • First-order (major premise missing): Sam is a lawyer, so Sam has studied contract law. Missing: All lawyers have studied contract law.
  • Second-order (minor premise missing): All lawyers have studied contract law, so Sam has studied contract law. Missing: Sam is a lawyer.
  • Third-order (conclusion missing): All lawyers have studied contract law, and Sam is a lawyer. Left for the hearer: Sam has studied contract law.

Whether ordinary arguments really have missing premises is disputed. Hitchcock argues that the traditional treatment of enthymemes rests on a mistake: many arguments with a single stated premise are perfectly good as they stand, because they are underwritten by an implicit inference rule or warrant licensing the step, and not by an unstated premise that must be added to make them deductively valid.[14] On this view, insisting on supplying premises can distort an argument by forcing every good argument into deductive form.

3 Deductive arguments

3.1 Validity

A deductive argument is valid if and only if it is impossible for all its premises to be true and its conclusion false.[2,3,15] Equivalently, in every possible circumstance in which the premises are true, the conclusion is true as well. An argument that fails this condition is invalid.

Validity is a relation between premises and conclusion and does not depend on whether the premises are in fact true. It is also usually understood as formal: whether an argument is valid depends on its logical form, the pattern obtained by abstracting from the specific content of its non-logical terms.[15] This is why validity can be studied by studying forms, and why an invalid form can be exposed by a counterexample: an argument of the same form with obviously true premises and an obviously false conclusion.[2,3]

Example 3.1 (validity is independent of actual truth)

PremisesConclusionPossible if valid?Instance
TrueTrueYesAll dogs are mammals; all mammals are animals; ∴ all dogs are animals.
FalseTrueYesAll fish are mammals; all whales are fish; ∴ all whales are mammals.
FalseFalseYesAll cats are birds; all birds have feathers; ∴ all cats have feathers.
TrueFalseNoNo valid argument has this combination: that is what validity rules out.

A consequence of the definition is that deductive validity is monotonic: adding premises to a valid argument cannot make it invalid. If it is impossible for premises P1…Pn to be true while C is false, then it is a fortiori impossible for P1…Pn together with a further premise Q to be true while C is false. Inductive support lacks this property, as §4.3 shows.

3.2 Soundness

A deductive argument is sound if and only if it is valid and all of its premises are true.[2,3] A sound argument therefore has a true conclusion. Soundness is the standard that a deductive argument must meet to establish its conclusion; validity alone shows only that the conclusion is true if the premises are. Thus the second and third rows of Example 3.1 are valid but unsound. To criticize a deductive argument one can therefore attack its form (show it invalid) or its content (show a premise false).[3]

3.3 Necessarily truth-preserving inference

The definition of validity can be restated by saying that valid inference is necessarily truth-preserving: truth is transmitted from premises to conclusion in every possible case, not merely in the actual one or in most cases.[1,15] Contemporary analyses of logical consequence take it to be both necessary and formal, and Tarski's model-theoretic account makes this precise: a conclusion follows from premises when every interpretation of the non-logical vocabulary that makes the premises true also makes the conclusion true.[15]

Necessary truth-preservation explains why deduction is non-ampliative: the conclusion of a valid argument cannot contain information that is not, in a sense, already implicit in the premises.[1] This is the price of certainty. Deduction can be enormously informative in the psychological sense (a mathematical theorem can surprise), but it cannot take us from data about observed cases to genuinely new claims about unobserved ones. That is the task of induction and abduction.

Two further properties follow directly from the definition. An argument whose conclusion is a necessary truth is valid whatever its premises, and an argument with jointly inconsistent premises is valid whatever its conclusion, since in neither case is there a possible situation with true premises and false conclusion.[15] Such arguments are valid but can never be used to establish a conclusion that is in doubt, which is one reason soundness, and not validity alone, matters in practice.

3.4 Common deductive patterns

Certain valid argument forms recur so often that they have traditional names. In the schemata below, p, q, r and s stand for any propositions, → for if … then, ∨ for inclusive or and ¬ for not.[2,3] Each valid form is paired with an invalid look-alike, a formal fallacy, since recognizing the fallacious twin is as important as recognizing the valid form.

Modus ponens (affirming the antecedent)

p → q
p

∴ q

Example: If the circuit is closed, the lamp is lit. The circuit is closed. So the lamp is lit.[2,3]

Invalid twin, affirming the consequent: p → q; q; ∴ p. If it rained, the street is wet; the street is wet; so it rained. A burst water main could have wet the street.[2,3]

Modus tollens (denying the consequent)

p → q
¬q

∴ ¬p

Example: If the patient had measles, she would have a rash. She has no rash. So she does not have measles.[2,3] Modus tollens underlies the testing of hypotheses: a hypothesis that implies a prediction is refuted, given its auxiliary assumptions, when the prediction fails.[16]

Invalid twin, denying the antecedent: p → q; ¬p; ∴ ¬q. If it rained, the street is wet; it did not rain; so the street is not wet.[2,3]

Hypothetical syllogism (chain argument)

p → q
q → r

∴ p → r

Example: If interest rates rise, borrowing falls. If borrowing falls, investment slows. So if interest rates rise, investment slows.[2,3] The form shows that conditionals can be chained; the conclusion is itself conditional.

Invalid variant: p → q; r → q; ∴ p → r. Two conditions that each lead to the same result need not lead to each other.

Disjunctive syllogism

p ∨ q
¬p

∴ q

Example: The key is either in the kitchen or in the car. It is not in the kitchen. So it is in the car.[2,3]

Invalid twin, affirming a disjunct: p ∨ q; p; ∴ ¬q. Because logical or is inclusive, both disjuncts may be true. The inference is valid only if the disjunction is known to be exclusive.[3]

Constructive dilemma

(p → q) · (r → s)
p ∨ r

∴ q ∨ s

Example: If we raise prices, we lose customers; and if we keep prices, we lose money. We will either raise prices or keep them. So we will lose customers or lose money.[2,3] A dilemma can be resisted by going between the horns (rejecting the disjunctive premise), grasping a horn (rejecting a conditional), or constructing a counter-dilemma.[2,3] Its counterpart, destructive dilemma, combines two modus tollens steps: (p → q) · (r → s); ¬q ∨ ¬s; ∴ ¬p ∨ ¬r.[3]

Reductio ad absurdum (indirect proof)

Assume p.
Derive a contradiction q · ¬q.

∴ ¬p

To prove a proposition, assume its negation and derive a contradiction; since a contradiction cannot be true, the assumption must be false.[2,3]

Classic example: √2 is irrational. Suppose √2 = a/b with a, b whole numbers having no common factor. Then 2b² = a², so a² is even, so a is even, say a = 2k. Then 2b² = 4k², so b² = 2k² is even and b is even. Hence a and b share the factor 2, contradicting the assumption. So √2 is not rational.

In informal argument, reductio takes the form of showing that an opponent's thesis leads to consequences the opponent must reject.[8]

The validity of these forms can be checked mechanically with truth tables, since each involves only truth-functional connectives.[2,3] Table 1 checks modus ponens against its fallacious twin. An argument form is valid if no row makes every premise true and the conclusion false.

4 Inductive arguments

An inductive argument is one whose premises are intended to make its conclusion probable but not certain.[2,3] Because the conclusion goes beyond the premises, it is always logically possible for the premises of even an excellent inductive argument to be true and its conclusion false.[1,17] Inductive arguments are therefore not assessed as valid or invalid but as stronger or weaker.

4.1 Strength

An inductive argument is strong if, assuming its premises true, its conclusion is probably true; it is weak if the premises, even if true, do not make the conclusion probable.[3] Unlike validity, strength comes in degrees: one inductive argument can be stronger than another.[3,18] Strength, like validity, concerns the link between premises and conclusion and not the actual truth of the premises.[3]

4.2 Cogency

A cogent inductive argument is one that is strong and has all true premises; cogency is thus the inductive analogue of soundness.[3] Hurley adds a further condition: the premises must not omit relevant available evidence that would change the strength of the argument (sometimes called the total evidence requirement).[3] The extra condition is needed because, as the next section explains, induction is sensitive to added information in a way deduction is not.

Example 4.1

(1) Ninety-five per cent of the town's residents surveyed oppose the new road. (2) The survey was a large random sample of residents. So (C) most of the town's residents oppose the new road. This argument is strong; it is cogent if both premises are true and there is no further relevant evidence, such as evidence that opponents were far likelier to answer the survey.

4.3 Probabilistic support

The degree to which premises support a conclusion is naturally represented by conditional probability. In Hawthorne's formulation of inductive logic, a support function assigns to each pair of a conclusion C and a body of premises B a value P[C | B] between 0 and 1, read as the degree to which B supports C; deductive entailment is the limiting case in which the value is 1.[17] Bayesian inductive logic analyses how evidence bears on hypotheses through likelihoods, the probability of the evidence given each hypothesis, combined with prior probabilities of the hypotheses; on this view evidence supports a hypothesis largely by disfavouring its competitors.[17,19]

Probabilistic support is non-monotonic: adding a true premise can lower the support for a conclusion.

Example 4.2 (non-monotonicity)

(1) Tweety is a bird. So, probably, (C) Tweety can fly.
Add: (2) Tweety is a penguin. Now the premises make C highly improbable.

Compare §3.1: had the original argument been deductively valid, no additional premise could have undermined it.

Two classic problems bound what inductive support can achieve. First, Hume argued that inductive inference presupposes that unobserved cases resemble observed ones, and that this uniformity cannot be established either by demonstrative reasoning (its denial is not contradictory) or by probable reasoning (which would assume what it sets out to prove).[20,21] Responses range from pragmatic vindications to Bayesian and explanationist treatments, and the problem remains debated.[21] Second, high probability is not closed under conjunction. Kyburg's lottery paradox shows this: in a fair lottery with 1,000 tickets, it is highly probable of each ticket that it will lose, yet certain that one ticket will win; if one accepts every highly probable claim, one is committed to a contradiction.[22]

4.4 Enumerative induction

Enumerative induction infers a general conclusion from an enumeration of instances: all observed As have been B, so all As are B (or: the next A will be B).[3,21]

Enumerative induction

a1, a2, …, an are As and are B. No observed A is not B.

∴ (Probably) all As are B.

The form is fragile. Russell's illustration is the chicken that has been fed every day and so expects to be fed again, until the day the farmer wrings its neck: repeated past association alone cannot tell us when a regularity is about to fail.[23] Goodman's new riddle of induction shows that the problem is not only about justification but about which predicates are projectible. Define an object as grue if it is examined before a time t and is green, or is not so examined and is blue. Every emerald examined before t is both green and grue, so the same evidence seems to support all emeralds are green and all emeralds are grue, which make incompatible predictions about emeralds first examined after t.[24] Good enumerative induction therefore requires a principled restriction to projectible predicates, which Goodman tied to the history of successful projection.[24]

4.5 Statistical generalization

A statistical generalization infers a proportion in a population from the proportion in a sample: x% of the observed Ss are P, so approximately x% of all Ss are P.[3,25] Its strength depends chiefly on the size of the sample and on its representativeness: a random sample, in which each member of the population has an equal chance of selection, is the standard means of avoiding bias.[3,25] Statistical theory quantifies how closely a random sample's proportion is likely to approximate the population's, which is the basis of reported confidence intervals and margins of error.[25]

Example 4.3 (a biased sample)

In 1936 the Literary Digest magazine conducted a very large mail poll and predicted that Alf Landon would defeat Franklin Roosevelt; Roosevelt won in a landslide. Squire's analysis attributes the failure both to a sampling frame skewed away from Roosevelt's supporters and to non-response bias, since those who returned ballots differed from those who did not.[26] Size cannot compensate for bias: a huge unrepresentative sample supports a statistical generalization worse than a small random one.

4.6 Statistical syllogism

A statistical syllogism runs in the opposite direction, from a statistical claim about a group to a conclusion about an individual member.[3,25]

Statistical syllogism

x% of Fs are Gs (with x high).
a is an F.

∴ (Probably, to degree about x%) a is a G.

Its central difficulty is the reference class problem: an individual belongs to many classes with different frequencies, which can yield conflicting conclusions. Suppose 90% of smokers in some population develop a certain condition, but only 10% of smokers who exercise daily do; for a daily-exercising smoker, the two classes point in opposite directions. Hempel showed that this ambiguity of inductive-statistical reasoning means two statistical syllogisms with true premises can support contradictory conclusions, and proposed the requirement of maximal specificity: the reference class used should be the narrowest one, relevant to the conclusion, for which reliable statistics are available.[27] This requirement is closely related to the total-evidence condition on cogency.[3,27]

4.7 Prediction

An inductive prediction infers a claim about a future (or otherwise unobserved) case from premises about past or observed cases, for example from the observed pattern of seasonal flu outbreaks to the claim that cases will rise next winter.[3,18] Predictive arguments are often reconstructable as enumerative or statistical inductions with a singular conclusion, and they inherit the problems of those forms, including Hume's problem.[21] Their strength depends on the stability of the underlying regularity and, ideally, on knowledge of the mechanism that produces it, since a pattern that is understood causally is more safely projected than a merely observed correlation.[28,29] In science, as §5.5 discusses, the success of predictions also feeds back into the evaluation of the theories that generated them.[30]

4.8 Causal inference

A causal inference concludes that one event or factor causes another. Mill systematized several methods of experimental inquiry for identifying causes:[2,31]

  • Agreement: if all cases of an effect share one and only one circumstance, that circumstance is probably the cause (or part of it).
  • Difference: if a case where the effect occurs and a case where it does not differ in only one circumstance, that circumstance is probably the cause (or an indispensable part of it).
  • Joint method of agreement and difference, combining the two.
  • Residues: subtract from a phenomenon the part known to be the effect of known causes; the remainder is the effect of the remaining antecedents.
  • Concomitant variation: a phenomenon that varies whenever another varies in a particular way is connected with it causally.

The methods presuppose that the relevant circumstances have been correctly identified, so they function as tools for testing candidate causes rather than as mechanical discovery procedures.[2,18] Correlation alone does not establish causation: an association between A and B may arise because A causes B, B causes A, a common cause produces both (confounding), or chance. Inferring causation from mere temporal succession is the fallacy of post hoc ergo propter hoc.[2,3] Modern causal inference represents these alternatives explicitly with causal models and distinguishes observing an association from intervening on a variable.[29]

Example 4.4 (Snow and cholera)

During the 1854 London cholera outbreak, John Snow compared households supplied by two water companies that served intermingled streets, one drawing water from the Thames downstream of sewage outflows and one from further upstream. He reported a death rate from cholera of 315 per 10,000 houses among customers of the Southwark and Vauxhall Company against 37 per 10,000 for the Lambeth Company.[32] Because the customers of the two companies were otherwise similar, this approximated Mill's method of difference and supported the conclusion that cholera was transmitted by contaminated water.[31,32]

In epidemiology, Bradford Hill proposed nine viewpoints for judging whether an association is causal: strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment and analogy. Hill stressed that none is a necessary or sufficient condition for causation.[28]

4.9 Arguments from analogy

An argument from analogy infers that because two things are alike in certain known respects, they are probably alike in a further respect.[2,33]

Argument from analogy

The source S has properties P1…Pn and Q.
The target T has properties P1…Pn.

∴ (Probably) T has Q.

Textbook criteria for evaluating analogical arguments, surveyed by Bartha, include the following: more similarities strengthen an analogy and more differences weaken it; greater ignorance about the two domains weakens it; a weaker (more modest) conclusion is more plausible; causal and structural similarities count for more than superficial ones; the relevance of the similarities to the conclusion is crucial; and several independent analogies supporting the same conclusion strengthen it.[2,33] Relevance is the decisive criterion: two cars may share colour, model year and owner, but only similarities such as engine type and maintenance history bear on their fuel economy.[2,3]

Example 4.5

Testing a drug in mice to predict its effects in humans is an argument from analogy whose strength depends on whether the physiological pathways the drug acts on are shared by the two species: a relevant, causal similarity. A historically famous case of a disputed analogy is the design argument, which reasons from the resemblance between the universe and a human artefact to a designer; the critique Hume voices through the character Philo in his Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion presses on the many dissimilarities between the cases.[33]

4.10 Inductive generalization

Inductive generalization is the umbrella term for inferences from a sample to a population.[3] Enumerative induction (§4.4) yields universal generalizations (all As are B); statistical generalization (§4.5) yields proportional ones (x% of As are B). The same evaluative questions apply to both: Is the sample large enough? Is it representative of the population, or could the selection process be correlated with the property in question? Is the property projectible? Is there available evidence of counter-instances or of variation across sub-populations?[3,24,25] The characteristic fallacy is hasty generalization, drawing a general conclusion from a sample that is too small or atypical.[2,3]

Harman influentially argued that enumerative induction is best understood not as a separate form of inference but as a special case of inference to the best explanation: we are entitled to generalize from observed instances when the generalization is part of the best explanation of why the observed instances are as they are.[5,34] This suggestion leads naturally to abductive reasoning.

5 Abductive reasoning

Abduction is reasoning from observed facts to hypotheses that would explain them. The term is Peirce's, who described the form of abductive inference as follows: The surprising fact, C, is observed; But if A were true, C would be a matter of course, Hence, there is reason to suspect that A is true.[35] (Collected Papers, vol. 5, para. 189.) Peirce's abduction was primarily a logic of generating hypotheses worth testing. In contemporary philosophy, abduction usually refers instead to the justification of hypotheses by their explanatory merits, that is, to inference to the best explanation.[5]

Formally, abduction has the shape of affirming the consequent (if A then C; C; so A), and as a deduction it is invalid (§3.4). Its claim is not deductive: it is an ampliative inference whose force depends on comparing A with rival explanations of C.[5,36]

5.1 Inference to the best explanation

The phrase inference to the best explanation (IBE) was introduced by Harman, who characterized it as inferring, from the premise that a hypothesis would explain the evidence better than any other hypothesis, the conclusion that the hypothesis is true.[34] A common schematic version is:[5,36]

Inference to the best explanation

D is a body of data.
Hypothesis H, if true, would explain D.
No available competing hypothesis explains D as well as H does.

∴ (Probably) H is true.

Lipton's systematic development of IBE distinguishes the likeliest explanation, the one most probably true given the evidence, from the loveliest explanation, the one that would, if true, provide the most understanding. His thesis is that explanatory loveliness is a guide to likeliness: we use the explanatory virtues of hypotheses as a guide to inference.[36] Lipton also stresses that much explanation is contrastive, explaining why P rather than Q, and that contrastive questions focus the search for evidence.[36]

Example 5.1 (Semmelweis and childbed fever)

Hempel's well-known reconstruction of Ignaz Semmelweis's work at the Vienna General Hospital in the 1840s shows IBE combined with testing. Mortality from childbed fever was much higher in the First Maternity Division, staffed by physicians and medical students, than in the Second, staffed by midwives. Semmelweis considered and rejected rival explanations (epidemic influences, overcrowding, rough examinations by students, the fear induced by a priest's procession, the delivery position) because each failed to explain the contrast between the divisions or failed when tested. After a colleague died of an illness resembling childbed fever following a wound received during an autopsy, he hypothesized that cadaveric matter carried on the hands of physicians who came from the autopsy room caused the disease. Requiring hand-washing in a solution of chlorinated lime was followed by a sharp fall in mortality in the First Division.[16] Lipton uses the case to show how contrastive explanation guides the elimination of rival hypotheses.[36]

What makes one explanation better than another? The literature identifies a set of explanatory virtues, including explanatory power, simplicity, consilience and predictive success, discussed in §§5.2–5.5.[5,36,37]

5.2 Explanatory power

A hypothesis has explanatory power with respect to some evidence to the extent that it makes that evidence less surprising: given the hypothesis, the evidence is to be expected. Schupbach and Sprenger made this intuition precise, deriving from a set of adequacy conditions a probabilistic measure of the power of hypothesis h to explain evidence e:[38]

ℰ(e, h) = [ P(h | e) − P(h | ¬e) ] / [ P(h | e) + P(h | ¬e) ]

The measure ranges from −1 (the hypothesis makes the evidence maximally surprising) to +1 (maximal explanatory power), with 0 when the hypothesis and the evidence are probabilistically independent.[38] Informally, explanatory power also covers scope (how much of the data a hypothesis explains) and the detail of the mechanism it provides.[36,37]

5.3 Simplicity

Other things equal, simpler explanations are preferred. Baker distinguishes two senses of simplicity: syntactic simplicity or elegance, the number and conciseness of a theory's basic principles, and ontological simplicity or parsimony, the number of kinds of entities it postulates. The two can conflict: postulating an extra entity can make the laws simpler, as when the postulation of the then-unobserved planet Neptune allowed the orbit of Uranus to be explained by Newtonian mechanics without modification.[39] Ontological parsimony is the principle often called Ockham's razor.[39,40]

Why should simplicity indicate truth? One answer comes from statistics. Forster and Sober argued, using the Akaike framework for model selection, that models with fewer adjustable parameters are less prone to overfitting noise in the data and can therefore be expected to make more accurate predictions; on this account simplicity has a justification rooted in predictive accuracy.[41] Sober argues more generally that parsimony arguments are legitimate only when backed by substantive assumptions about the problem at hand, so that the razor is not a single a priori principle.[40] Psychologically, people show a strong preference for simpler explanations: in Lombrozo's experiments participants preferred explanations invoking fewer causes and required disproportionately strong probabilistic evidence before choosing a more complex one.[42]

5.4 Consilience

Whewell introduced the term consilience of inductions for the situation in which a hypothesis adopted to explain one class of facts turns out also to explain facts of a different kind, which it was not designed to explain; he regarded such consilience as among the strongest marks of a true theory.[43] Thagard made consilience one of three central criteria of the best explanation, alongside simplicity and analogy, defining a theory as more consilient than another if it explains more classes of facts.[37]

Example 5.2 (Darwin)

Darwin defended natural selection largely by consilience: in the final chapter of the sixth edition of On the Origin of Species, he argued that it can hardly be supposed that a false theory would explain, in so satisfactory a manner as natural selection does, the several large classes of facts he had surveyed, including geographical distribution, the geological succession of organisms, classification, morphology and embryology.[44] Thagard uses Darwin's argument as a central illustration of IBE.[37]

5.5 Predictive success

A hypothesis gains credibility when it successfully predicts phenomena not used in constructing it. Predictivism is the thesis that evidence confirms a theory more strongly when it was predicted than when it was accommodated (built into the theory after the fact).[30] Standard illustrations include Mendeleev's periodic table, which predicted the existence and properties of then-unknown elements, and the prediction by Adams and Le Verrier of the location of Neptune.[30]

The thesis is controversial. Some argue that only the logical relation between theory and evidence matters, not the timing of discovery; others defend predictivism on the grounds that successful prediction is evidence that the theory was produced by a reliable method, or that accommodation risks constructing a theory around the data.[30] Hitchcock and Sober connect the issue to model selection: accommodating data with a flexible model risks overfitting, and a successful prediction is evidence that a model has not overfitted, so predictive success can be a symptom of a virtue rather than a virtue in its own right.[45]

5.6 Competing hypotheses

IBE is essentially comparative: a hypothesis is inferred because it is better than its rivals, so its credibility depends on the range of rivals considered.[5,36] Good abductive practice therefore requires generating plausible alternatives and finding evidence that discriminates between them, as Semmelweis did (Example 5.1). Evidence that all rivals predict equally provides no reason to prefer one; the most useful observations are those on which hypotheses disagree.[17,36]

The comparative character invites a serious objection. Van Fraassen's argument from the bad lot holds that IBE can at most tell us which of the hypotheses we have actually considered is best; unless we have reason to think the true hypothesis is likely to be among them, the best of the lot may still be false, and probably is, since the space of possible hypotheses is vast.[5,46] Defenders reply in several ways: by weakening the conclusion of IBE to comparative claims (H is closer to the truth than its rivals), by noting that our background knowledge, itself the product of earlier successful inferences, constrains the hypotheses we consider, or by allowing the set of rivals to include a catch-all none of the above.[5,36]

5.7 Bayesian interpretations of abduction

Bayesian epistemology offers a precise model of how evidence should change confidence. By Bayes' theorem, the posterior probability of hypothesis H given evidence E is[19]

P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E)

and, comparing two rival hypotheses,

P(H1 | E) / P(H2 | E) = [ P(E | H1) / P(E | H2) ] × [ P(H1) / P(H2) ]

The posterior odds equal the likelihood ratio times the prior odds.[17,19] A hypothesis that makes the evidence much more expected than its rivals do (a large likelihood ratio) gains in probability relative to them, which is a probabilistic counterpart of explanatory power.[17,38]

Example 5.3 (worked Bayesian comparison; numbers illustrative)

Let E be the observation that mortality differs sharply between two wards of the same hospital. Consider two exhaustive rival hypotheses: H1, an agent carried between patients by staff who work in one ward only; H2, a general atmospheric influence. Suppose priors P(H1) = 0.2 and P(H2) = 0.8, and likelihoods P(E | H1) = 0.8 and P(E | H2) = 0.05 (an atmospheric influence should affect both wards alike).

P(E) = 0.8 × 0.2 + 0.05 × 0.8 = 0.16 + 0.04 = 0.20.
P(H1 | E) = 0.16 / 0.20 = 0.80; P(H2 | E) = 0.04 / 0.20 = 0.20.

The likelihood ratio of 16 reverses the prior odds of 1 : 4 into posterior odds of 4 : 1. The hypothesis that better explains the contrast becomes the more probable one, as IBE would recommend.

How IBE and Bayesianism relate is contested, and three main positions can be distinguished.[5,47]

  • Compatibilism (heuristic role). Lipton argues that explanatory considerations help agents assign the priors and likelihoods that Bayesian updating requires; IBE is then a practical heuristic for doing what Bayesianism requires, and the two are complementary.[5,36]
  • Incompatibilism. Van Fraassen argued that if IBE means giving a hypothesis a bonus in probability beyond what Bayes' theorem delivers because it explains well, then the agent violates Bayesian conditionalization and is vulnerable to a dynamic Dutch book, a series of bets that guarantees a loss; IBE so understood is incoherent.[5,46]
  • Explanationist alternatives. Douven has argued, partly through computer simulations, that update rules giving explanatory bonuses can converge on the truth faster than strict conditionalization, which suggests that the Dutch book argument does not settle the matter.[5] Weisberg argues that subjective Bayesianism can accommodate IBE only by making it subservient to conditionalization in a way that drains it of substance, and proposes that explanatory considerations find a more substantial role in an objective Bayesian framework, for example in fixing priors.[47]

The Bayesian perspective clarifies each explanatory virtue. Explanatory power corresponds to how strongly a hypothesis raises the probability of the evidence; simplicity and analogy are naturally modelled as influences on prior probability; consilience corresponds to a hypothesis accumulating high likelihood ratios across independent bodies of evidence; and the bad-lot problem appears as the question of how much prior probability to reserve for hypotheses not yet conceived.[5,36,38]

6 Comparing the three modes

In practice the three modes are interwoven. A scientist abduces a hypothesis, deduces predictions from it together with auxiliary assumptions, and tests those predictions inductively; a failed prediction refutes the conjunction of hypothesis and auxiliaries by modus tollens, while a successful one confirms the hypothesis only inductively.[16,35] Peirce himself described inquiry in just these terms, with abduction proposing hypotheses, deduction drawing out their consequences and induction testing them against experience.[5,35] The structural tools of Section 2 apply to all three: whatever kind of support an argument claims, it must first be laid out clearly, with its premises, intermediate conclusions and unstated assumptions made explicit, before that support can be judged.
