1. Introduction: what informal logic is

Informal logic is the branch of logic that studies reasoning and inference as they actually occur in public debate, education, interpersonal exchange, law, medicine and other everyday contexts[1]. The term came into wide use in the 1970s, when Ralph Johnson and J. Anthony Blair at the University of Windsor founded the Informal Logic Newsletter (1978–1983) and then the journal Informal Logic[1]. The movement grew out of dissatisfaction with formal logic as a vehicle for teaching people how to evaluate the arguments they actually meet, and out of the wider critical-thinking movement in education[1].

Formal logic assesses an argument by its form: an argument is deductively valid when no interpretation makes its premises true and its conclusion false. Informal logic does not reject this, but it adds three observations. First, real arguments arrive in prose, with unstated premises, rhetorical flourishes and ambiguous words, so they must be identified and reconstructed before any test of form can be applied[1,2]. Second, much everyday reasoning is defeasible: it confers reasonable support on its conclusion while remaining open to withdrawal in the light of new information[3,4]. Third, arguments are usually moves in a dialogue between people, and whether a move is reasonable often depends on the dialogue it belongs to[5–7].

Johnson and Blair proposed a widely used standard for evaluating such arguments, known by its initials ARS[1,8]:

  • Acceptability: the premises are true or otherwise credible to the audience;
  • Relevance: the premises bear on the conclusion, giving it some support;
  • Sufficiency: taken together, the premises give enough support to warrant accepting the conclusion.

An argument that fails any of these is defective, and much of the traditional catalogue of informal fallacies can be read as recurring ways of failing one of them[1,8]. The rest of this article follows the order in which an analyst actually works: first find and reconstruct the argument, then clarify its language, then evaluate it against the fallacies and against the dialectical standards of argumentation theory.

2. Natural-language arguments

2.1 Identifying arguments

An argument is a set of statements in which some (the premises) are offered as support for another (the conclusion)[4,9]. Argumentation, by contrast, is the communicative activity of producing and exchanging such reasons in order to support or challenge positions[4]. Not every passage that contains statements is an argument: reports, descriptions, warnings, pieces of advice, conditional statements and explanations all assert things without inferring one from another[9,10].

The main textual clue is the inference indicator. Conclusion indicators include therefore, so, thus, hence, consequently, it follows that; premise indicators include because, since, for, given that, as shown by[9,10]. Indicators are neither necessary nor sufficient for an argument: many arguments have none, and words such as since and because also have temporal or explanatory uses[1,9].

Example

(a) “Since the bridge was closed, traffic has been terrible.” Here since may be temporal (“ever since”) or causal; either way it is not inferential.

(b) “The defendant's fingerprints are on the weapon and she had no alibi. She must have been at the scene.” No indicator word appears, but the second sentence is plainly inferred from the first: must signals an inferential claim.

A useful test is to ask whether the passage makes an inferential claim: does it present one statement as following from, or being supported by, others[9]? If so, it is an argument, good or bad. If it merely asserts things side by side, it is not.

2.2 Distinguishing arguments from explanations

Explanations share the vocabulary of arguments (because, so, therefore) but do a different job. In Hempel and Oppenheim's terminology an explanation consists of an explanandum, the fact to be explained, and an explanans, the statements that do the explaining[11]. An argument tries to show that something is true; an explanation tries to show why or how something already accepted as true came about[2,9].

Contrast

Explanation: “The ice on the pond melted because the temperature rose above freezing.” Nobody doubts that the ice melted; the speaker is saying why.

Argument: “The temperature has been above freezing all week, so the ice on the pond will have melted.” The melting is in question and the temperature is offered as evidence for it.

The practical test is to ask which statement is in doubt for the audience. If the “conclusion” is treated as an accepted fact, the passage is an explanation; if it is a claim that needs support, the passage is an argument[2,9]. The distinction matters for evaluation. An explanation is not refuted by noting that its explanandum is unsupported, because it never claimed to support it; and a speaker who treats an explanation as if it were proof of its own explanandum is reasoning badly[2]. The two activities are nevertheless linked: inference to the best explanation (abduction) is an argument whose premise is that some hypothesis would best explain observed facts[4].

2.3 Clarifying ambiguous language

Before an argument can be evaluated its key terms must be fixed, because an argument can look valid only because one of its words shifts meaning between premises (equivocation; see §5.4)[9,12]. Clarification has three standard steps: identify the words or phrases on which the inference turns; check whether each admits more than one reading in context (ambiguity) or lacks sharp boundaries (vagueness); and, where needed, replace it with a precising definition that fits the speaker's evident purpose[10,13]. Section 4 gives the theory of ambiguity and vagueness that underlies this step. The pragma-dialectical usage rule states the corresponding obligation for both sides of a discussion: a party must not use formulations that are insufficiently clear or confusingly ambiguous, and must interpret the other party's formulations as carefully and accurately as possible[14].

2.4 Identifying hidden assumptions

Ordinary arguments are usually enthymemes: they leave a premise, or even the conclusion, unstated because the speaker assumes the audience will supply it[9,10]. Grice's account of conversation explains why this works. Speakers are presumed to follow a cooperative principle and maxims of quantity, quality, relation and manner, so hearers can infer what is meant but not said (a conversational implicature)[15].

Example

“Of course he'll be a good senator; he was a successful businessman.”

Stated premise: He was a successful businessman.
Conclusion: He will be a good senator.
Missing premise needed to connect them: Anyone who is a successful businessman will be a good senator (or, more modestly, success in business is good evidence of being a good senator).

Two kinds of unstated assumption should be distinguished[1,2]. A missing premise is a statement the argument needs in order for its stated premises to support its conclusion; it belongs to the argument's logical structure. A background assumption is a belief the arguer takes for granted that makes the argument seem natural but is not strictly needed for the inference. Making missing premises explicit is valuable because the missing premise is frequently the weakest part of the argument: in the example above, the generalization about businessmen is far more contestable than the stated fact. The pragma-dialectical unexpressed premise rule forbids both denying a premise one has left implicit and falsely attributing an unexpressed premise to the other party[14].

2.5 The principle of charity

Because missing premises and ambiguous phrasing leave room for several readings, the analyst needs a rule for choosing among them. The principle of charity says that, among interpretations consistent with the text and context, one should prefer the one that makes the argument most reasonable[1,2]. In the philosophy of language, Davidson made a related principle constitutive of interpretation itself: to understand a speaker at all we must assume that most of their beliefs are true and consistent by our lights[16].

In argument analysis, charity has two limits. First, it is constrained by fidelity: the reconstruction must still be recognisably the arguer's argument, supported by what they said and the context in which they said it[1,2]. Second, it does not license supplying whatever premise would make an argument valid. One common guideline is to add the logically minimal premise needed to link the stated reasons to the conclusion, and then to strengthen it only as far as the context warrants[1]. Charity is not only courtesy. An uncharitable reading attacks an argument nobody made, which is the straw man fallacy, and so wastes the discussion[9,17].

2.6 Argument reconstruction

Reconstruction puts an argument into standard form: premises listed and numbered, the conclusion below a line, inference indicators removed, rhetorical material dropped, and implicit premises added and marked[2,9,10]. For extended arguments, a diagram shows how statements support one another[1]. Two support relations are basic[1,2]:

  • Linked premises work only together: each needs the other to support the conclusion (for example, “All metals conduct electricity” and “Copper is a metal”).
  • Convergent premises each give independent support, so the conclusion keeps some support if one of them fails (for example, three independent witnesses each placing a suspect at the scene).

Worked reconstruction

Original: “We shouldn't build the new stadium. Similar projects in other cities never delivered the promised jobs, and besides, the money is needed for schools. Anyone who has looked at the numbers knows this.”

  1. Comparable stadium projects in other cities did not deliver the jobs that were promised.
  2. [Implicit] If comparable projects failed to deliver promised jobs, this one probably will too.
  3. [Implicit] A public project that probably will not deliver its promised benefits should not be funded.
  4. The money is needed for schools.
  5. [Implicit] Money needed for schools should not be diverted to a stadium.

C. The city should not build the new stadium.

Structure: (1)+(2)+(3) form one linked argument and (4)+(5) another; the two lines converge on C. The sentence “Anyone who has looked at the numbers knows this” is rhetorical reinforcement rather than evidence and is omitted. Premise (2) is an analogical inference and is open to the questions in §5.2; premise (5) is a value claim an opponent may reject.

Once reconstructed, the argument can be evaluated on two separate axes: whether the premises are acceptable, and whether the inference from them is strong enough (the A versus the R and S of ARS)[1,8]. Keeping these apart prevents a common confusion: a valid argument may have false premises, and true premises may fail to support a conclusion.

3. Definitions

A definition explains the meaning of a term, the definiendum, by means of other expressions, the definiens[10,13]. Definitions serve different purposes, and a definition that is excellent for one purpose may be defective for another, so the first question about any definition is what kind it is[9,10].

3.1 Lexical definitions

A lexical (or descriptive, or reportive) definition reports how a term is already used in a language community, which is what dictionaries try to do[10,13]. Because it makes a claim about usage, it can be true or false: a lexical definition that misses common uses is inaccurate, and, as Gupta and Mackereth note, a lack of fit with existing usage is an objection to it[13]. Standard rules for good lexical definitions, found in the logic textbook tradition, include that a definition should state the essential attributes, avoid circularity, be neither too broad nor too narrow, avoid ambiguous, obscure or figurative language, and be affirmative rather than negative where possible[9,10].

Example

Defining bird as “an animal that flies” is too broad (it includes bats and bees) and too narrow (it excludes penguins and ostriches).

3.2 Stipulative definitions

A stipulative definition assigns a meaning to a term, either a new term or an old term in a new use, with no commitment that the assigned meaning agrees with prior usage[13]. Because it is a proposal rather than a report, it is neither true nor false; it can only be more or less useful, clear and consistent[9,10]. Science supplies many examples: quark was coined for a new kind of particle, and in a mathematics paper “call a number sparse if it has at most two prime factors” introduces a term for the length of the paper. The danger is a stipulation that silently replaces an ordinary meaning in a way that slants an argument, for instance redefining unemployed so that the unemployment figure falls[10].

3.3 Precising definitions

A precising definition reduces the vagueness of an existing term for a particular purpose[9,10]. It goes beyond lexical usage, because ordinary usage leaves borderline cases undecided, but unlike a pure stipulation it must remain faithful to the term's clear cases. Carnap's notion of explication is a refined version of the same idea: an inexact everyday concept (the explicandum) is replaced by an exact one (the explicatum) that should be similar to it, exact, fruitful and simple[13,18]. Gupta and Mackereth describe an explication as respecting some central uses of a term while being stipulative on others[13].

Example

A statute that says “for the purposes of this Act, a vehicle is any device with an engine capable of exceeding 25 km/h” precises a vague word. Bicycles and lorries keep their ordinary classification; the statute decides the borderline cases such as electric scooters.

3.4 Theoretical definitions

A theoretical definition gives a term's meaning by embedding it in a theory, so that accepting the definition commits one to the theory[9,10]. Defining heat as “the mean kinetic energy of molecules” presupposes the kinetic theory of matter. Such definitions cannot be assessed by consulting usage; they stand or fall with the theory's adequacy. Gupta and Mackereth's distinction between nominal definitions, which concern the meaning and use of a word, and real definitions, which concern the nature of the thing the word denotes, captures part of the same contrast[13].

3.5 Operational definitions

An operational definition specifies a term's application by an experimental or measurement procedure and its result[9,19]. The idea derives from the physicist P. W. Bridgman, who wrote in The Logic of Modern Physics (1927) that “we mean by any concept nothing more than a set of operations; the concept is synonymous with the corresponding set of operations”[19,20]. Operational definitions are indispensable for testing (for example, “a solution is acidic if it turns blue litmus paper red”), but strict operationalism faces a well-known difficulty: if each procedure defines a distinct concept, then length measured by a ruler and length measured by triangulation would be different concepts, which seems to fragment science[19]. In informal reasoning the useful lesson is narrower: an operational definition makes a claim testable, but it may not capture all of what a term means (an IQ score operationalises one aspect of intelligence, not the whole concept).

3.6 Necessary and sufficient conditions

Many definitions take the form of necessary and sufficient conditions[9,13]:

  • A is a necessary condition for B if nothing is B without being A: if B then A. Having four sides is necessary for being a square.
  • A is a sufficient condition for B if being A guarantees being B: if A then B. Being a square is sufficient for having four sides.
  • A definition by genus and difference states conditions that are jointly necessary and sufficient: x is a square if and only if x is a rectangle (genus) with all sides equal (difference).

Counterexamples test such definitions in both directions: a case that satisfies the definiens but not the definiendum shows the conditions are not sufficient (the definition is too broad); a case of the definiendum that fails the definiens shows they are not necessary (too narrow)[9,10]. Confusing the two directions is a frequent error in informal reasoning: “Oxygen is needed for fire; there is oxygen here; so there will be a fire” treats a necessary condition as sufficient. Some concepts may resist this treatment altogether; for many everyday terms, philosophers have long found it hard to give exceptionless necessary and sufficient conditions[13].

3.7 Extension and intension

Terms have two dimensions of meaning[9,10,21]. The extension (denotation) of a general term is the class of things to which it applies; the intension (connotation) is the set of attributes something must have for the term to apply. The intension of triangle is “closed plane figure with three straight sides”; its extension is every such figure. The distinction descends from Frege's contrast between the sense (Sinn) of an expression and its reference (Bedeutung): “the Morning Star” and “the Evening Star” refer to the same planet but present it in different ways, which is why “the Morning Star is the Evening Star” can be informative[22]. Carnap later developed it into the systematic “method of extension and intension”[21].

Three consequences matter for argument analysis:

  • Intension determines extension, but not conversely. Two terms can share an extension while differing in intension; equilateral triangle and equiangular triangle pick out the same figures but mean different things. Substituting one co-extensive term for another can therefore change the truth of a sentence about what someone believes or knows[21,22].
  • Empty extension. A term such as unicorn has an intension but an empty extension, so meaningful terms need not denote anything[9].
  • Inverse variation. Ordered in a series where each term adds attributes (animal, mammal, dog, beagle), increasing intension typically goes with decreasing extension[9,10].

Definitions themselves divide along this line[9,10]. Extensional (denotative) definitions indicate members of the class: by pointing (demonstrative or ostensive definition), by naming examples (enumerative definition) or by naming subclasses. Intensional definitions state attributes: by synonym, by etymology, by operation, or by genus and difference. Ostensive definitions have the special feature that they can introduce terms that cannot be eliminated in favour of other words, so they connect language directly to experience[13].

4. Ambiguity and vagueness

Ambiguity and vagueness are different defects of precision, and confusing them leads to bad diagnoses[23,24]. An expression is ambiguous when it has two or more distinct meanings; it is vague when it has a single meaning whose application has borderline cases[23,24]. Bat is ambiguous but not obviously vague; bald is vague but not ambiguous[23]. Both should also be distinguished from mere generality: “I'm visiting my sister” does not say which sister, but the sentence is not ambiguous; it is simply unspecific[23].

4.1 Lexical ambiguity

Lexical ambiguity belongs to a single word[23]. Linguists distinguish homonymy, where the meanings are unrelated (bat the animal and bat the club; bank of a river and bank that lends money), from polysemy, where they are related senses of one word (paper as material, as a document, as a newspaper company)[23]. A single word may also be ambiguous between grammatical categories, as with duck as noun and verb[23]. Polysemy is the more dangerous in argument, because closely related senses slide into one another without being noticed.

Example

“The theory of evolution is only a theory.” In everyday use theory can mean a guess; in science it means a well-confirmed explanatory framework. An argument that moves from the scientific sense in its premise to the everyday sense in its conclusion trades on polysemy.

4.2 Syntactic ambiguity

Syntactic (or structural) ambiguity arises when a sentence admits more than one grammatical structure, even though none of its words is ambiguous[23]. Sennet's examples include “superfluous hair remover” (a remover that is superfluous, or a remover of superfluous hair) and “The chicken is ready to eat” (ready to be fed, or ready to be eaten)[23]. Other common sources are attachment of modifiers (“I saw the man with the telescope”) and the scope of negation (“All that glitters is not gold”: nothing that glitters is gold, or not everything that glitters is gold). Syntactic ambiguity is the basis of the fallacy of amphiboly[9,10].

4.3 Semantic ambiguity

The term semantic ambiguity is used in more than one way, so it is worth fixing its sense here. Broadly, it is ambiguity in what a sentence means (its truth conditions), as opposed to what a speaker merely conveys by it in context[15,23]. Lexical and syntactic ambiguity are its two most familiar sources, but semantic ambiguity can also arise where neither a single word nor surface grammar is responsible. The leading example is scope ambiguity: “Every woman squeezed a man” can mean that each woman squeezed some man or other, or that there is one man whom every woman squeezed, depending on which quantifier takes wide scope[23]. Other varieties met in practice include:

  • referential ambiguity, where a pronoun has more than one possible antecedent: “The manager told the clerk that she would be promoted”;
  • collective versus distributive readings: “The students lifted the piano” (together, or each in turn), which underlies the fallacies of composition and division[9,10].

Semantic ambiguity should be kept separate from pragmatic indeterminacy. “Some of the guests left early” normally implicates that not all did, but this is a conversational implicature that can be cancelled (“some, in fact all, left early”), not a second literal meaning[15].

4.4 Vagueness

A term is vague to the extent that it has borderline cases[24]. Tall, heap, bald, child, rich and red are standard examples. Vagueness is not a defect to be eliminated everywhere: ordinary communication relies on it, and replacing “come early” with “arrive between 18:47 and 18:52” would be absurd. It becomes a problem when an argument requires a sharp line the term does not draw.

The classic expression of the problem is the sorites paradox[24]:

  1. A one-day-old human is a child.
  2. For any n, if an n-day-old human is a child, then an (n+1)-day-old human is a child.

C. A 100-year-old human (36,525 days old) is a child.

Each premise seems true and the reasoning is a chain of valid steps, yet the conclusion is false[24]. Classically, denying premise 2 implies that there is a last day of childhood, a sharp threshold that seems not to exist[24]. The main responses are[24,25]:

  • Epistemicism (Williamson and others): vague terms do have sharp boundaries, but we cannot know where they lie.
  • Supervaluationism: a sentence is true if it is true on every admissible way of making the term precise, false if false on every one, and neither otherwise; classical theorems are preserved while borderline sentences lack a truth value.
  • Degree theories: truth comes in degrees between 0 and 1, so each step in the sorites loses a little truth; critics reply that this replaces one sharp line with infinitely many.
  • Contextualism: the extension of a vague term shifts with context, and the sorites exploits these shifts.

4.5 Borderline cases

A borderline case is one where a vague term neither clearly applies nor clearly fails to apply[24]. On Sorensen's account, what makes a case borderline is that it is inquiry-resistant: no amount of empirical investigation or conceptual analysis would settle it[24]. This is what distinguishes vagueness from ignorance. Whether a man with 3,000 hairs is bald is not settled by counting more carefully.

Borderline cases are themselves vague: there is no sharp line between the clear cases and the borderline ones. This is higher-order vagueness, and it defeats any attempt to solve the problem simply by adding a third category of “borderline” with sharp edges[24].

Two argumentative errors exploit borderline cases. The first infers from the existence of borderline cases that there is no real difference between the extremes (“there is no precise point where a fetus becomes a person, so there is no difference between a zygote and a newborn”); the existence of twilight does not show that noon and midnight are the same[24]. The second is the sorites-style slippery slope, which uses small steps to carry a listener from a clear case to its opposite[26]. The remedy for both is a precising definition (§3.3), adopted for the purpose at hand and defended on that basis, as the law does when it fixes an age of majority.

5. Informal fallacies

A fallacy is traditionally defined as an argument that seems valid but is not[5,12]. Hamblin's influential Fallacies (1970) called this the “standard definition”, analysed it into three conditions (that a fallacy is an argument, that it is invalid, and that it appears valid) and argued that the textbook treatment built on it was tradition-bound and inadequate[5,12]. Many items on the standard list are not invalid in the deductive sense, and some are not arguments at all, as with the complex question. An informal fallacy is one whose defect is found in content, context or use rather than in logical form alone[9,10].

The study goes back to Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations, which lists thirteen fallacies: six dependent on language (equivocation, amphiboly, combination of words, division of words, accent and form of expression) and seven independent of it (accident, consequent, secundum quid, begging the question, non-cause, ignoratio elenchi and many questions)[12,27]. Locke added the names of the ad arguments, distinguishing ad verecundiam, ad hominem and ad ignorantiam from sound argument ad judicium, which draws on the foundations of knowledge and probability[12,28]. Copi's Introduction to Logic of 1961 gave brief accounts of eighteen informal fallacies and fixed much of the modern textbook list[12].

Two modern approaches recast the subject. The pragma-dialectical school of van Eemeren and Grootendorst defines fallacies as violations of the rules for a critical discussion aimed at resolving a difference of opinion (see §6.3)[7,12]. Walton's pragmatic theory treats many fallacies as misuses of argument schemes that are reasonable in the right dialogue but used where they do not fit, or used without answering the critical questions they raise[12,29,30]. Both explain an important fact: most fallacy names pick out patterns that are sometimes perfectly good arguments. The analyst's job is therefore not to match a passage to a label but to show why, in this context, the move fails.

The four-part classification below (relevance, weak induction, presumption, ambiguity) follows the standard textbook groupings[9,10]. Textbooks differ in detail: Hurley places appeal to unqualified authority among the fallacies of weak induction and treats composition and division as fallacies of “grammatical analogy”, whereas Copi, Cohen and Rodych count composition and division among the fallacies of ambiguity and use “defective induction” for the second group[9,10].

5.1 Fallacies of relevance

In fallacies of relevance the premises are logically irrelevant to the conclusion but are psychologically persuasive, often because they change the subject or engage emotion instead of evidence[9,10]. In ARS terms, they fail the R condition[8].

Ad hominem (against the person)

An ad hominem argument rejects a claim or argument by attacking the person who advanced it[9,12]. Three forms are standard[9,10,12]:

  • Abusive: the view should not be accepted because its proponent has some unfavourable property. “Her economic proposal is worthless; she's never held a real job.”
  • Circumstantial: the proponent's circumstances, such as self-interest, give a motive to hold the view. “Of course the oil executive says the pipeline is safe.”
  • Tu quoque (“you too”): the view is rejected because the proponent does not follow it. “My doctor says I should stop smoking, but she smokes.”

The fallacy is that the truth of a claim and the cogency of an argument do not depend on who offers them. Attacks on the person are relevant, however, when the issue is the person's reliability as a source, as when a witness's credibility is challenged in court; Walton accordingly treats ad hominem as an argument scheme that can be reasonable or fallacious according to its use in the dialogue[29,30]. The doctor who smokes may be a hypocrite, but her advice is no less sound.

Appeal to authority (ad verecundiam)

Reliance on experts is indispensable, since nobody can verify everything for themselves, so appeal to expert opinion is in itself a reasonable, defeasible argument[29,30]. It becomes fallacious when the cited authority is not an expert in the relevant field, is not trustworthy, is misquoted, or when experts disagree and the arguer cites only one side[9,10]. Walton, Reed and Macagno give the scheme for argument from expert opinion together with critical questions that test it[30]:

Major premise: Source E is an expert in subject domain S containing proposition A.
Minor premise: E asserts that A is true (false).
Conclusion: A may plausibly be taken to be true (false).

Critical questions

  1. Expertise: How credible is E as an expert source?
  2. Field: Is E an expert in the field that A is in?
  3. Opinion: What did E assert that implies A?
  4. Trustworthiness: Is E personally reliable as a source?
  5. Consistency: Is A consistent with what other experts assert?
  6. Backup evidence: Is E's assertion based on evidence?

Example: “A Nobel laureate in physics says this diet cures arthritis, so it must work” fails the field question. Locke's original ad verecundiam was broader: deferring to someone's social standing out of modesty rather than to their knowledge[12,28].

Appeal to popularity (ad populum)

The appeal to popularity argues that a claim is true, or a product good, because many people accept it[9,10]. The direct form works on a crowd's emotions (nationalist rallies); the indirect forms include the bandwagon (“everyone is switching to…”), appeal to vanity and appeal to snobbery[9]. Popularity is evidence of truth only in special cases: where the question is itself about what people believe or prefer, or where the many are independent and well-informed judges, so that their agreement is a form of expert consensus. “Millions of people use this remedy, so it works” fails because use is not evidence of effectiveness.

Appeal to emotion

An appeal to emotion substitutes the arousal of fear, anger, pride, guilt or hope for reasons that bear on the conclusion[9,10]. Emotion is not irrelevant to every conclusion: in practical reasoning about what to do, people's feelings and interests are legitimate considerations, and a vivid description can convey morally relevant facts[29]. The fallacy lies in using emotion to stand in for evidence on a factual question, or to pre-empt scrutiny of the other premises. “Think of the children!” offered as the whole case for a surveillance law tells us that children's safety matters, which nobody disputes, but not that the law would protect them. The appeals to force, pity and popularity are specific forms of this pattern[10].

Appeal to force (ad baculum)

The appeal to force tries to secure acceptance of a conclusion by threat, explicit or veiled[9,10]. “I'm sure you'll agree the report's figures are correct, if you want to keep your consulting contract.” A threat may be a good practical reason to act (paying a fine to avoid prison) but it is never evidence that a claim is true. Walton, Reed and Macagno include argument from threat in their compendium, which illustrates the general point that such moves are assessed by whether they fit the type of dialogue: a threat may be a legitimate move in a negotiation and illegitimate in a critical discussion[6,30]. In pragma-dialectics, it violates the freedom rule, because it prevents the other party from casting doubt on a standpoint[14].

Appeal to pity (ad misericordiam)

The appeal to pity seeks acceptance of a conclusion by evoking sympathy rather than presenting evidence relevant to it[9,10]. “I deserve an A on this essay; I worked on it all night and my scholarship depends on it.” Effort and need are irrelevant to the essay's quality. Pity is relevant when the conclusion is about whether to show mercy, as in sentencing or in a hardship appeal, so the test is whether the conclusion concerns desert or concerns compassion.

Genetic fallacy

The genetic fallacy, a term usually traced to Cohen and Nagel's An Introduction to Logic and Scientific Method (1934), evaluates a belief or practice by its origin rather than by present evidence for it[31]. “The idea of the weekend came from religious observance, so secular people have no reason to keep it.” Origins may be relevant to why someone believes something without being relevant to whether it is true; the circumstantial ad hominem is a special case aimed at the person rather than the idea. Origins are, however, relevant when the origin bears on reliability, as with evidence about the provenance of a document or the chain of custody for a forensic sample.

Red herring

A red herring diverts attention from the issue by introducing a different topic, often a related one, and then drawing a conclusion about the new topic or simply leaving the original question unanswered[9]. Aristotle's ignoratio elenchi, missing the point of the refutation, is the classical ancestor of this family[12,27].

Example

Asked whether the company's product contains a harmful chemical, a spokesperson replies: “Our company has created thousands of jobs in this region and donates generously to local schools.”

Straw man

The straw man fallacy distorts an opponent's argument, by exaggeration, oversimplification or selective quotation, then attacks the distorted version and claims to have refuted the original[9,10].

Example

A: “We should require background checks for gun purchases at gun shows.”
B: “My opponent wants to take away everyone's guns, and that would leave law-abiding citizens defenceless.”

It differs from the red herring in that the straw man appears to engage the opponent; the topic stays nominally the same while the position attacked is not the one held. In pragma-dialectics it violates the standpoint rule, under which an attack must relate to the standpoint the other party has actually advanced[14]. Its correction is the principle of charity (§2.5) and its opposite is steelmanning (§6.8)[17].

5.2 Fallacies of weak induction

In these fallacies the premises are relevant to the conclusion but too weak to support it[9]. They fail the S (sufficiency) condition rather than R[8]. Because inductive strength is a matter of degree, the line between weak and acceptable inductions is often contestable, and the fallacy label is a conclusion that must be argued for.

Hasty generalization

A hasty generalization draws a general conclusion from a sample that is too small or unrepresentative[9,10]. Aristotle's secundum quid, failing to distinguish what holds absolutely from what holds only with qualification, is an ancestor of the idea[12,27]. “Both students I met from that school were rude; the school must teach bad manners.” Questions to ask are how large the sample is, how it was selected, and how much variation the population shows; a single blood sample suffices to type a person's blood, but a single interview does not establish a nation's opinion.

Weak analogy

An argument from analogy has the form: A and B share properties a, b and c; A has z; so B probably has z. It is a weak analogy when the shared properties are not relevant to z, or when there are relevant differences[9,10]. Copi, Cohen and Rodych list criteria for appraising analogies: the number of entities compared, their variety, the number of respects in which they are similar, the relevance of those respects, the number of disanalogies, and the modesty of the conclusion relative to the premises[10].

Example

“A country is like a household: a household that spends more than it earns goes bankrupt, so a government should never run a deficit.” The disanalogies are relevant: a government issues its own currency (in many cases), sets taxes, and borrows on a time horizon no household has.

Appeal to ignorance (ad ignorantiam)

The appeal to ignorance concludes that a claim is true because it has not been proved false, or false because it has not been proved true[9,10]. “No one has proved that ghosts don't exist, so they do.” Walton showed that a structurally similar argument is often reasonable: “if A were true, we would know it; we do not know it; so A is (presumably) false”[30,32]. Its strength depends on how thoroughly the relevant ground has been searched. A careful check of the train timetable that shows no 3 p.m. train is good evidence that there is none. The same reasoning underlies the legal presumption of innocence, where failure to prove guilt licenses a verdict of not guilty; the argument there is valid because of an agreed allocation of the burden of proof (see §6.1)[32,33].

Slippery slope

A slippery slope argument claims that a first step will lead, through a chain of further steps, to an undesirable outcome, so the first step should not be taken[9,26]. It is fallacious when the links in the chain are not supported, so that the overall probability of reaching the bottom is low. Walton distinguishes several types, including causal slopes (one event causes the next), precedent slopes (one exception will force others by consistency) and verbal or sorites slopes that exploit vagueness (§4.5)[26]. Each link of a causal slope is a causal claim that can be checked; if each link has a probability of 0.8, a five-link chain has a probability of about 0.33 (0.85) of running to the end, assuming independence. Some slopes are well grounded: a precedent set in law may genuinely bind later decisions.

False cause

False cause covers arguments whose conclusion depends on a causal connection that probably does not exist[9,10]. Aristotle's “non-cause as cause” is its ancestor[12,27]. Common forms include treating mere correlation as causation (non causa pro causa), overlooking a common cause (ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer), reversing cause and effect, and oversimplified cause, which picks one of many contributing causes[9,10]. The gambler's fallacy, believing independent chance events are “due” to even out, is sometimes classified here[9].

Post hoc reasoning

Post hoc ergo propter hoc (“after this, therefore because of this”) is the form of false cause that infers causation from temporal sequence alone[9,10]. “I wore my lucky socks and we won; the socks helped us win.” Temporal priority is a necessary feature of a cause but far from sufficient. Distinguishing real causes requires controlled comparison, the logic of which informal logic shares with scientific method: does the effect occur without the supposed cause, and does it fail to occur when the cause is absent but everything else is the same?

5.3 Fallacies of presumption

Fallacies of presumption build an unjustified assumption into the premises or the framing of the argument, so that the conclusion appears to follow only because something questionable has been taken for granted[9,10]. They often fail the A (acceptability) condition of ARS[8].

Begging the question (petitio principii)

An argument begs the question when it assumes what it is supposed to prove[9,12]. This can happen in several ways: a premise restates the conclusion in other words; a premise could only be accepted by someone who already accepts the conclusion; or a chain of reasoning circles back to its starting point[10,12]. Begging the question is one of Aristotle's language-independent fallacies[12,27].

Examples

“Capital punishment is justified for murder because it is right to put murderers to death.” (Premise restates conclusion.)

“This book is reliable because it says it is the word of God, and anything God says is true.” (Accepting the premise requires accepting the conclusion.)

Such arguments are deductively valid; their defect is dialectical. They cannot give a person who doubts the conclusion any reason to accept it, which is precisely the job an argument is meant to do in a dispute[29].

Complex question

A complex question (Aristotle's “many questions”) asks two or more questions as one, so that any direct answer commits the answerer to a presupposition they may reject[12,27]. Hansen's example is “Are you still a member of the Ku Klux Klan?”: either “yes” or “no” implies past membership[12]. Strictly it is not an argument, which is one reason Hamblin rejected the standard definition[5,12]. The fallacy is completed when the arguer treats the forced answer as an admission. The remedy is to divide the question: deny the presupposition before answering the rest.

False dilemma

A false dilemma (or false dichotomy) presents a disjunction of options as exhaustive when it is not, usually so that rejecting one option seems to force acceptance of the other[9]. “Either we cut public services or the country goes bankrupt.” The disjunctive premise is unacceptable because further options exist, such as raising revenue or reforming services. A genuine dilemma is not fallacious: “the number is even or odd” is exhaustive. The test is whether contraries (which cannot both be true but may both be false) are being passed off as contradictories (exactly one of which must be true).

Suppressed evidence

The fallacy of suppressed evidence ignores evidence that would count significantly against the conclusion[9]. In inductive reasoning this matters especially, because inductive strength depends on the total evidence available: an inference that is strong relative to some evidence may be weak relative to all of it[9,10]. “Our clinical trial showed the drug reduced symptoms in 60% of patients” suppresses evidence if the placebo group improved at the same rate. Cherry-picking studies, quoting selectively and advertising that reports only favourable results are the everyday forms.

5.4 Fallacies of ambiguity

Fallacies of ambiguity arise when an argument's apparent force depends on a word, phrase or grammatical construction being understood in different ways at different points[9,10]. Five of Aristotle's six fallacies dependent on language are the ancestors of this group[12,27].

Equivocation

Equivocation occurs when a word or phrase shifts meaning between premises, or between premise and conclusion[9,10]. It rests on lexical ambiguity[12].

Example

  1. The end of a thing is its perfection.
  2. Death is the end of life.

C. Death is the perfection of life.

“End” means goal in premise 1 and termination in premise 2. On either uniform reading one premise is false; on the mixed reading the argument has four terms and is invalid.

The diagnostic method follows from §2.3: fix the meaning of the key term and rewrite each premise with that meaning; if no single meaning makes all the premises acceptable, the argument equivocates.

Amphiboly

Amphiboly arises from a sentence whose grammatical structure allows two readings, when the arguer draws a conclusion from the reading that was not intended or is false[9,10]. It rests on syntactic ambiguity. A classical illustration is the oracle reported to have told Croesus that if he attacked Persia he would destroy a great empire; he attacked, and the empire destroyed was his own[34]. A modern one: “The sign said ‘Fine for parking here’, so I parked.”

Accent

Accent is a fallacy in which a shift of emphasis changes the meaning of a statement, and a conclusion is drawn from the shifted meaning[9,10]. In Aristotle the fallacy concerned differences of pronunciation in Greek words written alike[12,27]; modern textbooks extend it to stress, italics and quotation out of context[9,10]. “We should not speak ill of our friends” read with emphasis on friends seems to permit speaking ill of everyone else. A film review that said “a stunning failure” and is advertised as “Stunning!” is accent by selective quotation.

Composition

The fallacy of composition infers that a whole has a property because its parts have it, or that a class collectively has a property because its members individually have it[9,10]. “Every player on the team is excellent, so the team is excellent” ignores the possibility that the players do not play well together. The inference is sometimes correct (if every brick is red, the wall is red), so the question is whether the property is one that transfers from parts to wholes. Properties like weight add up; properties like being well designed or cheap do not (each part of a car may be cheap while the car is expensive).

Division

Division is the converse: it infers that the parts or members have a property because the whole or the class has it[9,10]. “The university is excellent, so every department in it is excellent.” “Americans consume more sugar than any other nation, so this American consumes more sugar than any foreigner” confuses the distributive and collective readings of “Americans” (§4.3). In Aristotle's list, “combination” and “division” of words referred to a different phenomenon, ambiguity in how words in a sentence are grouped, and the modern part–whole fallacies are a later development[12].

6. Argumentation theory

Argumentation theory studies argument as an activity: two or more parties, with commitments, taking turns to put forward, question and defend claims[4,7]. Hamblin's critique of the fallacy tradition was a turning point, because he proposed to study fallacies within systems of “formal dialectic” in which participants make moves governed by rules and accumulate commitment stores[4,5]. Three research programmes have dominated since: pragma-dialectics (van Eemeren and Grootendorst), the dialogue-and-schemes approach (Walton and collaborators), and the formal study of defeasible argument in philosophy and artificial intelligence[1,4,35].

6.1 Burden of proof

The burden of proof determines under what conditions an arguer is obliged to support a claim with an argument, and how strong that argument must be[33]. Its companion notion is presumption: a proposition that is tentatively accepted unless and until someone gives a reason against it, which shifts the burden to whoever disputes it[33]. The pragma-dialectical burden of proof rule states the basic principle: a party that advances a standpoint is obliged to defend it if asked to do so by the other party[14].

Several distinctions are needed in practice[33]:

  • The burden can be global, fixed for the whole dialogue (in a criminal trial the prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt), or local, shifting as the dialogue proceeds (once the prosecution produces evidence, the defence may need to answer it).
  • The standard of proof differs by context: scientific acceptance, civil liability (balance of probabilities) and criminal guilt (beyond reasonable doubt) demand different strengths of support.
  • Many disputes are really about who has the burden. Illegitimately shifting it, “prove that my claim is false”, is the root of most fallacious appeals to ignorance[32,33].

Common heuristics allocate the burden to the party who makes a positive claim, who proposes a change from the status quo, or who asserts something contrary to well-established knowledge; these are defaults that depend on the type of dialogue, not universal laws[33].

6.2 Dialectical reasoning

Dialectical reasoning is reasoning conducted, or assessed, as an exchange between parties with different views. Aristotle distinguished demonstration, which proceeds from premises that are true and primary, from dialectic, which reasons from endoxa, opinions accepted by everyone, by most people or by the wise[36]. The modern revival treats arguments as moves in a dialogue, and evaluates them by how they contribute to its goal[4–6].

Walton and Krabbe classified the main types of dialogue according to the initial situation, the participants' aims and the goal of the dialogue as a whole[6]:

TypeInitial situationGoal of the dialogue
PersuasionConflict of opinionsResolve the conflict on the merits
InquiryNeed for proofProve or disprove a hypothesis
NegotiationConflict of interestsReach a settlement both parties can live with
Information-seekingOne party lacks informationTransfer of knowledge
DeliberationNeed to actDecide the best course of action
EristicPersonal conflictAir grievances, establish a winner

This framework explains why the same move can be fallacious in one dialogue and legitimate in another. A threat is an acceptable move in a negotiation but a fallacy in a persuasion dialogue; a shift from one dialogue type to another without the participants' agreement is itself a source of fallacy[6,29].

6.3 Critical discussion

The critical discussion is the ideal model of argumentative dialogue in pragma-dialectics, a discussion aimed at resolving a difference of opinion on the merits[7]. It passes through four stages[7,14]: the confrontation stage, where the difference of opinion is externalised; the opening stage, where the parties fix their roles and shared starting points; the argumentation stage, where standpoints are defended and criticised; and the concluding stage, where the result is determined. Ten rules govern the discussion[7,14]:

#RuleContentTypical violation
1FreedomParties must not prevent each other from advancing or doubting standpoints.Ad baculum, ad hominem
2Burden of proofA party who advances a standpoint must defend it if asked.Evading or shifting the burden
3StandpointAn attack must relate to the standpoint actually advanced.Straw man
4RelevanceA standpoint may be defended only by argumentation relating to it.Red herring, appeals to emotion

On this view, any violation of a rule that hinders resolution of the difference of opinion is a fallacy, which explains why the traditional list is so heterogeneous[7,12]. (The “typical violation” column gives standard illustrations; the mapping is many-to-many.)

6.4 Argument schemes

Argument schemes are forms of argument that represent the structure of common types of argument used in everyday discourse and in special contexts such as law and science[30]. Unlike deductive forms, most schemes are defeasible: they create a presumption in favour of their conclusion that can be defeated. Each scheme comes with a set of critical questions, and an instance of the scheme is evaluated by whether its premises are acceptable and whether the critical questions that arise can be answered[29,30]. Walton, Reed and Macagno's compendium describes more than sixty schemes, from argument from threat to abductive argumentation and the full slippery slope argument[30].

Some widely used schemes are:

  • Argument from expert opinion (see §5.1).
  • Argument from consequences: if A is brought about, good (bad) consequences will plausibly follow; therefore A should (not) be brought about. Critical questions ask how likely the consequences are, what evidence supports them, and whether there are opposite consequences to weigh[30].
  • Argument from analogy: case C1 is similar to case C2; A is true in C1; so A is plausibly true in C2. Critical questions ask about relevant differences and about other cases where A is false[30].
  • Argument from sign, from cause to effect, from established rule, from precedent, from ignorance and practical reasoning (goal-to-means) are also standard[30].

Schemes unify the informal fallacy tradition with a positive theory of good reasoning: many fallacies are failed instances of reasonable schemes, in which a critical question was ignored[1,29,30]. They are also the basis of the pragma-dialectical argument scheme rule (rule 7 above)[14].

6.5 Rebuttals

In Toulmin's model of argument, first published in 1958, an argument has six parts[37]: a claim; the data (grounds) offered for it; a warrant licensing the step from data to claim; backing for the warrant; a qualifier (“presumably”, “probably”) expressing the strength of the step; and a rebuttal, the conditions of exception under which the warrant does not apply.

Toulmin's example, schematised

Data: Harry was born in Bermuda. Warrant: A man born in Bermuda will generally be a British subject. Backing: the relevant statutes and legal provisions. Qualifier: presumably. Claim: Harry is a British subject. Rebuttal: unless both his parents were aliens, or he has become a naturalised American[37].

Toulmin's rebuttal is internal to the argument: it marks the known exceptions that the qualifier allows for. In dialectical usage, a rebuttal is also the move by which an opponent argues for one of those exceptions, or for the falsity of the claim itself[4,37]. Building the rebuttal conditions into the argument is honest reasoning; omitting them presents a defeasible argument as if it were conclusive.

6.6 Defeaters

A defeater is new information that removes the support a reason gives for a conclusion. Pollock's work made the concept central to the theory of defeasible reasoning, and he distinguished two kinds[3,35]:

  • A rebutting defeater is a reason for believing the negation of the conclusion. If the thing looks red but a reliable spectrometer reports that it reflects only green light, I have a reason to think it is not red.
  • An undercutting defeater is a reason for doubting that the premise supports the conclusion in the circumstances, without being a reason against the conclusion itself. If I learn the thing is illuminated by red light, its looking red is no longer good evidence that it is red, but nor is it evidence that it is not red[3,35].

Many critical questions of argument schemes correspond to undercutters: asking whether an expert is biased does not show that their claim is false, but it weakens the link between their testimony and the conclusion[30,35]. Pragmatically, the distinction matters because an undercutter returns the question to neutral ground, while a rebutter creates a reason on the other side that must be weighed[35].

6.7 Counterarguments

A counterargument is an argument against a standpoint or against an argument for it. There are three main points of attack, corresponding to the parts of an argument[2,8,35]:

  1. Attack a premise: argue that a premise is false or unacceptable (failing A).
  2. Attack the inference: argue that the premises, even if true, do not support the conclusion sufficiently, for example by raising an undercutting defeater or an unanswered critical question (failing R or S).
  3. Attack the conclusion: give an independent argument for its negation, a rebutting defeater.

The first two leave the conclusion open; only the third gives positive reason to reject it. A frequent error is to treat a successful attack of type 1 or 2 as if it were of type 3, inferring that a conclusion is false because one argument for it fails. The pragma-dialectical closure rule draws the correct line: a failed defence obliges the proponent to retract the standpoint, not the opponent to accept its contrary[14]. Good argumentative writing anticipates counterarguments and either answers them or qualifies the conclusion to accommodate them[2].

6.8 Steelmanning

Steelmanning (in the academic literature, “iron manning”) is the practice of reconstructing an opponent's argument in its strongest form before evaluating it, the deliberate opposite of the straw man[17]. It extends the principle of charity (§2.5) from interpretation to improvement: one supplies stronger premises or better evidence than the opponent did, and asks whether the position survives. Its rationale is epistemic: refuting the best version of a view is more informative than refuting a weak one, and if the strong version survives, one has learned something.

Aikin and Casey argue that iron manning can itself be done inappropriately[17]. Reconstructing a stronger version of an opponent's argument can misrepresent what they actually hold, and it can make the critic appear to have engaged with the opponent when in fact they are debating a position of their own design. They use the distinction between appropriate and inappropriate iron manning to clarify the boundaries of the virtue of open-mindedness[17]. A practical rule: one should steelman and say that one is doing so, keeping separate the question of what the opponent argued from the question of what the best argument for their view is.

6.9 Dialogue logic

Dialogical logic is a formal approach in which the meaning of the logical constants and the validity of formulas are defined in terms of games between two players[38,39]. It was developed by Paul Lorenzen and Kuno Lorenz from the late 1950s in connection with constructive mathematics, and it draws on the ancient tradition of dialectic[38,39]. The Proponent (P) asserts a thesis and the Opponent (O) challenges it[38]. Two kinds of rule govern play[38]:

  • Particle rules fix how each logical constant may be challenged and defended. To challenge a conjunction A ∧ B, the challenger chooses a conjunct and the defender must assert it; to challenge a disjunction A ∨ B, the challenger asks and the defender chooses which disjunct to assert; to challenge a conditional A → B, the challenger asserts A and the defender must then assert B.
  • Structural rules fix how a play begins, whose turn it is, and when it ends. A key one, the formal rule, allows P to assert an atomic formula only if O has already asserted it, so that P's win depends on form alone.

A formula is valid when P has a winning strategy, a plan that wins no matter how O plays[38]. Variations in the structural rules yield different logics: the original Lorenzen rules yield intuitionistic logic, and relaxing them yields classical logic[38].

A short dialogue for p → p

  1. P: p → p (thesis)
  2. O: p (challenges the conditional by asserting its antecedent)
  3. P: p (defends by asserting the consequent; permitted by the formal rule because O asserted p at move 2)

O has no further legal move, so P wins. Since this holds whatever atomic sentence p is, the thesis is valid.

Dialogue logic and informal argumentation theory share a root conviction, that logical consequence can be understood through the rights and obligations of people arguing with each other. Walton and Krabbe's Commitment in Dialogue explicitly combined the Lorenzen tradition with Hamblin's study of dialogue and commitment in order to analyse fallacies[6], and formal dialogue systems are now used in artificial intelligence to model argument, burden of proof and defeat[33,35].
