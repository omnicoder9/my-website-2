1. Introduction: what a proof system is

The previous article in this series defined consequence semantically: a set of formulas Γ entails φ, written Γ ⊨ φ, when every truth assignment that makes all of Γ true makes φ true (see Classical Propositional Logic, §6.7)[1]. A proof system (also called a deductive system or calculus) captures consequence syntactically instead. It fixes a stock of formal objects (axioms, rules, sequents or clauses) and says precisely which finite arrangements of them count as proofs, so that whether something is a proof can be checked mechanically, by inspecting its shape, without any appeal to meaning or truth[1–3]. If there is a proof of φ from premises Γ in a system S, we write Γ ⊢S φ, dropping the subscript when the system is clear[3,4].

Two metatheorems tie the syntactic relation to the semantic one:

Soundness and completeness

Soundness: if Γ ⊢ φ then Γ ⊨ φ. Everything provable is valid; the system never proves a falsehood from truths.

Completeness: if Γ ⊨ φ then Γ ⊢ φ. Every valid consequence has a proof.

All five systems presented here are sound and complete for classical propositional logic[3–7]. They differ in what a proof looks like, in how easy proofs are to find, in how long proofs must be, and in what each reveals about the structure of logic. Cook and Reckhow gave the general definition now used in complexity theory: a propositional proof system is a polynomial-time computable function whose range is exactly the set of tautologies, so that every string encodes a proof of some tautology and every tautology has at least one proof[8].

Historically the systems arrived in roughly the order treated below, though not in this order of presentation. Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) contained the first axiomatic system of modern logic, with modus ponens as its rule of inference[9,10]; Hilbert and his school developed axiomatic calculi systematically in the 1910s and 1920s, and Hilbert and Ackermann's textbook of 1928 presented an axiomatic system for logic together with the question of its completeness[10–12]. In 1934–35 Jaśkowski and Gentzen independently introduced natural deduction, and Gentzen's same work introduced the sequent calculus and proved the cut-elimination theorem[10,13–15]. Semantic tableaux were introduced by Beth in 1955 and independently by Hintikka, and were later streamlined by Smullyan[6,16,17]. Robinson's resolution principle of 1965 was designed for computers rather than for people[18,19].

Conventions. Lower-case p, q, r, s are propositional variables; Greek φ, ψ, χ are metavariables over formulas; Γ, Δ, Π, Λ range over finite collections of formulas. ⊥ (falsum) is a constant that is false under every assignment. Every concrete derivation in this article was verified by a small proof checker written for the purpose, which also confirmed by exhaustive truth-table evaluation that each conclusion really follows from its premises.

2. Natural deduction

Natural deduction was designed to mirror how mathematicians actually reason: they make assumptions, reason under them, and then discharge them, rather than starting from logical axioms[14,15]. Gentzen described his aim as a formalism that comes as close as possible to actual reasoning, and Jaśkowski's paper of the same year pursued the same goal with a different notation[13–15]. The defining features are that a natural deduction system usually has no logical axioms, that it allows temporary assumptions, and that each connective is governed by a pair of rules, one for introducing it and one for eliminating it[15,20].

2.1 Assumptions

A proof starts from premises, which stand for the whole proof, and may at any point introduce an assumption (also called a hypothesis or supposition): a formula entered without justification, “for the sake of argument”[15,21]. Every line derived after an assumption depends on it, and depends on it until the assumption is discharged by a rule designed for the purpose, such as conditional proof or reductio[4,15,20]. A proof is complete only when its final line depends on no undischarged assumptions other than the stated premises[4,21]. A formula derived with no premises and no undischarged assumptions is a theorem, written ⊢ φ[4].

The importance of assumptions is that some rules need them. To prove p → r it is natural to assume p, derive r, and then conclude p → r, at which point the assumption p is no longer needed: the conditional itself carries the information that r holds given p[15,21].

2.2 Subproofs

The stretch of a proof that lies within the scope of an assumption is a subproof (a subordinate proof in Fitch's terminology, or a box in Jaśkowski's)[13,15,22]. Three notations for keeping track of subproofs are in common use[15]:

  • Fitch style. Jaśkowski drew boxes around subproofs; Fitch simplified the boxes to vertical lines, with a short horizontal line under each assumption[15,22]. This is the format used for the linear proofs in this article. A subproof opens with its assumption and closes when the vertical line ends; nested subproofs give nested lines. Huth and Ryan's textbook uses boxes in the same way[21].
  • Gentzen (tree) style. Proofs are trees with the conclusion at the root and premises and assumptions at the leaves; an assumption that has been discharged is written in square brackets and numbered to match the rule that discharged it[4,14,20].
  • Suppes–Lemmon style. Proofs are numbered lists, and each line carries an explicit list of the assumptions on which it depends; discharging removes a number from that list[15].

Subproofs impose a scope restriction: a line inside a subproof may use lines from the enclosing proof (Fitch's rule of reiteration, abbreviated R), but once a subproof is closed, none of its lines may be cited individually again, because they depended on an assumption that is no longer in force[15,21,22]. Only the subproof as a whole may be cited, by a rule that discharges its assumption. Violating the restriction makes proofs unsound: from the premise p → q one could assume p, derive q, close the subproof and then “reuse” q, concluding q from p → q alone, which is invalid. (The proof checker used for this article rejects exactly that move.)

An introduction rule for a connective says how to infer a formula whose main connective is that connective: it states the grounds on which such a formula may be asserted[15,20]. On this view the meaning of φ ∧ ψ is fixed by the fact that it may be asserted exactly when φ and ψ both may.

An elimination rule says what may be inferred from a formula with a given main connective[15,20]:

The elimination rules are in harmony with the introduction rules when they allow one to get out of a formula no more than the introduction rule required to put in. Prawitz made this precise as the inversion principle: anything that follows from a formula by an elimination rule already follows from the grounds for introducing it[15,20]. Harmony is what makes detours removable. If a proof introduces φ ∧ ψ by ∧I and immediately applies ∧E to get φ back, the two steps can simply be deleted, because φ was already available[10,20]. Prawitz's normalization theorem shows that every natural deduction proof can be transformed, by systematically removing such detours, into a normal proof in which no formula is first introduced and then eliminated; normal proofs have the subformula property, meaning every formula in them is a subformula of the conclusion or of an undischarged assumption[7,15,20].

2.5 Conditional proof

Conditional proof (CP) is the traditional name for →-introduction viewed as a proof strategy: to prove φ → ψ, assume φ, derive ψ within the subproof, and discharge the assumption to conclude φ → ψ[15,21,23]. Conditional proof is the natural-deduction counterpart of the deduction theorem for axiomatic systems (section 4.3): if Γ, φ ⊢ ψ then Γ ⊢ φ → ψ. In natural deduction this is a primitive rule; in a Hilbert system it is a theorem about the system that must be proved[3,15].

2.6 Proof by contradiction

Two different forms of argument travel under the name “proof by contradiction”, and natural deduction separates them cleanly[4,15].

  • Negation introduction (¬I): assume φ, derive ⊥, conclude ¬φ. This proves a negative statement by refuting its opposite, and is acceptable in intuitionistic as well as classical logic[4,20].
  • Indirect proof or reductio ad absurdum (IP, also called RAA): assume ¬φ, derive ⊥, conclude φ. This proves a positive statement and is characteristically classical; adding it (or an equivalent rule) to intuitionistic natural deduction yields classical logic[4,15,20].

Introductory texts usually present the method as a single rule: assume the negation of what is to be proved, derive a contradiction, and conclude the desired statement[23]. The distinction matters because IP proves formulas that ¬I alone cannot, such as double-negation elimination, the law of excluded middle and Peirce's law[4,20].

3. Common rules

The rules below form the standard natural deduction system for classical propositional logic, in the form found in Gentzen, Prawitz, van Dalen and Huth and Ryan; rule names and minor details vary between textbooks[4,14,15,20,21]. In linear (Fitch-style) proofs a rule is justified by citing line numbers, and a rule that discharges an assumption cites the whole subproof as a range such as 3–5. Each rule is sound: if its premises are true under an assignment, so is its conclusion, and when it discharges an assumption, the conclusion is true under every assignment that makes the remaining open assumptions true[4,21].

4. Axiomatic systems

An axiomatic or Hilbert-style system proves theorems from a set of logical axioms using a very small number of inference rules, often only one[3,10,12]. A proof (or derivation) of φ from hypotheses Γ is a finite sequence of formulas, each of which is an axiom, a member of Γ, or follows from earlier formulas by a rule, and whose last formula is φ[2,3]. There are no assumptions to be discharged and no subproofs; every line is either unconditionally given or derived.

4.1 Axiom schemas

Modern presentations specify the axioms by axiom schemas: patterns containing metavariables, every substitution instance of which is an axiom[2,3]. A single schema therefore stands for infinitely many axioms. Mendelson's system, based on the connectives ¬ and →, has three schemas[3]:

Axiom schemas of Mendelson's system L

(A1) φ → (ψ → φ)

(A2) (φ → (ψ → χ)) → ((φ → ψ) → (φ → χ))

(A3) (¬ψ → ¬φ) → ((¬ψ → φ) → ψ)

For example, (q → r) → (p → (q → r)) is an instance of A1 with φ := q → r and ψ := p, while p → (q → q) is not an instance of A1, since A1 requires the innermost consequent to be the same formula as the antecedent. All three schemas are tautologies, which is the first step towards soundness[3]. (The checker for this article confirmed by truth tables that A1–A3 are valid, and verified each axiom instance below by pattern matching.) The other connectives are introduced as abbreviations: φ ∧ ψ for ¬(φ → ¬ψ), φ ∨ ψ for ¬φ → ψ, and φ ↔ ψ for (φ → ψ) ∧ (ψ → φ)[3].

An alternative to schemas, used by Frege and in early axiomatizations, is to state finitely many specific axioms and add a rule of substitution allowing a variable to be replaced uniformly by any formula throughout a theorem[1,10]. The two approaches produce the same theorems.

4.2 Inference rules

Mendelson's system has a single rule of inference, modus ponens: from φ and φ → ψ, infer ψ[3]. Modus ponens preserves truth under every assignment, and every axiom is a tautology, so a simple induction on the length of proofs shows that every theorem is a tautology (soundness)[2,3]. A characteristic feature of Hilbert systems is this division of labour: the axioms carry nearly all the logical content, and the rules are as few and as simple as possible[10,12].

A useful distinction is between a rule of inference, which applies to arbitrary premises (including hypotheses), and a rule that applies only to theorems, such as substitution in a system without schemas. Applying substitution to a hypothesis would be unsound: from the hypothesis p it would yield q, which does not follow from p.

The tool that makes Hilbert systems usable in practice is the deduction theorem: if Γ, φ ⊢ ψ then Γ ⊢ φ → ψ[2,3]. Its proof is an induction on the derivation of ψ that uses exactly A1 and A2, and it is effective: it transforms a derivation from Γ, φ into a derivation from Γ alone, at the cost of making it longer[3]. Applied to Example 4.3 it gives ⊢ ¬¬p → p; applied to Example 4.2 twice, it gives ⊢ (p → q) → ((q → r) → (p → r)). The deduction theorem is the metatheoretical counterpart of the natural deduction rule →I.

Three further properties of Mendelson's system are standard results:

  • Completeness. Every tautology is a theorem. Mendelson's proof, following Kalmár, shows how to imitate each row of a truth table by a derivation from the literals true in that row, and then eliminates the hypotheses one at a time using the deduction theorem[3].
  • Independence. No axiom schema can be derived from the other two. Independence is proved by finding, for each schema, a many-valued “truth table” under which the other schemas always take a designated value and modus ponens preserves it, but the schema in question does not[3].
  • Decidability. Because the system is sound and complete, theoremhood coincides with being a tautology and can therefore be decided by truth tables, even though searching for Hilbert-style proofs directly is impractical[2,3].

Many different Hilbert systems for classical propositional logic exist; they differ in their primitive connectives and axioms but have the same theorems[1,3]. Ben-Ari's system H, for instance, keeps A1 and A2 and replaces A3 by the contraposition schema (¬ψ → ¬φ) → (φ → ψ)[5]. From the standpoint of proof length, Cook and Reckhow showed that all standard Hilbert-type (“Frege”) systems and natural deduction systems are polynomially equivalent: each can simulate the others with at most a polynomial increase in proof size[8].

5. Sequent calculus

Gentzen introduced the sequent calculus in the same 1935 work as natural deduction, because he could not formulate the normal-form theorem he was after directly in natural deduction, especially for classical logic[10,12,14]. His classical calculus is called LK and his intuitionistic calculus LJ (from logistischer Kalkül)[7,14].

5.1 Sequents

A sequent has the form Γ ⇒ Δ, where the antecedent Γ and the succedent Δ are finite lists (or, in many modern treatments, multisets) of formulas[7,12]. Its intended meaning is that the conjunction of the formulas in Γ implies the disjunction of the formulas in Δ[12,14]. Semantically, Γ ⇒ Δ is valid when every assignment that makes all members of Γ true makes at least one member of Δ true[5,7]. Two limiting cases follow: ⇒ φ (empty antecedent) asserts that φ is valid, and the empty sequent ⇒ (both sides empty) can never be valid, since an empty disjunction is false[12].

Where a natural deduction proof manipulates formulas under assumptions, a sequent proof manipulates the judgements “these assumptions yield one of these conclusions” directly: the sequent Γ ⇒ φ records explicitly what a natural deduction proof of φ from open assumptions Γ establishes[7,24]. Every rule of the sequent calculus operates on the left or right side of a sequent, and instead of elimination rules there are left rules, which introduce a connective in the antecedent[7,12].

Proofs are trees whose leaves are initial sequents (axioms) of the form

φ ⇒ φAx

and the rules fall into three groups: structural rules, logical rules, and the cut rule[7,14].

5.2 Structural rules

Structural rules manipulate the arrangement of formulas in a sequent without regard to their logical form[7,12]. Gentzen's LK has three pairs (W for weakening, C for contraction, X for exchange; L and R for the side acted on):

Γ ⇒ Δφ, Γ ⇒ ΔWL
Γ ⇒ ΔΓ ⇒ Δ, φWR
φ, φ, Γ ⇒ Δφ, Γ ⇒ ΔCL
Γ ⇒ Δ, φ, φΓ ⇒ Δ, φCR
Γ, φ, ψ, Π ⇒ ΔΓ, ψ, φ, Π ⇒ ΔXL
Γ ⇒ Δ, φ, ψ, ΛΓ ⇒ Δ, ψ, φ, ΛXR
  • Weakening (thinning) adds an arbitrary formula to either side. It is sound because adding an assumption cannot invalidate an inference, and adding a disjunct cannot falsify a disjunction[7,12].
  • Contraction merges two occurrences of the same formula into one[7,12].
  • Exchange (permutation) swaps adjacent formulas, so that order does not matter[7,12]. When antecedent and succedent are taken to be multisets, as in the derivations below, exchange becomes implicit[7,24].

Structural rules matter far beyond classical logic. Dropping or restricting them yields substructural logics: relevant logics reject weakening, and linear logic, in which premises are resources that cannot be reused or ignored, rejects both weakening and contraction[25].

5.3 Logical rules

Each connective has a left rule and a right rule (∧ and ∨ have two variants of one of them). In each rule the displayed compound formula in the conclusion is the principal formula, the displayed components in the premises are the active formulas, and Γ, Δ, Π, Λ are the unchanged context[7,14]:

The right rules correspond to natural deduction's introduction rules: ∧R, ∨R and →R are ∧I, ∨I and →I in sequent form[7,24]. The left rules play the role of elimination rules, but they work by introducing the connective on the left, so that every rule except cut builds a larger formula from smaller ones[7,12]. Negation simply moves a formula across the arrow: ¬φ on the left behaves like φ on the right, and vice versa[12,14]. The symmetry between left and right rules for every connective, including negation, is what makes LK suitable for classical logic[12].

LJ, the intuitionistic calculus, is obtained from LK by allowing at most one formula in the succedent[7,14]. The two derivations below both need two formulas on the right at some point, which is why they are classical.

Example 5.1 (⇒ p ∨ ¬p in LK)

Read from the top: the axiom, then ¬R moves p across the arrow, the two ∨R rules build the disjunction twice, and contraction (CR) merges the two copies.

p ⇒ pAx⇒ p, ¬p¬R⇒ p, p ∨ ¬p∨R2⇒ p ∨ ¬p, p ∨ ¬p∨R1⇒ p ∨ ¬pCR

Example 5.2 (Peirce's law in LK)

Weakening (WR) introduces q, which never needs to be proved; →L splits the context between its two premises; contraction then merges the two occurrences of p.

p ⇒ pAxp ⇒ p, qWR⇒ p, p → q→Rp ⇒ pAx(p → q) → p ⇒ p, p→L(p → q) → p ⇒ pCR⇒ ((p → q) → p) → p→R

Example 5.3 (modus tollens in LK: p → q, ¬q ⇒ ¬p)

p ⇒ pAxq ⇒ qAxp → q, p ⇒ q→Lp → q, p, ¬q ⇒¬Lp → q, ¬q ⇒ ¬p¬R

Example 5.4 (distribution, using contraction on the left)

Because ∧L uses only one conjunct at a time, the premise is first duplicated by contraction (reading downwards, CL is the last step) so that both conjuncts can be extracted.

p ⇒ pAxp, q ⇒ pWLq ⇒ qAxp, q ⇒ qWLp, q ⇒ p ∧ q∧Rp, q ⇒ (p ∧ q) ∨ (p ∧ r)∨R1p ⇒ pAxp, r ⇒ pWLr ⇒ rAxp, r ⇒ rWLp, r ⇒ p ∧ r∧Rp, r ⇒ (p ∧ q) ∨ (p ∧ r)∨R2p, q ∨ r ⇒ (p ∧ q) ∨ (p ∧ r)∨Lp, p ∧ (q ∨ r) ⇒ (p ∧ q) ∨ (p ∧ r)∧L2p ∧ (q ∨ r), p ∧ (q ∨ r) ⇒ (p ∧ q) ∨ (p ∧ r)∧L1p ∧ (q ∨ r) ⇒ (p ∧ q) ∨ (p ∧ r)CL

5.4 Cut rule

The cut rule is[12,14]:

Γ ⇒ Δ, φφ, Π ⇒ ΛΓ, Π ⇒ Δ, ΛCut

The formula φ is the cut formula. Cut expresses the transitivity of consequence: if φ follows from Γ (or one of Δ holds), and φ together with Π yields Λ, then the intermediate result φ can be dropped[7,12]. It is the formal counterpart of proving a lemma and then using it, and it plays the role that modus ponens plays in Hilbert systems[7,24]. Cut differs from every other rule in one important respect: the cut formula disappears from the conclusion, so the conclusion does not show which formulas were used to derive it[12].

Example 5.5 (a derivation with a cut)

To show p ∧ q ⇒ q ∨ r, prove the “lemma” q from p ∧ q, prove q ∨ r from q, and cut on q:

q ⇒ qAxp ∧ q ⇒ q∧L2q ⇒ qAxq ⇒ q ∨ r∨R1p ∧ q ⇒ q ∨ rCut

The same end-sequent has a derivation without cut, obtained by applying the left rule beneath the right rule:

q ⇒ qAxq ⇒ q ∨ r∨R1p ∧ q ⇒ q ∨ r∧L2

5.5 Cut elimination

Gentzen's Hauptsatz (main theorem), or cut-elimination theorem, states that if a sequent is derivable in LK, then it is derivable without the cut rule; the same holds for LJ[7,12,14]. The proof is constructive. It gives a procedure that removes cuts by permuting them upward through the derivation, replacing each cut on a compound formula by cuts on its components, until all cuts are on axioms and can be deleted[7,10,14]. Example 5.5 shows one such reduction in miniature.

The theorem has several important consequences:

  • Subformula property. Every rule other than cut has the property that each formula in its premises is a subformula of some formula in its conclusion. A cut-free derivation therefore contains only subformulas of the formulas in its end-sequent[7,10,12].
  • Consistency. The empty sequent ⇒ has no cut-free derivation, since no rule other than cut can have an empty conclusion and the axioms are not empty. By cut elimination, it has no derivation at all, so the calculus is consistent. This is a purely syntactic consistency proof that makes no use of semantics[12].
  • Decidability and proof search. Because of the subformula property, a search for a cut-free proof of a propositional sequent can be organised backwards from the goal, considering only finitely many subformulas, which yields a decision procedure[7,14,19]. Cut-free LK is in this sense the basis of automated proof search in sequent form[19].
  • Analyticity. Cut-free proofs are analytic: they never introduce concepts not already present in the statement being proved. Natural deduction's normalization theorem (section 2.4) is the counterpart result for natural deduction[7,15,24].

Eliminating cuts has a cost: cut-free derivations can be very much longer than derivations with cut, and the procedure in general increases proof size greatly[7]. For propositional logic, D'Agostino showed that cut-free systems such as analytic tableaux cannot even polynomially simulate truth tables: some formulas have short truth tables but only very long cut-free proofs[26].

Later work refined Gentzen's calculi. Ketonen (1944) gave classical rules that are invertible: whenever the conclusion of a rule is derivable, so are its premises[10]. Kleene's G3 calculi of 1952 build weakening and contraction into the axioms and logical rules, so that no structural rules are needed at all[7,10,27]. Some rules of the classical calculus G3c are shown below; note that the axiom allows arbitrary side formulas and that →L copies its context to both premises[7,24].

p, Γ ⇒ Δ, pAx
φ, ψ, Γ ⇒ Δφ ∧ ψ, Γ ⇒ Δ∧L
Γ ⇒ Δ, φ, ψΓ ⇒ Δ, φ ∨ ψ∨R
Γ ⇒ Δ, φψ, Γ ⇒ Δφ → ψ, Γ ⇒ Δ→L

In G3c every rule is invertible and every premise is simpler than the conclusion, so backward proof search can never go wrong by choosing the “wrong” rule. That observation is the bridge to semantic tableaux[19,24].

6. Semantic tableaux

The method of semantic tableaux (also called analytic tableaux or truth trees) was introduced by Beth in 1955 and independently by Hintikka, and was given its now-standard streamlined form by Smullyan[6,16,17]. A tableau is a systematic search for an assignment that makes a given set of formulas true. To prove φ one searches for an assignment making ¬φ true; to show that Γ ⊨ φ one searches for an assignment making all of Γ and ¬φ true. If every avenue of search ends in contradiction, no such assignment exists and the formula or argument is valid; if some avenue survives, it describes a counterexample[5,6,28]. Tableaux are therefore refutation procedures, and the same tree is both a proof and a decision procedure[6,28].

6.1 Tableau construction

A tableau is a tree whose nodes are labelled by formulas. Its construction proceeds as follows[5,6,28]:

  1. Place the formulas to be tested (premises and negated conclusion) in a single column at the root.
  2. Choose a formula on an open branch that is not a literal and has not yet been used on that branch, and apply the rule for its form (section 6.2), extending every open branch passing through it.
  3. Mark a branch closed as soon as it contains both a formula and its negation.
  4. Stop when every branch is closed, or when on some open branch every non-literal formula has been used.

The process always terminates for propositional formulas, because every rule replaces a formula by strictly shorter ones[6,28]. The tableau is closed if all its branches are closed. A closed tableau for ¬φ is a tableau proof of φ[6].

6.2 Branching rules

Smullyan's uniform notation sorts compound formulas into two types[6,28]. An α formula (conjunctive type) is true exactly when both its components are true; its rule adds both components to the same branch. A β formula (disjunctive type) is true exactly when at least one component is true; its rule splits the branch in two, one component on each side. Double negation is a non-branching rule with a single component.

Table 2. Tableau rules for the five connectives. The biconditional rules each put two formulas on each new branch.
Non-branching (α)Adds to the branchBranching (β)Left branch | right branch
φ ∧ ψφ, ψφ ∨ ψφ  |  ψ
¬(φ ∨ ψ)¬φ, ¬ψ¬(φ ∧ ψ)¬φ  |  ¬ψ
¬(φ → ψ)φ, ¬ψφ → ψ¬φ  |  ψ
¬¬φφφ ↔ ψφ, ψ  |  ¬φ, ¬ψ
¬(φ ↔ ψ)φ, ¬ψ  |  ¬φ, ψ

Each rule reflects a line of the truth table. For instance, φ → ψ is true if and only if φ is false or ψ is true, which gives the branches ¬φ and ψ; and ¬(φ → ψ) is true only in the single case where φ is true and ψ false[5,6]. Since the order in which rules are applied does not affect the outcome, a useful heuristic is to apply α rules before β rules, and among β rules to prefer those for which one new branch closes at once; both keep the tree small[5,28].

The tableau rules are the rules of a cut-free sequent calculus, written upside down: a tableau is, in effect, a cut-free LK derivation in which sets of formulas take the place of sequents[6,19]. An α rule corresponds to a one-premise sequent rule, and a β rule to a two-premise rule.

6.3 Closed branches

A branch is closed when it contains some formula φ together with ¬φ (or contains ⊥)[6,28]. No assignment can make every formula on a closed branch true, and the rules guarantee that any assignment making the root true makes every formula on at least one branch true. Hence if every branch closes, the root formulas are jointly unsatisfiable[5,6]. In the figures below, × marks a closed branch, with the numbers of the two clashing lines; the annotation after each formula gives the rule and the line it came from.

Example 6.1 (a tableau proof)

To prove ((p → q) ∧ (q → r)) → (p → r), start from its negation. Two α steps (lines 2–7) break the root into the literals and conditionals it requires; two β steps then split on the conditionals, and every branch closes.

Example 6.2 (testing an argument)

The argument p ∨ q, p → r, q → r ∴ r (a form of constructive dilemma) is valid if its premises together with ¬r are unsatisfiable.

6.4 Open branches

A branch that is not closed is open. A branch is complete (or finished) when every non-literal formula on it has had its rule applied on that branch[6,28]. A complete open branch is the significant case: Smullyan showed that the set of formulas on such a branch is a Hintikka set, a set that is downward saturated (it contains the components required by each of its α and β formulas) and contains no complementary pair, and that every Hintikka set is satisfiable[6,28]. An open branch that is not yet complete proves nothing, since further expansion might close it.

The completeness theorem for tableaux follows: if φ is valid, then every complete tableau for ¬φ is closed, because a complete open branch would yield an assignment making ¬φ true[6,28]. Together with soundness, this makes the tableau method a decision procedure for propositional validity[5,6].

6.5 Countermodels

A complete open branch yields a countermodel directly: assign T to every variable p that occurs as a formula on the branch and F to every variable whose negation ¬p does; variables that occur in neither form may be assigned either value[5,6]. This assignment makes every formula on the branch true, in particular the root formulas[6]. For an argument, it is an assignment under which the premises are true and the conclusion false.

Example 6.3 (finding a countermodel)

Is ((p → q) → r) → (p → r) valid? The tableau for its negation does not close.

The open branch contains the literals p, ¬r and ¬q. Under p = T, q = F, r = F: p → q is F, so (p → q) → r is T, while p → r is F; the formula is therefore false, as the open branch predicted.

Example 6.4 (a countermodel to an argument)

The countermodel in Example 6.4 can also be found by hand: if p is false then p ↔ q forces q to be false, and then q ∨ r forces r to be true. A closed tableau, in contrast, is a certificate that no countermodel exists. The fallacy of affirming the consequent, p → q, q ∴ p, behaves the same way: its tableau has an open branch giving p = F, q = T.

7. Resolution

Resolution, introduced by J. A. Robinson in 1965, is a refutation method with a single rule of inference that operates on formulas in clausal form[18,19]. Robinson designed it for first-order logic, where it is combined with unification; the propositional case treated here is its ground (variable-free) core[5,18]. Its economy (one rule, one data format) made it the dominant method of automated theorem proving for decades[19].

7.1 Literals

A literal is a propositional variable or the negation of one: p is a positive literal and ¬p a negative literal[5,19]. The complement of a literal ℓ, written ℓ̄, is ¬p if ℓ = p and p if ℓ = ¬p. A pair of complementary literals cannot both be true[5].

7.2 Clauses

A clause is a finite disjunction of literals, conventionally written as the set of its literals, so that ¬p ∨ q becomes {¬p, q}[5,19]. A set of clauses is understood as their conjunction, so a clause set is a formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF)[5,21]. Every formula can be converted into an equivalent CNF by eliminating → and ↔, pushing negations inward with De Morgan's laws, and distributing ∨ over ∧; for large formulas, the Tseitin transformation produces an equisatisfiable CNF of linear size instead[5,29]. The previous article describes both procedures in detail.

Two boundary cases are central to the method. The empty clause, written □, is the disjunction of no literals; it is false under every assignment, and a clause set containing it is unsatisfiable[5,18]. A clause containing a complementary pair, such as {p, ¬p, q}, is a tautology and can be deleted from any clause set without changing its satisfiability[5,30].

7.3 Resolution rule

The resolution rule

From clauses C1 containing a literal ℓ and C2 containing its complement ℓ̄, infer the resolvent

(C1 ∖ {ℓ}) ∪ (C2 ∖ {ℓ̄}).

For example, {¬p, q} and {¬q, r} resolve on q to give {¬p, r}: this is hypothetical syllogism in clausal dress. {p} and {¬p, q} resolve to {q}, which is modus ponens; and {p} and {¬p} resolve to the empty clause[5,19]. The rule is sound: any assignment satisfying both parents satisfies the resolvent, since whichever value ℓ takes, the remaining literals of one parent must be true[5,18]. Resolution can also be seen as a restricted form of the cut rule applied to clauses, with the resolved literal as the cut formula[7].

7.4 Refutation

Resolution is used to show that a clause set is unsatisfiable. A resolution refutation of a clause set S is a sequence of clauses, each either a member of S or a resolvent of two earlier clauses, that ends in □[5,18]. To show Γ ⊨ φ, convert Γ ∪ {¬φ} to clausal form and refute it; to show ⊨ φ, refute the clausal form of ¬φ[5,19].

Example 7.1 (refuting a negated theorem)

To prove ((p → q) ∧ (q → r)) → (p → r) (the formula of Example 6.1), negate it. Its negation is equivalent to (¬p ∨ q) ∧ (¬q ∨ r) ∧ p ∧ ¬r, which gives four clauses:

1{¬p, q}given
2{¬q, r}given
3{p}given
4{¬r}given
5{q}resolve 3, 1 on p
6{r}resolve 5, 2 on q
7□resolve 6, 4 on r

Example 7.2 (the argument of Example 6.2, by resolution)

The premises p ∨ q, p → r, q → r and the negated conclusion ¬r give the clauses below.

1{p, q}given
2{¬p, r}given

Example 7.3 (all four combinations excluded)

The clause set below rules out every assignment to p and q, one clause per row of the truth table, so it is unsatisfiable.

1{p, q}given
2{¬p, q}given
3{p, ¬q}given
4{¬p, ¬q}given
5{q}resolve 1, 2 on p
6{¬q}resolve 3, 4 on p
7□resolve 5, 6 on q

Resolution is refutation complete: if a clause set is unsatisfiable, the empty clause can be derived from it[5,18,19]. It is not complete in the stronger sense of deriving every consequence directly. From {p} alone, for instance, resolution cannot derive the logically implied clause {p, q}, since there is nothing to resolve; this is why resolution is always used by refutation[5,28].

When a clause set is satisfiable, resolution eventually stops producing new clauses without reaching □. For the invalid argument p → q, q ∴ p (affirming the consequent), the clause set is {¬p, q}, {q}, {¬p}. No two clauses contain a complementary pair, so the set is already saturated, the empty clause is not derivable, and the argument is invalid; p = F, q = T satisfies all three clauses. Because only finitely many clauses can be built from finitely many variables, saturation always terminates in the propositional case, so resolution is also a decision procedure[5,28].

7.5 Automated theorem proving

Automated reasoning, the use of computer programs to find proofs, was the original motivation for resolution[18,19]. Several strands of propositional automated reasoning are worth distinguishing.

  • The Davis–Putnam procedure. Davis and Putnam (1960) proposed a procedure for clause sets which, among other rules, eliminates variables one at a time by forming all resolvents on that variable[30]. Their procedure is resolution-based in this sense, and predates Robinson's general formulation of resolution[19,30].
  • DPLL. Davis, Logemann and Loveland (1962) replaced variable elimination, which can use very large amounts of memory, with splitting: choose a variable, try it true and then false, simplifying the clause set each time and propagating forced (unit) literals[19,31]. The DPLL procedure remains one of the best complete satisfiability algorithms, and modern solvers optimize and generalize it[19,29].
  • Conflict-driven clause learning. Current SAT solvers extend DPLL by learning new clauses from conflicts, and the learned clauses are themselves derivable by resolution, so a run of such a solver on an unsatisfiable instance implicitly yields a resolution refutation[29]. SAT solvers are used in hardware and software verification, planning and many other areas[19,29].
  • Search strategies. Unrestricted resolution generates many useless clauses. Strategies such as the set of support strategy, which forbids resolving two clauses that both come from the original axioms, cut the search space while preserving refutation completeness[19]. Deleting tautologies and subsumed clauses (clauses that contain another clause in the set) is also standard[19,28].
  • Other calculi. Tableaux and cut-free sequent calculi are also used in automated reasoning, especially for non-classical logics, because their rules decompose formulas in a goal-directed way[17,19].

Resolution has an inherent limitation. Haken proved in 1985 that the propositional formulas expressing the pigeonhole principle (that n + 1 pigeons cannot be placed in n holes with at most one pigeon per hole) have no resolution refutations of size polynomial in n: every refutation is exponentially long[32]. Since unsatisfiability is coNP-complete, no proof system can have polynomial-size proofs of all tautologies unless NP = coNP, which is why lower bounds of this kind are a central topic of proof complexity[8].

Two different notions of equivalence between proof systems should be kept apart. All five systems are extensionally equivalent: they prove exactly the tautologies, and derive φ from Γ exactly when Γ ⊨ φ[3–6]. They are not all equally efficient. Hilbert-style systems and natural deduction polynomially simulate one another[8]; resolution requires exponentially long refutations of the pigeonhole formulas[32]; and cut-free tableaux cannot polynomially simulate even truth tables on some families of formulas[26]. Cook and Reckhow showed that NP = coNP if and only if some propositional proof system has polynomial-size proofs of every tautology, so separating proof systems by efficiency bears directly on one of the central open problems of computational complexity[8].
