Introduction

A block cipher, a hash function or a signature scheme is a tool. A cryptographic protocol is a plan for using those tools between two or more parties who do not fully trust the network between them, and usually do not fully trust each other. The protocol says who sends which message, in what order, with what contents, and what each party must check before believing anything. Almost every real-world cryptographic failure of the last thirty years has been a protocol failure rather than a broken primitive: an unauthenticated negotiation (FREAK, Logjam), a reused nonce, a message accepted in the wrong state (SKIP-TLS), a key bound to the wrong identity (Lowe's attack), or a missing check of a certificate's name.[1,2,3,4]

This article works through the protocols that protect most of the world's traffic. Part 1 sets out the vocabulary: handshakes, authentication, key exchange, freshness, state machines, downgrade resistance and channel binding. Part 2 covers transport security, from the broken SSL versions to TLS 1.3, DTLS and QUIC. Part 3 covers network-layer and infrastructure protocols: IPsec and IKEv2, WireGuard, OpenVPN, SSH and the DNS security protocols. Part 4 turns to end-to-end messaging (Signal, MLS and post-quantum ratchets), Part 5 to email, files, documents and software updates, and Part 6 to Kerberos, password-authenticated key exchange and the Noise framework. Every part has runnable code, and the listings in §7 were checked against published test vectors wherever these exist.

Where this fits in the series. The primitives used here are developed in earlier articles: block ciphers and AEAD in symmetric-key cryptography, Diffie–Hellman, ECC and KEMs in public-key cryptography, HMAC and HKDF in message authentication and key derivation, and signatures, X.509 and PKI in digital signatures and PKI. Formal security notions (IND-CCA, EUF-CMA, games and reductions) are in security theory. This article assumes those and concentrates on how they are combined.

Notation: A, B honest parties (Alice, Bob); M or Eve the attacker; NA a nonce chosen by A; {m}K encryption of m under K; SigA(m) A's signature; gx a Diffie–Hellman share; H a hash; ‖ concatenation; RTT = network round trip.

1. Protocol fundamentals

1.1 Protocol design

Protocol designers start from a threat model. The standard one, due to Dolev and Yao (1983), gives the attacker complete control of the network: it can read, delete, delay, reorder, replay and inject any message, and it can run sessions of its own with honest parties, but it cannot break the primitives (it cannot decrypt without a key or forge a MAC).[5] Computational models used in proofs (Bellare–Rogaway, Canetti–Krawczyk) give the attacker the same network control plus the ability to corrupt parties and reveal session keys, and ask that the keys of untouched sessions remain indistinguishable from random.[6,7] Modern models add state reveal (malware reads a device's memory) and quantum adversaries who record traffic today to decrypt later.

Abadi and Needham's eleven principles of "prudent engineering" (1996) remain the best short checklist. The most important are: every message should say what it means, so its interpretation depends only on its content; if the identity of a principal matters to a message's meaning, name it explicitly in the message; be clear about why encryption is being used (secrecy, authentication or binding); and do not assume that a nonce, sequence number or timestamp guarantees what it doesn't.[8] Anderson and Needham called the task "programming Satan's computer": the program is a handful of messages, and the adversary picks the inputs.[9]

Engineering experience adds more rules. Authenticate the transcript (every byte negotiated), not just the key. Separate keys by purpose with labelled KDF outputs. Encode messages unambiguously, with lengths or a fixed schema, so that two different messages can never have the same encoding. Keep options few: every cipher suite, extension and fallback path is state the attacker can steer. Prefer one well-analysed core (TLS 1.3, Noise, MLS) over a bespoke design, and analyse new designs with a tool (§1.12) before deployment.[10,11]

1.2 Security handshakes

Most protocols have two phases. A handshake negotiates parameters, authenticates the parties and establishes shared keys. A record layer (or transport phase) then protects application data with an AEAD under those keys, with sequence numbers for ordering and replay protection. Handshakes trade round trips against properties. One-message ("zero round-trip") designs, such as Noise K/X or TLS 0-RTT, cannot have a fresh responder contribution, so their first message lacks forward secrecy and replay protection. A two-message (1-RTT) handshake gives the initiator full security on receipt of message 2 but leaves the responder unsure the initiator is live until message 3 or the first data. Three messages give mutual authentication with identity hiding (SIGMA-I, Noise XX, IKEv2 IKE_AUTH).[12,13]

1.3 Authentication protocols

Entity authentication protocols prove liveness and identity. ISO/IEC 9798 standardised the basic shapes: one-pass with a timestamp, two-pass challenge–response with a nonce, and three-pass mutual authentication, each built from symmetric encryption, MACs or signatures.[14] The basic challenge–response is: B sends a fresh nonce NB; A returns SigA(NB ‖ B) or MACK(NB ‖ B). Including B's name stops reflection attacks, where an attacker bounces B's challenge back to B in a parallel session.

The cautionary tale is the Needham–Schroeder family (1978).[15] The symmetric version, which used a trusted server, was shown by Denning and Sacco to let an attacker who obtains an old session key replay the server's ticket and impersonate A forever; their fix, timestamps, became the basis of Kerberos.[16] The public-key version was believed correct for 17 years until Gavin Lowe found a man-in-the-middle attack with the model checker FDR in 1995.[3]

Authentication factors are also protocol inputs. A password can be checked by sending it over a server-authenticated channel (HTTPS login, SSH password auth), by a challenge–response that keeps it off the wire (SCRAM, RFC 5802), or by a PAKE that never exposes anything an eavesdropper or a fake server could test offline (§6.2).[17] Hardware authenticators sign a server challenge together with the origin they see (WebAuthn), which is why they resist phishing; this is covered in the signatures and PKI article.

1.4 Key exchange protocols

Unauthenticated Diffie–Hellman (A sends ga, B sends gb, both compute gab) is secure only against passive eavesdroppers: a man in the middle runs one exchange with each side.[18] An authenticated key exchange (AKE) fixes this by binding the shares to long-term identities. The main families are:

  • Signed DH: each side signs its share. The naive version suffers from identity misbinding; Diffie, van Oorschot and Wiener's Station-to-Station (STS) protocol signs both shares and encrypts the signatures,[19] and Krawczyk's SIGMA ("SIGn-and-MAc") signs both shares and MACs the signer's own identity under a key derived from gab. SIGMA is the template for IKEv2 and TLS 1.3.[12]
  • Implicitly authenticated DH: long-term DH keys are mixed into the key derivation, as in MQV/HMQV, the "3DH" combination used by Signal's X3DH, and Noise patterns such as KK and IK.[13,20,21] No signature is needed; only the holder of the right private key can compute the session key.
  • KEM-based: an ephemeral or static KEM replaces DH, which is how post-quantum ML-KEM (FIPS 203) enters TLS, SSH, IKEv2 and Signal.[22] Because a KEM is not symmetric like DH, KEM-only AKEs (KEMTLS, for example) need care about which party authenticates first.
  • Key transport: one side picks the key and encrypts it to the other's public key, as in RSA key exchange in TLS 1.2 or OpenPGP. Key transport cannot give forward secrecy unless the key-encryption key is itself ephemeral, which is why TLS 1.3 removed RSA key transport.[23]
  • Password-based (PAKE) and third-party (Kerberos) key establishment, covered in Part 6.

A subtle requirement is resistance to unknown key-share (UKS) attacks, where A and B end up with the same key but A thinks it is shared with B while B thinks it is shared with Eve. UKS was formalised by Blake-Wilson, Johnson and Menezes; the defence is to include both identities in what is signed, MACed or hashed into the key.[24] Related is key-compromise impersonation (KCI): an attacker who steals A's long-term key should not be able to impersonate others to A. Signed-DH designs resist KCI; some implicitly authenticated designs do not.[25]

1.5 Session establishment

A session is the state that both sides keep after the handshake: peer identity, negotiated algorithms, traffic keys, sequence numbers, and resumption secrets. Good protocols derive all of this from a single secret through a labelled key schedule, so that keys for different directions, epochs and purposes are independent. TLS 1.3 derives separate client and server handshake keys, application keys, an exporter secret and a resumption secret from one chain of HKDF calls (§2.5); WireGuard derives one sending and one receiving key from its Noise chaining key; IKEv2 derives SK_d, SK_ai, SK_ar, SK_ei, SK_er, SK_pi and SK_pr from SKEYSEED.[23,26,27] A session also needs a session identifier that both sides agree on (typically the transcript hash), a limit on how much data each key may protect (AEAD usage limits), a way to rekey (TLS 1.3 KeyUpdate, SSH re-exchange, IKEv2 CREATE_CHILD_SA, WireGuard's two-minute rekey) and an authenticated close, so that an attacker cannot truncate a stream undetected.[11]

1.7 Nonces and timestamps

The word nonce ("number used once") covers three different things that must not be confused:

  1. Protocol freshness nonces, such as ClientHello.random: unpredictable values (typically 32 random bytes) that make each run's transcript unique, so signatures and MACs from one run are useless in another. Predictability matters: if an attacker can predict B's challenge, it can obtain A's response in advance. Netscape's early SSL generated its randoms from the time of day and process IDs, and Goldberg and Wagner showed that the seed, and hence the session keys, could be recovered by a short search.[30]
  2. AEAD nonces: values that must never repeat under one key, but need not be secret or random. Protocols usually build them from a sequence number XORed with a per-key IV (TLS 1.3, QUIC) or from a counter (WireGuard, SSH ChaCha20-Poly1305). Reusing one with AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305 leaks the XOR of plaintexts and the authentication key; the symmetric article shows the forbidden attack in detail.
  3. Signature nonces (ECDSA's k), which must be secret and uniform; see the PKI article.

Timestamps avoid a round trip (the receiver needs no prior challenge) at the cost of clock synchronisation and a replay cache spanning the acceptance window. Kerberos's default window is five minutes.[31] WireGuard uses a 12-byte TAI64N timestamp in its first handshake message only to reject replays of an old initiation; the responder keeps the largest timestamp seen per peer, so no cache is needed.[26] Practical systems often combine both: a timestamp to bound the cache, and a nonce to make each message unique.

1.8 Sequence numbers

Record layers number every message. The number does three jobs: ordering, replay detection and nonce construction. Over a reliable byte stream (TLS over TCP, SSH) the number is implicit: both ends count, it is never sent, and any deletion, reordering or replay makes the next AEAD check fail. Over datagrams (DTLS, QUIC, ESP, WireGuard) packets can legitimately be lost or reordered, so the number is sent explicitly and the receiver keeps a sliding window: a high-water mark plus a bitmap of recently seen numbers.[29,32,33]

Sequence numbers must never wrap under one key. ESP's 32-bit counter (or 64-bit with extended sequence numbers) forces a rekey before wrap-around.[29] TLS 1.3 uses a 64-bit counter and additionally limits the number of records per key for AES-GCM, because of the AEAD's confidentiality bounds.[23] The 2023 Terrapin attack shows what happens when the number is not fully bound to the transcript: SSH's implicit counters were not reset when encryption started, so a network attacker could add messages during the unauthenticated part of the handshake and then delete the same number of messages after it, silently removing a security-relevant extension message. OpenSSH's fix, "strict KEX", resets the counters at each key change.[35]

1.9 Protocol state machines

Each party in a protocol is a state machine: in each state only certain messages are acceptable, and each accepted message moves it to a new state. Specifications often describe the "happy path" as a message sequence chart and leave the rest implicit, and implementations then accept messages they should reject. Beurdouche et al. tested TLS libraries systematically in 2015 and found that several accepted handshakes in which the server simply omitted messages: the SKIP-TLS attack let a network attacker impersonate any server to Java's JSSE client by leaving out the messages that carry the server's certificate and its key-exchange signature, and FREAK (accepting an export RSA key when none was offered) came from the same study.[1] Protocol-state fuzzing, which learns an implementation's actual state machine from its responses, found similar bugs in TLS, SSH and DTLS stacks.[36]

The defence is to write the state machine down and implement it as an explicit table, as TLS 1.3 does in its Appendix A.[23] Listing 7.3 implements the TLS 1.3 client table and rejects a skipped CertificateVerify, application data before Finished, and a second HelloRetryRequest. Other state-machine rules worth knowing: reject a message in an unexpected state with a fatal alert, not by ignoring it; never accept application data before authentication completes; treat renegotiation and post-handshake messages as new state transitions to be verified, not as side channels (the 2009 TLS renegotiation attack, fixed by RFC 5746, injected a prefix into a victim's renegotiated session).[37]

1.10 Downgrade resistance

Negotiation lets old and new implementations interoperate, and gives attackers a lever: make both sides agree on the weakest option they share. Downgrades have taken three forms:

  • Parameter downgrades inside one version. FREAK and Logjam tricked TLS 1.2 peers into using 512-bit "export-grade" RSA or Diffie–Hellman, which the attacker could break during the connection. Logjam worked because the server's signature in ServerKeyExchange covered the DH parameters and the randoms, but not the client's list of offered cipher suites, and the Finished MACs that do cover the list were keyed from the weak secret.[1,2]
  • Version downgrades. Browsers once retried failed connections with lower versions ("fallback dance"), so an attacker who broke the first attempt got SSL 3.0 and then POODLE.[38] TLS_FALLBACK_SCSV (RFC 7507) signalled such retries,[39] and TLS 1.3 servers put a sentinel (44 4F 57 4E 47 52 44 01, "DOWNGRD" + 01) in the last 8 bytes of ServerHello.random when negotiating TLS 1.2; the random is covered by the server's signature, so a TLS 1.3 client that sees it knows it is being downgraded.[23]
  • Protocol downgrades: stripping STARTTLS from SMTP or IMAP, or redirecting HTTPS to HTTP (SSL stripping), countered by HSTS, MTA-STS and implicit TLS.[40,41]

Bhargavan et al. formalised the property: a protocol is downgrade-resilient if the parameters it negotiates are those that the honest parties' configurations would choose without an attacker. The general recipe is to authenticate the full negotiation transcript with a key or signature the attacker cannot forge even if it breaks the weakest offered option.[42] TLS 1.3 does this by having the server sign a hash of the entire transcript (CertificateVerify). 1.11 Channel binding

Protocols are layered: a user logs in with SCRAM, Kerberos or a password inside a TLS tunnel. If the inner authentication is not tied to the outer channel, a man in the middle can terminate TLS with the victim, open a second TLS connection to the server, and relay the inner authentication between them. Channel binding feeds a value that uniquely identifies the outer channel into the inner authentication, so the relay fails because the two TLS connections have different values.[43]

RFC 5929 defined three TLS channel bindings: tls-unique (the first Finished message), tls-server-end-point (a hash of the server certificate) and tls-unique-for-telnet.[44] The 2014 triple-handshake attack showed that tls-unique was not unique in TLS 1.2: an attacker could arrange two connections with the same master secret and Finished values, which broke renegotiation and channel binding together; the extended master secret extension (RFC 7627) fixed it by hashing the whole handshake into the master secret.[45,46] For TLS 1.3, RFC 9266 defines tls-exporter: 32 bytes from the TLS exporter with label EXPORTER-Channel-Binding, and states that tls-unique is not defined for TLS 1.3.[47] Listing 7.1 computes it; Listing 7.2 shows that OpenSSL still returns 48 bytes for "tls-unique" on a TLS 1.3 connection, which is exactly the kind of value applications should not use.

Channel binding is used by SCRAM-SHA-256-PLUS in PostgreSQL and XMPP, by Kerberos and NTLM's Extended Protection for Authentication on Windows, by EAP methods, and in a different form by WebAuthn and token-bound OAuth (mTLS-bound access tokens, RFC 8705).[17,48]

1.12 Formal analysis

Because protocol flaws are logical rather than mathematical, they are well suited to automated analysis. Symbolic tools model messages as terms and the attacker as Dolev–Yao. ProVerif (Blanchet, 2001) and Tamarin (Meier, Schmidt, Cremers and Basin, 2013) can prove secrecy and authentication properties for unbounded numbers of sessions or produce an attack trace.[49,50] Computational proofs (pen-and-paper, or with CryptoVerif and EasyCrypt) reduce protocol security to the hardness of the primitives. TLS 1.3 was the first major protocol designed with both kinds of analysis in the loop: Tamarin models found a flaw in a draft's post-handshake authentication, and multiple computational proofs covered the final handshake.[51,52] Signal, Noise, WireGuard, MLS, PQXDH and OPAQUE all have published symbolic or computational analyses.[53,54,55] The analyses also have limits: they prove properties of a model, and the Selfie attack on TLS 1.3 external PSKs and the ALPACA cross-protocol attack both exploited assumptions the models had not stated.[56,57]

2. Transport security

Transport security protocols turn an untrusted byte stream or datagram service into an authenticated, confidential channel between two endpoints. TLS is by far the most widely deployed cryptographic protocol: it protects HTTPS, email submission and relay, DNS (DoT and DoH), VPNs, databases and most service-to-service traffic. Its history is also the best catalogue of protocol mistakes and their fixes.

2.1 SSL and its historical weaknesses

Netscape designed the Secure Sockets Layer for its browser. SSL 1.0 was never released; SSL 2.0 shipped in 1995 and SSL 3.0, a near-complete redesign by Freier, Karlton and Kocher, in 1996.[58,59] The IETF adopted SSL 3.0 with small changes as TLS 1.0 in 1999, followed by TLS 1.1 (2006), TLS 1.2 (2008) and TLS 1.3 (2018); a revised TLS 1.3 specification, RFC 9846, replaced RFC 8446 in July 2026 without changing the wire protocol.[23,60,61,62]

SSL 2.0 had structural flaws: the handshake was not authenticated, so an attacker could delete strong cipher suites from the client's list; the same key protected integrity and confidentiality in export mode; MACs used MD5 with a weak construction; and connection closure relied on the TCP FIN, so an attacker could truncate data undetected. Wagner and Schneier's 1996 analysis of SSL 3.0 found it much improved but already flagged the version-rollback and cipher-suite-rollback risks that would return for twenty years.[63] SSL 2.0 was prohibited in 2011 and SSL 3.0 deprecated in 2015; TLS 1.0 and 1.1 followed in 2021.[64,65,66]

2.2 Transport Layer Security (TLS)

TLS has two layers. The handshake protocol negotiates a version and algorithms, authenticates the server (and optionally the client) with certificates or pre-shared keys, and derives keys. The record protocol splits data into records of at most 214 bytes, protects each with the negotiated AEAD using an implicit 64-bit sequence number, and carries handshake, alert and application data as separate content types.[23] Extensions carry most features: Server Name Indication (SNI) tells a shared server which site the client wants; Application-Layer Protocol Negotiation (ALPN) picks HTTP/2 or HTTP/1.1; supported_groups and signature_algorithms list acceptable algorithms.[76,77] To keep the extension mechanism usable, clients send random reserved values (GREASE) that servers must ignore, so that buggy implementations that reject unknown values are found early.[78]

TLS 1.2 computes a 48-byte master secret with its HMAC-based PRF from the pre-master secret and the two randoms (or, with RFC 7627, from a hash of the whole handshake), and derives MAC keys, encryption keys and IVs from it. A cipher suite such as TLS_ECDHE_RSA_WITH_AES_128_GCM_SHA256 (0xC02F) fixes the key exchange (ECDHE), authentication (RSA signatures), record protection (AES-128-GCM) and PRF hash (SHA-256) together.[61,79,80] Secure TLS 1.2 today means: ECDHE or FFDHE key exchange only, AEAD suites only (AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305), extended master secret, secure renegotiation or none, and no compression.[11]

2.4 TLS 1.3

TLS 1.3 was rebuilt around a SIGMA-style signed Diffie–Hellman exchange with a single key schedule.[12,23] The client guesses the server's preferred group and sends a key share in its first message, so a full handshake takes one round trip. Everything after the ServerHello, including the server certificate, is encrypted under handshake traffic keys. The server proves possession of its private key by signing the transcript hash (CertificateVerify), and both sides confirm the keys and transcript with Finished MACs.

Compatibility forced some odd details. The legacy_version field still says 0x0303 (TLS 1.2); the real version, 0x0304, is negotiated in the supported_versions extension, because too many middleboxes broke on a new value. For the same reason TLS 1.3 sends a dummy ChangeCipherSpec and a fake session ID in "middlebox compatibility mode".[23] The design was analysed formally during standardisation (Tamarin, ProVerif, and several computational proofs), the first time this happened for a protocol of TLS's scale.[51,52]

2.5 The TLS 1.3 key schedule

Every TLS 1.3 secret comes from one chain of HKDF-Extract and HKDF-Expand calls. Three extractions add, in turn, the PSK (or zeros), the (EC)DHE shared secret (or zeros), and nothing; between them Derive-Secret(secret, label, messages) = HKDF-Expand-Label(secret, label, Hash(messages), Hash.length) binds each output to the transcript up to that point.[23,81]

Three properties follow from this structure. Each key depends on the transcript, so a different negotiation gives unrelated keys. Handshake and application keys are separate, so a compromised application key reveals nothing about the handshake. And because the resumption secret is derived after the client's Finished, it is bound to the full authenticated handshake, which closes the triple-handshake class of attacks by design. The exporter lets applications derive their own keys bound to the connection (RFC 5705-style; used by QUIC, channel binding and EAP-TLS).[23,47]

2.7 Certificate validation

TLS server authentication is only as good as the client's certificate checks. A client must: build a chain from the leaf to a trust anchor in its root store, verifying each signature; check validity periods, basic constraints, key usage and name constraints; check that the leaf's extended key usage permits serverAuth; check that the requested host name matches a subjectAltName dNSName or iPAddress entry (the subject common name is no longer used for this); and apply the revocation and Certificate Transparency policy of its platform.[87,88] The details of X.509, chains, OCSP and CT are in the PKI article.

Implementations have repeatedly got this wrong. Georgiev et al. found in 2012 that payment SDKs, cloud clients and middleware disabled or mis-used certificate checks so often that they called validation code "the most dangerous code in the world"; Fahl et al. found similar problems in about 8% of a large sample of Android apps.[4,89] Apple's 2014 "goto fail" bug (CVE-2014-1266), a duplicated line that skipped the signature check on ServerKeyExchange, let any network attacker impersonate any TLS 1.0–1.2 server to iOS and macOS clients.[90] The lesson for application code: use a library's high-level "verify everything" defaults (Python's ssl.create_default_context(), Go's tls.Config without InsecureSkipVerify), and never turn checks off to make an error go away. 2.8 Session resumption

A full handshake costs public-key operations and, in TLS 1.2, two round trips. Resumption reuses secrets from an earlier connection. TLS 1.2 had two methods: session IDs (the server caches the master secret under an ID) and session tickets (RFC 5077: the server encrypts the session state under a ticket key and gives the blob to the client).[61,91] Both resumed the old master secret, so a stolen ticket key decrypted every session resumed with it. Springall, Durumeric and Halderman measured in 2016 that many large sites reused ticket keys for weeks, undermining the forward secrecy that ECDHE was supposed to provide.[92]

TLS 1.3 replaced both with pre-shared keys. After a handshake the server sends NewSessionTicket messages; each yields a PSK derived from resumption_master_secret and a per-ticket nonce. To resume, the client offers the ticket in the pre_shared_key extension with a binder (an HMAC over the partial ClientHello under a key from the PSK), which proves it holds the PSK and binds the ticket to this ClientHello. In psk_dhe_ke mode the resumed handshake also does a fresh ECDHE, restoring forward secrecy; psk_ke mode skips it. Tickets live at most seven days, and clients obfuscate the ticket age so passive observers cannot link connections by it.[23] Listing 7.2 resumes a real TLS 1.3 session with Python's ssl module.

2.9 Zero-round-trip data (0-RTT)

With a PSK the client can send application data in its very first flight, encrypted under client_early_traffic_secret. This saves a full round trip, but has two inherent weaknesses:[23,93]

  1. No forward secrecy with respect to the PSK: early data depends only on the PSK (no fresh DH contribution from the server), so anyone who later obtains the ticket-encryption key and the ticket can decrypt it.
  2. Replay: the server has not contributed anything fresh, so an attacker can copy the ClientHello and early data and send it again, possibly to another server in the same cluster.

TLS 1.3 describes server-side defences (single-use tickets, recording ClientHellos, and freshness checks based on ticket age) and requires that applications decide what is safe to send early.[23] For HTTP, RFC 8470 says clients should send only safe, idempotent requests as early data, and servers can answer 425 (Too Early) to force a retry after the handshake.[94] Many deployments simply disable 0-RTT, or allow it only for GET requests.

2.10 Encrypted Client Hello and post-quantum TLS

TLS 1.3 encrypts the server certificate, but the ClientHello is still in clear, and its SNI reveals which site the user is visiting. Encrypted Client Hello (ECH), published as RFC 9849 in March 2026, splits the ClientHello into an outer one, naming a shared "client-facing" server (such as a CDN), and an inner one encrypted with HPKE to a public key the client fetched from the DNS HTTPS record. The client-facing server decrypts the inner ClientHello and routes the handshake to the real backend.[95,96,97] ECH hides the name only if many sites share the client-facing server, and its benefits require encrypted DNS as well (§3.9–3.10).

The other major change is post-quantum key exchange. An attacker who records TLS traffic now could decrypt it once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists ("harvest now, decrypt later"). TLS therefore uses hybrid groups that concatenate a classical ECDH share with an ML-KEM encapsulation and feed both shared secrets into the key schedule, so the connection is secure if either component is.[22,84,98] X25519MLKEM768 became the default in Chrome during 2024 and is widely enabled by servers and CDNs; its key shares are about 1.2 kB, which pushes the ClientHello beyond one packet and exposed middleboxes that could not handle that.[99,100] Post-quantum authentication (ML-DSA certificates) is less urgent, because signatures cannot be broken retroactively, and is harder, because ML-DSA certificate chains are several kilobytes larger.

2.11 Datagram TLS (DTLS)

TLS assumes reliable, ordered delivery: a lost or reordered record breaks its implicit sequence numbers. DTLS adapts TLS to UDP for real-time media, VPNs and IoT. DTLS 1.2 (RFC 6347) is based on TLS 1.2; DTLS 1.3 (RFC 9147, 2022) on TLS 1.3.[32,101] The adaptations are:

  • Explicit record numbers: each record carries an epoch (incremented at each key change) and a sequence number, and the receiver uses a sliding replay window (§1.8). DTLS 1.3 encrypts the sequence number, as QUIC does, to reduce linkability.
  • Handshake reliability: handshake messages carry message sequence numbers and fragment offsets, and are retransmitted on timers; DTLS 1.3 adds an explicit ACK message.
  • Denial-of-service protection: a spoofed ClientHello could make a server do expensive work or send large replies to a victim. The server can answer with a stateless cookie (HelloVerifyRequest in 1.2, a cookie in HelloRetryRequest in 1.3) that the client must echo, proving it can receive at its claimed address.
  • No stream ciphers and invalid records are silently discarded rather than terminating the connection, since an attacker can inject datagrams cheaply. This made DTLS's CBC mode a better padding oracle than TLS's: AlFardan and Paterson recovered plaintext from OpenSSL and GnuTLS DTLS in 2012.[102]

DTLS secures WebRTC (it keys SRTP for media through DTLS-SRTP), CoAP for IoT devices, and several VPNs. Connection IDs (RFC 9146) let a session survive NAT rebinding.[103,104]

2.12 QUIC security

QUIC (RFC 9000, 2021) is a UDP-based transport with streams, congestion control and connection migration, and is the basis of HTTP/3.[33,105] It does not run TLS on top; it embeds the TLS 1.3 handshake and uses its own packet protection (RFC 9001).[106] TLS handshake messages travel in QUIC CRYPTO frames, and TLS hands QUIC the traffic secrets for each encryption level (Initial, 0-RTT, Handshake, 1-RTT).

QUIC's security mechanisms beyond TLS:

  • Initial packet keys are derived from the client's Destination Connection ID and a version-specific public salt, so anyone can compute them. They provide integrity against off-path tampering and some resistance to ossification, but not confidentiality; real secrecy starts at the Handshake level.[106]
  • Header protection: after AEAD encryption, a mask computed from a sample of the ciphertext (with AES-ECB or ChaCha20) hides the packet number and some header bits, so middleboxes cannot track or depend on them.
  • Address validation and amplification limits: until a client's address is validated, a server may send at most three times the bytes it received; a Retry packet with a token, protected by a fixed-key integrity tag, can validate addresses statelessly.[33]
  • Connection migration and PATH_CHALLENGE/PATH_RESPONSE frames let connections move between networks; connection IDs change to prevent linking the old and new paths.
  • Key updates without a handshake message, signalled by a key-phase bit; and stateless reset tokens to close connections after a server loses state.

QUIC version 2 (RFC 9369) is functionally identical to version 1 but uses different salts and labels, mainly to stop middleboxes from ossifying around version 1's fixed values.[108]

3. Network security protocols

TLS protects one application connection. The protocols in this part protect whole networks (IPsec, WireGuard, OpenVPN), remote administration (SSH) and the naming system that every connection starts with (DNSSEC, DoT, DoH).

3.1 IPsec

IPsec protects IP packets themselves, below the transport layer, so it covers all traffic between two hosts or networks regardless of application. The architecture (RFC 4301) has three databases: the Security Policy Database (SPD) says, for each traffic selector (addresses, protocol, ports), whether to protect, bypass or discard; the Security Association Database (SAD) holds the keys and state for each one-directional security association (SA), identified by a 32-bit Security Parameter Index (SPI); and the Peer Authorization Database (PAD) links IKE identities to the policies they may request.[109] Keys normally come from IKEv2 (§3.2). 3.3 Authentication Header (AH)

AH (IP protocol 51, RFC 4302) provides integrity, data-origin authentication and replay protection, but no confidentiality. Unlike ESP it also authenticates the immutable fields of the outer IP header, such as source and destination addresses.[118] That is also its weakness: NAT changes those fields, so AH cannot traverse NAT, and ESP with integrity-only ("NULL") encryption gives almost the same protection for the payload. Current algorithm guidance therefore steers implementers toward ESP with authenticated encryption, and AH survives mainly in specialised settings such as some IPv6 routing protocols.[114]

3.4 Encapsulating Security Payload (ESP)

ESP (IP protocol 50, RFC 4303) provides confidentiality, integrity and anti-replay for the packet payload. Its header is just the SPI and a 32-bit sequence number; with extended sequence numbers (ESN), a 64-bit counter is used, and the high 32 bits are not sent but included in the integrity check.[29] Modern ESP uses AES-GCM (RFC 4106) or ChaCha20-Poly1305, with a 4-byte salt from key derivation and an 8-byte explicit IV per packet that must never repeat under one key.[114,119] Listing 7.5 builds a toy ESP-like SA with exactly this layout and a 64-packet replay window.

Encryption-only ESP configurations (allowed by older standards) were shown to be insecure in practice: Paterson and Yau, then Degabriele and Paterson, recovered plaintext by modifying packets and watching how receivers reacted.[120,121] The general lesson repeats TLS's: always use authenticated encryption.

3.5 WireGuard

WireGuard, designed by Jason Donenfeld and published at NDSS 2017, is a deliberately minimal VPN protocol in about four thousand lines of kernel code. It was merged into Linux 5.6 in 2020.[26] Its design choices are the opposite of IPsec's:

  • No negotiation: one fixed suite (Curve25519, ChaCha20-Poly1305, BLAKE2s, SipHash, HKDF). If a primitive breaks, the protocol is versioned, not negotiated, so there is nothing to downgrade.
  • Cryptokey routing: each peer is identified by its static Curve25519 public key, and the configuration maps each key to the inner IP addresses it may use. A packet that decrypts under peer P's key is accepted only if its inner source address belongs to P.
  • A 1-RTT Noise handshake: Noise_IKpsk2_25519_ChaChaPoly_BLAKE2s, in which the initiator already knows the responder's static key and sends its own static key encrypted. The initiation message is 148 bytes and the response 92 bytes. An optional pre-shared key is mixed in at the end of message 2, which allows a post-quantum PSK to be layered on top.[13,26]
  • Silence and DoS resistance: WireGuard never responds to unauthenticated packets. Every handshake message carries mac1, keyed with a hash of the responder's public key, so a scanner that does not know the key gets no reply. Under load the responder replies with an encrypted cookie that must appear in mac2, which proves the initiator's address.
  • Timers instead of state machines: sessions rekey every two minutes and old keys are discarded after three, which bounds the window of any key compromise and gives forward secrecy on a short cycle.

The handshake has been analysed computationally (Dowling and Paterson) and with CryptoVerif.[54] Its main weaknesses are deliberate: static peer keys are long-lived identifiers, the responder's identity is not hidden, and the protocol leaves key distribution, dynamic IP addressing and user authentication to other layers. Post-quantum variants (PQ-WireGuard, and the Rosenpass tool which feeds a post-quantum key into WireGuard's PSK slot) are under development.[122] Listing 7.6 implements the Noise framework and runs WireGuard's exact pattern, checked against published test vectors.

3.6 OpenVPN

OpenVPN (first released in 2001) is a user-space VPN that tunnels IP packets or Ethernet frames over UDP or TCP. It uses TLS for its control channel, running TLS over its own reliability layer, and derives keys for a separate data channel, which protects tunnelled packets with AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305 and a packet-ID counter for replay protection.[123] Because the control channel is ordinary TLS with X.509 certificates, OpenVPN inherits TLS's maturity, and also its large configuration surface.

Three optional pre-shared-key wrappers protect the control channel itself: tls-auth adds an HMAC to every control packet, so unauthenticated packets are dropped before any TLS processing (which hides the server from scanners and limits DoS); tls-crypt also encrypts the control channel, hiding the certificates; and tls-crypt-v2 gives each client its own wrapping key.[123] OpenVPN has had protocol-level problems too: the 2018 VORACLE attack used data-channel compression as a CRIME-style length oracle (compression is now discouraged), and Xue et al. showed that OpenVPN's opcode patterns and packet sizes make it easy to fingerprint and block, even with obfuscation wrappers.[124]

3.7 Secure Shell (SSH)

Tatu Ylönen wrote SSH in 1995 at the Helsinki University of Technology after a password-sniffing attack on the university network.[125] The SSH-2 protocol, standardised in 2006, has three layers: the transport layer (server authentication, key exchange, encryption, integrity), the user authentication protocol (public key, password, keyboard-interactive, GSSAPI/Kerberos) and the connection protocol, which multiplexes channels for shells, commands, port forwarding and agent forwarding.[126,127,128,129]

Key exchange is signed DH. Both sides send KEXINIT lists of algorithms, exchange DH (or hybrid KEM) shares, and compute an exchange hash H = HASH(V_C ‖ V_S ‖ I_C ‖ I_S ‖ K_S ‖ e ‖ f ‖ K) over both version strings, both KEXINIT messages, the server host key, both shares and the shared secret. The server signs H with its host key; the first H becomes the permanent session identifier, and keys are derived as HASH(K ‖ H ‖ letter ‖ session_id) with letters "A" to "F" for IVs, encryption keys and MAC keys in each direction.[128] User public-key authentication then signs data that includes the session identifier, which binds the user's authentication to this transport connection (a built-in form of channel binding).[127]

Server authentication is usually trust on first use (TOFU): the client stores the host key in known_hosts on first connection and warns loudly if it changes. Alternatives are SSHFP records in DNSSEC-signed zones and OpenSSH certificates signed by an organisational CA.[130] OpenSSH's defaults have moved steadily: curve25519-sha256 (RFC 8731), Ed25519 host and user keys (RFC 8709), SHA-1 ssh-rsa signatures disabled in 2021, the hybrid post-quantum sntrup761x25519-sha512 as the default key exchange in OpenSSH 9.0 (April 2022), and mlkem768x25519-sha256 as the default in OpenSSH 10.0 (April 2025).[131,132,133,134]

SSH's record layer has been attacked twice in notable ways. Albrecht, Paterson and Watson recovered plaintext from CBC-mode SSH in 2009 by exploiting the encrypted packet-length field.[135] The 2023 Terrapin attack (§1.8) exploited the fact that sequence numbers continue across the unauthenticated part of the handshake; it affected the popular chacha20-poly1305@openssh.com mode and Encrypt-then-MAC CBC modes, and was fixed by "strict key exchange", which resets the counters and forbids unexpected messages during key exchange.[35]

3.8 DNSSEC

The DNS has no built-in authentication. An attacker who can guess a resolver's 16-bit query ID and source port can inject forged answers; Dan Kaminsky showed in 2008 how to do this repeatedly for any name in a domain, which led to emergency source-port randomisation across the industry.[136] The DNS Security Extensions (RFC 4033–4035) solve the integrity problem with signatures over DNS data, not over the channel.[137,138,139]

  • Each signed zone publishes DNSKEY records. Usually a key-signing key (KSK, flags 257) signs the DNSKEY set, and a zone-signing key (ZSK, flags 256) signs everything else; a single combined key is also allowed.
  • Every record set (all records with the same name, class and type) has an RRSIG with an inception and expiration time, computed over a canonical wire encoding of the set.
  • The parent zone publishes a DS record, a hash of the child's KSK, and signs it with the parent's keys. Following DS records from the root's trust anchor down gives a chain of trust.
  • Authenticated denial of existence uses NSEC records (which list the next existing name, and so allow "zone walking") or NSEC3 (which uses hashed names; current guidance is zero extra iterations, since iterations add little privacy and enable denial of service).[140,141]

Algorithm recommendations moved from RSA/SHA-1 to RSA/SHA-256 (algorithm 8), ECDSA P-256 (13) and Ed25519 (15); RFC 9904 (2025) replaced the fixed recommendations of RFC 8624 with an IANA-registry process, so the lists can be updated without a new RFC.[143,144,145] DNSSEC's limits are important to understand. It provides integrity and origin authentication, not confidentiality: queries and answers are still visible. Validation usually happens at the recursive resolver, so the "last mile" to the stub resolver on the device must be protected some other way. Deployment is incomplete, and misconfigured or expired signatures make domains unreachable for validating users. Signature verification also has a cost: the 2024 KeyTrap attack showed that a single crafted response with many colliding keys and signatures could keep a validating resolver busy for hours.[146] DNSSEC's biggest benefit may be what it enables: DANE (TLSA records) lets domains publish their TLS keys in signed DNS, which is widely used to secure SMTP between mail servers.[147,148]

3.9 DNS over TLS (DoT)

DNSSEC authenticates DNS data but does not hide it. RFC 7858 (2016) runs ordinary DNS messages over a TLS connection to port 853 between a stub and a recursive resolver.[149] RFC 8310 defines two usage profiles: strict, where the client authenticates the resolver (by name or SPKI pin) and fails if it cannot, and opportunistic, where the client uses TLS when available but falls back to clear text, which protects against passive eavesdroppers only.[150] DoT is used by Android's "Private DNS" setting and by systemd-resolved. Its dedicated port makes it easy for network operators to see and, if they wish, to block.

3.10 DNS over HTTPS (DoH)

RFC 8484 (2018) carries DNS messages over HTTPS, either in a POST body or base64url-encoded in a GET request with the media type application/dns-message.[151] Because DoH uses port 443 and normal HTTP semantics, it looks like other web traffic and benefits from HTTP/2 or HTTP/3 multiplexing and caching. 4. Secure messaging

Transport security protects data between a device and a server. Messaging needs more: the server that relays messages should not be able to read them, messages must be deliverable when the recipient is offline, and conversations last for years on phones that can be lost, seized or infected. These requirements produced the most sophisticated key-management protocols in everyday use.

4.1 End-to-end encryption

In end-to-end encryption (E2EE) only the communicating endpoints hold the keys; servers store and forward ciphertext. Unger et al.'s 2015 systematisation of secure messaging separated three problems that any E2EE system must solve: trust establishment (how do I know this key is really Bob's?), conversation security (how are messages protected, including forward secrecy and deniability?) and transport privacy (who can see that Alice talks to Bob?).[157] Most deployed systems trust the server to distribute public keys and let users verify them out of band by comparing "safety numbers" or scanning QR codes; key transparency logs, now deployed by WhatsApp and Apple, make silent key substitution by the server detectable.[158]

The phone-centric setting forces asynchrony: Alice must be able to start an encrypted conversation with Bob while Bob's phone is off. That rules out interactive handshakes like TLS and leads to the prekey designs below.

4.2 The Signal Protocol

The Signal Protocol, designed by Trevor Perrin and Moxie Marlinspike for TextSecure (2013–2016), combines an asynchronous key agreement (X3DH, now PQXDH) with a continuous key-update mechanism (the Double Ratchet). It protects Signal, WhatsApp (whose rollout to all users completed in 2016), Google Messages and other apps, which makes it the end-to-end protocol of several billion people.[21,159,160] Its formal analysis by Cohn-Gordon et al. proved the core key exchange and ratchet secure in a multi-stage model that captures forward secrecy and post-compromise security.[53]

4.3 X3DH and PQXDH

PQXDH (Signal, 2023) adds a post-quantum KEM: Bob also publishes signed ML-KEM prekeys, and Alice encapsulates a shared secret SS to one of them and appends it to the DH outputs before the KDF. Messages are then protected against a future quantum attacker who records them today, while authentication still relies on elliptic-curve keys.[161] A formal analysis by Bhargavan, Jacomme, Kiefer and Schmidt during PQXDH's design found that an attacker could make Alice use a KEM key in place of a DH key in an early draft, which was fixed by encoding key types unambiguously.[55] Apple's iMessage PQ3 protocol (2024) takes a similar hybrid approach.[162]

4.4 The Double Ratchet algorithm

After X3DH, the Double Ratchet derives a new key for every message, from two interleaved mechanisms:[159]

  • A symmetric-key ratchet: each sending and receiving chain is a KDF chain, (CKi+1, MKi) = KDF_CK(CKi) with HMAC. Each message key is used once and deleted. Because the KDF is one-way, stealing the current chain key does not reveal earlier message keys: forward secrecy within a chain.
  • A Diffie–Hellman ratchet: every message header carries the sender's current ratchet public key. When a party receives a new one, it computes a DH output with its own ratchet private key, generates a fresh ratchet key pair, and updates the root key twice, (RK, CK) = KDF_RK(RK, DH(·,·)), starting new receiving and sending chains. Because fresh randomness enters on every round trip, an attacker who stole a device's state is locked out again once the parties exchange new ratchet keys: post-compromise security.

Real networks reorder and drop messages, so the header also carries the message number and the length of the previous sending chain. A receiver that sees message 5 before message 3 derives and stores the skipped keys, up to a limit (MAX_SKIP) that prevents an attacker from forcing huge computations. A failed decryption must roll back any state changes. Listing 7.7 implements all of this: it delivers messages out of order, shows that a deleted key cannot decrypt the same message twice, and lets "Eve" steal Bob's full state, read two more messages, and then lose access after one round trip of fresh ratchet keys.

Alwen, Coretti and Dodis gave a modular security proof of the Double Ratchet and showed that a variant with immediate decryption under message loss is achievable.[163] The optional header encryption variant also hides the ratchet public keys and message numbers from the server.

4.5 Post-quantum ratcheting

PQXDH protects the initial key against harvest-now-decrypt-later, but the Double Ratchet's later DH steps are still elliptic-curve only. If a quantum attacker could break one ratchet step, healing after a compromise would also be lost. Replacing each DH step with a KEM is expensive: ML-KEM-768 public keys are 1,184 bytes and ciphertexts 1,088 bytes, and a KEM needs one party to send a public key and the other to reply with a ciphertext.[22]

Two deployed answers exist. Apple's PQ3 (iMessage, 2024) adds a periodic post-quantum rekeying step to its ratchet, sending fresh Kyber/ML-KEM key material at intervals rather than with every message.[162] Signal's SPQR ("Sparse Post-Quantum Ratchet", 2025) runs a separate ML-KEM-based ratchet, the ML-KEM Braid, alongside the Double Ratchet. It splits each large key and ciphertext into small chunks sent with ordinary messages, using erasure coding so that lost messages do not stall progress, and derives each message key from both ratchets ("Triple Ratchet"). An attacker must then break both the elliptic-curve and the post-quantum component.[164,165]

4.6 Messaging Layer Security (MLS)

Pairwise Double Ratchets do not scale to large groups: sending to n members costs n encryptions, and healing after a compromise needs every pair to ratchet. MLS (RFC 9420, 2023) is the IETF's group key agreement protocol, with an architecture document in RFC 9750.[166,167] Its core is TreeKEM, a ratchet tree in which members are leaves and each internal node holds an HPKE key pair known only to the members below it.[96]

An MLS group moves through epochs. Members send proposals (Add, Update, Remove, PreSharedKey, and others), and any member can send a Commit that applies a set of proposals and (usually) refreshes its own path in the tree. The commit secret, the previous epoch's secret and the group context (including a transcript hash of all commits) go into a key schedule that yields the new epoch's secrets: an encryption secret feeding a "secret tree" of per-sender message keys, an exporter secret, an authentication secret for out-of-band verification, and more. New members receive a Welcome message containing the group state encrypted to their published KeyPackage.[166] Removing a member blanks its leaf and its path, so the next commit encrypts only to keys the removed member never knew; Listing 7.8 shows this, and the cost growing from 3 to 4 ciphertexts because blank nodes widen the set of recipients (the "resolution").

MLS is used by Cisco Webex, by Discord for end-to-end encrypted voice and video, and in the GSMA's RCS Universal Profile 3.0 (2025), which brings interoperable end-to-end encryption to RCS messaging between Android and iPhone.[168] The design descends from Cohn-Gordon et al.'s asynchronous ratcheting trees (ART), the first group protocol with post-compromise security for asynchronous groups.[169]

Rösler, Mainka and Schwenk found in 2018 that in WhatsApp's design the server could add members to a group without the administrators' cryptographic consent, and that Signal and Threema groups had weaker integrity guarantees than their pairwise channels.[170] Albrecht et al. found practically exploitable flaws in the Matrix protocol's group key sharing in 2022.[171] MLS addresses this by authenticating membership changes cryptographically, so every member agrees on the member list in each epoch.

4.8 Forward secrecy

Forward secrecy means that compromise of long-term keys, or of a device at time t, does not expose messages exchanged before t. It requires two things: session keys derived from ephemeral secrets (ephemeral DH or KEM), and the deletion of those secrets and of old message keys. In TLS 1.3 and SSH this gives per-connection forward secrecy; in the Double Ratchet it is per message. Forward secrecy is lost wherever secrets persist: TLS 1.2 session-ticket keys reused for weeks, cloud message backups encrypted under a recoverable key, or a server that logs session keys.[92]

4.9 Post-compromise security

Post-compromise security (PCS, also called "future secrecy" or healing) means that after an attacker loses access to a compromised device, security is restored. Cohn-Gordon, Cremers and Garratt formalised the notion in 2016 and showed that it requires a stateful protocol that keeps mixing in fresh randomness the attacker does not see.[172] A purely symmetric ratchet cannot provide PCS: whoever holds the chain key can compute every future key.

PCS has limits. An attacker who stays active, or who steals the long-term identity key and impersonates the victim, is not locked out; PCS is about a passive attacker who copied the state once. Healing also requires both parties to send messages, so a silent conversation does not heal.

4.10 Metadata protection

E2EE hides content, but the server still learns who talks to whom, when, how often and from where. A former director of the NSA and CIA said in 2014, "We kill people based on metadata."[173] Approaches, from cheapest to strongest:

  • Minimise what the server stores, and protect contact discovery: Signal uses secure enclaves to match phone numbers without revealing users' address books to the operator.[174]
  • Sealed sender: Signal (2018) encrypts the sender's identity inside the envelope, so the server sees only the recipient; a delivery token prevents spam.[175] Martiny et al. showed that delivery receipts and timing let a server link sealed-sender messages back to senders, and proposed fixes.[176]
  • Mix networks, first proposed by Chaum in 1981, batch, shuffle and re-encrypt messages so that inputs cannot be linked to outputs; modern designs such as Loopix and Vuvuzela add cover traffic and differential privacy.[177,178]
  • Onion routing (Tor) hides the network location of both parties with low latency, but a global observer can correlate traffic timing.[179]

Hiding metadata from a global adversary costs latency and bandwidth, which is why no mass-market messenger does it fully.

5. Email and file security

Email and files are store-and-forward: there is no live session, the recipient may read the message years later, and the "channel" includes mail servers, backups and archives. Security therefore attaches to the object itself, with encryption to the recipient's public key and a signature by the sender. Transport protection (STARTTLS, MTA-STS and DANE between mail servers) is a separate, complementary layer.[41,148]

5.1 OpenPGP

Phil Zimmermann released PGP ("Pretty Good Privacy") in 1991 so that individuals could use strong cryptography for email; the US government's three-year criminal investigation of him under export-control law ended without charges in 1996.[180] The IETF standardised the format as OpenPGP (RFC 2440, then RFC 4880 in 2007), and RFC 9580 (2024) modernised it with a version 6 key format, Ed25519 and X25519 as standard algorithms, AEAD encryption (OCB by default, with GCM and EAX), and Argon2 for passphrase-protected keys.[181,182] GnuPG and Sequoia-PGP are the main implementations.

OpenPGP data is a sequence of typed packets: public-key and secret-key packets, user-ID packets, signature packets, public-key-encrypted session key (PKESK) packets, and encrypted data packets. A certificate (often called a "public key") bundles a primary key, subkeys for signing and encryption, user IDs and the self-signatures that bind them. Trust is decentralised: instead of certificate authorities, users sign each other's keys in a web of trust, or simply verify fingerprints directly. The web of trust never reached mass use, and the 2020 SHA-1 chosen-prefix collision by Leurent and Peyrin was demonstrated specifically against PGP key certifications, showing that SHA-1 signatures in the web of trust could be forged.[183]

5.2 PGP encryption

PGP encryption is hybrid: a random session key encrypts the message with a symmetric cipher, and the session key is encrypted separately to each recipient's public encryption subkey (one PKESK packet per recipient, plus one for the sender, so the sender can read their own sent mail). Signing hashes the message and signs the hash with the sender's signing key. The common "sign then encrypt" order puts the signature inside the encryption, which hides who signed it.[182]

OpenPGP's weaknesses are mostly about integration and legacy. Messages have no forward secrecy: anyone who later obtains the recipient's private key can decrypt every archived message. Headers such as subject, sender and recipients are not protected by default. Usability has been a problem since Whitten and Tygar's 1999 study "Why Johnny Can't Encrypt", in which most participants could not encrypt and sign a message correctly within 90 minutes using PGP 5.0.[184] And the 2018 EFAIL attacks (§5.4) showed how mail clients could leak decrypted plaintext.[185] Listing 7.14 generates an Ed25519/Curve25519 key with GnuPG, makes and verifies a detached signature, shows a modified file failing verification, and signs-and-encrypts a message.

5.3 S/MIME

S/MIME (version 4.0, RFC 8551) secures MIME email using the Cryptographic Message Syntax (CMS, RFC 5652) and X.509 certificates, so trust comes from certificate authorities rather than a web of trust.[186,187] A signed message is a CMS SignedData structure; an encrypted message is EnvelopedData or, in S/MIME 4.0, AuthEnvelopedData with AES-GCM, in which a content-encryption key is wrapped for each recipient by RSA-OAEP or ECDH key agreement. Because enterprises already run X.509 PKIs and Outlook and Apple Mail support S/MIME natively, it is common inside organisations and governments, often with keys on smart cards. Its problems mirror OpenPGP's: no forward secrecy, unprotected headers by default, and certificate distribution for external recipients. The CA/Browser Forum's S/MIME Baseline Requirements (2023) now govern publicly trusted S/MIME certificates.[188]

5.4 MIME signing

MIME security multiparts (RFC 1847) give a standard envelope for both PGP/MIME (RFC 3156) and S/MIME. A multipart/signed message has two parts: the content, exactly as signed, and the signature (application/pgp-signature or application/pkcs7-signature), with a micalg parameter naming the hash. A multipart/encrypted message carries control information and the ciphertext.[189,190] Signers must canonicalise the signed part (line endings, transfer encoding) because mail servers routinely rewrite messages, and a single changed byte invalidates the signature. Listing 7.14 produces such a message with openssl cms.

MIME structure is also an attack surface. EFAIL (Poddebniak et al., 2018) had two variants. In "direct exfiltration", an attacker wraps a captured encrypted part between two attacker-controlled HTML parts, so that a client which decrypts and renders the whole message sends the plaintext to the attacker's server inside an image URL. In "CBC/CFB gadgets", the attacker modifies the ciphertext itself, which works because S/MIME's CBC and OpenPGP's CFB modes were malleable and clients ignored or did not enforce integrity checks.[185] The fixes are protocol-level (authenticated encryption, as in RFC 9580 and S/MIME 4.0's AES-GCM) and client-level (never render mixed encrypted and unencrypted parts together). Signature spoofing attacks on the same clients showed similar MIME-confusion problems for signatures.[191]

5.5 age

age (pronounced like the Italian "aghe"), designed by Filippo Valsorda and Ben Cartwright-Cox, is a small file-encryption format and tool built as a deliberate contrast to PGP: no signatures, no key servers, no configuration, small explicit keys.[192,193] An age file has a text header listing one stanza per recipient, each wrapping a random 128-bit file key, followed by a MAC over the header and the payload:

age-encryption.org/v1
-> X25519 <ephemeral share, base64>
<wrapped file key>
--- <HMAC-SHA-256 over the header>
<binary payload: 16-byte nonce, then ChaCha20-Poly1305 in 64 KiB chunks>

Recipient types include X25519 keys (age1…), passphrases (scrypt), SSH Ed25519 and RSA keys, and plugins (hardware tokens, post-quantum hybrid keys). The payload uses the STREAM construction: each 64 KiB chunk is sealed with a nonce containing a counter and a "last chunk" flag, so chunks cannot be reordered, dropped or truncated undetected. Listing 7.14 shows a single modified byte making decryption fail. Because age does not sign, a recipient learns nothing about who made a file; for authenticity it is combined with a signature tool such as minisign or ssh-keygen -Y sign.[192]

5.6 File encryption

File encryption has requirements that differ from messages:

  • Streaming and random access: large files need chunked AEAD (STREAM, as in age and Tink's streaming AEAD) rather than one huge AEAD call, and disks need length-preserving modes such as AES-XTS with sector numbers as tweaks, which give confidentiality but no integrity (full-disk encryption: BitLocker, FileVault, LUKS).[194]
  • Key wrapping and escrow: the data key is wrapped under a key-encryption key that can be rotated without re-encrypting data, and organisations often need a recovery key (cloud KMS envelope encryption follows the same pattern).
  • Password-based keys: passphrases must go through a memory-hard KDF (scrypt, Argon2); see the KDF article.
  • Metadata: file names, sizes and timestamps often remain visible, even in encrypted archives (ZIP encrypts contents but not names).

5.7 Digital document signatures

Signed documents need long-term verifiability: a contract signed today must still be verifiable in 20 years, after the signer's certificate has expired and its algorithms have aged. The European standards family addresses this with advanced electronic signature formats: CAdES (CMS), XAdES (XML Signature), PAdES (PDF) and ASiC containers, each with levels that add a trusted timestamp (RFC 3161), embed the certificate chain and revocation data, and periodically add archive timestamps.[195,196] The EU's eIDAS regulation gives "qualified" electronic signatures, made with a qualified certificate on a qualified device, the legal effect of handwritten signatures.[197]

PDF signatures show how hard it is to sign a complex format. A PDF signature covers byte ranges of the file, and incremental updates may legally append content after signing. Mladenov et al. tested 22 PDF viewers in 2019 and found that 21 of them could be fooled by at least one of three attack classes (universal signature forgery, incremental saving attacks and signature wrapping) into showing a valid signature on modified content.[198] XML signatures have a comparable history of "signature wrapping" attacks, in which the verified element is not the one the application processes.[199] The general rule: verify, then use exactly what you verified.

5.8 Secure software update signing

Software updates are the most powerful code-execution channel there is, so they are a prime target. Signing the package is necessary but not sufficient. Cappos et al. showed in 2008 that package managers that verified signatures were still vulnerable to attackers who served old, validly signed but vulnerable versions (rollback), stale metadata so users never saw updates (freeze), or endless data.[200] The Update Framework (TUF) answers with signed metadata roles (root, targets, snapshot, timestamp) that carry version numbers, expiry times and file hashes, with thresholds of keys for each role and offline root keys, so that compromising one key or one mirror is not enough.[201] Listing 7.13 implements the core checks: threshold signatures, monotonically increasing versions, expiry, and hash binding.

Real incidents show each failure mode. The Flame malware (2012) used an MD5 chosen-prefix collision to forge a Microsoft code-signing certificate and spread through Windows Update.[202] The SolarWinds compromise (2020) inserted a backdoor into the build system, so the malicious update was legitimately signed.[203] The xz-utils backdoor (2024) was introduced by a maintainer identity built up over two years. Signatures prove who released something, not that it is safe, so modern supply-chain security adds: reproducible builds (anyone can rebuild and compare the binary); provenance attestations describing how an artefact was built (SLSA, in-toto);[204] and transparency logs. Sigstore combines short-lived certificates bound to an OpenID Connect identity (Fulcio), a public transparency log (Rekor) and client tooling (cosign), so developers can sign without managing long-term keys and anyone can audit what was signed.[205]

6. Authentication and key exchange

This part covers protocols whose main job is to authenticate a user or device and give it a key: a trusted third party (Kerberos), a shared password (PAKEs), and the framework that many modern designs use to build handshakes from DH operations (Noise). It ends with the formal notion of AKE security and with key confirmation.

6.1 Kerberos

Kerberos was designed at MIT's Project Athena in the 1980s to authenticate users and services on an open campus network without sending passwords over it.[206] Version 5 (RFC 4120) is the authentication protocol of Microsoft Active Directory and of most enterprise Unix environments.[31] It descends from the Needham–Schroeder symmetric protocol, with Denning and Sacco's timestamps added (§1.3). A Key Distribution Center (KDC), consisting of an Authentication Service (AS) and a Ticket-Granting Service (TGS), shares a long-term key with every principal; for users that key is derived from the password.

Kerberos gives single sign-on, mutual authentication, and a session key per service, using only symmetric cryptography. Its known weaknesses come from the same design:[31,207]

  • Password guessing: the AS reply is encrypted under a password-derived key, so anyone who captures it can test guesses offline. Pre-authentication (the client first proves knowledge of the key with an encrypted timestamp) stops attackers simply asking the KDC for such replies, but an eavesdropper can still guess from captured traffic. FAST armoring (RFC 6113) and PKINIT (RFC 4556, public-key login with smart cards) close this gap.[208,209]
  • Kerberoasting: any authenticated user can request a service ticket for any service principal name, and the ticket is encrypted under the service account's key. If that account has a human-chosen password, the ticket can be cracked offline. Listing 7.11 shows the attack recovering "Summer2026!". The defence is long random keys for service accounts (managed service accounts) and AES-only encryption types.[210]
  • KDC compromise is total: whoever has the krbtgt key can mint TGTs for anyone ("golden ticket").
  • Clock dependence and replay caches, as in §1.7; and legacy RC4-HMAC encryption types, being phased out in favour of AES with SHA-2 (RFC 8009).[211]

6.2 Password-authenticated key exchange (PAKE)

Typing a password into a TLS-protected web form trusts the server certificate: if a phishing site or a misissued certificate intervenes, the password goes to the attacker. A PAKE lets two parties who share only a password establish a strong key, such that an attacker, even one who impersonates the server, can test at most one password guess per protocol run. Passive eavesdroppers learn nothing that allows offline guessing.[212,213]

  • Balanced (symmetric) PAKEs assume both sides know the password: EKE, SPAKE2, CPace, Dragonfly/SAE. Uses: Wi-Fi WPA3 (SAE), device pairing, Magic Wormhole, Matter commissioning (SPAKE2+).
  • Augmented (asymmetric, aPAKE) PAKEs are for client–server login: the server stores a verifier derived from the password, so a stolen database does not directly reveal the password or let the thief log in as the user. Examples: SRP, SPAKE2+, OPAQUE.

Bellovin and Merritt's Encrypted Key Exchange (1992) started the field; the CFRG selected CPace (balanced) and OPAQUE (augmented) for standardisation in 2020.[212,213] PAKEs are subtle to implement. The Dragonblood attacks found timing and cache side channels in WPA3's SAE that leaked enough information about the password for offline dictionary attacks, and a downgrade to WPA2.[214]

6.3 SRP

The Secure Remote Password protocol (Wu, 1998; RFC 2945, and RFC 5054 for TLS) is the most widely deployed augmented PAKE: Apple uses it for iCloud Keychain recovery, and 1Password and Proton use it for login.[215,216] In SRP-6a, over a safe-prime group with generator g:

registration:  salt s,  x = H(s ‖ H(I ":" P)),  verifier v = g^x mod N      (server stores I, s, v)
k = H(N ‖ PAD(g))
client:  A = g^a                     server:  B = k·v + g^b
u = H(PAD(A) ‖ PAD(B))
client:  S = (B − k·g^x)^(a + u·x)     server:  S = (A·v^u)^b         both = g^(b(a + u x))
K = H(S), then key-confirmation messages M1 (client) and M2 (server)

The server must abort if A ≡ 0 (mod N) (and the client if B ≡ 0), or an attacker who sends A = 0 forces S = 0 and logs in without the password. Listing 7.9 implements SRP-6a and reproduces every intermediate value of the RFC 5054 test vector, including the full 1024-bit premaster secret. SRP's drawbacks are that it is tied to finite-field groups (no elliptic-curve version is standard), it sends the salt in clear before authentication (allowing precomputation against a targeted user), and it has no formal proof in a modern model.[213]

6.4 SPAKE2

SPAKE2 (Abdalla and Pointcheval, 2005; RFC 9382) is a balanced PAKE in any prime-order group with two fixed points M and N whose discrete logarithms nobody knows. With password-derived scalar w:[217,218]

A:  x random,  T = x·G + w·M   →
                                 ←  S = y·G + w·N   :B,  y random
A:  K = x·(S − w·N)             B:  K = y·(T − w·M)          (both = xy·G)
transcript TT = A ‖ B ‖ T ‖ S ‖ K ‖ w;  keys and confirmation MACs from Hash(TT)

Without w, the messages look like random group elements, and an active attacker can test only one guess per run. RFC 9382 specifies the transcript and key confirmation; SPAKE2+ (RFC 9383) is the augmented variant used to commission Matter smart-home devices.[219] The PKI article includes a toy SPAKE2 implementation.

6.5 OPAQUE

OPAQUE (Jarecki, Krawczyk and Xu, 2018; RFC 9807, July 2025) is an augmented PAKE secure against precomputation: even the salt is never revealed, so an attacker cannot prepare a dictionary before stealing the server database.[220,221] It combines three parts:

  1. An oblivious pseudorandom function (OPRF, RFC 9497). The client blinds its password, M = H(pw)r; the server applies its per-user key, Z = Mk; the client unblinds, Z1/r = H(pw)k, and hashes the result into rwd. The server never sees the password and the client never learns k.[222]
  2. An envelope stored by the server, which can be opened only with rwd (after a key-stretching function such as Argon2) and yields the client's long-term private key.
  3. An authenticated key exchange (3DH in RFC 9807) between the client's recovered key and the server's static key.

Listing 7.10 implements this structure over a finite-field group: the blinded values are fresh every time, the right password yields matching session keys, and a wrong password cannot even open the envelope. An attacker who steals the database must also have the OPRF key and must then do one OPRF and KSF computation per guess per user, with no precomputation.

6.6 The Noise Protocol Framework

Noise (Trevor Perrin, revision 34, 2018) is a framework for building DH-based handshakes from a compact pattern language.[13] A protocol name such as Noise_XX_25519_ChaChaPoly_SHA256 fixes a handshake pattern, a DH function, an AEAD and a hash. Each party keeps a chaining key ck and a handshake hash h. The tokens e and s send an ephemeral or static public key (the static encrypted once a key exists); ee, es, se, ss perform a DH and mix the result into ck with HKDF; every payload is encrypted with the current key and h as associated data, then hashed into h. At the end, Split() derives two transport keys. Because h covers every byte sent, the handshake authenticates its whole transcript.

6.7 Authenticated key exchange (AKE)

Formally, an AKE is secure if no efficient adversary controlling the network, able to start sessions, corrupt long-term keys, and reveal session keys of other sessions, can distinguish the key of a fresh target session from random. Freshness definitions are what distinguish the models: Bellare–Rogaway (1993) introduced the game; Canetti–Krawczyk (2001) added session-state reveals and composability with secure channels; and eCK (LaMacchia, Lauter and Mityagin, 2007) allows the attacker to learn either the long-term or the ephemeral key of each party, but not both.[6,7,25] Multi-stage models, used for TLS 1.3 and Signal, treat each derived key (handshake key, application key, resumption key, ratchet keys) as a separate stage with its own security.[52,53]

6.8 Session-key confirmation

Implicit key authentication says that only the intended peer could compute the key. Key confirmation additionally proves that the peer did compute it, usually by sending a MAC under a key derived from the session secret over the transcript: TLS Finished, SSH's signed exchange hash, IKEv2's AUTH payloads, SRP's M1/M2, SPAKE2's confirmation MACs, Kerberos's AP-REP.[23,218] Key confirmation turns some attacks from silent into detectable: an unknown key-share or a downgrade causes the MACs to disagree and the handshake to abort before any data is sent.

Fischlin, Günther, Schmidt and Warinschi formalised key confirmation in 2016 and showed that TLS 1.3's Finished messages provide it.[223] The ordering matters: confirmation keys must be derived separately from traffic keys (TLS's finished_key is its own HKDF output), and a party should not send sensitive data until it has received confirmation, or else it is relying only on implicit authentication. In asynchronous protocols such as X3DH, explicit confirmation arrives only with the peer's first reply, which is why their first messages have weaker guarantees.

7.1 The TLS 1.3 key schedule, checked against NIST ACVP and RFC 8448

HKDF-Extract/Expand, HKDF-Expand-Label and Derive-Secret implement Figure 6. The assertions reproduce every secret in a NIST ACVP TLS 1.3 KDF test case and the server handshake key and IV from RFC 8448, then compute record nonces, a Finished MAC and the RFC 9266 tls-exporter channel binding.[47,82,224]

code/tls13_key_schedule.py
"""TLS 1.3 key schedule (RFC 8446 / RFC 9846 section 7.1) from HKDF primitives.

Checked against two published sets of test vectors:
  * NIST ACVP TLS-v1.3-KDF vectors (as used by Go's crypto/tls tests);
  * RFC 8448 section 3: server handshake traffic secret -> write key and IV.
Educational code: real stacks use a vetted TLS library.
"""
import hashlib, hmac

H = hashlib.sha256
HLEN = H().digest_size

def hkdf_extract(salt: bytes, ikm: bytes) -> bytes:
    return hmac.new(salt or b"\0" * HLEN, ikm, H).digest()

def hkdf_expand(prk: bytes, info: bytes, length: int) -> bytes:
    out, t, i = b"", b"", 1
    while len(out) < length:
        t = hmac.new(prk, t + info + bytes([i]), H).digest()
        out += t; i += 1
    return out[:length]

def hkdf_expand_label(secret: bytes, label: str, context: bytes, length: int) -> bytes:
    full = b"tls13 " + label.encode()
    info = length.to_bytes(2, "big") + bytes([len(full)]) + full + bytes([len(context)]) + context
    return hkdf_expand(secret, info, length)

def derive_secret(secret: bytes, label: str, transcript_hash: bytes) -> bytes:
    return hkdf_expand_label(secret, label, transcript_hash, HLEN)

EMPTY_HASH = H(b"").digest()

def key_schedule(psk: bytes | None, dhe: bytes | None, th):
    """th: dict of transcript hashes at the points the RFC needs them:
       'CH' (ClientHello), 'SH' (..ServerHello), 'SF' (..server Finished), 'CF' (..client Finished)."""
    zeros = b"\0" * HLEN
    early = hkdf_extract(zeros, psk or zeros)
    s = {"early_secret": early,
         "c_e_traffic": derive_secret(early, "c e traffic", th["CH"]),
         "e_exp_master": derive_secret(early, "e exp master", th["CH"])}
    hs = hkdf_extract(derive_secret(early, "derived", EMPTY_HASH), dhe or zeros)
    s["handshake_secret"] = hs
    s["c_hs_traffic"] = derive_secret(hs, "c hs traffic", th["SH"])
    s["s_hs_traffic"] = derive_secret(hs, "s hs traffic", th["SH"])
    ms = hkdf_extract(derive_secret(hs, "derived", EMPTY_HASH), zeros)
    s["master_secret"] = ms
    s["c_ap_traffic"] = derive_secret(ms, "c ap traffic", th["SF"])
    s["s_ap_traffic"] = derive_secret(ms, "s ap traffic", th["SF"])
    s["exp_master"] = derive_secret(ms, "exp master", th["SF"])
    s["res_master"] = derive_secret(ms, "res master", th["CF"])
    return s

def traffic_keys(secret: bytes, key_len=16, iv_len=12):
    return hkdf_expand_label(secret, "key", b"", key_len), hkdf_expand_label(secret, "iv", b"", iv_len)

def finished_mac(base_key: bytes, transcript_hash: bytes) -> bytes:
    fk = hkdf_expand_label(base_key, "finished", b"", HLEN)
    return hmac.new(fk, transcript_hash, H).digest()

def per_record_nonce(iv: bytes, seq: int) -> bytes:
    """RFC 8446 5.3: the 64-bit record sequence number, left-padded, XORed into the IV."""
    padded = seq.to_bytes(len(iv), "big")
    return bytes(a ^ b for a, b in zip(iv, padded))

def exporter(secret: bytes, label: str, context: bytes, length: int) -> bytes:
    """RFC 8446 7.5 TLS-Exporter; RFC 9266 'tls-exporter' channel binding uses
    label 'EXPORTER-Channel-Binding', empty context, length 32."""
    tmp = derive_secret(secret, label, EMPTY_HASH)
    return hkdf_expand_label(tmp, "exporter", H(context).digest(), length)

7.3 An explicit TLS 1.3 client state machine

Transition-table implementation of the client side of RFC 8446 Appendix A. Skipping CertificateVerify (SKIP-TLS), sending data before Finished or a second HelloRetryRequest all cause an unexpected_message alert (§1.9).[1]

code/state_machine.py
"""An explicit TLS 1.3 client state machine (after RFC 8446 Appendix A.1).

Implementations that accept messages in the wrong order have shipped real
attacks: "SKIP-TLS" clients accepted a handshake with the server's
CertificateVerify (or ServerKeyExchange) simply left out (Beurdouche et al., 2015).
An explicit table of allowed transitions rejects anything not on the path.
"""
TRANSITIONS = {   # state -> {incoming message: next state}
    "WAIT_SH":   {"ServerHello": "WAIT_EE", "HelloRetryRequest": "WAIT_SH"},
    "WAIT_EE":   {"EncryptedExtensions": "WAIT_CERT_CR"},
    "WAIT_CERT_CR": {"Certificate": "WAIT_CV", "CertificateRequest": "WAIT_CERT",
                     "Finished": "CONNECTED_PSK"},          # Finished directly only in PSK mode
    "WAIT_CERT": {"Certificate": "WAIT_CV"},
    "WAIT_CV":   {"CertificateVerify": "WAIT_FINISHED"},
    "WAIT_FINISHED": {"Finished": "CONNECTED"},
    "CONNECTED": {"NewSessionTicket": "CONNECTED", "KeyUpdate": "CONNECTED", "ApplicationData": "CONNECTED"},
}

class Client:
    def __init__(self, psk_mode=False): self.state, self.psk, self.hrr = "WAIT_SH", psk_mode, 0
    def receive(self, msg):
        nxt = TRANSITIONS.get(self.state, {}).get(msg)
        if nxt == "CONNECTED_PSK":
            nxt = "CONNECTED" if self.psk else None          # no cert auth without a PSK = fatal
        if msg == "HelloRetryRequest":
            self.hrr += 1
            if self.hrr > 1: nxt = None                       # at most one HRR per handshake
        if nxt is None:
            raise ValueError(f"unexpected_message alert: {msg} in state {self.state}")
        self.state = nxt

def run(msgs, psk=False):
    c = Client(psk)
    try:
        for m in msgs: c.receive(m)
        return f"ok -> {c.state}"
    except ValueError as e:
        return str(e)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    full = ["ServerHello", "EncryptedExtensions", "Certificate", "CertificateVerify", "Finished", "ApplicationData"]
    print("normal certificate handshake :", run(full))
    print("SKIP CertificateVerify       :", run([m for m in full if m != "CertificateVerify"]))
    print("skip Certificate + CV        :", run(["ServerHello", "EncryptedExtensions", "Finished"]))
    print("same, but PSK resumption     :", run(["ServerHello", "EncryptedExtensions", "Finished"], psk=True))
    print("data before Finished         :", run(full[:4] + ["ApplicationData"]))
    print("two HelloRetryRequests       :", run(["HelloRetryRequest", "HelloRetryRequest"]))

7.4 A downgrade attack in miniature (Logjam structure)

A man in the middle removes X25519 from the offer and breaks a 16-bit toy group. With TLS 1.2-style signatures it forges both Finished MACs; with a TLS 1.3-style transcript signature the client aborts (§1.10).[2,42]

code/downgrade_demo.py
"""Why handshakes must authenticate the *whole* negotiation (Logjam/FREAK in miniature).

A client offers a strong group (X25519) and a legacy weak group (a toy 16-bit
DH group an attacker can break in milliseconds). A man-in-the-middle deletes
the strong option from the ClientHello.
  * "TLS 1.2 style": the server signs only (client_random, server_random, params);
    the transcript is protected only by Finished MACs keyed from the (weak) secret.
    The attacker breaks the weak DH, recomputes both Finished MACs: undetected.
  * "TLS 1.3 style": the server signs a hash of the entire transcript
    (CertificateVerify), so the edit is detected even though the weak DH was broken.
Needs: pip install cryptography
"""
import hashlib, hmac, json, os, secrets
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.asymmetric.ed25519 import Ed25519PrivateKey

P, G = 65537, 3                                  # toy "export-grade" group: order 2^16
def toy_dh_keypair(): x = secrets.randbelow(P - 2) + 1; return x, pow(G, x, P)
def toy_dlog(y):                                 # brute force: the whole point of "weak"
    acc = 1
    for x in range(P):
        if acc == y: return x
        acc = acc * G % P

def enc(m): return json.dumps(m, sort_keys=True).encode()
def H(*msgs): return hashlib.sha256(b"".join(enc(m) for m in msgs)).digest()
def finished(secret, label, th): return hmac.new(secret, label + th, hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()

server_sig_key = Ed25519PrivateKey.generate()     # certified long-term key
server_pub = server_sig_key.public_key()

def run(mode, attacker):
    ch = {"random": os.urandom(8).hex(), "groups": ["x25519", "toy16"]}
    ch_seen = dict(ch, groups=["toy16"]) if attacker else ch          # the MITM's edit
    group = ch_seen["groups"][0]
    if group != "toy16":
        return "negotiated x25519 (no downgrade)"
    b, B = toy_dh_keypair()
    sh = {"random": os.urandom(8).hex(), "group": group, "share": B}
    if mode == "1.2":   # ServerKeyExchange signature: randoms + params only
        signed = enc([ch_seen["random"], sh["random"], sh["share"]])
    else:               # CertificateVerify: hash of the full transcript as the server saw it
        signed = H(ch_seen, sh)
    sig = server_sig_key.sign(signed)
    # ---- client side ----
    a, A = toy_dh_keypair()
    client_signed = enc([ch["random"], sh["random"], sh["share"]]) if mode == "1.2" else H(ch, sh)
    try:
        server_pub.verify(sig, client_signed)
    except Exception:
        return "client ABORTS: signature over transcript does not match what it sent"
    k_client = pow(B, a, P).to_bytes(4, "big")
    cf = finished(k_client, b"client", H(ch, sh, {"share": A}))
    if not attacker:
        return "weak group negotiated honestly (client allowed it)"
    # ---- attacker: break the weak DH and patch the Finished MACs on both sides ----
    k_attacker = pow(A, toy_dlog(B), P).to_bytes(4, "big")
    assert k_attacker == k_client
    cf_for_server = finished(k_attacker, b"client", H(ch_seen, sh, {"share": A}))
    k_server = pow(A, b, P).to_bytes(4, "big")
    ok_server = hmac.compare_digest(cf_for_server, finished(k_server, b"client", H(ch_seen, sh, {"share": A})))
    return f"DOWNGRADE UNDETECTED: server accepts forged Finished = {ok_server}; attacker knows session key {k_attacker.hex()}"

7.5 Replay protection: sliding windows, timestamps and nonces

An RFC 4303 bitmap window driving a toy ESP-like AES-GCM security association (SPI, sequence number, explicit IV), a Kerberos-style timestamp replay cache, and a nonce challenge (§1.6–1.8, §3.4).[29,119]

code/replay_protection.py
"""Three replay defences side by side.

1. Sequence numbers + sliding window (IPsec ESP, RFC 4303 sec 3.4.3; DTLS; WireGuard)
   wrapped around a toy ESP-like AES-GCM packet: SPI | seq | ciphertext.
2. Timestamps + a replay cache (Kerberos authenticators, RFC 4120 sec 3.2.3).
3. Nonce challenge-response (fresh random value per run; no clocks needed).
Needs: pip install cryptography
"""
import os, struct, time
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.ciphers.aead import AESGCM

class ReplayWindow:
    """Bitmap window of size W anchored at the highest authenticated sequence number."""
    def __init__(self, size=64):
        self.size, self.top, self.bitmap = size, 0, 0   # bit i set => (top - i) seen

    def check_and_update(self, seq: int) -> str:
        # NB: call only AFTER the integrity check passes, or forged packets slide the window.
        if seq == 0:
            return "reject: seq 0 is never sent"
        if seq > self.top:                       # new right edge: shift window
            shift = seq - self.top
            self.bitmap = ((self.bitmap << shift) | 1) & ((1 << self.size) - 1)
            self.top = seq
            return "accept (new high)"
        offset = self.top - seq
        if offset >= self.size:
            return "reject: too old (left of window)"
        if self.bitmap >> offset & 1:
            return "reject: REPLAY"
        self.bitmap |= 1 << offset
        return "accept (late but inside window)"

class EspLikeSA:
    """Toy security association: AES-128-GCM, 4-byte salt + 8-byte IV as in RFC 4106."""
    def __init__(self, key, salt, spi):
        self.aead, self.salt, self.spi = AESGCM(key), salt, spi
        self.tx_seq, self.window = 0, ReplayWindow(64)

    def seal(self, payload: bytes) -> bytes:
        self.tx_seq += 1
        if self.tx_seq >= 2**32:
            raise RuntimeError("sequence space exhausted: rekey (IKEv2 CREATE_CHILD_SA)")
        hdr = struct.pack("!II", self.spi, self.tx_seq)
        iv = self.tx_seq.to_bytes(8, "big")       # unique per packet under this key
        return hdr + iv + self.aead.encrypt(self.salt + iv, payload, hdr)

    def open(self, pkt: bytes):
        hdr, iv, ct = pkt[:8], pkt[8:16], pkt[16:]
        spi, seq = struct.unpack("!II", hdr)
        if spi != self.spi:
            return "reject: unknown SPI", None
        try:
            pt = self.aead.decrypt(self.salt + iv, ct, hdr)   # header is authenticated
        except Exception:
            return "reject: ICV check failed", None
        verdict = self.window.check_and_update(seq)
        return verdict, (pt if verdict.startswith("accept") else None)

class TimestampReplayCache:
    def __init__(self, skew=300):              # Kerberos default: 5 minutes
        self.skew, self.seen = skew, {}
    def check(self, client, ts, now=None):
        now = time.time() if now is None else now
        for k in [k for k, t in self.seen.items() if t < now - self.skew]:
            del self.seen[k]                    # entries older than the window can be forgotten
        if abs(now - ts) > self.skew:
            return "reject: clock skew too great"
        if (client, ts) in self.seen:
            return "reject: REPLAY (seen in cache)"
        self.seen[(client, ts)] = ts
        return "accept"

class CipherState:
    def __init__(self, k=None): self.k, self.n = k, 0
    def _nonce(self): return b"\0\0\0\0" + self.n.to_bytes(8, "little")
    def encrypt(self, ad, pt):
        if self.k is None: return pt
        ct = ChaCha20Poly1305(self.k).encrypt(self._nonce(), pt, ad); self.n += 1; return ct
    def decrypt(self, ad, ct):
        if self.k is None: return ct
        pt = ChaCha20Poly1305(self.k).decrypt(self._nonce(), ct, ad); self.n += 1; return pt

class SymmetricState:
    def __init__(self, name: bytes, H):
        self.H, self.hlen = H, H().digest_size
        self.h = name.ljust(self.hlen, b"\0") if len(name) <= self.hlen else H(name).digest()
        self.ck, self.cs = self.h, CipherState()
    def hkdf(self, ikm, n):
        tmp = hmac.new(self.ck, ikm, self.H).digest()
        outs, prev = [], b""
        for i in range(1, n + 1):
            prev = hmac.new(tmp, prev + bytes([i]), self.H).digest(); outs.append(prev)
        return outs
    def mix_hash(self, data): self.h = self.H(self.h + data).digest()
    def mix_key(self, ikm):
        self.ck, k = self.hkdf(ikm, 2); self.cs = CipherState(k[:32])
    def mix_key_and_hash(self, ikm):
        self.ck, th, k = self.hkdf(ikm, 3); self.mix_hash(th); self.cs = CipherState(k[:32])
    def encrypt_and_hash(self, pt):
        ct = self.cs.encrypt(self.h, pt); self.mix_hash(ct); return ct
    def decrypt_and_hash(self, ct):
        pt = self.cs.decrypt(self.h, ct); self.mix_hash(ct); return pt
    def split(self):
        k1, k2 = self.hkdf(b"", 2); return CipherState(k1[:32]), CipherState(k2[:32])

def parse(protocol_name):
    m = re.fullmatch(r"Noise_([A-Z]+)((?:psk\d)(?:\+psk\d)*)?_25519_ChaChaPoly_(SHA256|BLAKE2s)", protocol_name)
    if not m: raise ValueError("unsupported: " + protocol_name)
    base, mods, hname = m.groups()
    pre_i, pre_r, msgs = PATTERNS[base]
    msgs = [list(x) for x in msgs]
    psk_pos = [int(p[3:]) for p in mods.split("+")] if mods else []
    for p in psk_pos:
        if p == 0: msgs[0].insert(0, "psk")
        else: msgs[p - 1].append("psk")
    return pre_i, pre_r, msgs, HASHES[hname], bool(psk_pos), len(PATTERNS[base][2]) == 1

class HandshakeState:
    def __init__(self, protocol_name, initiator, prologue=b"", s=None, e=None, rs=None, psks=()):
        self.pre_i, self.pre_r, self.msgs, H, self.has_psk, self.one_way = parse(protocol_name)
        self.ss = SymmetricState(protocol_name.encode(), H)
        self.ss.mix_hash(prologue)
        self.initiator, self.s, self.e, self.rs, self.re = initiator, s, e, rs, None
        self.psks, self.idx = list(psks), 0
        for who, pre in (("i", self.pre_i), ("r", self.pre_r)):
            for tok in pre:   # pre-message public keys are hashed in, initiator's first
                mine = (who == "i") == initiator
                self.ss.mix_hash(pub(self.s) if mine else self.rs)

    def _dh(self, tok):
        a, b = tok
        if a == b == "e": return dh(self.e, self.re)
        if a == b == "s": return dh(self.s, self.rs)
        if tok == "es": return dh(self.e, self.rs) if self.initiator else dh(self.s, self.re)
        if tok == "se": return dh(self.s, self.re) if self.initiator else dh(self.e, self.rs)

    def write(self, payload=b""):
        out = b""
        for tok in self.msgs[self.idx]:
            if tok == "e":
                self.e = self.e or X25519PrivateKey.generate()
                out += pub(self.e); self.ss.mix_hash(pub(self.e))
                if self.has_psk: self.ss.mix_key(pub(self.e))
            elif tok == "s": out += self.ss.encrypt_and_hash(pub(self.s))
            elif tok == "psk": self.ss.mix_key_and_hash(self.psks.pop(0))
            else: self.ss.mix_key(self._dh(tok))
        out += self.ss.encrypt_and_hash(payload)
        self.idx += 1
        return out

    def read(self, msg):
        for tok in self.msgs[self.idx]:
            if tok == "e":
                self.re, msg = msg[:32], msg[32:]; self.ss.mix_hash(self.re)
                if self.has_psk: self.ss.mix_key(self.re)
            elif tok == "s":
                n = 48 if self.ss.cs.k is not None else 32
                self.rs, msg = self.ss.decrypt_and_hash(msg[:n]), msg[n:]
            elif tok == "psk": self.ss.mix_key_and_hash(self.psks.pop(0))
            else: self.ss.mix_key(self._dh(tok))
        self.idx += 1
        return self.ss.decrypt_and_hash(msg)

    def done(self): return self.idx == len(self.msgs)

7.7 X3DH and the Double Ratchet

Follows the Signal specifications, with the simplifications noted in the docstring. It verifies the signed prekey, handles out-of-order delivery with skipped message keys, rolls back state on failed decryption, and demonstrates forward secrecy and post-compromise security against an attacker who steals Bob's state (§4.3–4.4, §4.8–4.9).[21,159]

code/signal_x3dh_ratchet.py
"""X3DH key agreement + the Double Ratchet, following Signal's published specifications.

Simplifications (flagged in the article): Bob's prekey signature uses a separate
Ed25519 identity key instead of XEdDSA on the X25519 identity key, and message
encryption uses AES-256-GCM with a key/nonce derived from the message key instead
of the spec's AES-CBC + HMAC. No PQXDH/ML-KEM here. Educational only.
Needs: pip install cryptography
"""
import os
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives import hashes, hmac
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.asymmetric.x25519 import X25519PrivateKey, X25519PublicKey
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.asymmetric.ed25519 import Ed25519PrivateKey, Ed25519PublicKey
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.ciphers.aead import AESGCM
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.kdf.hkdf import HKDF

raw = lambda k: k.public_bytes(Encoding.Raw, PublicFormat.Raw)
pk = lambda priv: raw(priv.public_key())
def DH(priv, pub_bytes): return priv.exchange(X25519PublicKey.from_public_bytes(pub_bytes))
def hkdf(ikm, salt, info, n): return HKDF(hashes.SHA256(), n, salt, info).derive(ikm)

# ---------------- X3DH ----------------
class Bob:
    def __init__(self):
        self.IK, self.IK_sig = X25519PrivateKey.generate(), Ed25519PrivateKey.generate()
        self.SPK = X25519PrivateKey.generate()
        self.OPKs = {i: X25519PrivateKey.generate() for i in range(3)}
    def bundle(self):   # fetched from the server; the server hands out (and forgets) one OPK
        opk_id = min(self.OPKs)
        return {"IK": pk(self.IK), "IKsig": pk(self.IK_sig), "SPK": pk(self.SPK),
                "SPKsig": self.IK_sig.sign(pk(self.SPK)), "OPK": (opk_id, pk(self.OPKs[opk_id]))}

def x3dh_kdf(km): return hkdf(b"\xff" * 32 + km, b"\0" * 32, b"X3DH-demo", 32)

def alice_x3dh(IK_A, bundle):
    Ed25519PublicKey.from_public_bytes(bundle["IKsig"]).verify(bundle["SPKsig"], bundle["SPK"])  # raises if forged
    EK = X25519PrivateKey.generate()
    opk_id, OPK = bundle["OPK"]
    km = DH(IK_A, bundle["SPK"]) + DH(EK, bundle["IK"]) + DH(EK, bundle["SPK"]) + DH(EK, OPK)
    SK = x3dh_kdf(km)
    AD = pk(IK_A) + bundle["IK"]
    return SK, AD, {"IK_A": pk(IK_A), "EK_A": pk(EK), "opk_id": opk_id}

def bob_x3dh(bob, init):
    OPK = bob.OPKs.pop(init["opk_id"])          # one-time prekey is deleted after use
    km = DH(bob.SPK, init["IK_A"]) + DH(bob.IK, init["EK_A"]) + DH(bob.SPK, init["EK_A"]) + DH(OPK, init["EK_A"])
    return x3dh_kdf(km), init["IK_A"] + pk(bob.IK)

# ---------------- Double Ratchet ----------------
MAX_SKIP = 100
def KDF_RK(rk, dh_out):
    out = hkdf(dh_out, rk, b"DR-root", 64); return out[:32], out[32:]
def KDF_CK(ck):
    def h(b):
        m = hmac.HMAC(ck, hashes.SHA256()); m.update(b); return m.finalize()
    return h(b"\x02"), h(b"\x01")             # (next chain key, message key)
def ENCRYPT(mk, pt, ad):
    k = hkdf(mk, b"\0" * 32, b"DR-msg", 44); return AESGCM(k[:32]).encrypt(k[32:], pt, ad)
def DECRYPT(mk, ct, ad):
    k = hkdf(mk, b"\0" * 32, b"DR-msg", 44); return AESGCM(k[:32]).decrypt(k[32:], ct, ad)
def header(dh_pub, pn, n): return dh_pub + pn.to_bytes(4, "big") + n.to_bytes(4, "big")

class Ratchet:
    @classmethod
    def alice(cls, SK, bob_spk):
        s = cls(); s.DHs = X25519PrivateKey.generate(); s.DHr = bob_spk
        s.RK, s.CKs = KDF_RK(SK, DH(s.DHs, s.DHr)); s.CKr = None; return s
    @classmethod
    def bob(cls, SK, bob_spk_priv):
        s = cls(); s.DHs, s.DHr, s.RK, s.CKs, s.CKr = bob_spk_priv, None, SK, None, None; return s
    def __init__(self): self.Ns = self.Nr = self.PN = 0; self.skipped = {}

    def clone(self):   # what an attacker who steals the device's state gets
        c = Ratchet(); c.__dict__.update(self.__dict__); c.skipped = dict(self.skipped)
        c.DHs = X25519PrivateKey.from_private_bytes(
            self.DHs.private_bytes(Encoding.Raw, PrivateFormat.Raw, NoEncryption()))
        return c

    def encrypt(self, pt, AD):
        self.CKs, mk = KDF_CK(self.CKs)
        hdr = header(pk(self.DHs), self.PN, self.Ns); self.Ns += 1
        return hdr, ENCRYPT(mk, pt, AD + hdr)

    def decrypt(self, hdr, ct, AD):
        # Spec: if decryption fails, discard all state changes made while trying.
        saved = dict(self.__dict__); saved["skipped"] = dict(self.skipped)
        try:
            return self._decrypt(hdr, ct, AD)
        except Exception:
            self.__dict__.update(saved); raise

    def _decrypt(self, hdr, ct, AD):
        dh_pub, pn, n = hdr[:32], int.from_bytes(hdr[32:36], "big"), int.from_bytes(hdr[36:], "big")
        if (dh_pub, n) in self.skipped:                     # out-of-order message
            return DECRYPT(self.skipped.pop((dh_pub, n)), ct, AD + hdr)
        if dh_pub != self.DHr:                               # new ratchet public key => DH step
            self._skip(pn); self._dh_ratchet(dh_pub)
        self._skip(n)
        self.CKr, mk = KDF_CK(self.CKr); self.Nr += 1
        return DECRYPT(mk, ct, AD + hdr)

    def _skip(self, until):
        if self.CKr is None: return
        if self.Nr + MAX_SKIP < until: raise ValueError("too many skipped messages")
        while self.Nr < until:
            self.CKr, mk = KDF_CK(self.CKr); self.skipped[(self.DHr, self.Nr)] = mk; self.Nr += 1

    def _dh_ratchet(self, dh_pub):
        self.PN, self.Ns, self.Nr, self.DHr = self.Ns, 0, 0, dh_pub
        self.RK, self.CKr = KDF_RK(self.RK, DH(self.DHs, self.DHr))
        self.DHs = X25519PrivateKey.generate()               # fresh key: post-compromise healing
        self.RK, self.CKs = KDF_RK(self.RK, DH(self.DHs, self.DHr))

7.8 TreeKEM: O(log n) group rekeying

A simplified MLS ratchet tree with HPKE-style encryption of path secrets to copath resolutions. It counts ciphertexts for groups of 8 and 1,024 members and shows that a removed member cannot decrypt the next commit (§4.6).[166]

code/treekem_toy.py
"""TreeKEM in miniature: how MLS (RFC 9420) rekeys a group in O(log n) messages.

Heap-indexed binary tree: node 1 is the root, leaves are nodes n..2n-1.
Each node holds an X25519 key pair derived from a "path secret". A committer
refreshes its leaf, derives path secrets up to the root, and encrypts each one
to the *resolution* of the sibling subtree (the HPKE step). Everyone else
decrypts once and derives the rest. Removing a member blanks its path, so the
next commit never encrypts to keys the removed member knows.
Simplified: no transcript hashes, key schedule, signatures or parent hashes.
Needs: pip install cryptography
"""
import os
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives import hashes
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.asymmetric.x25519 import X25519PrivateKey, X25519PublicKey
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.ciphers.aead import AESGCM
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.kdf.hkdf import HKDF
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.serialization import Encoding, PublicFormat

def kdf(secret, label, n=32): return HKDF(hashes.SHA256(), n, None, label.encode()).derive(secret)
def node_keys(ps):
    sk = X25519PrivateKey.from_private_bytes(kdf(ps, "node"))
    return sk, sk.public_key().public_bytes(Encoding.Raw, PublicFormat.Raw)

def hpke_seal(pub, pt):                              # toy single-shot HPKE (RFC 9180 in spirit)
    e = X25519PrivateKey.generate(); epub = e.public_key().public_bytes(Encoding.Raw, PublicFormat.Raw)
    k = kdf(e.exchange(X25519PublicKey.from_public_bytes(pub)) + epub + pub, "hpke", 44)
    return epub, AESGCM(k[:32]).encrypt(k[32:], pt, None)
def hpke_open(sk, pub, ct):
    epub, c = ct
    k = kdf(sk.exchange(X25519PublicKey.from_public_bytes(epub)) + epub + pub, "hpke", 44)
    return AESGCM(k[:32]).decrypt(k[32:], c, None)

class Group:
    def __init__(self, n):
        self.n = n
        self.pub = {}                                 # public tree: node -> public key (None = blank)
        self.priv = {m: {} for m in range(n)}         # member -> {node: private key}
        for m in range(n):                            # bootstrap: every node keyed, members hold their paths
            leaf = n + m; self._install(m, leaf, os.urandom(32))
        for node in range(1, n):
            ps = os.urandom(32); sk, pk = node_keys(ps); self.pub[node] = pk
            for m in self.members_under(node): self.priv[m][node] = sk

        return [x - self.n for x in range(lo, hi + 1) if (x - self.n) in self.priv]

        if self.pub.get(node) is not None: return [node]
        if node >= self.n: return []

        self._install(sender, node, ps)
        ciphertexts = []                              # (target node, encrypted path secret, parent)
        while node > 1:

            ps = kdf(ps, "path")                      # path_secret[parent] = DeriveSecret(path_secret[child], "path")
            for r in self.resolution(sibling):

            self._install(sender, parent, ps)
            node = parent

            if m == sender: continue

                    while True:                       # derive the rest of the path locally

                        if node == 1: break
                        node //= 2; ps2 = kdf(ps2, "path")

        return len(ciphertexts), roots

        while node >= 1: self.pub[node] = None; node //= 2     # blank leaf and direct path

def wire_name(name):                          # canonical (lower-case) uncompressed wire format
    labels = [l for l in name.lower().rstrip(".").split(".") if l]
    return b"".join(bytes([len(l)]) + l.encode() for l in labels) + b"\0"

def dnskey_rdata(flags, protocol, alg, key): return struct.pack("!HBB", flags, protocol, alg) + key

def key_tag(rdata):                           # RFC 4034 Appendix B (all algorithms except 1)
    ac = sum(b if i & 1 else b << 8 for i, b in enumerate(rdata))
    return (ac + (ac >> 16)) & 0xFFFF

def ds_digest(owner, rdata, digest="sha256"):
    return hashlib.new(digest, wire_name(owner) + rdata).hexdigest().upper()

def rrsig_input(owner, rtype, ttl, rdatas, alg, labels, expiration, inception, tag, signer):

7.13 TUF-style signed update metadata

Threshold signatures, version numbers, expiry and file hashes defeat rollback, freeze, tampering and single-key compromise (§5.8).[201]

code/update_signing.py
"""Signed software-update metadata in the style of The Update Framework (TUF).

A signature on the package alone is not enough: a mirror can replay an *old*,
validly signed, vulnerable release (rollback) or keep serving stale metadata
forever (freeze). TUF-style clients check a signed version number, an expiry
time and a threshold of signatures, and bind the metadata to the file hash.
Needs: pip install cryptography
"""
import hashlib, json, time
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.asymmetric.ed25519 import Ed25519PrivateKey
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.serialization import Encoding, PublicFormat

keys = [Ed25519PrivateKey.generate() for _ in range(3)]          # 3 release keys, threshold 2
trusted = {k.public_key().public_bytes(Encoding.Raw, PublicFormat.Raw).hex(): k.public_key() for k in keys}
THRESHOLD = 2

def release(pkg: bytes, version: int, expires: int, signers):
    body = {"version": version, "expires": expires,
            "targets": {"app.tar.gz": {"sha256": hashlib.sha256(pkg).hexdigest(), "length": len(pkg)}}}
    canon = json.dumps(body, sort_keys=True, separators=(",", ":")).encode()
    sigs = [{"keyid": k.public_key().public_bytes(Encoding.Raw, PublicFormat.Raw).hex(), "sig": k.sign(canon).hex()}
            for k in signers]
    return {"signed": body, "signatures": sigs}

class Client:
    def __init__(self): self.version = 0
    def update(self, meta, pkg, now=None):
        now = time.time() if now is None else now
        canon = json.dumps(meta["signed"], sort_keys=True, separators=(",", ":")).encode()
        good = set()
        for s in meta["signatures"]:
            k = trusted.get(s["keyid"])
            try:
                if k: k.verify(bytes.fromhex(s["sig"]), canon); good.add(s["keyid"])
            except Exception: pass
        if len(good) < THRESHOLD: return f"reject: {len(good)} valid signature(s), need {THRESHOLD}"
        m = meta["signed"]
        if m["version"] < self.version: return f"reject: rollback ({m['version']} < {self.version})"
        if m["expires"] < now: return "reject: metadata expired (freeze attack?)"
        t = m["targets"]["app.tar.gz"]
        if len(pkg) != t["length"] or hashlib.sha256(pkg).hexdigest() != t["sha256"]:
            return "reject: package does not match signed hash"
        self.version = m["version"]; return f"install version {m['version']}"

if __name__ == "__main__":
    now = int(time.time()); c = Client()
    v1, v2 = b"app v1 (has CVE)", b"app v2 (patched)"
    m1 = release(v1, 1, now + 86400, keys[:2]); m2 = release(v2, 2, now + 86400, keys[1:])
    print("v2 normal update      :", c.update(m2, v2))
    print("mirror replays old v1 :", c.update(m1, v1))
    print("v2 with tampered file :", c.update(m2, b"app v2 + backdoor"))
    print("one stolen key only   :", c.update(release(b"evil", 3, now + 86400, keys[:1]), b"evil"))
    print("stale metadata        :", c.update(release(v2, 2, now - 60, keys[:2]), v2))

9. Exercises

Click an exercise to reveal its solution. Numerical answers were checked with the listings in §7.

9.1 Lowe's fix. Explain precisely which check Alice performs after Lowe's fix to Needham–Schroeder–Lowe, and why it defeats the attack of Figure 2.

Message 2 becomes {NA, NB, B}pk_A. In the attack, Bob produces this message believing he is talking to Alice, so it contains B. Mallory cannot replace it: to forge a version naming M she would need NB, which is encrypted to Alice. Alice, who started the run with Mallory, expects the name M and aborts on seeing B. The fix enforces agreement on the peer's identity.[3]

9.2 Replay window. A receiver uses a window of W = 8 and has accepted sequence numbers 15, 18 and 20 (so T = 20). Packets then arrive (all with valid ICVs) in the order 13, 12, 18, 25, 17, 19. Which are accepted?

13: offset 7 < 8, bit clear → accept. 12: offset 8 ≥ W → reject (too old). 18: bit set → reject (replay). 25: new high, window slides to 18–25 → accept. 17: offset 8 → reject (too old, even though it was never received). 19: offset 6, bit clear → accept. This is exactly what Listing 7.5's ReplayWindow outputs. Note that 17 is lost: a window that is too small turns heavy reordering into packet loss.[29]

9.3 Record nonces. For the RFC 8448 server handshake key, iv = 5d313eb2671276ee13000b30. What AEAD nonce protects record number 0x0102?

Left-pad the 64-bit sequence number to 12 bytes and XOR: only the last two bytes change, 0x0b30 ⊕ 0x0102 = 0x0a32, so the nonce is 5d313eb2671276ee13000a32. Because sequence numbers never repeat under one key, nonces never repeat.[23]

9.4 HKDF-Expand-Label. Write out, in hex, the HKDF info string TLS 1.3 uses to derive a 16-byte "key" from a traffic secret.

struct { uint16 length; opaque label<7..255>; opaque context<0..255>; } with label "tls13 key" (9 bytes) and empty context: 00 10 | 09 | 74 6c 73 31 33 20 6b 65 79 | 00 = 001009746c733133206b657900. Including the output length and a fixed prefix means keys of different lengths or purposes can never collide.[23]

9.5 Counting round trips. How many round trips pass before a client can send its first byte of application data in: (a) TLS 1.2 over TCP; (b) TLS 1.3 over TCP; (c) TLS 1.3 resumption with 0-RTT over TCP; (d) QUIC, first connection; (e) QUIC with 0-RTT?

(a) 1 (TCP) + 2 (TLS) = 3 RTT. (b) 1 + 1 = 2 RTT. (c) 1 RTT: the TCP handshake, then early data in the first TLS flight. (d) 1 RTT, because QUIC combines transport and cryptographic handshakes. (e) 0 RTT. TCP Fast Open could save another RTT in (a)–(c) but is rarely deployed.[23,33]

9.6 QUIC amplification. A client's first Initial datagram is padded to 1,200 bytes. How many bytes may the server send before it has validated the client's address? Why does the rule exist?

At most three times what it received: 3,600 bytes. Without the limit, an attacker could spoof a victim's address and use QUIC servers, whose first flight includes a certificate chain of several kilobytes, to amplify traffic towards the victim. That is also why clients must pad Initial packets to at least 1,200 bytes.[33]

9.7 Logjam. In Listing 7.4's "1.2-style" mode, list everything the attacker must do to complete the downgrade undetected, and identify the single change that stops it in "1.3-style" mode.

The attacker (1) rewrites the ClientHello to offer only the weak group; (2) forwards the server's genuine signed parameters (the signature covers only the randoms and DH share, so it still verifies); (3) solves the 16-bit discrete log of the server share; (4) computes the session key; and (5) recomputes the Finished MACs over each side's own transcript. In 1.3-style mode the server signs a hash of the whole transcript, including the modified ClientHello; the client hashes its original ClientHello, so the signature fails. Breaking the weak DH does not help, because the signature key is the server's long-term key.[2,42]

9.8 Channel binding. A user authenticates with SCRAM-SHA-256 (without channel binding) to a server, through a TLS connection that an attacker has intercepted with a certificate the user's client wrongly accepted. Describe the relay attack, and explain how SCRAM-SHA-256-PLUS with tls-exporter prevents it.

The attacker terminates TLS with the client, opens its own TLS connection to the real server, and relays SCRAM messages between them. SCRAM proves knowledge of the password to the server, which then authenticates the attacker's connection. With -PLUS, the client includes its connection's tls-exporter value in the SCRAM proof. The client's value comes from the client–attacker connection; the server computes its value from the attacker–server connection; they differ, so the proof fails.[17,47]

9.9 ESP nonces. RFC 4106 AES-GCM in ESP uses a 4-byte salt and an 8-byte explicit IV. Why is the salt not sent, and what goes wrong if two packets share an IV under the same key?

The salt comes from the key derivation (it is part of the keying material) and is constant for the SA, so sending it would waste bytes; it makes nonces unpredictable across SAs. A repeated (salt, IV) pair under one key reveals the XOR of the two plaintexts and lets the attacker recover the GHASH authentication key and forge packets. Implementations therefore use the sequence number, or a counter, as the IV.[119]

9.10 Key tag. Compute the RFC 4034 key tag of a DNSKEY with flags 256, protocol 3, algorithm 15 and a 32-byte key of all zeros.

RDATA = 01 00 03 0f followed by 32 zero bytes. Sum the bytes at even offsets shifted left by 8 and the bytes at odd offsets: 0x0100 + 0x0300 + 0x00 + 0x0f = 0x040f; add the carry (none) and keep 16 bits: 1039. Key tags are only hints: different keys can share a tag, so validators must try all matching keys (the root cause of the KeyTrap denial of service).[138,146]

9.11 X3DH without a one-time prekey. Bob's server has run out of OPKs. Which property does Alice's first message lose, and for how long?

Without DH4, the shared key depends only on long-term and medium-term keys plus Alice's ephemeral. An attacker can replay Alice's initial message to Bob, and Bob cannot detect this from the message itself, until he rotates the signed prekey. Forward secrecy for that message also depends on when Bob deletes the old SPK. The Double Ratchet restores full guarantees once Bob replies.[21]

9.12 Skipped keys. Bob has received only message 0 of Alice's current sending chain. He now receives a message whose header has a new ratchet key, pn = 2 and n = 3. How many skipped message keys does he store?

First he finishes the old chain up to pn = 2: he has Nr = 1, so he stores the key for message 1 (one key). Then he performs the DH ratchet step and advances the new receiving chain to n = 3, storing keys for messages 0, 1 and 2 (three keys), and decrypts message 3. Total: 4 stored keys, all subject to MAX_SKIP.[159]

9.13 TreeKEM cost. In a full group of 1,024 members with no blank nodes, how many HPKE ciphertexts does a commit with a path update contain? Compare with pairwise channels.

One per level of the direct path: log2 1024 = 10 (Listing 7.8 prints 10). Pairwise fan-out would need 1,023 encryptions. Blank nodes (after removals) and unmerged leaves increase the count because the resolution of a copath node can contain several nodes; in the worst case a commit costs O(n).[166]

9.14 Toy SRP. With N = 23, g = 5, k = 3, x = 6, a = 4, b = 3 and u = 2, compute v, A, B and both sides' S.

v = 56 mod 23 = 8; A = 54 mod 23 = 4; B = 3·8 + 53 = 149 ≡ 11. Client: (B − k gx)a+ux = (11 − 24)16 ≡ 1016 ≡ 4. Server: (A vu)b = (4·64)3 ≡ 33 ≡ 4. Both get S = 4.[216]

9.15 The A = 0 attack. Show what happens in SRP if a malicious client sends A = N and the server does not check it.

A ≡ 0 (mod N), so the server computes S = (0 · vu)b = 0. The attacker knows S = 0 too, computes K = H(0) and a valid M1, and logs in as any user without the password. RFC 5054 requires the server to abort if A mod N = 0; Listing 7.9 shows the check.[216]

9.16 Noise message sizes. With X25519, ChaChaPoly and empty payloads, how long are the three messages of Noise_XX?

Message 1 (e): 32 bytes; no key yet, so the empty payload is sent unencrypted. Message 2 (e, ee, s, es): 32 + (32 + 16 tag) + 16 (encrypted empty payload) = 96. Message 3 (s, se): 48 + 16 = 64. Listing 7.6 confirms 32, 96 and 64.[13]

9.17 WireGuard's 148 bytes. Account for every byte of WireGuard's handshake initiation.

Type and reserved bytes (4) + sender index (4) + unencrypted ephemeral (32) + encrypted static (32 + 16) + encrypted TAI64N timestamp (12 + 16) + mac1 (16) + mac2 (16) = 148. The Noise IK part is 32 + 48 + 28 = 108 bytes; the rest is WireGuard framing and DoS protection.[26]

9.18 Terrapin. Why does deleting one message after the SSH key exchange not normally break the connection, yet Terrapin succeeds?

Normally a deleted encrypted packet makes the receiver's implicit sequence number disagree with the sender's, so the next MAC/AEAD check fails. Terrapin first injects an IGNORE message during the unauthenticated handshake, which increments the receiver's counter by one; deleting one encrypted message afterwards brings the counters back into agreement. The handshake transcript (exchange hash) covers KEXINIT but not the injected message. Strict KEX resets counters at NEWKEYS and forbids unexpected messages during key exchange.[35]

9.19 0-RTT policy. Which of these may a server safely accept as TLS 1.3 early data: GET /news, POST /transfer, GET /search?q=x, DELETE /photo/7, a WebSocket upgrade?

Only safe, idempotent requests whose replay does no harm: GET /news and GET /search (if they have no side effects such as counting). POST /transfer and DELETE must not be processed as early data (DELETE is idempotent, but replaying it later may delete a newly uploaded photo with the same ID); the WebSocket upgrade starts a stateful session. Servers answer such requests with 425 Too Early.[94]

9.20 Choosing a tool. A colleague wants to send a confidential file to a partner and asks whether to use age, OpenPGP or S/MIME. What does each provide, and what does none of them provide?

age: modern authenticated encryption, small keys, no signatures (add minisign or SSH signatures), no key distribution. OpenPGP: encryption and signatures, web-of-trust or direct fingerprint verification, broad tool support. S/MIME: encryption and signatures with CA-issued certificates, best inside organisations with an existing PKI. None provides forward secrecy: compromise of the recipient's long-term key exposes archived files. For conversations, use a messaging protocol with ratcheting.[182,186,192]

15 July 2010. The current root KSK, KSK-2017, has key tag 20326; it replaced KSK-2010 on 11 October 2018.[142]

10.8 What was the Double Ratchet originally called, and why?

The Axolotl Ratchet, after the salamander that can regrow lost limbs, a reference to the protocol's self-healing (post-compromise security).[159]

10.9 What is Heartbleed's CVE number?

CVE-2014-0160.[71]

10.10 Roughly how many lines of code was WireGuard's kernel implementation when it was proposed?

About 4,000, compared with hundreds of thousands for typical IPsec or OpenVPN stacks.[26]

10.11 Which RFC replaced RFC 8446, and when?

RFC 9846, published July 2026. It is a revision ("8446bis") with clarifications, not a new protocol version.[62]

10.12 What is the first line of every age file?

age-encryption.org/v1, a version string that is also the specification's URL.[192]

10.13 What fraction of HTTPS servers did the DROWN authors estimate were vulnerable at disclosure?

About one third (33%), because many servers shared RSA keys or certificates with SSLv2-enabled services such as mail servers.[72]

10.14 How was PGP's source code legally exported from the US in 1995?

It was printed as a book (MIT Press), since books were protected speech and not subject to the export controls on cryptographic software; readers abroad scanned it back into code.[180]

10.15 What does GREASE stand for?

"Generate Random Extensions And Sustain Extensibility" (RFC 8701).[78]

10.16 Is QUIC an acronym?

Google's original protocol was "Quick UDP Internet Connections", but the IETF standard says QUIC is a name, not an acronym.[33,107]

10.17 In Noise pattern names, what do N, K, X and I mean?

For each party's static key: N = no static key, K = known to the peer in advance, X = transmitted ("Xmitted") during the handshake, I = transmitted immediately in the first message.[13]

10.18 Which post-quantum hybrid became OpenSSH's first default post-quantum key exchange?

sntrup761x25519-sha512 (Streamlined NTRU Prime + X25519) in OpenSSH 9.0, April 2022. OpenSSH 10.0 (April 2025) switched the default to mlkem768x25519-sha256.[133,134]

10.19 Who first proposed mix networks, and when?

David Chaum, in "Untraceable electronic mail, return addresses, and digital pseudonyms" (1981).[177]

10.20 What is the HTTP status code for "this request should not have been sent as 0-RTT data"?

425 (Too Early).[94]
