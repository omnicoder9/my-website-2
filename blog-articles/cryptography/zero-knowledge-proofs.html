Introduction

An ordinary proof hands over the evidence. To prove that a number is composite, you show a factor; to prove that you know a password, you type it. A zero-knowledge proof convinces a verifier that a statement is true while revealing nothing beyond that one bit of truth: no factor, no password, no witness. Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff introduced the idea in 1985, together with the notion of knowledge complexity that measures how much a proof leaks.[1] The paper is one reason the two first authors received the 2012 ACM Turing Award,[2] and the GMR paper with Babai and Moran's Arthur–Merlin games shared the first Gödel Prize in 1993.[3,4,5]

For two decades zero knowledge was mainly a theoretical tool: it made secure multi-party computation and chosen-ciphertext-secure encryption possible on paper, and it gave identification schemes such as Fiat–Shamir and Schnorr.[6,7] Since around 2013 that changed quickly. Pinocchio made verifiable computation "nearly practical",[8] Zerocash and Zcash put zk-SNARKs into a cryptocurrency in 2016,[9,10] Monero adopted Bulletproofs in 2018,[11,12] and today zk-rollups prove the correct execution of thousands of Ethereum transactions with a single succinct proof.[13] Zero-knowledge proofs now also check ages from a digital ID without revealing birth dates,[14] and post-quantum signature candidates are built from them.[15]

This article goes from the definitions to the systems people actually deploy. Part 1 sets up interactive proofs and the three properties every proof system must balance: completeness, soundness and zero knowledge, plus proofs of knowledge. Parts 2 and 3 cover Sigma protocols and the Fiat–Shamir transformation to non-interactive zero knowledge. Parts 4 to 9 cover the modern stack: arithmetization, polynomial commitments, zk-SNARKs, zk-STARKs, Bulletproofs and trusted setup.

1. Interactive proofs and zero knowledge

1.1 Interactive proof systems

A classical NP proof is a static string: the verifier reads a witness and checks it deterministically. Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff, and independently Babai, generalised this in two ways at once.[1,4] The verifier becomes randomised and may err with small probability, and the proof becomes a conversation: the prover and verifier exchange messages, and the verifier's questions may depend on coins the prover never sees.

Formally, an interactive proof system for a language L is a pair of interactive algorithms (P, V), where V runs in probabilistic polynomial time and P may be computationally unbounded. On common input x they exchange messages, and at the end V outputs accept or reject. We write ⟨P, V⟩(x) for the verifier's output.[1,16] The class of languages with such proofs is IP. It turned out to be far larger than NP: Lund, Fortnow, Karloff and Nisan showed that interaction can prove that a formula has no satisfying assignment, and Shamir completed the picture with IP = PSPACE.[17,18] The tool behind both results, the sumcheck protocol, reappears in modern SNARKs such as Spartan and in the GKR protocol for delegating computation.[19,20,21]

Two refinements matter in practice. In a public-coin (Arthur–Merlin) protocol the verifier's messages are just its random coins; Goldwasser and Sipser showed that private coins add no power, and public coins are exactly what the Fiat–Shamir transformation of §3 needs.[22,5] And when soundness only has to hold against computationally bounded provers, the protocol is called an argument rather than a proof.[23] Almost every succinct system deployed today, SNARKs, STARKs and Bulletproofs included, is technically an argument.[21]

The cave of Ali Baba. Quisquater and colleagues explained zero knowledge to children with a ring-shaped cave whose two passages meet at a door opened by a secret word.[24] Peggy enters and takes the left or right passage while Victor waits outside. Victor then shouts which side Peggy should come out of. If she knows the word she can always comply; if not, she succeeds only when Victor happens to name the side she chose, with probability ½. After 20 rounds a bluffer survives with probability 2−20, yet Victor learns nothing he could not have staged himself: a video of the rounds is easy to fake by editing out failures, which is precisely the idea of a simulator (§1.4).

1.2 Completeness

Completeness says that honest parties succeed. For every x ∈ L (with a valid witness, for NP relations), the honest prover makes the honest verifier accept with probability at least 1 − εc; the protocol has perfect completeness when εc = 0.[16] Schnorr's protocol, graph isomorphism and Groth16 all have perfect completeness. Some lattice-based protocols do not: Lyubashevsky's "Fiat–Shamir with aborts" deliberately restarts when a response would leak the secret key, which is why ML-DSA's signing loop runs a variable number of iterations.[25,26]

1.3 Soundness

Soundness says that a false statement is rejected: for every x ∉ L and every prover strategy P*, Pr[⟨P*, V⟩(x) = accept] ≤ εs, the soundness error.[1,16] Three variants appear throughout this article:

  • Statistical (information-theoretic) soundness holds against unbounded cheaters. Graph isomorphism and Schnorr's protocol (as a proof of membership) are proofs in this sense.
  • Computational soundness holds only against polynomial-time cheaters, under an assumption such as the hardness of discrete logarithms. These are arguments.[23] Bulletproofs, KZG-based SNARKs and STARKs (in the random-oracle model) are arguments.
  • Knowledge soundness strengthens soundness from "the statement is true" to "the prover knows a witness"; it is the subject of §1.5.

A protocol with soundness error ½ is not useful once, but sequential repetition k times drives the error to 2−k. Parallel repetition also reduces the error of interactive proofs, although it can destroy zero knowledge, as Goldreich and Krawczyk showed.[27] Listing 12.2 measures the effect: a cheater against graph isomorphism passes one round about half the time and ten rounds about once in a thousand runs.

1.4 Zero-knowledge property

What does it mean to learn "nothing"? GMR's answer is the simulation paradigm: the verifier learns nothing if everything it sees, its view (the statement, its random coins and the messages it receives), could have been produced by the verifier alone, without the prover.[1,28] Formally, (P, V) is zero-knowledge if for every probabilistic polynomial-time verifier V* there is a probabilistic polynomial-time simulator S such that for every x ∈ L

{ view_V*( P(x, w) ↔ V*(x, z) ) } ≈ { S(x, z) }

where z is any auxiliary input the verifier brings (previous transcripts, for example). Allowing auxiliary input is what makes zero knowledge survive sequential composition: running a ZK protocol many times one after another is still ZK.[29] The notion of indistinguishability "≈" gives three flavours:[16]

  • Perfect ZK: the two distributions are identical (graph isomorphism, Schnorr against an honest verifier).
  • Statistical ZK: their statistical distance is negligible.
  • Computational ZK: no efficient distinguisher tells them apart (the GMW 3-colouring protocol with computationally hiding commitments). Statistical ZK proofs exist only for languages in AM ∩ coAM, so NP-complete problems almost certainly need computational ZK or arguments.[30]

Weaker but often sufficient is honest-verifier zero knowledge (HVZK), where the simulator only has to reproduce the view of a verifier that follows the protocol. Sigma protocols are HVZK, and Fiat–Shamir turns them into non-interactive proofs where the "verifier" is a hash function that cannot deviate.[31] Two related, weaker properties are witness indistinguishability (the verifier cannot tell which of several witnesses was used) and witness hiding (it cannot compute a witness); both are preserved under parallel composition, unlike ZK itself.[32]

How can a simulator produce an accepting transcript without the witness? It cheats in a way a real prover cannot: it controls the verifier's coins, or rewinds the verifier. For graph isomorphism (§1.6) the simulator guesses the challenge bit, prepares an answer for that bit only, and if V* asks the other question it rewinds and tries again; each try succeeds with probability ½, so expected time stays polynomial.[33] Black-box simulation has limits: Goldreich and Krawczyk proved that constant-round public-coin protocols cannot be black-box ZK for languages outside BPP, and Barak later broke this barrier with a non-black-box simulator.[27,34] When many sessions run concurrently with an adversarial scheduler, rewinding becomes harder still, which motivated the study of concurrent zero knowledge.[35]

1.5 Proofs of knowledge

Some statements are trivially true. Every element y of a cyclic group has a discrete logarithm, so a proof that "y has a discrete log" says nothing. What an identification scheme needs is proof that the prover knows it. Bellare and Goldreich formalised this as a proof of knowledge with knowledge error κ: there is an extractor E that, given rewindable black-box access to any prover P* that convinces the verifier with probability ε > κ, outputs a valid witness in expected time polynomial in 1/(ε − κ).[36,37]

The extractor and the simulator are mirror images. The simulator shows the verifier learns nothing because transcripts can be made without the witness; the extractor shows the prover knows something because two related transcripts reveal the witness. Neither contradicts the other: the extractor's power comes from rewinding the prover to the same commitment, which no real verifier can do. For Sigma protocols, extraction is the special soundness property of §2.2, and in Fiat–Shamir proofs it is made rigorous with Pointcheval and Stern's forking lemma.[38]

1.6 Graph isomorphism, 3-colouring and all of NP

Graph isomorphism. Goldreich, Micali and Wigderson gave the textbook example.[33] The statement is that graphs G0 and G1 are isomorphic; the witness is a permutation π with π(G0) = G1. In each round:

  1. P picks a random permutation σ and sends H = σ(G1).
  2. V sends a random bit b.
  3. P replies with an isomorphism φ from Gb to H: φ = σ if b = 1, and φ = σ ∘ π if b = 0. V checks φ(Gb) = H.

Completeness is immediate. If the graphs are not isomorphic, H can be isomorphic to at most one of them, so any prover fails for one value of b: soundness error ½. And the view (H, b, φ) consists of a uniformly random permutation φ and the graph φ(Gb), which a simulator can produce by choosing b and φ first: perfect zero knowledge.

Graph 3-colouring and NP. The same paper's main theorem is that, assuming one-way functions exist, every language in NP has a computational zero-knowledge proof.[33] Because 3-colourability is NP-complete, it suffices to prove that a graph is 3-colourable. The prover randomly permutes the three colours, commits to the colour of every vertex, and the verifier picks one edge; the prover opens only its two endpoints, which must differ. A false colouring has at least one bad edge, caught with probability at least 1/|E| per round, so O(|E|·λ) rounds give soundness error 2−λ. The colour permutation makes the two opened colours a uniformly random pair of distinct colours, which the simulator can produce. Ben-Or and colleagues extended the result: everything provable interactively is provable in zero knowledge.[39]

The theorem is a feasibility result: reducing a real statement to 3-colouring and running hundreds of rounds is hopeless in practice. The rest of this article is the story of how cryptographers made general-purpose zero knowledge efficient by replacing graphs with polynomials.

2. Sigma protocols

2.1 Schnorr's protocol

Let g generate a group of prime order q in which discrete logarithms are hard, and let the prover know x with y = gx. Schnorr's identification protocol has three moves:[7]

  1. Commit. P picks random r ∈ ℤq and sends t = gr.
  2. Challenge. V sends random c ∈ ℤq (or from a smaller challenge space such as {0,1}128).
  3. Response. P sends s = r + c·x mod q. V accepts iff gs = t · yc.

Completeness: gr + cx = gr(gx)c. Special soundness: two answers s1, s2 to challenges c1 ≠ c2 for the same t give x = (s1 − s2)/(c1 − c2) (Figure 4), so a prover who can answer two challenges knows x, and the knowledge error is 1/|challenge space|. Honest-verifier ZK: pick c and s at random and set t = gsy−c; the triple has exactly the distribution of a real transcript. The protocol is standardised for non-interactive use in RFC 8235, and it is the core of Schnorr, EdDSA and BIP 340 signatures.[40,7]

2.2 The Sigma-protocol abstraction

Cramer's PhD thesis and his work with Damgård and Schoenmakers abstracted Schnorr's protocol into the notion of Σ-protocols.[41,31] A Sigma protocol for a relation R is a three-move public-coin protocol (a, e, z) with:

  • Completeness: an honest prover with (x, w) ∈ R always convinces.
  • Special soundness: from any two accepting transcripts (a, e, z) and (a, e′, z′) with e ≠ e′, an efficient extractor computes w. This implies a proof of knowledge with knowledge error 1/|E| for challenge space E.[31]
  • Special honest-verifier ZK: given x and any challenge e, a simulator outputs (a, z) distributed like a real transcript with that challenge.

The family is large. Guillou–Quisquater proves knowledge of an RSA e-th root; Okamoto's protocol proves knowledge of a representation y = g1x1g2x2, which is how one proves knowledge of the opening of a Pedersen commitment; and Maurer showed that all of these are instances of a single protocol for proving knowledge of a preimage under a group homomorphism.[42,43,44] Sigma protocols are only honest-verifier ZK as stated, because a malicious verifier could choose e as a hash of a. Fiat–Shamir sidesteps the issue by making the hash the verifier, and interactive uses can add a commitment to the challenge.[31]

2.3 AND, OR and equality proofs

Sigma protocols compose cleanly, which is why they remain the tool of choice for small algebraic statements such as e-voting, anonymous credentials and verifiable random functions.

  • AND. Run the protocols for x1 and x2 in parallel with the same challenge.
  • Equality of discrete logs (Chaum–Pedersen, DLEQ). To prove logg u = logh v, send (gr, hr) and answer both with one s = r + cx.[45] DLEQ proofs make ElGamal decryption verifiable in voting systems and prove correct evaluation in verifiable oblivious PRFs (RFC 9497) and Privacy Pass (RFC 9578).[46,47]
  • OR (Cramer–Damgård–Schoenmakers). To prove knowledge of x1 or x2 without revealing which, the prover simulates the branch it cannot prove, choosing that branch's challenge c2 itself, runs the real branch honestly, and sets c1 = c − c2 once the verifier's c arrives. The verifier checks both transcripts and that c1 + c2 = c. Because the prover controls only one of the two challenges, it must know at least one witness; because simulated and real transcripts look the same, the verifier cannot tell which.[41] Generalising to threshold "k of n" statements uses secret sharing of the challenge.

2.4 Commitments and Sigma protocols

A commitment scheme is the digital analogue of a sealed envelope: hiding (the commitment reveals nothing about the value) and binding (it cannot be opened to two values). The Pedersen commitment C = gvhγ, with independent generators whose relative discrete log nobody knows, is perfectly hiding and computationally binding, and it is additively homomorphic: C(v1)·C(v2) = C(v1 + v2).[48] A hash commitment H(nonce ‖ v) is the opposite trade-off: binding under collision resistance, hiding only computationally. No commitment can be both perfectly hiding and perfectly binding, and the choice mirrors the choice between statistical ZK arguments and computational ZK proofs.[16]

Pedersen commitments plus Sigma protocols already give practical privacy tools: proving that a committed value is a bit (an OR-proof of "C = hγ or C/g = hγ"), that two commitments hide equal values, or, by committing to each bit, that a value lies in a range. The bit-by-bit range proof costs O(n) group elements for an n-bit range; Bulletproofs (§8) reduce that to O(log n).[11] Anonymous credential systems such as Camenisch–Lysyanskaya and Direct Anonymous Attestation in TPMs are built from exactly these ingredients.[49,50]

3. Fiat–Shamir and non-interactive zero knowledge

3.1 The Fiat–Shamir transformation

Interaction is inconvenient: a proof cannot be posted to a blockchain, attached to a document, or checked by many verifiers. Fiat and Shamir's 1986 observation was that in a public-coin protocol the verifier's only job is to supply unpredictable randomness, and a hash function can do that.[6] The prover computes the challenge itself as c = H(statement, commitment) and sends (t, s); the verifier recomputes c and runs the usual check. Including a message in the hash turns a proof of knowledge into a signature: Schnorr signatures are exactly Fiat–Shamir applied to Schnorr's protocol, and Pointcheval and Stern proved them unforgeable in the random-oracle model with the forking lemma.[38,7] The transformation applies to any public-coin protocol with many rounds, hashing the whole transcript so far for each challenge; that is how Bulletproofs, PLONK and STARKs become non-interactive.

3.2 Strong vs weak Fiat–Shamir: the Frozen Heart

Bernhard, Pereira and Warinschi distinguished weak Fiat–Shamir, which hashes only the prover's commitment, from strong Fiat–Shamir, which hashes the statement as well.[52] With weak FS, a prover may choose the statement after seeing the challenge. For Schnorr the attack is simple: fix any t and s, compute c = H(t), then solve gs = t·yc for y = (gs/t)1/c. The result is a valid proof of knowledge of the discrete log of a y whose discrete log nobody knows. In Helios, weak FS in the ElGamal ciphertext proofs let a malicious voter or authority produce proofs that verified for ill-formed values.[52,53]

The mistake keeps recurring. In April 2022 Trail of Bits disclosed a family of such bugs, which they called Frozen Heart ("FoRging Of ZEro kNowledge proofs"), in implementations of Girault's proof of knowledge, Bulletproofs and PLONK, including ZenGo's zk-paillier, Adjoint's bulletproofs, Dusk Network's PLONK, iden3's SnarkJS and ConsenSys's gnark.[54] Papers often present the challenge as H(commitment) with the statement implicit; implementations that copied the formula literally were forgeable. Listing 12.1 reproduces the attack: the weak verifier accepts the forged proof and the strong one rejects it.

Rule. Hash the entire public input: group description and generators, the statement, every prover message so far, and a protocol- and context-specific domain separator. Transcript libraries such as merlin, used by dalek's Bulletproofs, exist to make this automatic: every message is appended to the transcript before any challenge is drawn.[54,55]

3.3 NIZK in the common reference string model

Without a random oracle, non-interactive zero knowledge for non-trivial languages is impossible in the plain model, because a simulator would itself be a valid prover. Blum, Feldman and Micali showed in 1988 that it becomes possible if prover and verifier share a common reference string (CRS) sampled by a trusted process; the simulator is allowed to generate the CRS itself, with a trapdoor.[56] That trapdoor is the origin of the "toxic waste" in trusted setups (§9): whoever knows it can simulate, which means they can forge.

Refinements matter for applications. A simulation-sound NIZK stays sound even after the adversary sees simulated proofs of false statements, which is what chosen-ciphertext-secure encryption needs.[57,58] A NIZK is simulation-extractable if a witness can be extracted from any new proof even then; Groth16 proofs are malleable and need extra care to achieve this.[59] Groth and Sahai gave efficient NIZKs for equations in bilinear groups without random oracles, used in group signatures and anonymous credentials.[60] Modern SNARKs are NIZK arguments in the CRS model (or with a transparent CRS), and most are additionally made non-interactive by Fiat–Shamir.

3.4 What the random oracle hides

Fiat–Shamir is proven secure when the hash is modelled as a random oracle, a truly random function every party can query.[51] Real hash functions are not random oracles, and there are contrived schemes that are secure in the ROM but insecure with every concrete hash.[61] Goldwasser and Kalai showed that the same is true of Fiat–Shamir itself: there exist interactive protocols whose Fiat–Shamir transform is insecure for any hash function.[62] For natural protocols no such attack is known, and research on correlation-intractable hash families has proven Fiat–Shamir sound in the standard model for some protocols. A practical lesson survives the theory: apply Fiat–Shamir to protocols with small soundness error per round. Applying it to a protocol with many rounds of soundness ½, such as parallel graph isomorphism, would let a prover grind hashes until each challenge comes out right.[21] Quantum adversaries add another wrinkle, since they can query the oracle in superposition; Unruh's transform and later QROM analyses address this for post-quantum signatures.[63]

4. Arithmetization

General-purpose proof systems do not reason about programs directly. They reason about polynomials over a finite field, because polynomial identities can be checked at a single random point (§5.1). Arithmetization is the compilation step that turns "I know w such that F(x, w) = y" into a set of polynomial equations that hold if and only if the computation was done correctly.[21,64]

4.1 Arithmetic circuits and R1CS

Take Buterin's well-known example: prove knowledge of x with x3 + x + 5 = 35 (the answer is 3).[66] First flatten the computation into steps with at most one multiplication each:

v1 = x * x v2 = v1 * x v3 = v2 + x out = v3 + 5

A rank-1 constraint system (R1CS) writes each step as ⟨Ai, w⟩ · ⟨Bi, w⟩ = ⟨Ci, w⟩ over a witness vector w = (1, x, out, v1, v2, v3). Multiplications cost one constraint each; additions and multiplications by constants are free because they fold into the linear combinations. With x = 3 the witness is (1, 3, 35, 9, 27, 30). R1CS is the native format of Groth16, circom and gnark.[67,59,68] Listing 12.4 builds this R1CS, and Listing 12.8 compiles the same circuit with circom, which reports three constraints.

4.2 Quadratic arithmetic programs

Gennaro, Gentry, Parno and Raykova turned R1CS into a single polynomial identity.[67] Assign each constraint i a field point ri; for each witness column j interpolate polynomials Aj(X), Bj(X), Cj(X) through that column's entries; and define A(X) = Σ wjAj(X) and likewise B and C. Then all constraints hold if and only if A(ri)B(ri) − C(ri) = 0 for every i, that is, if and only if the vanishing polynomial Z(X) = Π(X − ri) divides A(X)B(X) − C(X):

A(X) · B(X) − C(X) = H(X) · Z(X) for some polynomial H.

This quadratic arithmetic program (QAP) is what Pinocchio and Groth16 prove, by checking the identity "in the exponent" at a secret point τ fixed in the trusted setup.[8,59] Listing 12.4 computes the QAP for our example, confirms that Z divides AB − C for the true witness and not for a forged one, and checks the identity at a random point.

4.3 PLONKish arithmetization

PLONK uses a different format.[69] Every gate has the same shape, with selector constants that pick its behaviour:

q_L·a + q_R·b + q_O·c + q_M·a·b + q_C = 0

Setting q_M = 1, q_O = −1 gives a multiplication gate, q_L = q_R = 1, q_O = −1 an addition gate, and so on. Gates are separate rows of a table, so copy constraints are needed to say that the output wire of one gate equals an input wire of another; PLONK proves them all at once with a permutation argument over the wire columns. "PLONKish" systems such as Halo2 and Plonky2 generalise this to many columns, arbitrary-degree custom gates (for example, a whole elliptic-curve addition or a hash round in one row) and lookup arguments such as plookup, which prove that a value appears in a precomputed table.[70,71] Lookups make "non-arithmetic" operations such as range checks, bitwise XOR and byte decomposition cheap, and they are the main reason zkVMs became practical. Setty, Thaler and Wahby's customizable constraint systems (CCS) unify R1CS, PLONKish and AIR in one format.[72]

4.4 AIR for STARKs

STARKs arithmetize repeated computation as an execution trace: a table with one row per step of a machine and one column per register.[73] An algebraic intermediate representation (AIR) gives transition constraints, low-degree polynomials relating each row to the next, such as next.a = cur.a + cur.b for a Fibonacci machine, and boundary constraints pinning inputs and outputs. Each column is interpolated over a multiplicative subgroup of size T generated by ω; a transition constraint C(f(X), f(ωX)) = 0 must hold on all but the last point, so it must be divisible by the corresponding vanishing polynomial. The resulting quotient polynomials are checked to have low degree with FRI (§5.4). AIR is compact for uniform computation such as a CPU loop, which is why Cairo, RISC Zero and other zkVMs use it.[74,75]

4.5 Fields and ZK-friendly hashes

Arithmetization fixes the field. Pairing-based SNARKs work over the scalar field of a pairing-friendly curve: BN254 (a Barreto–Naehrig curve, precompiled in Ethereum since 2017) or BLS12-381 (designed for Zcash Sapling and used in Ethereum's consensus layer).[76,77,78] BN254 was designed for 128-bit security, but the extended tower number field sieve has reduced estimates to roughly 100 bits.[79] Hash-based systems are free to choose small, fast fields: Plonky2 introduced the 64-bit "Goldilocks" prime 264 − 232 + 1, and newer STARKs use 31-bit fields such as BabyBear and Mersenne-31 with extension fields for soundness; Circle STARKs make the Mersenne prime 231 − 1 usable even though its multiplicative group has no large power-of-two subgroup.[80,81]

Standard hashes are expensive in circuits: SHA-256 is designed for 32-bit words and bit operations, which cost many field constraints. Arithmetization-friendly hashes such as Poseidon use only field additions and low-degree power maps, so they cost a few hundred constraints per call instead of tens of thousands.[82] The trade-off is younger cryptanalysis, and algebraic attacks on such designs remain an active area.

5. Polynomial commitments

5.1 Polynomial IOPs and the Schwartz–Zippel lemma

The workhorse is the Schwartz–Zippel lemma: a non-zero polynomial of total degree d over a field 𝔽 vanishes at a uniformly random point with probability at most d/|𝔽|.[83,84] Two different polynomials of degree d therefore agree at a random point with probability at most d/|𝔽|, which is negligible for a 254-bit field: one evaluation replaces checking millions of constraints. A polynomial interactive oracle proof (polynomial IOP) is a protocol in which the prover sends polynomials as oracles and the verifier queries them at random points.[65,21]

A polynomial commitment scheme (PCS) compiles the oracle into cryptography, introduced by Kate, Zaverucha and Goldberg in 2010.[85] It has three operations: Commit(f) → C, a short binding (and optionally hiding) commitment to a polynomial of bounded degree; Open(f, z) → (v, π), a value v = f(z) and an evaluation proof; and Verify(C, z, v, π). 5.2 KZG commitments

KZG needs a pairing e: G1 × G2 → GT and a structured reference string ([1]1, [τ]1, [τ2]1, …, [τd]1, [τ]2), where [a]i denotes a times the generator of Gi and τ is a secret that must be destroyed.[85]

  • Commit: C = Σ fi[τi]1 = [f(τ)]1, computable from the SRS without knowing τ.
  • Open at z: since f(X) − f(z) vanishes at z, the quotient q(X) = (f(X) − v)/(X − z) is a polynomial; send v and π = [q(τ)]1.
  • Verify: e(C − [v]1, [1]2) = e(π, [τ]2 − [z]2), which checks f(τ) − v = q(τ)(τ − z) in the exponent.

Commitments and proofs are a single group element (48 bytes on BLS12-381) regardless of degree, and many openings can be batched into one. Ethereum's EIP-4844 "blob" transactions commit to rollup data with KZG over BLS12-381, using an SRS of 4096 powers produced by a public ceremony (§9).[88,89] The flip side is toxic waste: anyone who knows τ can open any commitment to any value. Listing 12.5 runs KZG with real BN254 pairings and then forges an opening using τ.

5.3 Inner-product arguments

A polynomial evaluation f(z) = ⟨f, (1, z, z2, …)⟩ is an inner product of the coefficient vector with a public vector, so a protocol proving inner products of committed vectors is a PCS. Bootle, Cerulli, Chaidos, Groth and Petit gave a logarithmic-size inner-product argument (IPA) over Pedersen vector commitments, and Bulletproofs improved its constants.[90,11] Each round the prover sends two group elements L, R, receives a challenge x, and both parties fold the vectors in half (a′ = x·alo + x−1·ahi, generators folded the opposite way), so a length-n statement needs log2 n rounds and 2 log2 n group elements. There is no trusted setup, only discrete-log hardness; the price is that the verifier must fold the generators itself, which takes linear time. Halo showed how to defer and amortise that linear work across a chain of recursive proofs, and Halo2 (PLONKish + IPA over the Pasta curves) runs Zcash's Orchard shielded pool.[86,71]

5.4 FRI

FRI (Fast Reed–Solomon Interactive Oracle Proof of Proximity), by Ben-Sasson, Bentov, Horesh and Riabzev, proves that a committed function is close to a polynomial of low degree using only a hash function.[87] The prover evaluates f (degree < d) on a domain ρ−1 times larger than d, where ρ is the code rate (for example 1/8), and commits the evaluations in a Merkle tree. Then it repeatedly folds: writing f(X) = fe(X2) + X·fo(X2), for a random β it commits

f'(X²) = f_e(X²) + β·f_o(X²) = (f(X) + f(−X))/2 + β·(f(X) − f(−X))/(2X)

which halves both the degree and the domain. After log d rounds a constant remains. The verifier then picks random query positions and checks, layer by layer, that the opened values (with Merkle paths) are consistent with the folding formula. If the committed function is far from every low-degree polynomial, each query catches it with constant probability, so the soundness error decreases exponentially with the number of queries. Turning FRI into a PCS for evaluations (the "DEEP" technique) checks that (f(X) − v)/(X − z) is also low degree.[73] Listing 12.6 implements FRI over the Goldilocks field: an honest proof verifies, and a cheater whose codeword is 20% corrupted passes one query in about two-thirds of runs (both opened points must be clean, roughly 0.82), 4 queries in 46 of 200 runs, 8 in 9, and 20 in none.

6. zk-SNARKs

6.1 Definition and history

A SNARK is a Succinct Non-interactive ARgument of Knowledge: a non-interactive argument whose proofs are short (polylogarithmic in the computation, often constant) and whose verification is much faster than re-running the computation, with knowledge soundness. A zk-SNARK is also zero-knowledge. The acronym comes from Bitansky, Canetti, Chiesa and Tromer in 2012.[91] The ideas are older. The PCP theorem says that every NP statement has a proof that can be checked by reading a constant number of random bits;[92] Kilian combined PCPs with Merkle trees to get succinct interactive arguments,[93] and Micali's CS proofs made them non-interactive with a random oracle.[94]

The practical era began with pairing-based SNARKs from QAPs by GGPR (2013) and Pinocchio, which verified computations in about 10 ms with 288-byte proofs.[67,8] Ben-Sasson, Chiesa, Tromer and Virza built a SNARK for a von Neumann architecture (BCTV14), which became the first zk-SNARK deployed at scale, in Zcash's Sprout protocol launched in 2016.[95,9,10] Succinctness has a theoretical cost: Gentry and Wichs proved that SNARGs for NP cannot be proven sound by a black-box reduction to any falsifiable assumption, so SNARK security rests on knowledge-type assumptions, the random-oracle model, or the algebraic group model.[96,97]

6.2 Groth16

Groth's 2016 SNARK for QAPs is still the smallest and fastest to verify.[59] A proof is three group elements (A, B, C) ∈ G1 × G2 × G1: 128 bytes on BN254 with point compression, 192 bytes on BLS12-381. Verification is one pairing-product equation with three pairings plus a multi-exponentiation over the public inputs. Groth also proved that pairing-based linear-interactive SNARKs need at least two group elements, so this is close to optimal. The costs are a circuit-specific trusted setup (a new ceremony for every circuit, §9) and a prover dominated by multi-scalar multiplications and FFTs of size proportional to the number of constraints. Groth16 is used in Zcash Sapling, Filecoin, Tornado-style mixers, and countless circom/snarkjs applications. Listing 12.8 runs a full Groth16 pipeline (ceremony, proof, verification, Solidity verifier).

6.3 PLONK and universal SNARKs

Sonic (2019) introduced a universal and updatable SRS: one ceremony serves every circuit up to a size bound, and anyone can add fresh randomness later.[98,99] PLONK, by Gabizon, Williamson and Ciobotaru, made the approach efficient with the PLONKish arithmetization and a KZG-based permutation argument.[69] Its universal SRS is just powers of tau, so it can reuse the outputs of existing Powers-of-Tau ceremonies; snarkjs's PLONK mode in Listing 12.8 uses the same file as Groth16 with no circuit-specific phase. Proofs are about half a kilobyte on BN254 and verification costs two pairings. Marlin, by Chiesa et al., is a similar universal SNARK built from an R1CS-based polynomial IOP and formalised the "polynomial IOP + PCS" methodology of Figure 9.[65] Transparent SNARKs avoid setup entirely: Spartan is built from sumcheck, and Hyrax and Ligero from discrete logs and error-correcting codes.[20,100,101]

6.4 Recursion, folding and zkVMs

If a SNARK verifier is itself a circuit, a proof can attest to the verification of other proofs. Recursive composition and proof-carrying data let a single short proof cover an unbounded chain of computation steps, the basis of "succinct blockchains" whose entire history is verified by one proof. Halo achieved recursion without a trusted setup using a cycle of elliptic curves and deferred IPA verification.[86] Folding schemes, introduced by Nova, go further: instead of verifying a SNARK at each step, the prover cheaply folds two instances of a relaxed R1CS into one and produces a single SNARK only at the end.[102]

Zero-knowledge virtual machines (zkVMs) combine these techniques with AIR or PLONKish arithmetization of a whole instruction set, typically RISC-V, so that developers write ordinary Rust and get a proof of correct execution. RISC Zero and Succinct's SP1 are examples, and Cairo is a STARK-friendly CPU architecture designed for the purpose.[75,103,74] Many such systems prove with STARKs over small fields and then "wrap" the result in a Groth16 or PLONK proof so it can be verified cheaply on Ethereum. Strictly, much of this is about succinctness (verifiable computation) rather than zero knowledge; the "zk" in "zk-rollup" often refers only to validity proofs.[13]

7. zk-STARKs

A STARK (Scalable Transparent ARgument of Knowledge) was introduced by Ben-Sasson, Bentov, Horesh and Riabzev in 2018.[73] The defining properties are in the name:

  • Transparent: all verifier randomness is public coins, so there is no trusted setup. The only "parameters" are a hash function and a field.
  • Scalable: proving time is quasi-linear in the computation, O(T log T), and verification time and proof size are polylogarithmic, O(log2 T).
  • Post-quantum plausibility: the only cryptographic assumption is a collision-resistant hash in the random-oracle model, which Grover's algorithm weakens only quadratically. Pairing- and discrete-log-based SNARKs and Bulletproofs fall to Shor's algorithm.

A STARK prover writes the execution trace, interpolates its columns, combines the AIR constraints into quotient polynomials, commits everything with Merkle trees, and runs FRI to prove that the quotients have low degree, with Fiat–Shamir making it non-interactive.[73,87] Zero knowledge is obtained by masking the trace with random rows and evaluating on a coset that excludes the trace domain. The price of transparency is size: STARK proofs are typically tens to a few hundred kilobytes, versus 128 to a few hundred bytes for pairing-based SNARKs. That is why rollups often verify a STARK inside a SNARK before posting to Ethereum.

Engineering since 2018 has focused on smaller fields and faster hashing: Plonky2 combined PLONKish arithmetization with FRI over Goldilocks; Plonky3, RISC Zero and SP1 use 31-bit fields; and Circle STARKs (2024), implemented in StarkWare's Stwo prover, work over the Mersenne-31 field using the circle group x2 + y2 = 1.[80,104,81,105] Chiesa and Yogev's textbook gives a rigorous treatment of hash-based SNARGs.[106]

8. Bulletproofs

Bulletproofs, by Bünz, Bootle, Boneh, Poelstra, Wuille and Maxwell (IEEE S&P 2018), are short non-interactive zero-knowledge arguments with no trusted setup, based only on the discrete-log assumption.[11] Their headline application is range proofs for confidential transactions: proving that a Pedersen commitment V = gvhγ hides v ∈ [0, 2n), so that hidden amounts cannot be negative and inflate the money supply.

The range proof works in three steps.[11] The prover writes v in binary as a bit vector aL and sets aR = aL − 1n. The conditions "aL are bits and represent v" are aL ∘ aR = 0n and ⟨aL, 2n⟩ = v. Random challenges y, z combine these 2n + 1 equations into a single inner-product statement t = ⟨l, r⟩ between two blinded vectors, whose value is tied to V by a commitment to the polynomial t(X). Finally the logarithmic inner-product argument of §5.3 proves the inner product. A 64-bit range proof is 2·log264 + 4 = 16 group elements and 5 scalars, about 672 bytes on a 256-bit curve, and m range proofs aggregate into one with only 2 log2 m additional elements. Listing 12.7 implements the full protocol for 16-bit ranges.

Monero activated Bulletproofs in its October 2018 network upgrade (v0.13), reducing transaction sizes, and later adopted the smaller Bulletproofs+ variant.[12,107] Bulletproofs also prove arbitrary arithmetic circuits; the trade-off against SNARKs is verification time, which is linear in the circuit (though batch verification helps), and proving time. The Rust dalek-cryptography implementation over Ristretto255 is a widely used reference.[108]

9. Trusted setup

Pairing-based SNARKs need a structured reference string (SRS) containing powers of a secret τ (and, for Groth16, circuit-specific secrets α, β, γ, δ). The secrets are toxic waste: they act as the simulation trapdoor of §3.3, so anyone who keeps them can produce proofs of false statements that verify. Leaking the trapdoor breaks soundness; zero knowledge can be preserved even against a maliciously generated SRS if the prover first checks that the SRS is well formed, a property called subversion zero knowledge.[59,56,109]

Multi-party ceremonies remove the single point of trust. Participants take turns: each takes the current SRS [τi], picks a fresh secret s, raises the elements to [(sτ)i], publishes a proof of correct update, and deletes s. The final τ is the product of all contributions, so the SRS is secure if at least one participant was honest and destroyed their secret: a "1-of-N" trust model.[110,111]

Real ceremonies have grown steadily:

  • Zcash Sprout (October 2016): six participants in a carefully designed, partly air-gapped multi-party computation for the BCTV14 parameters.[112,110]
  • Zcash Sapling (2017–2018): the Powers of Tau phase accepted 87 contributions between November 2017 and April 2018, followed by a Sapling-specific phase 2 with more than 90 contributions, using the random-beacon protocol of Bowe, Gabizon and Miers.[113,111]
  • Ethereum KZG ceremony (2023): 141,416 contributions over 208 days, from 13 January to 8 August 2023, producing the SRS for EIP-4844 blobs. Anyone could contribute from a browser.[89,88]

The stakes became concrete in 2019. On 1 March 2018 Ariel Gabizon discovered that the BCTV14 paper's parameter generation included extra elements that would have let an attacker forge proofs and create Zcash out of nothing. The flaw was quietly fixed when the Sapling upgrade moved Sprout proofs to Groth16 in October 2018, and disclosed on 5 February 2019 as CVE-2019-7167; no exploitation was found.[114,115] The lesson is that a ceremony is only as good as the paper and the code it implements.

Universal and updatable SRSs (Sonic, PLONK, Marlin) let one ceremony serve all circuits and let new participants keep strengthening it.[99,98] And transparent systems, Bulletproofs/IPA, STARKs, Spartan, Halo2, need no ceremony at all, which is the main reason they are favoured for new deployments where proof size is less critical.

10. Applications and pitfalls

10.1 Where zero-knowledge proofs are used

Private payments

Zerocash/Zcash hide sender, receiver and amount behind zk-SNARKs: BCTV14 in Sprout (2016), Groth16 on BLS12-381 in Sapling (2018), and Halo2 in Orchard, which needs no trusted setup.[9,10,71] Monero hides amounts with Bulletproofs range proofs.[12]

Blockchain scaling

zk-rollups execute transactions off-chain and post a validity proof that Ethereum verifies cheaply; zkVMs prove whole RISC-V or Cairo programs.[13,75,74]

Identity and credentials

Anonymous credentials (Camenisch–Lysyanskaya, Idemix), Direct Anonymous Attestation in TPMs, and Google's open-sourced Longfellow ZK library, which proves facts such as "over 18" from an ECDSA-signed mobile driving licence.[49,50,14,116]

Authentication and privacy protocols

Schnorr identification, verifiable OPRFs and Privacy Pass tokens (DLEQ proofs), and PAKEs; RFC 8235 standardises the Schnorr NIZK.[40,46,47]

Verifiable voting

Helios and similar systems prove that encrypted ballots are well formed and that tallies are correctly decrypted, with Chaum–Pedersen and OR-proofs.[53,41]

Post-quantum signatures

"MPC-in-the-head" (Ishai et al.) and its descendants turn ZK proofs for symmetric primitives into signatures: Picnic, and in NIST's third round of additional signatures (May 2026) FAEST (VOLE-in-the-head), SDitH and MQOM.[117,118,119,15]

10.2 How zero-knowledge systems fail

The proof systems themselves have held up well; most real failures are in how they are used. A 2024 systematisation of SNARK vulnerabilities found that bugs in circuits, especially missing constraints, dominate, and 0xPARC's community bug tracker lists under-constrained circuits as the most common class.[120,121]

  • Under-constrained circuits. In circom, <-- assigns a value without constraining it. If a gadget computes a "hint" (an inverse, a bit decomposition, a quotient) and forgets the constraint that pins it down, a malicious prover can choose any value. Listing 12.4 shows an IsZero gadget that "proves" 7 = 0 once one constraint is dropped. Field arithmetic adds traps: p·q = N over a 254-bit field has solutions with "factors" far larger than N unless range checks are added (Exercise 14.9).
  • Weak Fiat–Shamir. Omitting the statement or public inputs from the transcript hash (§3.2) allows forgery.[52,54]
  • Setup and specification errors. The BCTV14 flaw was in the paper's setup description, and it survived years of review (§9).[115,114]
  • Randomness misuse. A Sigma-protocol prover that reuses the commitment nonce r for two challenges hands the verifier the extractor's input (Figure 4), exactly as with ECDSA or Schnorr signatures.
  • "Zero-knowledge" that isn't. Many rollups and zkVMs use succinct proofs without the zero-knowledge property; public inputs, proof metadata and timing can also leak what the proof hides.

pi(G0) = G1. Each round the prover sends a random relabelling H of G1, the
verifier asks for b in {0, 1}, and the prover reveals an isomorphism Gb -> H.

def rand_graph(n, p=0.45, rng=random.Random(1)):
    return frozenset((i, j) for i in range(n) for j in range(i + 1, n) if rng.random() < p)

def apply(perm, g):
    return frozenset(tuple(sorted((perm[a], perm[b]))) for a, b in g)

def compose(p2, p1):            # (p2 o p1)(i) = p2[p1[i]]
    return [p2[p1[i]] for i in range(len(p1))]

def inverse(p):
    q = [0] * len(p)
    for i, v in enumerate(p): q[v] = i
    return q

def rand_perm(n):
    p = list(range(n)); random.SystemRandom().shuffle(p); return p

G1 = apply(pi, G0)

    def commit(self):
        self.sigma = rand_perm(N)
        return apply(self.sigma, G1)                 # H = sigma(G1)
    def respond(self, b):
        return self.sigma if b == 1 else compose(self.sigma, pi)  # Gb -> H

    """Does not know pi; prepares H from a guessed bit and hopes."""
    def commit(self):
        self.guess, self.sigma = secrets.randbelow(2), rand_perm(N)
        return apply(self.sigma, G0 if self.guess == 0 else G1)
    def respond(self, b):
        return self.sigma        # correct only if b == guess

        H = prover.commit()
        b = secrets.randbelow(2)
        phi = prover.respond(b)
        if apply(phi, G0 if b == 0 else G1) != H:
            return False
    return True

12.6 FRI low-degree testing over Goldilocks

code/fri.py
"""fri.py - a minimal FRI low-degree test (the engine inside zk-STARKs).

Field: Goldilocks, p = 2^64 - 2^32 + 1 (used by Plonky2/Plonky3), which has
multiplicative subgroups of every size 2^k up to 2^32. The prover commits to
evaluations of f on a domain 8x larger than deg f (rate 1/8) with a Merkle tree,
then repeatedly folds   f'(x^2) = (f(x)+f(-x))/2 + beta*(f(x)-f(-x))/(2x),
halving degree and domain, until a constant remains. Challenges come from
Fiat-Shamir over the Merkle roots. Only hashes are used: no trusted setup.
"""
import hashlib, random

P = 2**64 - 2**32 + 1
GEN = 7                                     # generator of the full group F_p^*

def root_of_unity(n):                       # n | p-1, n a power of two
    return pow(GEN, (P - 1) // n, P)

def H(*parts):
    h = hashlib.sha256()
    for x in parts: h.update(x if isinstance(x, bytes) else x.to_bytes(8, "big"))
    return h.digest()

class Merkle:
    def __init__(self, values):
        self.levels = [[H(v) for v in values]]
        while len(self.levels[-1]) > 1:
            lv = self.levels[-1]
            self.levels.append([H(lv[i], lv[i + 1]) for i in range(0, len(lv), 2)])
        self.root = self.levels[-1][0]
    def path(self, i):
        out = []
        for lv in self.levels[:-1]:
            out.append(lv[i ^ 1]); i //= 2
        return out
    @staticmethod
    def check(root, i, value, path):
        h = H(value)
        for sib in path:
            h = H(h, sib) if i % 2 == 0 else H(sib, h); i //= 2
        return h == root

class Transcript:                           # Fiat-Shamir sponge
    def __init__(self): self.state = b"fri-demo"
    def absorb(self, b): self.state = H(self.state, b)
    def challenge(self, mod):
        self.state = H(self.state, b"c"); return int.from_bytes(self.state, "big") % mod

INV2 = pow(2, -1, P)

def fold(values, omega, beta):
    n = len(values); half = n // 2; out = []
    for j in range(half):
        x = pow(omega, j, P); a, b = values[j], values[j + half]   # f(x), f(-x)
        fe = (a + b) * INV2 % P
        fo = (a - b) * INV2 * pow(x, -1, P) % P
        out.append((fe + beta * fo) % P)
    return out

def prove(evals, rounds, n_queries, salt=b"", shadow=None):
    """shadow: a cheater's trick, explained below. Honest provers pass None."""
    tr, layers, trees, omega = Transcript(), [evals], [], root_of_unity(len(evals))
    tr.absorb(salt)
    for i in range(rounds):
        t = Merkle(layers[-1]); trees.append(t); tr.absorb(t.root)
        beta = tr.challenge(P)
        src = shadow if (shadow is not None and i == 0) else layers[-1]
        layers.append(fold(src, omega, beta)); omega = omega * omega % P
    final = layers[-1][0]; tr.absorb(final)
    queries = []
    for _ in range(n_queries):
        j, q = tr.challenge(len(evals)), []
        for i, t in enumerate(trees):
            n = len(layers[i]); j %= n; s = (j + n // 2) % n
            q.append((layers[i][j], t.path(j), layers[i][s], t.path(s)))
        queries.append(q)
    return salt, [t.root for t in trees], final, queries

def verify(n, rounds, proof):
    salt, roots, final, queries = proof
    tr, betas = Transcript(), []
    tr.absorb(salt)
    for r in roots: tr.absorb(r); betas.append(tr.challenge(P))
    tr.absorb(final)
    for q in queries:
        j, omega, size, expect = tr.challenge(n), root_of_unity(n), n, None
        for i, (a, pa, b, pb) in enumerate(q):
            j %= size; s = (j + size // 2) % size
            if not (Merkle.check(roots[i], j, a, pa) and Merkle.check(roots[i], s, b, pb)):
                return False
            if expect is not None and a != expect: return False
            lo = min(j, s); fx, fmx = (a, b) if j == lo else (b, a)
            x = pow(omega, lo, P)
            expect = ((fx + fmx) * INV2 + betas[i] * (fx - fmx) * INV2 * pow(x, -1, P)) % P
            omega = omega * omega % P; size //= 2
        if expect != final: return False
    return True

def evaluate_on_domain(coeffs, n):
    w = root_of_unity(n)
    return [sum(c * pow(w, i * k, P) for k, c in enumerate(coeffs)) % P for i in range(n)]

DEG, BLOWUP, QUERIES = 32, 8, 20
N = DEG * BLOWUP                                   # 256-point domain
ROUNDS = DEG.bit_length() - 1                      # 5 folds: degree 32 -> 1
rng = random.Random(2026)

f = [rng.randrange(P) for _ in range(DEG)]         # deg f < 32

size = 32 * len(pf[1]) + 8 + sum(16 + 32 * (len(a[1]) + len(a[3])) for q in pf[3] for a in q)
print(f"proof size ~{size / 1024:.1f} KiB (Merkle paths dominate)")

# only 80% of the domain. Its best move is to commit the corrupted values in
# layer 0 but fold the nearby honest codeword ("shadow") into layer 1, so every
# later layer is perfect. It is caught only when a query touches a corrupted
# position in layer 0. Different salts give independent Fiat-Shamir queries.

# Each query passes with probability about 0.8^2 = 0.64 (both opened points
# must be clean), so the soundness error falls exponentially in the queries.

deployments use elliptic curves such as Ristretto255 or secp256k1.
"""
import secrets
from zkgroup import P, Q, H, hash_to_group, inv

NBITS = 16
g, h, u0 = (hash_to_group(s) for s in (b"bp-g", b"bp-h", b"bp-u"))
GV = [hash_to_group(b"bp-G" + i.to_bytes(2, "big")) for i in range(NBITS)]
HV = [hash_to_group(b"bp-H" + i.to_bytes(2, "big")) for i in range(NBITS)]

def multiexp(bases, exps):
    acc = 1
    for b, e in zip(bases, exps): acc = acc * pow(b, e % Q, P) % P
    return acc
ip = lambda a, b: sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b)) % Q
rnd = lambda: secrets.randbelow(Q)
powers = lambda x, n: [pow(x, i, Q) for i in range(n)]

# ---------------------------------------------------------- inner-product argument
def ipa_prove(gv, hv, u, a, b, ts):
    L_R = []
    while len(a) > 1:
        m = len(a) // 2
        cL, cR = ip(a[:m], b[m:]), ip(a[m:], b[:m])
        L = multiexp(gv[m:] + hv[:m] + [u], a[:m] + b[m:] + [cL])
        R = multiexp(gv[:m] + hv[m:] + [u], a[m:] + b[:m] + [cR])
        x = H(ts, L, R); xi = inv(x); ts = x; L_R.append((L, R))
        gv = [pow(gv[i], xi, P) * pow(gv[m + i], x, P) % P for i in range(m)]
        hv = [pow(hv[i], x, P) * pow(hv[m + i], xi, P) % P for i in range(m)]
        a = [(a[i] * x + a[m + i] * xi) % Q for i in range(m)]
        b = [(b[i] * xi + b[m + i] * x) % Q for i in range(m)]
    return L_R, a[0], b[0]

def ipa_verify(gv, hv, u, Pc, proof, ts):
    L_R, a, b = proof
    for L, R in L_R:
        m = len(gv) // 2
        x = H(ts, L, R); xi = inv(x); ts = x
        gv = [pow(gv[i], xi, P) * pow(gv[m + i], x, P) % P for i in range(m)]
        hv = [pow(hv[i], x, P) * pow(hv[m + i], xi, P) % P for i in range(m)]
        Pc = pow(L, x * x, P) * Pc * pow(R, xi * xi, P) % P
    return Pc == multiexp([gv[0], hv[0], u], [a, b, a * b])

# ---------------------------------------------------------- range proof
def commit(v, gamma): return pow(g, v, P) * pow(h, gamma, P) % P

def range_prove(v, gamma, n=NBITS):
    V = commit(v, gamma)
    aL = [(v >> i) & 1 for i in range(n)]; aR = [(x - 1) % Q for x in aL]
    alpha, rho = rnd(), rnd()
    A = pow(h, alpha, P) * multiexp(GV + HV, aL + aR) % P
    sL, sR = [rnd() for _ in range(n)], [rnd() for _ in range(n)]
    S = pow(h, rho, P) * multiexp(GV + HV, sL + sR) % P
    y = H(b"y", V, A, S); z = H(b"z", y)
    yn, twon = powers(y, n), powers(2, n)
    l0 = [(a - z) % Q for a in aL]; l1 = sL
    r0 = [(yn[i] * (aR[i] + z) + z * z * twon[i]) % Q for i in range(n)]
    r1 = [yn[i] * sR[i] % Q for i in range(n)]
    t1 = (ip(l0, r1) + ip(l1, r0)) % Q; t2 = ip(l1, r1)
    tau1, tau2 = rnd(), rnd()
    T1, T2 = commit(t1, tau1), commit(t2, tau2)
    x = H(b"x", z, T1, T2)
    l = [(l0[i] + l1[i] * x) % Q for i in range(n)]
    r = [(r0[i] + r1[i] * x) % Q for i in range(n)]
    that = ip(l, r)
    taux = (tau2 * x * x + tau1 * x + z * z * gamma) % Q
    mu = (alpha + rho * x) % Q
    w = H(b"w", x, that, taux, mu); u = pow(u0, w, P)
    hp = [pow(HV[i], inv(yn[i]), P) for i in range(n)]       # h'_i = h_i^(y^-i)
    ipa = ipa_prove(GV, hp, u, l, r, w)
    return V, (A, S, T1, T2, taux, mu, that, ipa)

def range_verify(V, proof, n=NBITS):
    A, S, T1, T2, taux, mu, that, ipa = proof
    y = H(b"y", V, A, S); z = H(b"z", y); x = H(b"x", z, T1, T2)
    yn, twon = powers(y, n), powers(2, n)
    delta = ((z - z * z) * sum(yn) - pow(z, 3, Q) * sum(twon)) % Q
    # (65): the committed polynomial t(X) evaluates to that at x
    if commit(that, taux) != pow(V, z * z, P) * pow(g, delta, P) * pow(T1, x, P) * pow(T2, x * x, P) % P:
        return False
    w = H(b"w", x, that, taux, mu); u = pow(u0, w, P)
    hp = [pow(HV[i], inv(yn[i]), P) for i in range(n)]
    Pc = A * pow(S, x, P) % P
    Pc = Pc * multiexp(GV, [-z] * n) * multiexp(hp, [z * yn[i] + z * z * twon[i] for i in range(n)]) % P
    Pc = Pc * pow(h, -mu % Q, P) * pow(u, that, P) % P      # move to IPA form
    return ipa_verify(GV, hp, u, Pc, ipa, w)

gamma = rnd()
V, pf = range_prove(41_000, gamma)
print(f"v = 41000 in [0, 2^{NBITS}):", range_verify(V, pf))

14. Exercises

Exercises 14.1–14.3 and 14.7 use the toy group p = 23, q = 11, g = 4 (the squares mod 23, of order 11) with secret x = 3.

14.1 Schnorr by hand. Compute y = g^x. The prover picks r = 5 and the verifier sends c = 7. Compute t and s and verify.

y = 43 mod 23 = 64 − 46 = 18. t = 45 = 1024 mod 23 = 12. s = 5 + 7·3 = 26 ≡ 4 (mod 11). Check: gs = 44 = 256 ≡ 3, and t·yc = 12 · 187 ≡ 3 (mod 23) ✓. Note that s is reduced mod q = 11, the group order, not mod p.[7]

14.2 Extraction. With the same commitment t = 12 the prover answers c₁ = 2 with s₁ = 0 and c₂ = 9 with s₂ = 10. Recover x.

x = (s1 − s2)/(c1 − c2) = (−10)/(−7) = 10 · 7−1 = 10 · 8 = 80 ≡ 3 (mod 11) ✓. This is exactly the extractor of §1.5, and it is also what happens if a real prover reuses r.[31]

14.3 Simulation. Without using x, produce an accepting Schnorr transcript with c = 5 and s = 8.

Set t = gs·y−c = 48 · 18−5 = 9 · 8 = 72 ≡ 3 (mod 23). Check: t·yc = 3 · 185 ≡ 3 · 3 = 9 = g8 ✓. A real transcript with challenge 5 has s uniform and t determined by it, so the distributions are identical: special honest-verifier ZK.

14.4 Soundness amplification. How many rounds of the graph-isomorphism protocol give soundness error 2⁻⁸⁰? How many rounds of GMW 3-colouring for a graph with 1,000 edges give 2⁻⁴⁰?

Graph isomorphism: error 2−k, so 80 rounds. 3-colouring: a cheater with one bad edge survives a round with probability 1 − 1/1000, so we need k ≥ 40 ln 2 / −ln(0.999) ≈ 27,713 rounds, each with 1,000 commitments. That is why NP-reduction proofs are feasibility results, not engineering.[33]

14.5 Why the challenge must come after the commitment. Show that a prover who knows c before sending t can convince the Schnorr verifier without knowing x.

It runs the simulator: pick s, set t = gsy−c, and send t then s. The verification equation holds. Soundness depends entirely on c being unpredictable when t is fixed, which is what Fiat–Shamir's hash of t enforces.[6]

14.6 Fiat–Shamir and small challenges. Someone makes graph isomorphism non-interactive by running 40 rounds in parallel and taking the 40 challenge bits from H(all commitments). How much work does a cheater need? What should they have done?

The cheater guesses 40 bits, prepares commitments that can answer exactly those bits, and re-randomises until the hash outputs that guess: about 240 hash evaluations, which is feasible. In the interactive protocol 40 rounds gives error 2−40 per attempt; offline, the cheater can try as many times as they can hash. Use at least 128 parallel rounds (or a protocol with large challenge space, like Schnorr) so that grinding costs 2128.[21,6]

14.7 Weak Fiat–Shamir forgery. A verifier computes c = H(t) without hashing y. With t = 8, s = 9 and H(8) = 3, find a public key y for which (t, s) is an accepting proof.

We need gs = t·yc, so y = (gs/t)1/c. g9 = 13, 8−1 = 3 (mod 23), so gs/t = 39 ≡ 16; 3−1 mod 11 = 4, so y = 164 ≡ 9 (mod 23). Check: 8 · 93 = 8 · 16 = 128 ≡ 13 = g9 ✓. In a real group, choosing t as a hash-derived element makes log y unknown to everyone, including the forger (Listing 12.1).[52,54]

14.8 Deterministic nonces. To avoid bad randomness, a library sets r = H(x) in Schnorr proofs. It proves knowledge of x in two different contexts, so the challenges differ. What happens, and how do EdDSA-style nonces avoid it?

Both proofs share t = gH(x) with different c, so anyone can run the extractor and compute x. Derive the nonce from the secret and everything hashed into the challenge, r = H(k ‖ statement ‖ context ‖ message), so a different challenge always comes with a different nonce, or use fresh randomness (preferably hedged, mixing both).

14.9 Arithmetize factoring. Write constraints proving knowledge of non-trivial factors p, q of N = 35. Why are p·q = N, p ≠ 1, q ≠ 1 not enough over the BN254 scalar field?

Natural constraints: p·q = N, (p − 1)·a = 1 and (q − 1)·b = 1 (an inverse exists only if the value is non-zero), so 3 R1CS constraints. But over 𝔽r, p = 2 and q = 35·2−1 mod r satisfy them, and 35 is not divisible by 2. Integers and field elements differ: add range checks (decompose p and q into, say, 6 bits each with bi(bi − 1) = 0), so that p·q < r and the field equation implies the integer one.[121]

14.10 Schwartz–Zippel. A QAP identity has degree about 2²¹ over the 254-bit BN254 scalar field. Bound the probability that a false identity passes a check at one random point.

At most d/|𝔽| ≈ 221/2254 = 2−233.[83] In a SNARK the point is the secret τ or a Fiat–Shamir challenge, and the bound is why a single evaluation suffices. Over a 31-bit field the same bound is useless (221/231 = 2−10), so small-field STARKs draw challenges from an extension field.

14.11 A KZG opening by hand. For f(X) = X² + 1 and z = 2, compute v and the quotient q(X), and state what the verifier checks.

v = f(2) = 5; q(X) = (X2 + 1 − 5)/(X − 2) = (X − 2)(X + 2)/(X − 2) = X + 2. The proof is π = [τ + 2]1 = [τ]1 + 2[1]1, and the verifier checks e(C − [5]1, [1]2) = e(π, [τ]2 − [2]2), i.e. τ2 − 4 = (τ + 2)(τ − 2) in the exponent.[85]

14.12 Toxic waste. Explain why knowing τ lets you open a KZG commitment to any value, and why a ceremony with N participants is safe if one is honest.

With τ, compute π = [(f(τ) − v′)/(τ − z)]1 directly; the pairing check only tests this relation at τ, which now holds for any v′ (Listing 12.5). In a ceremony τ = τ0s1…sN; if one si is uniformly random and deleted, τ is uniformly random and unknown even to a coalition of all others.[111]

14.13 One FRI fold. For f(X) = 1 + 2X + 3X² + 4X³ and β = 5, compute f_e, f_o and the folded polynomial.

f(X) = (1 + 3X2) + X(2 + 4X2), so fe(Y) = 1 + 3Y, fo(Y) = 2 + 4Y, and f′(Y) = fe + 5fo = 11 + 23Y: degree 3 became degree 1, and the domain of Y = X2 is half the size. Check at X = 1: (f(1) + f(−1))/2 + 5(f(1) − f(−1))/2 = (10 + (−2))/2 + 5·12/2 = 4 + 30 = 34 = f′(1) ✓.[87]

14.14 Bulletproofs sizes. How many group elements and scalars are in a 32-bit range proof? In an aggregated proof for eight 64-bit values? Estimate bytes with 32-byte encodings.

Single: 2·log232 + 4 = 14 elements + 5 scalars = 19 × 32 = 608 bytes. Aggregated over m·n = 512 bits: 2·9 + 4 = 22 elements + 5 scalars = 864 bytes, versus 8 × 672 = 5,376 bytes for separate proofs.[11]

14.15 The OR-proof. In the CDS OR-proof, why can't a prover who knows neither witness simulate both branches?

Simulating a branch means choosing its challenge before its commitment. To simulate both, the prover must fix c1 and c2 before sending (t1, t2), but the verifier then requires c1 + c2 = c for a challenge chosen afterwards (or H(t1, t2, …)), which holds with probability 1/q. By special soundness, a prover who succeeds for two different c reveals a witness for at least one branch.[41]

14.16 Under-constrained IsZero. In Listing 12.4, why does dropping a·out = 0 let a prover claim IsZero(7) = 1? Fix the circom snippet inv <-- a != 0 ? 1/a : 0; out <== 1 - a*inv;

With only out = 1 − a·inv, the prover may choose inv = 0 (the <-- hint is not a constraint), giving out = 1 for any a. Add a * out === 0;: if a ≠ 0 it forces out = 0, and then inv = 1/a is forced too. This is circomlib's IsZero template.[68,121]

14.17 Simulators vs extractors. A simulator produces accepting transcripts without the witness. Why doesn't this contradict soundness?

The simulator has powers no real prover has: it chooses the verifier's challenge (or rewinds the verifier, or programs the random oracle, or knows the CRS trapdoor). A real prover facing an honest verifier cannot. Likewise the extractor's rewinding of the prover is impossible for a real verifier, so extraction does not contradict zero knowledge.[1,28]

14.18 Design choice. A wallet must prove on-chain, cheaply, that a user is over 18 based on a government-signed credential, with no trusted setup and a path to post-quantum security. What would you choose?

A transparent hash-based proof for the credential logic (STARK or Ligero/sumcheck-style, as in Google's Longfellow ZK, which targets ECDSA-signed mdocs), then, if on-chain cost matters, a SNARK wrapper. Note the tension: a Groth16/PLONK wrapper reintroduces pairings (not post-quantum) and a setup; with today's tools you must choose between small on-chain proofs and setup-free post-quantum soundness.[116,73]

16. Practitioner's cheat sheet

Three properties

Completeness (honest proofs verify), soundness or knowledge soundness (false or witness-less proofs fail), zero knowledge (a simulator exists). Know which one each component of your system provides and under which assumption.

Fiat–Shamir

Hash the whole transcript: domain separator, parameters, statement and public inputs, every prover message. Use a transcript library. Large challenge spaces only.[54]

Circuits

Every <-- needs a matching constraint. Range-check anything that must behave like an integer. Test with malicious witnesses, not just honest ones, and audit.[121]

Setup

Prefer transparent or universal setups. If you need Groth16, reuse a public Powers of Tau and run a public phase 2 with independent contributors and a beacon.[111]

Choosing

Tiny on-chain proofs: Groth16/PLONK. No setup: STARK, Halo2, Bulletproofs. Post-quantum: hash-based. Discrete-log statements: Sigma protocols.

Privacy hygiene

Check that the system is really zero-knowledge (many "zk" systems are only succinct). Public inputs, metadata and timing leak. Use fresh or hedged prover randomness.
