1Fundamentals

1.1Definition and scope of cryptography

The word comes from the Greek kryptós ("hidden") and gráphein ("to write")[1]. For most of its history cryptography meant exactly that: secret writing, the art of making a message unreadable to anyone but its intended recipient. The modern discipline is much broader. The Handbook of Applied Cryptography defines it as

"the study of mathematical techniques related to aspects of information security such as confidentiality, data integrity, entity authentication, and data origin authentication."[2]

Ronald Rivest offered an even more compact formulation: cryptography is about communication in the presence of adversaries[3]. Katz and Lindell describe modern cryptography as the scientific study of techniques for securing digital information, transactions and distributed computations, and stress that since the late 1970s it has moved from an art practised mainly by the military to a science built on precise definitions, explicit assumptions and proofs[4].

Today the scope includes:

  • Secure communication: encryption of data in transit (TLS, Signal, Wi-Fi) and at rest (disk and database encryption).
  • Integrity and authenticity: message authentication codes (MACs), digital signatures, code signing and certificates.
  • Key management: generating, distributing, storing, rotating and destroying keys[5].
  • Advanced functionality: secret sharing, threshold signing, zero-knowledge proofs, secure multiparty computation, private information retrieval, electronic voting and anonymous credentials[4,6].

Cryptography is one part of information security, not the whole of it. It provides strong guarantees about data under well-defined assumptions, but it cannot by itself fix software bugs, stolen passwords or untrustworthy insiders; section 2.11 returns to this point[7].

1.2Cryptography versus cryptology

Cryptology is the umbrella term for the whole field: the study of both making and breaking secret systems. Cryptography is the constructive half (designing systems), and cryptanalysis is the analytical half (attacking them)[2,8]. In everyday technical writing, "cryptography" is frequently used loosely to mean the entire field, and conferences such as CRYPTO and EUROCRYPT publish both design and attack papers. When precision matters, as in standards glossaries, the distinction is kept[8].

1.3Cryptography versus cryptanalysis

Cryptanalysis is "the study of mathematical techniques for attempting to defeat cryptographic techniques"[2]. The two activities are inseparable. A design can only be trusted after skilled people have tried hard to break it, which is why modern standards are chosen in open public competitions (AES, SHA-3 and the post-quantum process are examples; see 5.9). The cryptographer Bruce Schneier summarised the point (now called Schneier's law): anyone can design a cipher that they themselves cannot break[10].

Cryptanalysts classify attacks by what the adversary can see or do[2,4]:

The weakest attack applies to everything: exhaustive key search (brute force), trying every key. A cipher is considered broken in the academic sense when some attack is significantly faster than brute force, even if that attack is still far from practical[12].

1.4Cryptography versus steganography

Cryptography hides the meaning of a message; steganography (from Greek steganós, "covered") hides its existence[1,9]. An encrypted email is obviously secret, which in itself tells an observer that something is being protected. A steganographic message is meant to pass unnoticed.

  • Herodotus records that Histiaeus tattooed a message on a slave's shaved head and sent him once the hair had regrown, and that Demaratus warned Sparta by writing on the wooden base of a wax tablet and then covering it with fresh wax[1,9].
  • The term itself comes from Johannes Trithemius' Steganographia, written around 1499 and printed in 1606, a work that disguised a treatise on cryptography as a book of magic[1].
  • Twentieth-century spies used invisible inks and microdots; modern digital steganography hides bits in the least significant bits of image pixels, in audio, or in network timing[9].

A closely related technique, digital watermarking, embeds information that should survive copying or editing, for example to trace leaked media. Unlike steganography, its goal is robustness rather than secrecy of existence[9]. In practice the two are combined: encrypting a message before hiding it means that even if the hidden data is detected, it cannot be read. Note that classical steganography usually relied on the method staying secret, which violates Kerckhoffs' principle (section 1.9); modern schemes, like ciphers, use a secret key instead[9].

A cryptographic hash function must satisfy three properties[2]: preimage resistance (given a hash value, it is infeasible to find any input with that hash), second-preimage resistance (given one input, it is infeasible to find a different input with the same hash) and collision resistance (it is infeasible to find any two distinct inputs with the same hash). Because the output is fixed-length, collisions must exist; the requirement is only that nobody can find them. MD5 and SHA-1 both failed this test: practical MD5 collisions were published in 2004–2005 and the first full SHA-1 collision in 2017[16,17].

Common pitfall

Storing passwords as plain SHA-256 hashes is not "encrypting" them, and it is not enough. Fast hashes can be tested billions of times per second on GPUs, and identical passwords give identical hashes. Password storage needs a deliberately slow, salted password-hashing function such as scrypt, Argon2 or PBKDF2 (section 3.6)[18,19].

1.6Cryptographic primitives versus protocols

A primitive is a basic, well-studied building block with a narrowly defined job: a block cipher, a hash function, a digital-signature scheme, a key-agreement function or a pseudorandom generator[2]. A scheme or mode combines primitives into a usable tool, for example AES (a primitive) in Galois/Counter Mode to give authenticated encryption[20]. A protocol is a distributed algorithm: a sequence of messages exchanged between two or more parties to achieve a security goal, such as the TLS 1.3 handshake, which agrees keys, authenticates the server and then protects application data[21].

The distinction matters because protocols fail even when their primitives are perfect. The Needham–Schroeder public-key protocol of 1978[22] used sound encryption, yet in 1995–96 Gavin Lowe used a model checker to find a man-in-the-middle attack that had gone unnoticed for about 17 years[23]. The fix was to add one identity field to one message. Lessons like this are why modern protocols such as TLS 1.3 were analysed with formal tools before standardisation[21].

1.7Information-theoretic versus computational security

There are two fundamentally different ways to argue that a system is secure.

Information-theoretic (unconditional) security holds against an adversary with unlimited computing power. Claude Shannon proved in 1949 that the one-time pad, which adds (XORs) a truly random key as long as the message, achieves perfect secrecy: the ciphertext gives no information whatsoever about the plaintext. He also proved the price: any perfectly secret cipher needs at least as many possible keys as possible messages, so keys must be as long as all the data they ever protect and can never be reused[24]. Other information-theoretic constructions include Shamir secret sharing (section 4.5) and Wegman–Carter one-time message authentication[25,26].

Computational security accepts that an adversary could in principle break the system, but requires that doing so takes infeasible resources: for instance, an expected 2128 operations. Security is defined against "efficient" adversaries, and success probabilities are required to be negligibly small[4,27]. Almost all deployed cryptography (AES, RSA, elliptic curves, ML-KEM) is computationally secure, because it lets a short key such as 256 bits protect gigabytes of data.

python · s4_otp.pyimport secrets
def xor(a, b): return bytes(x ^ y for x, y in zip(a, b))

p1, p2 = b"MEET AT NOON", b"RUN AWAY NOW"
key = secrets.token_bytes(len(p1))       # truly random, as long as message
c1 = xor(p1, key)
print("c1 =", c1.hex(), " decrypts to", xor(c1, key))

# The fatal mistake: reusing the pad ("two-time pad")
c2 = xor(p2, key)
print("c1 XOR c2 == p1 XOR p2 ?", xor(c1, c2) == xor(p1, p2))  # key cancels out

The second half of this program shows the one-time pad's fatal failure mode. If the same pad encrypts two messages, XORing the ciphertexts cancels the key and leaves the XOR of the two plaintexts, from which natural-language messages can usually be recovered. This is exactly how the US Venona project read thousands of Soviet messages whose one-time pads had been reused during the Second World War[28].

1.8Cryptographic assumptions

Since computational security cannot (at present) be proven unconditionally, every scheme rests on assumptions. The ideal is a reduction: a proof that any efficient attack on the scheme could be converted into an efficient solution of a well-studied hard problem[4,29]. The assumptions fall into several kinds:

  • Hardness assumptions about specific mathematical problems: factoring large integers (RSA), computing discrete logarithms in finite fields or elliptic-curve groups (Diffie–Hellman, ECDSA)[30,31], and lattice problems such as Learning With Errors (ML-KEM, ML-DSA)[32].
  • Generic assumptions, such as the existence of one-way functions. This is the minimal assumption for most of symmetric cryptography; it implies P ≠ NP, so it cannot be proven with current knowledge[29].
  • Assumptions about primitives: that AES behaves as a pseudorandom permutation, or that SHA-256 is collision resistant. These are supported by decades of failed cryptanalysis rather than by a reduction[4].
  • Idealised models, such as the random-oracle model, which treats a hash function as a truly random function in a proof. This is a heuristic that has been extremely useful but is known not to hold for every possible scheme[33].
  • Physical and operational assumptions: that keys are generated from good randomness, that secret keys stay secret, that devices do not leak through side channels, and that the adversary does not own a large quantum computer[7,11].

Assumptions can fail. Shor's 1994 quantum algorithm shows that factoring and discrete logarithms are easy for a sufficiently large quantum computer[34], which is why the field is now replacing those assumptions (section 4.6).

1.9Threat models and trust models

A security claim is meaningless without saying whom it holds against. A threat model states the adversary's goals, capabilities and resources; a trust model states which parties, keys and components are assumed to behave correctly.

Kerckhoffs' principle

In 1883 Auguste Kerckhoffs set out six requirements for military ciphers. The second, now known as Kerckhoffs' principle, says that the system must not require secrecy and must be able to fall into enemy hands without inconvenience[35]. Shannon restated it as "the enemy knows the system being used"[24]. All security should rest on the key, which is small and easy to change, rather than on the algorithm, which is hard to change and will eventually leak. This is why reputable algorithms are public.

Adversary models

  • Passive (eavesdropper, "Eve"): reads traffic but does not alter it.
  • Active (man-in-the-middle, "Mallory"): can intercept, modify, replay, delete and inject messages. The Dolev–Yao model formalises this: the network is the adversary, but the adversary cannot break the underlying primitives[36].
  • Corruption: the adversary may compromise some participants, either before the protocol starts (static) or during it (adaptive); this underlies forward secrecy and threshold cryptography.
  • Physical and side-channel adversaries who can measure or disturb a device[11].
  • Future quantum adversaries who record traffic today and decrypt it once quantum computers exist, the "harvest now, decrypt later" threat[37,38].

Trust models

Every real system has to trust something, and the trust model makes this explicit[2,7]:

  • Trusted third party / public-key infrastructure (PKI): certificate authorities vouch for the binding between a name and a public key, as in X.509 certificates on the web[39].
  • Web of trust: users sign each other's keys, as in PGP.
  • Trust on first use (TOFU): accept a key the first time and alert if it changes, as SSH does for host keys.
  • Distributed trust: no single party is trusted; a threshold of them must cooperate (section 4.5).
  • Hardware roots of trust: secure elements, TPMs and hardware security modules that are trusted to protect keys.

2Security objectives

Cryptographic mechanisms are chosen to meet specific objectives. The Handbook of Applied Cryptography names four central ones, namely confidentiality, data integrity, authentication and non-repudiation, from which many others are derived[2]. Modern protocols add goals about time (freshness, forward secrecy, post-compromise security) and about identity (anonymity, unlinkability).

2.1Confidentiality

Confidentiality keeps information from all but those authorised to see it[2]. The modern definition is demanding. Semantic security, introduced by Goldwasser and Micali, requires that a ciphertext reveals nothing about the plaintext that an efficient adversary could not already have guessed without it; equivalently, the adversary cannot tell which of two messages of its choice was encrypted (indistinguishability)[27]. One immediate consequence is that encryption must be randomised: if encrypting the same message twice produced the same ciphertext, an observer would learn when messages repeat.

Confidentiality usually protects content but not metadata: the length of a message, when it was sent, and between whom. Traffic analysis of metadata can be as revealing as content, which motivates padding schemes and anonymity systems (section 2.10).

2.2Integrity

Data integrity is the ability to detect unauthorised alteration of data, including insertion, deletion and substitution[2]. Cryptography cannot prevent an attacker from changing bits on a wire, but it can make every change detectable. Non-cryptographic checksums such as CRC-32 detect accidental errors but not deliberate ones, because an attacker can simply recompute the checksum. A keyed MAC such as HMAC[40] or the tag of an authenticated-encryption scheme cannot be recomputed without the key.

Encryption alone does not provide integrity. Stream ciphers and counter mode are malleable: flipping bit i of the ciphertext flips bit i of the plaintext. For this reason modern practice is to use authenticated encryption with associated data (AEAD), which provides confidentiality and integrity together[41,42].

python · s5_aesgcm.py# pip install cryptography

2.4Data-origin authentication

Data-origin authentication (message authentication) gives assurance of the identity of the party that originated a message. It implies data integrity: if a message were modified, it would no longer originate from the claimed sender[2]. It is provided by MACs when sender and receiver share a key, and by digital signatures when they do not.

python · s6_hmac.pyimport hmac, hashlib, secrets
key = secrets.token_bytes(32)
msg = b"amount=100&to=bob"
tag = hmac.new(key, msg, hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()
print("tag:", tag)
# Verify in constant time to avoid timing side channels
ok  = hmac.compare_digest(tag, hmac.new(key, msg, hashlib.sha256).hexdigest())
bad = hmac.compare_digest(tag, hmac.new(key, b"amount=900&to=eve", hashlib.sha256).hexdigest())
print("genuine:", ok, " forged:", bad)

Data-origin authentication says nothing about when the message was created. A genuine message recorded last week still verifies today, which is why freshness is a separate objective.

2.5Entity authentication

Entity authentication (identification) assures one party of the identity of another party who is actively participating at that moment[2]. The emphasis on liveness distinguishes it from data-origin authentication. Techniques, in rough order of strength, are[2,43]:

  • Something you know: passwords and PINs, which are weak because they can be guessed, phished and replayed.
  • Challenge–response: the verifier sends a fresh random challenge, and the prover returns a MAC or signature over it. This proves possession of the key without revealing it, and prevents replay.
  • Zero-knowledge identification: the prover convinces the verifier that it knows a secret while revealing nothing else about it.
  • Public-key authenticators such as FIDO2 security keys sign a challenge bound to the website's origin, which also defeats phishing.

2.6Non-repudiation

Non-repudiation prevents an entity from denying previous commitments or actions[2]. Its distinctive feature is that a third party, such as a judge, an auditor or a blockchain validator, must be able to resolve the dispute. Digital signatures are the core mechanism, because only the holder of the private key could have produced a valid signature, and anyone with the public key can check it[30,44]. MACs cannot provide non-repudiation, because both parties hold the same key.

python · s7_ed25519.pyfrom cryptography.hazmat.primitives.asymmetric.ed25519 import Ed25519PrivateKey

sk = Ed25519PrivateKey.generate()      # private signing key (kept secret)
pk = sk.public_key()                   # public verification key (shared)
doc = b"I, Alice, owe Bob 10 EUR."
sig = sk.sign(doc)
print("signature:", sig.hex()[:32], "...", len(sig), "bytes")

try:
    pk.verify(sig, b"I, Alice, owe Bob 1000 EUR.")

In practice cryptographic non-repudiation is only as strong as the surrounding processes. A signer can claim that the private key was stolen, so real systems add trusted timestamps, certificate revocation, secure key storage and legal frameworks[7]. Some protocols deliberately aim for the opposite property, deniability: Signal-style messaging authenticates messages to the recipient without producing evidence that could convince a third party[45].

2.7Freshness

Freshness assures that a message is recent and has not been seen before. Without it, an attacker can mount a replay attack: recording a valid message ("unlock the door", "transfer €100") and sending it again later. Three mechanisms provide freshness[2,43]:

  • Nonces: a random challenge chosen by the verifier, which the response must include.
  • Timestamps: valid only within a time window, which requires synchronised clocks.
  • Sequence numbers: monotonically increasing counters; TLS uses an implicit record sequence number in each record's nonce[21].

TLS 1.3 illustrates the trade-off. Its optional "0-RTT" mode lets a client send data in its very first flight for lower latency, but the standard warns explicitly that this early data is not protected against replay and must only be used for idempotent requests[21].

2.8Forward secrecy

Forward secrecy (often called perfect forward secrecy) means that compromising long-term keys does not compromise past session keys[2,46]. It is achieved with ephemeral key agreement: each session runs a fresh Diffie–Hellman exchange with throw-away key pairs, uses the long-term keys only to authenticate that exchange, and erases the ephemeral secrets afterwards. An attacker who recorded the traffic and later steals the server's private key still cannot derive the session keys.

This was a major design change in TLS 1.3. Earlier TLS versions allowed RSA key transport, in which the client encrypted the session secret under the server's long-term RSA key, so a single stolen key could decrypt years of recorded traffic. TLS 1.3 removed RSA key transport; all its handshakes provide forward secrecy except the pure pre-shared-key mode[21].

2.9Post-compromise security

Post-compromise security (PCS), also called future secrecy or self-healing, is the mirror image of forward secrecy: after a compromise, security is restored for future messages once the attacker loses access[47]. Cohn-Gordon, Cremers and Garratt formalised it in 2016 and showed that it requires each party to keep injecting fresh randomness into its state. The Signal protocol's Double Ratchet does this: every time the direction of conversation changes, the parties perform a new Diffie–Hellman exchange, so an attacker who copied the state once is locked out after the next round trip unless they remain active[45,47].

2.10Anonymity and unlinkability

Pfitzmann and Hansen's widely used terminology defines anonymity of a subject as not being identifiable within a set of subjects, the anonymity set, and unlinkability of two or more items (messages, actions, identities) as the attacker being unable to sufficiently distinguish whether they are related[48]. Related notions are pseudonymity, undetectability and unobservability.

Ordinary encryption does not provide anonymity, since IP addresses and timing still reveal who talks to whom. Dedicated techniques include:

  • Mix networks, proposed by David Chaum in 1981, which batch, re-order and re-encrypt messages so that inputs cannot be matched to outputs[49].
  • Onion routing, as used by Tor, in which traffic is wrapped in layers of encryption and relayed through three volunteer nodes, none of which knows both source and destination[50].
  • Blind signatures (Chaum, 1983), letting a bank sign a digital coin without seeing it, so that spending cannot be linked to withdrawal[51]. Group signatures, ring signatures and anonymous credentials are later developments.

2.11Availability and limitations of cryptography

Availability, ensuring that systems and data are accessible when needed, is one of the classic three goals of information security alongside confidentiality and integrity, but cryptography contributes little to it[7]. Encryption cannot stop someone from cutting a cable or flooding a server. Cryptography can even reduce availability: losing a key means losing the data, expensive public-key operations can be abused for denial-of-service, and ransomware is, in effect, cryptography used against the data owner. Protocols therefore add DoS-resistance mechanisms such as cookies and client puzzles.

More generally, cryptography is rarely the weakest link. Real-world failures typically come from[7,12]:

  • Implementation bugs: Heartbleed (2014), a missing bounds check in OpenSSL, leaked server memory, including private keys, from a large fraction of HTTPS servers without breaking any algorithm[52].
  • Side channels: timing, power and cache behaviour leak secrets from correct algorithms[11].
  • Bad randomness (section 3.7)[53,54].
  • Key management: keys left in source code, never rotated, or stored next to the data they protect[5].
  • Usability: the 1999 study "Why Johnny can't encrypt" found that only one-third of its twelve participants managed to sign and encrypt an email correctly with PGP within 90 minutes[55].
  • Endpoint compromise: end-to-end encryption does not help if malware reads the screen.

3Fundamental terminology

Precise vocabulary prevents real mistakes; confusing a nonce with a salt, or an IV with a key, has broken deployed systems. The definitions below follow the IETF security glossary (RFC 4949)[8], the Handbook of Applied Cryptography[2] and Katz and Lindell[4].

3.1Plaintext and ciphertext

Definitions

Plaintext (or cleartext) is the original, intelligible data input to encryption. Ciphertext is the encrypted, unintelligible output[8]. Strictly, "cleartext" sometimes means data that was never encrypted, while "plaintext" means the input to a cipher[8].

Modern ciphers operate on bytes, not letters, so the "plaintext" may be an image, a database page or another ciphertext. Ciphertext from a good cipher is indistinguishable from random bytes and is usually slightly longer than the plaintext, because it carries a nonce and an authentication tag.

3.2Encryption and decryption

Encryption (encipherment) is the transformation of plaintext into ciphertext under the control of a key; decryption (decipherment) is the inverse transformation using the corresponding key[8]. Formally, a (symmetric) encryption scheme is a triple of algorithms[4]:

  • Gen: a randomised algorithm that outputs a key k;
  • Enc: takes k and a message m and outputs a ciphertext c (usually randomised);
  • Dec: takes k and c and outputs m or an error,

such that Deck(Enck(m)) = m for every key and message (correctness). "Deciphering" or "decrypting" a message you have no key for is properly called cryptanalysis or breaking it.

3.3Keys and keyspace

A key is a parameter that determines the specific transformation performed by a cryptographic algorithm; without it, the algorithm's output cannot be reproduced or reversed[8]. Keys come in several kinds:

  • Secret (symmetric) keys, shared by all authorised parties.
  • Private/public key pairs in asymmetric cryptography: the private key is never shared, and the public key may be published.
  • Long-term versus session (ephemeral) keys, and key-encrypting versus data-encrypting keys in key hierarchies[5].

The keyspace is the set of all possible keys; its size bounds the cost of exhaustive search. A large keyspace is necessary but not sufficient: simple substitution has an enormous keyspace yet falls to frequency analysis in minutes (section 5.2).

3.4Ciphers, cryptosystems, cryptographic algorithms and protocols

Cipher
An algorithm for encryption and decryption[8]. Historically ciphers are divided into substitution ciphers (replace symbols) and transposition ciphers (rearrange them); modern block ciphers combine both in many rounds, following Shannon's principles of confusion and diffusion[24]. Block ciphers (AES) encrypt fixed-size blocks; stream ciphers (ChaCha20) generate a keystream that is XORed with the data[59,61]. A code, by contrast, replaces whole words or phrases using a codebook ("EAGLE" = "attack at dawn")[1].
Cryptosystem
The complete set of algorithms plus the spaces of plaintexts, ciphertexts and keys; for public-key systems, also key generation. RSA, for example, is a cryptosystem comprising key generation, encryption and decryption (and a signature variant)[2,31].
Cryptographic algorithm
A well-defined computational procedure that takes inputs, including possibly keys, and produces outputs: ciphers, hash functions, signature schemes, key-derivation functions and random-bit generators are all cryptographic algorithms[5,8].
Cryptographic protocol
A distributed algorithm defined by a sequence of steps that specify the actions of two or more entities to achieve a security objective[2]. TLS, SSH, Kerberos and Signal are protocols (section 1.6).

3.5Security parameters

In theoretical cryptography the security parameter (written n or λ) is an integer, typically the key length, that tunes security. Honest algorithms must run in time polynomial in λ, while every efficient adversary must succeed with only negligible probability in λ, meaning a probability that shrinks faster than any inverse polynomial[4]. In practice, standards speak of security strength: a scheme has s-bit strength if the best known attack costs about 2s operations[5].

3.7Randomness and entropy

Cryptography consumes randomness constantly: for keys, nonces, IVs, salts, signature values and padding. If an attacker can predict it, the cryptography fails no matter how strong the algorithm[67].

Entropy measures unpredictability in bits. For N equally likely values it is log2 N; a uniformly random 128-bit key has 128 bits of entropy. Standards use the more conservative min-entropy, determined by the single most likely value, because an attacker will guess the most likely value first[68].

Real systems combine two components[68,69]:

  • An entropy source (true random number generator) that samples a physical noise process such as thermal noise, oscillator jitter or interrupt timing, and is health-tested;
  • A deterministic random bit generator (DRBG), also called a cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator (CSPRNG), that expands a seed from the entropy source into a large amount of output computationally indistinguishable from random.

Application code should use the operating system's CSPRNG (getrandom(), /dev/urandom, BCryptGenRandom, or language wrappers such as Python's secrets), never a general-purpose generator like random or Math.random().

4Types of cryptography

Cryptographic techniques are usually classified by how keys are used (symmetric, asymmetric, keyless), by how they are combined (hybrid, threshold) and by the threat they are designed to resist (post-quantum) or the physics they rely on (quantum).

4.1Symmetric-key cryptography

In symmetric-key (secret-key) cryptography, the same key, or keys trivially derivable from each other, is used to encrypt and decrypt, or to create and verify a MAC[2]. Symmetric algorithms are fast, often several gigabytes per second per core with hardware AES instructions, and have compact keys. They are the workhorses that protect virtually all bulk data.

  • Block ciphers: AES (Rijndael) has a 128-bit block and 128-, 192- or 256-bit keys[59,70]. A block cipher must be used in a mode of operation, such as CTR, CBC or, preferably, an AEAD mode such as GCM[20,64].
  • Stream ciphers: ChaCha20, combined with the Poly1305 authenticator, is the main alternative to AES-GCM, particularly on devices without AES hardware[61].
  • MACs: HMAC[40], GMAC and Poly1305.

The fundamental limitation is key distribution. Both parties must already share a secret, and in a network of n users who all want to talk pairwise, n(n−1)/2 keys are needed, which is almost 500 000 keys for 1000 users. This problem motivated public-key cryptography[30].

4.2Asymmetric-key (public-key) cryptography

In asymmetric cryptography each party has a key pair: a public key that may be published and a private key kept secret, related so that it is computationally infeasible to derive the private key from the public one[2,30]. Diffie and Hellman's 1976 paper introduced the idea in the open literature, along with two applications[30]:

  • Key agreement: two parties exchange public values over an open channel and derive a shared secret that an eavesdropper cannot compute (Diffie–Hellman; today usually X25519 or ECDH on NIST curves[71]).
  • Digital signatures: sign with the private key, and anyone verifies with the public key (RSA, ECDSA and EdDSA in FIPS 186-5[44]; ML-DSA and SLH-DSA post-quantum[72,73]).

Public-key encryption and key encapsulation mechanisms (KEMs) complete the toolkit: RSA-OAEP, ECIES-style constructions and ML-KEM[31,62]. Elliptic-curve cryptography, proposed independently by Koblitz and Miller in 1985, gives the same security as RSA with far smaller keys[74,75].

Public-key operations are orders of magnitude slower than symmetric ones and have size overheads, so they are used to establish keys and sign, rarely to encrypt bulk data. They also move the problem from secrecy of keys to authenticity of public keys: if Mallory can substitute her public key for Bob's, she can read Alice's messages. Certificates and PKI exist to solve this (section 1.9)[39].

4.3Hash-based cryptography

The term is used in two senses.

1. Cryptography built from hash functions. Hash functions are "keyless" primitives[2] that underpin MACs (HMAC), key derivation (HKDF), password hashing, commitments, Merkle trees, blockchains and pseudorandom generators. NIST's approved families are SHA-2 (SHA-224 to SHA-512)[14] and SHA-3 (Keccak), with the extendable-output functions SHAKE128 and SHAKE256[15].

2. Hash-based digital signatures. Lamport showed in 1979 that a secure one-time signature can be built from any one-way function: publish hashes of random secrets, and reveal one secret of each pair according to the bits of the message hash[76]. Merkle then showed how to authenticate many one-time public keys with a single root hash using a binary hash tree[77]. Because security depends only on properties of the hash function, these schemes are regarded as the most conservative post-quantum signatures[78].

Stateful versus stateless

XMSS and LMS are stateful: the signer must never use the same one-time key twice, so the index must be reliably updated, even across backups and crashes. NIST therefore restricts them to settings such as firmware signing in hardware modules[81]. SLH-DSA avoids state by using a huge hypertree and pseudorandom index selection, at the cost of larger signatures (about 7.8–50 kB)[73].

4.4Hybrid cryptography

Hybrid encryption combines the convenience of public-key cryptography with the speed of symmetric cryptography: a public-key mechanism establishes a fresh symmetric key, which then encrypts the data with an AEAD[4,82]. This "KEM/DEM" pattern (key-encapsulation mechanism plus data-encapsulation mechanism) is used by TLS, PGP, S/MIME, and IETF Hybrid Public Key Encryption (HPKE, RFC 9180)[21,82].

python · s8_hybrid.py# Hybrid encryption: ephemeral X25519 key agreement + HKDF + AES-GCM (an ECIES/HPKE-like pattern)
import os
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.asymmetric.x25519 import X25519PrivateKey
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.kdf.hkdf import HKDF
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives import hashes, serialization
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.ciphers.aead import AESGCM

def raw(pub):
    return pub.public_bytes(serialization.Encoding.Raw, serialization.PublicFormat.Raw)

def kdf(shared, info):
    return HKDF(algorithm=hashes.SHA256(), length=32, salt=None, info=info).derive(shared)

# Bob's long-term key pair
bob_sk = X25519PrivateKey.generate(); bob_pk = bob_sk.public_key()

# --- Alice encrypts to Bob ---
eph = X25519PrivateKey.generate()                      # fresh per message
k = kdf(eph.exchange(bob_pk), b"demo hybrid v1")       # asymmetric part -> symmetric key
nonce = os.urandom(12)
ct = AESGCM(k).encrypt(nonce, b"bulk data, any length", None)
packet = (raw(eph.public_key()), nonce, ct)

# --- Bob decrypts ---
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.asymmetric.x25519 import X25519PublicKey
epk, n, c = packet
k2 = kdf(bob_sk.exchange(X25519PublicKey.from_public_bytes(epk)), b"demo hybrid v1")
print(AESGCM(k2).decrypt(n, c, None))

During the post-quantum migration, "hybrid" has acquired a second meaning: combining a classical and a post-quantum algorithm, for example X25519 together with ML-KEM-768 in a TLS key exchange, so that the result stays secure if either component holds[38]. This hedges against both quantum computers and undiscovered weaknesses in the newer algorithms.

4.5Threshold cryptography

Threshold cryptography distributes a secret or a cryptographic capability among n parties so that any t of them can act together but fewer than t learn nothing and can do nothing[83]. It removes single points of failure and compromise.

  • Secret sharing: in 1979 Shamir and Blakley independently proposed (t, n) threshold schemes[25,84]. Shamir's scheme hides the secret as the constant term of a random polynomial of degree t−1 and gives each party one point; any t points determine the polynomial, while t−1 reveal nothing at all, which is information-theoretic security.
  • Threshold cryptosystems: Desmedt and Frankel showed how to decrypt or sign without ever reconstructing the key in one place[85]. Modern examples include threshold Schnorr signatures (FROST, RFC 9591)[86] used for cryptocurrency custody and certificate-authority keys.
  • NIST runs a multi-party threshold cryptography project to standardise such schemes[83].
python · s10_shamir.py# Toy Shamir (t, n) secret sharing over a prime field -- for learning only
import secrets
P = 2**127 - 1                                   # a Mersenne prime

def split(secret, t, n):
    coeffs = [secret] + [secrets.randbelow(P) for _ in range(t - 1)]
    f = lambda x: sum(c * pow(x, i, P) for i, c in enumerate(coeffs)) % P
    return [(x, f(x)) for x in range(1, n + 1)]

def combine(shares):                             # Lagrange interpolation at x = 0
    s = 0
    for j, (xj, yj) in enumerate(shares):
        num = den = 1
        for m, (xm, _) in enumerate(shares):
            if m != j:
                num = num * (-xm) % P
                den = den * (xj - xm) % P
        s = (s + yj * num * pow(den, -1, P)) % P
    return s

shares = split(123456789, t=3, n=5)
print("any 3 shares :", combine(shares[:3]), combine([shares[0], shares[2], shares[4]]))
print("only 2 shares:", combine(shares[:2]), "(meaningless)")

4.6Post-quantum cryptography

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) means classical algorithms, running on ordinary computers, that are believed to resist attack by both classical and quantum computers[38,78]. The motivation is Shor's algorithm, which on a large fault-tolerant quantum computer would factor integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time, breaking RSA, Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography entirely[34]. Symmetric cryptography is affected much less: Grover's search algorithm gives at most a quadratic speed-up[60], and in practice its poor parallelism makes AES-128 far harder to attack than the naive "64-bit" figure suggests; AES-256 is considered comfortable[38].

Even though no cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists yet, the migration is urgent. Mosca's inequality states it: if the time data must stay secret (x) plus the time needed to migrate (y) exceeds the time until a quantum computer arrives (z), data is already at risk[37]. Adversaries can harvest encrypted traffic now and decrypt it later.

4.7Quantum cryptography

Quantum cryptography uses quantum physics itself to achieve security goals; it is not the same thing as post-quantum cryptography. Its best-known application is quantum key distribution (QKD). In the BB84 protocol of Bennett and Brassard (1984), single photons are sent in randomly chosen polarisation bases; the no-cloning theorem and the disturbance caused by measurement mean that an eavesdropper unavoidably introduces detectable errors[90]. Ekert's 1991 protocol achieves the same using entangled pairs and Bell's inequality[91]. The idea goes back to Stephen Wiesner's "conjugate coding", written around 1970 but published only in 1983[92].

Post-quantum cryptography

Mathematics; runs on existing hardware and networks; security is computational; drop-in replacement for RSA/ECC; standardised by NIST in 2024[62].

Quantum cryptography (QKD)

Physics; needs dedicated optical hardware and links; security is information-theoretic given an authenticated classical channel; limited distance without trusted relays[90,93].

QKD has been demonstrated over fibre networks and from the Micius satellite to ground stations more than 1000 km away[94]. Agencies including the US National Security Agency nonetheless do not recommend it for national security systems. Their reasons are that it only solves key distribution, still needs classical authentication, requires special-purpose hardware, often relies on trusted relay nodes, and that practical implementations have been attacked through hardware imperfections[93].

5Historical development

David Kahn's The Codebreakers[1] remains the standard general history; Simon Singh's The Code Book[57] is a more accessible account. The story is a long contest between code-makers and code-breakers, in which each advance in one provoked an advance in the other.

5.1Ancient cryptography

  • Egypt, around 1900 BCE. In the tomb of the nobleman Khnumhotep II, a scribe used unusual hieroglyphic substitutions. Kahn regards this as the earliest known deliberate transformation of writing, although the aim was probably to lend dignity or mystery rather than secrecy[1].
  • Mesopotamia, around 1500 BCE. A small cuneiform tablet records a recipe for pottery glaze in deliberately obscured writing, an early example of protecting trade secrets[1].
  • Hebrew scribes used Atbash, a reversed-alphabet substitution (first letter ↔ last); "Sheshach" in the Book of Jeremiah is Atbash for "Babel"[1].
  • Sparta: the scytale, a strip of leather wound around a staff of fixed diameter, is the classic example of a transposition cipher. The historian Thomas Kelly has argued that the ancient sources do not clearly support its use for secrecy, rather than as a message token[95].
  • Greece: Polybius described a 5 × 5 grid for signalling letters by pairs of torches; the Polybius square later became a component of many ciphers, including ADFGVX[1].
  • Rome: Suetonius records that Julius Caesar wrote confidential letters by replacing each letter with the one three places further on in the alphabet: the Caesar cipher[1].
  • India: the Kama Sutra lists secret writing (mlecchita-vikalpa) among the 64 arts recommended for women[1].

5.2Medieval cryptanalysis

The decisive conceptual leap, from making ciphers to systematically breaking them, came in the Islamic world. The philosopher Abu Yusuf Ya'qub al-Kindi (c. 801–873) wrote A Manuscript on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages, which describes frequency analysis: count how often each symbol appears in the ciphertext, and match the most common symbols to the most common letters of the language. The manuscript was rediscovered in an Istanbul archive in the twentieth century and published by Ibrahim al-Kadi and colleagues; it is the oldest known description of cryptanalysis[56,57]. Later Arab scholars, including Ibn ad-Durayhim (1312–1361), extended the work, and their methods were preserved in al-Qalqashandi's fourteen-volume encyclopaedia of 1412[1,56].

Frequency analysis destroys every monoalphabetic substitution, however large its keyspace, because the cipher preserves the statistics of the language. In English, E, T, A, O, I and N are the most common letters, and Q, X and Z the rarest.

5.3Renaissance cryptography

In Renaissance Europe, rival Italian city-states, the papacy and royal courts maintained permanent cipher secretaries, and diplomatic correspondence relied on nomenclators, which combined a substitution alphabet with a codebook of names and words[1]. Key advances:

  • Leon Battista Alberti (c. 1466–67) described a cipher disk of two concentric rotating alphabets and the idea of changing the alignment during a message. This was the first polyalphabetic cipher, which Kahn considers the reason to call Alberti the "Father of Western Cryptology"[1].
  • Johannes Trithemius' Polygraphia (1518), the first printed book on cryptography, introduced the tabula recta, a square table of shifted alphabets[1].
  • Giovan Battista Bellaso (1553) proposed using a memorable keyword to select alphabets. The scheme is now misattributed to Blaise de Vigenère, whose 1586 Traicté des chiffres actually described a stronger autokey cipher[1]. The "Vigenère cipher" was called le chiffre indéchiffrable and resisted general solution for about three centuries[57].
  • Mary, Queen of Scots used a nomenclator in the 1586 Babington plot; Elizabeth I's cryptanalyst Thomas Phelippes broke it, and the decrypted letters helped convict her. She was executed in 1587[57].
  • In seventeenth-century France, Antoine and Bonaventure Rossignol devised Louis XIV's Great Cipher, a syllabic nomenclator that stayed unbroken until Étienne Bazeries solved it in the 1890s[1,57].

Try it: Vigenère cipher

With keyword LEMON, "ATTACK AT DAWN" becomes "LXFOPV EF RNHR". The four A's encrypt to four different letters (L, O, E and N), which flattens the frequency distribution. The weakness is the repeating key: Babbage (c. 1854, unpublished) and Kasiski (1863) found the key length from repeated ciphertext fragments, which reduces the problem to several Caesar ciphers[1,57].

5.4Mechanical cryptography

The telegraph (1840s) and radio (1890s) made enormous volumes of messages interceptable, and machines were needed to encipher them quickly[1].

  • Wheel ciphers: Thomas Jefferson described a cylinder of 26 lettered wheels in the 1790s; a similar design was adopted as the US Army M-94 in 1922[1].
  • The Playfair cipher (Charles Wheatstone, 1854), which encrypts pairs of letters, was used by the British in the Boer War and the First World War[57].
  • The one-time pad. The banker Frank Miller published a one-time-pad scheme for telegraph codes in 1882, decades before it was reinvented[96]. In 1917 AT&T engineer Gilbert Vernam built a teleprinter that XORed message tape with key tape[97]; Joseph Mauborgne recognised that the key must be random and never reused[1].
  • Rotor machines: around 1917–1919 Edward Hebern (USA), Arthur Scherbius (Germany, patent filed 1918), Hugo Koch (Netherlands) and Arvid Damm (Sweden) independently invented machines that pass each letter through rotating wired wheels, changing the substitution at every key press[1]. Scherbius's machine was marketed commercially as the Enigma from 1923 and adopted by the German navy in 1926 and the army in 1928[1,57].

5.5World War I and World War II cryptography

World War I

  • At Tannenberg in August 1914, Russian armies transmitted orders by radio in clear, because their cipher distribution had failed, and German forces read them[1].
  • The British Admiralty's Room 40 decrypted the Zimmermann telegram (January 1917), in which Germany proposed an alliance with Mexico against the United States. Its publication in March 1917 helped bring the US into the war the following month[1].
  • French cryptanalyst Georges Painvin broke the German ADFGVX field cipher, which combined a Polybius-square substitution with a transposition, in June 1918, helping to anticipate a German offensive[1].

World War II

  • Poland: in December 1932 Marian Rejewski used permutation theory and intelligence material to reconstruct the wiring of the military Enigma's rotors. With Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski he developed methods and the electromechanical bomba, and in July 1939, weeks before the invasion, the Poles gave their results to British and French intelligence[98].
  • Bletchley Park: Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman designed the British bombe, first operational in 1940, which searched for Enigma settings consistent with a crib[57,99]. For the Lorenz SZ40/42 teleprinter cipher ("Tunny"), Bill Tutte deduced the machine's logical structure without ever seeing one, and Tommy Flowers built Colossus, an electronic programmable computer using around 1600–2400 valves, which went into operation in early 1944[99].
  • United States: William Friedman's Signal Intelligence Service team, with key insight from Genevieve Grotjan, broke the Japanese diplomatic machine Purple in 1940. Navy cryptanalysts' partial reading of the naval code JN-25 contributed decisively to victory at Midway in June 1942[1].
  • The US Marine Corps used Navajo code talkers, a spoken code based on the Navajo language, for rapid tactical voice communication in the Pacific[1].
  • Allied machines such as the American SIGABA are not known to have been broken during the war[1].

Notably, the Enigma was not broken because of its keyspace, which was astronomically large for its day[57], but because of its structure (no letter enciphers to itself), predictable messages that supplied cribs, and procedural mistakes by operators.

5.6Shannon's information-theoretic foundations

Claude Shannon wrote a classified Bell Labs memorandum, "A Mathematical Theory of Cryptography", in 1945; a declassified version appeared in 1949 as "Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems"[24]. It turned cryptography from a craft into a science. It:

  • Modelled a secrecy system mathematically, as in Figure 4, with the explicit assumption that the enemy knows the system;
  • Defined perfect secrecy and proved that the one-time pad achieves it and that perfect secrecy requires at least as much key as message;
  • Introduced the unicity distance: the amount of ciphertext beyond which only one key gives a meaningful decryption. For simple substitution of English it is only a few dozen letters;
  • Named confusion (making the relationship between key and ciphertext complex) and diffusion (spreading the influence of each plaintext bit over many ciphertext bits), and proposed building strong ciphers as product ciphers that alternate simple substitutions and transpositions. This is the blueprint of every modern block cipher[24,100].

5.7Development of computer cryptography

By the late 1960s banks and companies needed to protect computerised data, but almost all cryptographic expertise was inside government agencies.

  • At IBM, Horst Feistel designed Lucifer and the Feistel network structure, popularised in his 1973 Scientific American article[100].
  • The US National Bureau of Standards solicited a public standard. A modified Lucifer became the Data Encryption Standard (DES), published as FIPS 46 in January 1977, with a 64-bit block and a 56-bit key[101]. Diffie and Hellman immediately warned that 56 bits was too short, estimating that a $20 million machine could find a key in about a day[102].
  • Public research then created modern cryptanalysis: differential cryptanalysis (Biham and Shamir, 1990–91)[103] and linear cryptanalysis (Matsui, 1993)[104].
  • In 1998 the Electronic Frontier Foundation's "Deep Crack" machine, built for under $250 000, recovered a DES key in 56 hours[58]. Triple DES served as a stop-gap until AES.

5.8The public-key revolution

For millennia, secure communication required a secret key shared in advance. Three papers changed that:

  • Ralph Merkle devised "Merkle's puzzles" as a student project in 1974, the first published scheme for key agreement over an open channel; the paper appeared in 1978[105].
  • Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, "New Directions in Cryptography" (1976), opened with "We stand today on the brink of a revolution in cryptography." It introduced public-key cryptosystems, digital signatures and Diffie–Hellman key exchange[30].
  • Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman (1977, published 1978) gave the first practical public-key encryption and signature scheme, RSA, based on the difficulty of factoring[31]. A challenge cipher published by Martin Gardner in 1977, using a 129-digit modulus, was finally solved in 1994 by a distributed computation of some 600 volunteers; the plaintext was "The magic words are squeamish ossifrage"[106].

It later emerged that James Ellis (1970), Clifford Cocks (1973, an RSA-like scheme) and Malcolm Williamson (1974, a Diffie–Hellman-like scheme) at the British GCHQ had made the same discoveries in secret; this was made public in 1997[57,107]. Diffie and Hellman received the 2015 ACM A.M. Turing Award, and Rivest, Shamir and Adleman the 2002 award[108]. ElGamal (1985) and elliptic-curve cryptography (1985) followed[74,75,109], and Phil Zimmermann's PGP (1991) put strong public-key encryption in the hands of the public, triggering the 1990s "crypto wars" over export controls[57].

5.9Modern standardised cryptography

From the late 1990s, standards have been chosen by open international competitions with public cryptanalysis:

  • AES: NIST announced the competition in 1997 and received 15 candidates. The five finalists were MARS, RC6, Rijndael, Serpent and Twofish. Rijndael, by Belgian cryptographers Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen, was selected in October 2000 and published as FIPS 197 in November 2001[59,110].
  • Hash functions: MD5 collisions (2004–2005) and the first practical SHA-1 collision, "SHAttered" (2017)[16,17], pushed the move to SHA-2. NIST's SHA-3 competition (2007–2012) selected Keccak, standardised as FIPS 202 in 2015[15,111].
  • Signatures and curves: FIPS 186-5 (2023) added EdDSA (Ed25519) alongside RSA and ECDSA[44]; X25519 is standardised in RFC 7748[71].
  • Protocols: TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446, 2018) removed static RSA key transport, CBC-mode ciphers and other legacy options, and mandates AEAD and forward secrecy for certificate-based handshakes[21].
  • Lessons in trust: the withdrawal of Dual_EC_DRBG in 2015 showed that standards themselves must be scrutinised[69].

5.10The post-quantum transition

  • 1994: Peter Shor publishes polynomial-time quantum algorithms for factoring and discrete logarithms[34]; Grover's search algorithm follows in 1996[60].
  • 2016: NIST calls for post-quantum proposals; 82 submissions are received, of which 69 are accepted for the first round in 2017[112].
  • July 2022: NIST selects CRYSTALS-Kyber, CRYSTALS-Dilithium, Falcon and SPHINCS+ for standardisation[112]. In the same year two candidates, Rainbow and SIKE, are broken[88,89]. The NSA announces its CNSA 2.0 suite[113].
  • 13 August 2024: NIST publishes FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA)[62,72,73].
  • March 2025: NIST selects HQC as a code-based backup KEM to ML-KEM, with a final standard expected in 2027[87].
  • April 2025: OpenSSL 3.5, a long-term-support release, ships ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA[114].
  • October 2026: NIST IR 8547, Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards, is finalised. It deprecates quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms (RSA, ECDSA, EdDSA, finite-field and elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman) after 2030 and disallows them after 2035; FN-DSA (Falcon) is still being developed as FIPS 206[38].

The practical challenges are an inventory of where cryptography is used, crypto-agility (being able to swap algorithms without redesigning systems), larger keys and signatures in constrained protocols, and hybrid deployments during the transition[38].
