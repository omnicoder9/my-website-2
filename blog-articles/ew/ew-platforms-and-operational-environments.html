How electronic warfare is carried into the air, at sea, on land, and in space, and how urban terrain, contested logistics, borders, critical infrastructure, and unmanned systems change what EW must do.

1Introduction and scope

US joint doctrine defines electromagnetic warfare (EW) as military action that uses electromagnetic and directed energy to control the electromagnetic spectrum (EMS) or to attack an enemy, and divides it into electromagnetic attack (EA), electromagnetic support (ES), and electromagnetic protection (EP)[1,2]. A Congressional Research Service (CRS) primer summarises the same three divisions as protecting friendly access to the spectrum, degrading or denying enemy use of it, and identifying and cataloguing emissions[3]. This article keeps the older and still common names electronic attack, electronic support, and electronic protection, as the companion articles in this series do.

Those three functions are the same everywhere, but the platform that carries them, and the environment it works in, decide almost everything about how they are engineered. Altitude sets radio line of sight, and so the range at which an aircraft can hear or jam. A ship has abundant power and space but cannot hide its radar cross-section. A ground vehicle is close to its targets but is masked by terrain. A satellite sees a whole hemisphere but has a fixed orbit, a limited power budget, and links that an adversary on the ground can reach[4,5,6]. The CRS primer groups US capabilities in the same way, into airborne, ground and terrestrial, naval, and space systems[3].

Sections 2 to 5 cover the four platform domains: airborne, naval, ground-based, and space. Section 6 covers five operational environments that cut across them: urban operations, contested logistics, border monitoring, critical infrastructure protection, and unmanned or autonomous systems. Section 7 compares them. Named systems are described only at the level of their public fact sheets, test reports, and official releases. The article gives no performance parameters beyond what those sources publish.

Terminology

The 2020 edition of JP 3-85 renamed "electronic warfare" as "electromagnetic warfare" and placed it within joint electromagnetic spectrum operations (JEMSO)[1]. The acronyms EA, ES, and EP are unchanged, and the platform literature cited here still mostly says "electronic."

2Airborne EW

Height is the airborne platform's main advantage. Radio line of sight grows with the square root of antenna height, so an aircraft at altitude can receive emitters, and project jamming, far beyond the horizon of a ground or ship sensor[4]. The cost is that aircraft are limited in size, weight, power, and cooling, and are themselves high-value targets for radar-guided weapons. Airborne EW therefore splits into four roles: dedicated EW aircraft that attack or exploit the spectrum on behalf of a force, self-protection suites that keep each combat aircraft alive, airborne early warning aircraft whose radars must work under jamming, and unmanned aircraft that carry EW payloads forward at lower risk.

Dedicated electronic warfare aircraft

Airborne electronic attack. The US Navy's EA-18G Growler is a variant of the F/A-18E/F Super Hornet with a dedicated EW suite. Its mission is airborne electronic attack, and it keeps the multi-role capability of the fighter it is derived from. The Navy's fact file lists an ALQ-218 receiver, ALQ-99 tactical jamming pods, an ALQ-227 communications countermeasures set, and an APG-79 active electronically scanned array (AESA) radar. It also names the AN/ALQ-249 Next Generation Jammer as the ALQ-99's successor. The Growler replaced the EA-6B Prowler, the US Navy is its only Department of Defense (DoD) operator, and the Royal Australian Air Force also flies it[7]. The Next Generation Jammer Mid-Band (NGJ-MB) pod reached initial operational capability in December 2024, and Naval Air Systems Command described it as a large gain in power, target flexibility, and jamming techniques over legacy systems[8]. The design pattern is a fighter-class airframe that can escort strike packages, a wideband receiver to find and identify threat emitters, and podded jammers that can be replaced as threats change.

Wide-area communications attack. The US Air Force's Compass Call takes a different approach. Air Combat Command's 2023 fact sheet describes the EC-37B, a modified Gulfstream G550, as a wide-area airborne electronic attack aircraft that disrupts adversary communications, information processing, navigation, radar, and radio-controlled threats. It carries two pilots and up to seven mission crew, including electronic warfare officers and cryptologic linguists. Its open "plug-and-play" architecture and spiral upgrades are intended to keep pace with new communications technology. Up to ten aircraft are planned to replace the EC-130H[9]. In October 2023 the aircraft was redesignated EA-37B, with the "A" (attack) prefix chosen to reflect its mission[10]. Business-jet platforms trade the payload of a transport aircraft for higher altitude and speed, and therefore a larger radio horizon.

Airborne electronic support. Not every EW aircraft jams. The RC-135V/W Rivet Joint is a heavily modified C-135 whose sensors detect, identify, and geolocate signals across the spectrum. Its crew of more than 30 includes electronic warfare officers and intelligence operators, and it passes intelligence in near real time to theatre and national users[11]. Platforms of this kind supply the threat libraries and electronic order of battle that the attack and self-protection systems below depend on. The companion article on electronic support and SIGINT covers the receiving and geolocation techniques in detail.

Fighter aircraft self-protection systems

A self-protection suite has to work automatically, inside a few seconds, against threats that are already tracking the aircraft. The textbook architecture has four parts. A radar warning receiver (RWR) detects and identifies threat radars and warns the crew. Missile and laser warning sensors detect weapons in flight. An onboard or towed jammer uses deception and noise techniques against tracking radars and missile seekers. A dispenser releases chaff against radar guidance and flares against infrared guidance[4,5]. These functions share sensors, timing, and a mission data file of threat parameters. They are increasingly integrated, so that warning and response are a single processing chain rather than separate boxes.

Three current US suites show the trend. The AN/ALQ-214 Integrated Defensive Electronic Countermeasures (IDECM) system protects F/A-18C/D/E/F aircraft against radio-frequency (RF) guided threats. It identifies, prioritises, and counters each threat autonomously, uses techniques intended to deny, disrupt, delay, and degrade a threat's launch and engagement sequence, and is reprogrammable for each theatre[12]. The F-15's Eagle Passive Active Warning Survivability System (EPAWSS) replaces three separate legacy units: the ALR-56C radar warning receiver, the ALQ-135 internal countermeasures set, and the ALE-45 dispenser. Operational testers describe it as scanning the RF environment, giving the crew threat identification and location, and responding with jamming or expendables[13]. On the F-35, the AN/ASQ-239 combines radar warning, targeting support, and RF and infrared countermeasures into one system with 360-degree situational awareness[14]. In all three, the receiver that protects the aircraft also feeds targeting and situational awareness. Self-protection and electronic support converge in the same hardware.

Airborne early warning

Airborne early warning and control (AEW&C) aircraft lift a surveillance radar high enough to see low-flying aircraft and ships beyond the horizon of surface radars. Their relevance to EW is twofold. They are prime targets for jamming, because degrading them blinds the whole air picture. They also carry passive receivers that contribute to electronic support.

  • The US Air Force's E-3 Sentry carries a rotodome 30 feet in diameter. Its radar range is given as more than 250 miles, and its look-down capability, combined with identification friend or foe (IFF), lets it track low-flying aircraft by rejecting ground clutter. The fact sheet lists passive detection among its sensors, able to "detect, locate and ID emitters," and notes that the Radar System Improvement Program strengthened the radar's electronic counter-countermeasures[15].
  • The US Navy's carrier-based E-2D Advanced Hawkeye is a tactical battle-management AEW and command-and-control aircraft. NAVAIR credits its radar with a "two-generation leap in capability" and better performance over land and in littoral waters[16].
  • The Royal Australian Air Force's E-7A Wedgetail uses a multi-role electronically scanned array (MESA) radar with a range beyond 400 km. It tracks air and maritime targets at the same time and carries self-protection measures against electronic threats[17].

The move from mechanically rotated antennas to electronically scanned arrays matters for EW. A phased array can dwell, change waveform, and place low sidelobes or nulls under software control. These are the electronic protection measures described in the companion EP and radar articles[5].

Unmanned aircraft systems

Unmanned aircraft change the EW problem in both directions. As EW platforms, they can carry jammers and receivers closer to the target, and for longer, than a crewed aircraft can at acceptable risk. The CRS primer noted Army plans for long-endurance unmanned airborne EW systems[3]. The Army's Multi-Function Electronic Warfare-Air Large (MFEW-AL) pod, shown on the MQ-1C Gray Eagle, was planned as the first brigade-level airborne electronic attack asset, offering jamming, electronic support and sensing, and limited cyber effects delivered over RF. Officials describe it as platform-agnostic, needing only power and an Ethernet connection[18].

As EW targets, unmanned aircraft depend on two links that are easy to reach: the command-and-control and video datalink to the operator, and satellite navigation. A 2012 demonstration at White Sands Missile Range, run by the University of Texas Radionavigation Laboratory at the invitation of the Department of Homeland Security, showed that a civil GPS spoofer could capture a small unmanned aircraft's GPS tracking loops and induce false position and velocity, driving the aircraft downward[19]. Section 6 returns to unmanned systems as an operational environment in their own right.

3Naval EW

A warship is a large, slow, radar-reflective target that carries a great deal of power, antenna space, and processing. Naval EW is therefore dominated by one threat, the radar-guided anti-ship missile, and by one sequence: detect the missile's search and seeker emissions early, deny or deceive its targeting, and seduce or distract the seeker in the final seconds. It also has to protect the ship's own communications and navigation without giving its position away[5,4].

Shipboard radar warning and emitter detection

The US Navy's main surface EW system is the AN/SLQ-32(V), which is being upgraded in increments under the Surface Electronic Warfare Improvement Program (SEWIP). Block 1 addressed obsolescence and added special signal intercept receivers to improve anti-ship missile defence, counter-targeting, and counter-surveillance. Block 2 upgraded the electronic support antenna, receiver, and open combat-system interface to improve detection and accuracy, and entered full-rate production in September 2016. Block 3 adds an active electronic attack capability common to surface combatants. Block 4 is planned to add electro-optical and infrared capabilities. In 2016 there were 234 systems in six variants, on 126 US Navy ships, 17 US Coast Guard cutters, and 91 foreign military sales transfers to 12 countries[20].

The engineering logic follows the threat sequence. A wideband intercept receiver must detect a missile's seeker emissions early enough to react, sort them from the dense emitter environment of a task group and a busy coastline, and pass bearing and identity to the combat system. That is the electronic support layer. The electronic attack layer then jams or deceives targeting radars and seekers. The decoy layer, below, provides an alternative target[5].

Electronic decoys

Decoys exploit the fact that a seeker can lock on only one target at a time. Two families are in US service:

  • Passive expendables. The MK 36 Super Rapid Blooming Offboard Chaff (SRBOC) system is a deck-mounted launcher. It fires chaff cartridges to create false radar returns against RF-guided missiles and radars, and infrared decoys against heat-seeking missiles, mainly in the terminal-defence phase[21].
  • Active offboard decoys. The MK 53 Nulka decoy launching system is a rapid-response active expendable decoy against radar-guided anti-ship missiles. Once launched, the decoy radiates a signature resembling a large ship and flies a trajectory that draws the missile away from the real one. Australia developed the hovering rocket and the United States the electronic payload. Nulka is an Aboriginal word meaning "be quick." By 2018 it was installed on more than 122 US ships and on Royal Australian Navy vessels[22].

Chaff and active decoys complement each other. Chaff is cheap and fills a volume with returns. An active decoy can reproduce a ship's signature more convincingly and move away from the ship, so the seeker is drawn clear of the target rather than merely confused[5].

Protection of maritime communications and sensors

The maritime domain depends heavily on two signals that are easy to interfere with: satellite navigation and the Automatic Identification System (AIS). The US Maritime Administration has reported significant GPS interference worldwide in the maritime domain. Lost or inaccurate GPS affects bridge navigation, GPS-based timing, and communications equipment, including satellite communications. AIS is an open, unencrypted VHF-FM system whose data can be spoofed. The advisory tells mariners never to rely on AIS alone for collision avoidance or navigation, and to report interference to the US Coast Guard Navigation Center[23]. GNSS signals reach the receiver at very low power, which is why jamming is easy and spoofing is possible. Receiver-level defences against both are reviewed in the GNSS literature[24,25].

For warships, protection also means emission control (EMCON): limiting radar and radio transmissions in time, power, direction, and frequency so that the adversary's electronic support cannot detect and locate the ship. The CRS primer describes the related Marine Corps concept of signature management as limiting emissions so that adversaries have less precise information about friendly locations[3]. The usual tools are directional and low-probability-of-intercept links, passive sensing in place of active radar, and backup navigation by radar fixes and dead reckoning, which the MARAD advisory also recommends[23,4].

4Ground-based EW

Ground EW works close to its targets but under the worst propagation conditions. Terrain and buildings mask line of sight, so ground systems have short ranges, must be mobile, and must be numerous. They also operate in the most congested part of the spectrum, among friendly radios, enemy radios, and civilian networks. Army doctrine places EW alongside cyberspace operations and spectrum management within cyberspace and electromagnetic activities at each echelon[2].

Mobile electronic support systems

Tactical electronic support detects, identifies, and locates enemy emitters to provide warning and targeting. The basic tasks are search, intercept, direction finding, and emitter location, usually by triangulating bearings from several dispersed receivers[26]. The US Army's Terrestrial Layer System–Brigade Combat Team (TLS-BCT) Manpack shows how this is now packaged. It is a configurable dismounted system for RF survey, signals collection and direction finding, electromagnetic attack and force protection, and spectrum visualisation. The Army describes it as its first tailorable, modular terrestrial capability that integrates signals intelligence and EW collection, processing, exploitation, reporting, and effects within one team. It was fielded to the first units in summer 2024, with plans to equip up to two brigade combat teams a month[27].

Integrating ES with SIGINT and EA in one team shortens the loop from detection to effect. It also raises the electronic protection problem. A receiver team that transmits, or that is co-located with transmitters, becomes an emitter that the enemy can locate.

Tactical communications protection

Tactical radio nets are the most exposed part of a ground force's spectrum use. Adamy's treatment of tactical communications EW sets out the defender's levers: link margin and jamming-to-signal ratio, frequency hopping and other spread-spectrum techniques, directional antennas, terrain masking, power control, and the geometry between transmitter, receiver, and jammer[26]. The open record of the war in Ukraine shows why these levers matter. The Royal United Services Institute (RUSI) reported in 2023 that Russian forces deployed at least one major EW system covering each 10 km of front, mainly against drones, and that they intercepted and decrypted unprotected commercial tactical radios in real time[28].

Because no single measure is sufficient, doctrine and civil practice both plan for communications to be degraded. The primary, alternate, contingency, and emergency (PACE) planning method, used in military planning and adopted by CISA and SAFECOM for emergency communications, assigns a different bearer to each level so that the loss of one link does not end command and control[29].

Radar countermeasures

Ground forces face radar from aircraft (airborne surveillance, synthetic aperture radar, and fire-control radars), from counter-battery and ground surveillance radars, and from radar-guided munitions. The countermeasures are the general radar EA techniques, applied from ground platforms: noise and deception jamming of surveillance and tracking radars, decoys and radar-reflective chaff, and reducing the vehicle's radar signature with camouflage and terrain[5,4]. A ground jammer against an airborne radar is at a geometric disadvantage. It must overcome the radar's antenna gain, usually through the sidelobes, and it reveals its own position by transmitting. That is why ground radar countermeasures are usually combined with emission discipline and mobility[5].

A specialised case is the radio-controlled improvised explosive device. The CRS primer cites the vehicle-mounted CREW Duke and the dismounted Thor III counter radio-controlled IED electronic warfare (CREW) systems, which jam the radio links that trigger such devices[3].

Spectrum monitoring and EW management

A brigade can field hundreds of emitters of its own, and civilian networks share the same bands. Spectrum monitoring measures what is actually on the air: occupancy, unknown or unauthorised emitters, and interference. In civil administration the same discipline is codified in the ITU Handbook on Spectrum Monitoring, a practical reference for national regulators and radio engineers[30]. On the military side, the Army's Electronic Warfare Planning and Management Tool (EWPMT) was introduced to replace improvised spreadsheets and slides used to deconflict a crowded spectrum. It lets EW officers plan and coordinate EA, EP, and ES, model the effect of terrain on EW system range, and display the electromagnetic order of battle in the mission-command picture[31].

The RUSI observation that Russian EW systems were generally not coordinated to avoid interfering with one another shows why this management layer matters. Uncoordinated jamming degrades friendly systems as readily as enemy ones[28].

5Space and satellite EW

Space systems carry communications, positioning, navigation and timing (PNT), missile warning, and remote sensing for military and civil users worldwide. Most counterspace activity seen in current operations is electronic. The Secure World Foundation's 2025 open-source assessment found that non-destructive capabilities, including EW, are the only counterspace capabilities being used actively against satellites in current military operations[32].

The three segments

US Space Force capstone doctrine describes every space system as three segments. The orbital segment is the spacecraft. The terrestrial segment is the ground equipment, such as control stations and user terminals. The link segment is the set of signals in the EMS that connect the two. The doctrine warns that electromagnetic energy can disrupt or deny those links, isolating a spacecraft from its operators and users, and that false data can be injected through the EMS if links are unprotected[33]. For EW the link segment is the main point of attack, and it splits into two cases with very different geometry[6,32]:

  • Uplink jamming radiates toward the satellite from the ground. It affects every user served by the jammed transponder or beam, but the jammer must sit within the satellite's receive footprint. The Secure World Foundation cites the US Counter Communications System as an uplink jammer against geostationary communications satellites[32].
  • Downlink jamming radiates toward user terminals on the ground. It needs much less power because it competes with a weak signal from orbit, but it affects only receivers within the jammer's local coverage. Local GNSS jamming is the most common example[24,32].

Adamy's EW 105 treats the same problems from the engineering side: orbit geometry and visibility, propagation over satellite links, link vulnerability, and both intercepting and jamming ground targets from space[6].

Satellite communications resilience

Satellite communications resilience uses the same electronic protection toolkit as terrestrial links (spread spectrum, frequency hopping, antenna nulling, onboard processing, and diverse paths), applied to a long, power-limited link. The US Space Force's Protected Tactical Waveform (PTW) illustrates the approach. It is an anti-jam waveform that uses frequency hopping. In a 2025 demonstration it ran on five terminals over the air at varying interference levels, through a Joint Hub of the Protected Tactical Enterprise Service ground infrastructure. The plan is to field PTW first over the Wideband Global SATCOM system and then extend it to commercial, future military, and allied constellations[34]. Carrying a protected waveform over many constellations is a resilience measure in itself, because an adversary then has to jam many different satellites and beams rather than one.

The threat side is documented in open assessments. The Secure World Foundation reports that Russia has mobile systems able to jam specific satellite user terminals and can probably jam communications satellite uplinks over a wide area from fixed sites, that China likely has significant EW counterspace capability against GNSS and satellite communications, and that Iran has persistently interfered with commercial satellite broadcasts[32]. The US Defense Intelligence Agency's 2022 report Challenges to Security in Space likewise described Chinese and Russian development of jammers to deny satellite communications and GPS[35].

Interference detection and spectrum awareness

Satellite operators see interference as degraded carriers. Resolving it requires characterising the signal, geolocating its source, and getting the responsible administration to act. The international legal basis is the ITU Constitution, Article 45 of which requires every station to be operated so as not to cause harmful interference to other members' radio services. The ITU defines harmful interference as interference that endangers a radionavigation or other safety service, or that seriously degrades, obstructs, or repeatedly interrupts a radiocommunication service. Under Article 15 of the Radio Regulations, cases are first handled directly between administrations, then by the Radiocommunication Bureau, and then by the Radio Regulations Board[36]. For space services, the Bureau's Satellite Interference Reporting and Resolution System (SIRRS) is the online channel through which administrations and operators report harmful interference, exchange case information, and request assistance[37].

Spectrum awareness from space and from the ground is the technical basis for this process: monitoring stations that measure carriers and geolocate uplink interferers, and the regulator-level monitoring practices in the ITU handbook[30,36].

Protection of space-based navigation and sensing

GNSS is the space service most exposed to EW, because its signals arrive below the thermal noise floor and every receiver in range of a jammer is affected[24]. In March 2025 the ITU, the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), and the International Maritime Organization (IMO) issued a joint statement of "grave concern" at rising jamming and spoofing of satellite navigation. They called on states to prevent harmful interference, build resilience into GNSS-dependent systems, keep conventional navigation aids as backup, and report cases. The ITU Radio Regulations Board also urged specific administrations to stop interference affecting neighbouring territories[38]. CSIS's 2025 Space Threat Assessment likewise recorded widespread GPS jamming and spoofing in and around conflict zones[39].

Receiver-level protection includes antenna nulling, signal-quality and power monitoring, consistency checks against inertial and other sensors, and authentication of the navigation message. Psiaki and Humphreys review the spoofing detection methods[25,24]. Space-based sensing, such as the infrared missile-warning satellites the CRS primer lists among US space EW-relevant assets, is protected mainly through its link and ground segments, with the same anti-jam, encryption, and diversity measures used for communications[3,33].

6Other operational environments

The environments below are not separate domains. They are settings that combine the platforms above with particular propagation, legal, and civilian constraints.

Urban operations

Cities are the hardest radio environment. Buildings block line of sight, create multipath and deep fading, and make propagation depend strongly on street geometry. ITU-R Recommendation P.1411 exists specifically to model short-range outdoor propagation in such environments, including below-rooftop and street-canyon paths[40]. For EW this has several consequences. Direction finding suffers from reflected bearings. Jammer and receiver ranges shrink and become unpredictable. GNSS degrades from multipath and blockage before any jamming[24,26]. The spectrum is also crowded with civilian cellular, broadcast, and Wi-Fi signals that ES must sort through and that EA must avoid harming, which places a premium on spectrum monitoring and deconfliction[30,2].

Contested logistics

Logistics was long treated as a rear-area function. An Army analysis proposes defining the contested logistics environment as one in which an adversary deliberately acts, in any domain, to deny, disrupt, destroy, or defeat friendly logistics. It gives an EW attack during a non-combatant evacuation as an example[41]. The EW exposure of logistics comes from its dependence on commercial and civil systems. Shipping depends on GPS and AIS, which the MARAD advisories show are being jammed and spoofed[23]. Airlift depends on GNSS-based navigation and surveillance, which EASA's safety bulletin on GNSS outages addresses for civil aviation[42]. Supply-chain tracking depends on commercial networks and timing. The protective measures are the same as elsewhere: PACE communications planning, alternative PNT, and crews trained to recognise and work through interference[29,43].

Border monitoring

Borders are where electromagnetic effects most often cross from one jurisdiction to another. Jamming generated on one side of a border affects aviation, shipping, and infrastructure on the other. In March 2025 the ITU Radio Regulations Board urged specific administrations to investigate and stop interference affecting neighbouring territories[38]. EASA's Safety Information Bulletin on GNSS outages and alterations, revised in July 2026, is addressed to authorities, air navigation service providers, operators, and manufacturers[42]. Monitoring along borders therefore serves two purposes. It provides early warning and situational awareness of military emitters, using the ES techniques of Section 4. It also produces the evidence (measured signal characteristics and geolocated sources) that the ITU's administration-to-administration process for resolving harmful interference relies on[30,36].

Critical infrastructure protection

Electric grids, telecommunications networks, financial systems, and transport all depend on GNSS timing and positioning. US Executive Order 13905 (2020) made the resilience of PNT-dependent critical infrastructure a national policy objective[44]. The DHS Resilient PNT Conformance Framework sets out what resilient equipment must do: prevent corruption of the PNT solution, respond to detected anomalies, and recover to a proper working state[43]. NIST's Foundational PNT Profile applies the Cybersecurity Framework to the responsible use of PNT services[45].

The legal setting for civilian operators is very different from the military one. In the United States, federal law prohibits the operation, marketing, or sale of any type of jamming equipment by the public, so infrastructure owners cannot answer interference with interference[46]. Their EW posture is therefore defensive and observational: interference detection, resilient receivers, and backup timing. Against drones, CISA's UAS detection guidance covers selecting and siting detection sensors and advises owners to consult legal counsel before deploying them[47].

Unmanned and autonomous systems

Small unmanned aircraft are now the most common target of tactical EW, and their growing autonomy is the main reason that EW against them is changing. CRS describes the US counter-UAS toolkit as layered. Detection comes from radar, electro-optical and infrared sensors, and RF sensors that pick up the link between a drone and its operator. Defeat options include RF jammers that break that link, directed energy, and kinetic interceptors. DoD treats some handheld jammers as a last resort, and domestic use raises legal and public-safety questions[48]. Research on RF, radar, acoustic, and visual drone detection increasingly uses machine learning to classify drones against clutter and other emitters[49].

The EW vulnerability of an unmanned system is its dependence on external signals: the control and video links and GNSS. Spoofing can steer a vehicle as well as blind it[19,25]. RUSI's estimate that Ukraine was losing about 10,000 drones a month in 2023, with Russian EW a major factor, shows the scale[28]. The natural responses are frequency-agile and directional links, multi-constellation and authenticated GNSS, inertial and vision-based navigation, and autonomy that lets the vehicle complete a task without a continuous link. Each reduces the leverage of jamming, so counter-UAS increasingly combines EW with sensing and physical defeat rather than relying on jamming alone[48,4].

Three themes recur across every domain and environment. First, electronic support comes first. Every attack and protection decision depends on knowing what is on the air, so receivers, threat libraries, and spectrum monitoring are common to all platforms[1,4]. Second, dependence on GNSS and open civil links (AIS, commercial radios, commercial satellite communications) is the most common vulnerability, from ships and drones to logistics and power grids[23,38,43]. Third, the spectrum is shared. Military EW operates alongside civil services protected by international law, and in peacetime environments such as borders and critical infrastructure, detection, attribution, and resilience are the main tools available[36,46].

8Conclusion

The physics of EW does not change between domains, but the platform decides its balance. Aircraft trade power for altitude. Ships trade concealment for power and aperture. Ground systems trade range for proximity and numbers. Satellites trade flexibility for global reach. Each platform's EW design follows from those trade-offs: integrated self-protection suites on fighters, layered warning, jamming, and decoys on ships, modular combined ES and EA teams on the ground, and anti-jam waveforms spread across many constellations in space. The newer environments of cities, contested supply lines, borders, civil infrastructure, and autonomous systems add civilian spectrum, legal limits, and very large numbers of cheap emitters. In those settings resilience and attribution often matter more than raw jamming power.
