Electronic Warfare Systems Engineering

An electronic warfare system is a chain of disciplines that must agree with one another at every interface: the antenna sets what the receiver sees, the converter sets what the processor can resolve, the processor sets what the tracker can believe, and the systems engineer has to prove that the whole chain does what the mission needs. This article walks that chain, from RF hardware to verification, using the open textbook and peer-reviewed literature.

1Introduction and scope

US joint doctrine defines electronic warfare (EW) as military action involving the use of electromagnetic energy and directed energy to control the electromagnetic spectrum or to attack an adversary, and divides it into three activities: electronic attack (EA), electronic protection (EP) and electronic warfare support (ES) [1]. Joint Publication 3-85 places these activities inside the broader framework of joint electromagnetic spectrum operations (JEMSO), which also covers spectrum management and the coordination of friendly emitters [1]. Companion articles in this series treat EA, ES and EP in their own right. This article is about the engineering that sits underneath all three.

Whatever its mission, a modern EW system has the same basic anatomy. Antennas collect or radiate energy over a wide bandwidth. An RF front end filters, amplifies and frequency-converts the signal. Converters move it between the analog and digital domains. Real-time digital hardware channelizes the band, detects pulses or bursts and measures their parameters. Processors sort, identify and locate emitters, track them over time and fuse the results with other sensors. A resource manager closes the loop by deciding where to look and, for an EA system, which technique to transmit [2,3]. Wiley describes the same chain from the point of view of the ELINT analyst, where each stage adds measurements and each stage also adds error [4].

The sections that follow take each discipline in turn. Each section states the governing relationships, shows how they bear on EW performance and notes the main design trades. The final sections show how the disciplines couple and how the resulting system is specified and verified.

2RF and microwave engineering

Sensitivity and noise

The first figure of merit for any EW receiver is sensitivity, the weakest signal it can detect with a specified reliability. Thermal noise sets the floor. The available noise power from a matched source at the standard temperature T0 = 290 K is kT0B, which equals −174 dBm in a 1 Hz bandwidth [6]. A receiver with noise figure F, noise bandwidth B and a required detection signal-to-noise ratio SNRreq therefore has a sensitivity of

Smin (dBm) = −174 + 10 log10 B + NF + SNRreq(1)

with B in hertz and NF in decibels [2,6]. The noise figure of a cascade of stages follows Friis' formula,

F = F1 + (F2 − 1)/G1 + (F3 − 1)/(G1G2) + …(2)

so the first stage dominates as long as its gain is high [6,7]. This explains a recurring EW design tension. Any loss ahead of the low-noise amplifier (LNA), such as a cable run, a limiter that protects against high-power illumination, or a preselector filter, adds directly to the system noise figure. Yet EW receivers often need those protective and selective elements precisely because they operate near friendly and hostile high-power transmitters.

Equation (1) also exposes the central trade between sensitivity and bandwidth. A wide instantaneous bandwidth raises the probability of intercepting an unknown emitter, but every tenfold increase in B costs 10 dB of sensitivity. Wideband EW receiver architectures exist largely to manage this trade [4,5].

Dynamic range and linearity

EW receivers face very large signal-level disparities, with a nearby emitter possibly many tens of decibels above a distant one in the same band. Nonlinearity in amplifiers and mixers then produces intermodulation products that can look like real signals. The third-order intercept point (IP3) characterizes this behaviour, and the spur-free dynamic range (SFDR), the span between the noise floor and the input level at which third-order products reach the noise floor, is approximately

SFDR (dB) ≈ ⅔ (IIP3 − Nfloor)(3)

where both quantities are referred to the input in dBm [6,8]. Because the noise floor rises with bandwidth, SFDR falls as instantaneous bandwidth increases, which reinforces the bandwidth trade above. Gain distribution through the chain is chosen so that no single stage limits linearity while the cascade noise figure stays low [8]. Local-oscillator phase noise matters as well: reciprocal mixing transfers LO noise onto strong signals and can bury weak neighbours [8].

The trend described in the literature is toward digital channelized receivers, in which the analog chain is kept short and broadband and the selectivity is implemented in digital filter banks (Section 4) [3,5].

Transmitters and the jamming equation

For EA, the RF problem reverses: generate enough effective radiated power (ERP) at the right frequencies to achieve a required jamming-to-signal ratio (J/S) at the victim receiver. For a self-protection jammer on a target of radar cross-section σ (m2) at range R (km) from a monostatic radar, Adamy gives

J/S (dB) = 71 + ERPJ − ERPR + 20 log10 R − 10 log10 σ(4)

with both ERPs in dBm [2]. The jamming signal travels one way and the skin return travels two ways, so J/S improves as range increases. This is the physical basis of burn-through range, the range at which the radar's echo once again dominates [2].

Solid-state power amplifiers for EA are increasingly built on gallium nitride (GaN) high-electron-mobility transistors. GaN's wide bandgap and high breakdown field allow high operating voltages and power densities well above those of gallium arsenide devices, and GaN-on-silicon-carbide offers good thermal conductivity for removing the resulting heat [9,10]. For a wideband jammer, the amplifier's bandwidth, efficiency and linearity are traded against one another, and the thermal design is often as limiting as the electrical design [10].

3Antenna and phased-array design

Wideband elements

EW antennas must usually cover multiple octaves, which rules out many resonant designs. Frequency-independent antennas such as spirals and log-periodic structures, and tapered-slot (Vivaldi) elements, are standard choices because their radiation characteristics are set by geometry that scales with wavelength [11,12]. Cavity-backed spirals are widely used in radar warning receivers because they are compact and provide circular polarization, which responds to linearly polarized emitters of any orientation at a fixed 3 dB polarization loss [2,11].

Array factor, beamwidth and grating lobes

A phased array of N elements with spacing d, excited with a progressive phase that steers the beam to angle θ0, has an array factor whose main-beam width for uniform illumination is approximately 0.886λ/(Nd cos θ0) radians [11,12]. The beam therefore broadens as it scans away from broadside. To avoid grating lobes, which are additional full-strength beams in unwanted directions, the element spacing must satisfy

d / λ ≤ 1 / (1 + |sin θmax|)(5)

at the highest operating frequency and largest scan angle [12]. For a wideband EW array this condition is set at the top of the band, which forces close spacing, many elements and mutual coupling at the bottom of the band. Mailloux treats the resulting scan-impedance and scan-blindness effects in detail [12].

Beam squint and true time delay

Phase shifters impose a phase that is correct at one frequency only. An array steered with phase shifters to θ0 at centre frequency f0 points at a different angle at frequency f, given by sin θ = (f0/f) sin θ0. This beam squint limits the instantaneous bandwidth of large phase-steered arrays [12]. True-time-delay steering, applied at the element or subarray level, removes the squint because the delay is frequency independent. This is a primary reason why wideband EW arrays favour time-delay units or digital beamforming, where delay can be applied numerically [12,13].

From passive to digital arrays

Herd and Conway trace the evolution of array architectures from passive arrays fed by a single transmitter, through active electronically scanned arrays (AESAs) with a transmit/receive module behind each element, to digital arrays in which each element or subarray has its own receiver and converter [13]. Digital beamforming allows multiple simultaneous beams, adaptive nulling and true-time-delay steering in software, at the cost of many more converters, much higher data rates and greater power dissipation [13]. For EW, simultaneous beams are attractive because they allow a single aperture to search, track and jam in different directions at once [3].

Direction finding with antennas

ES systems estimate angle of arrival with arrays as well. Amplitude-comparison direction finding compares the outputs of squinted directional antennas and is simple and robust but of modest accuracy. Phase interferometry measures the phase difference φ = 2πd sin θ / λ between elements separated by d. Accuracy improves with baseline, but baselines longer than half a wavelength create ambiguities that must be resolved with multiple baselines [2,4]. Statistical high-resolution methods that operate on the array covariance are discussed in Section 8.

Adaptive arrays

Adaptive arrays place nulls on interference automatically. Applebaum derived the weight vector that maximizes signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio as proportional to R−1s, where R is the interference-plus-noise covariance and s the steering vector, and showed how it could be realized as an analog feedback loop [14]. Widrow and colleagues reached a similar result through least-mean-square adaptation [15]. Reed, Mallett and Brennan showed that when R is estimated from K independent snapshots, the expected output SNR is within 3 dB of optimum once K is about twice the number of adaptive degrees of freedom [16]. That result is still the starting point for sizing training data in adaptive EP and sidelobe-cancellation designs.

4Analog and digital signal processing

Sampling

The sampling theorem states that a signal band-limited to W Hz is completely determined by samples taken at a rate of 2W per second [17]. For a bandpass signal occupying fL to fH, the sampling rate need only exceed twice the bandwidth, provided that it lies in one of the allowed ranges

2fH / n ≤ fs ≤ 2fL / (n − 1)(6)

for integer n [18]. Bandpass (undersampling) receivers exploit this to digitize an IF or even an RF band directly, but the analog anti-alias filter then has to reject every other band that would fold onto the band of interest [5,18].

Converters

The analog-to-digital converter (ADC) is frequently the component that bounds EW receiver performance. An ideal N-bit quantizer with a full-scale sine input has a signal-to-quantization-noise ratio of approximately 6.02N + 1.76 dB [19,20]. Real converters fall short of this, which is captured by the effective number of bits (ENOB). Walden's survey identified aperture jitter as a principal limit at high input frequencies [19]. For an rms sampling-clock jitter σj, the jitter-limited SNR for an input at frequency f is

SNRjitter = −20 log10(2π f σj)(7)

so sampling a high RF directly demands a very clean clock [19,20]. Spurious-free dynamic range, which is limited by converter nonlinearity rather than noise, is often the more relevant ADC specification for EW, because a converter spur can be mistaken for an emitter [5,20].

Converter resolution and the analog front end must be matched. Tsui discusses how the analog gain ahead of the ADC is set so that thermal noise occupies a few least-significant bits. Too little gain wastes sensitivity; too much gain wastes dynamic range [5].

Spectral analysis and the FFT

The discrete Fourier transform is the workhorse of wideband digital EW receivers. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) of Cooley and Tukey reduces its cost from order N2 to order N log N operations [21]. Windowing controls spectral leakage: Harris showed, for example, that the Hann window has a highest sidelobe near −32 dB while the minimum four-term Blackman-Harris window reaches about −92 dB, at the cost of a wider main lobe and therefore coarser frequency resolution [22]. For an EW receiver, window sidelobes set how strong a signal may be before its leakage masks a weak one in a nearby bin, so the window is part of the dynamic-range budget [5,22]. Oppenheim and Schafer give the general theory of discrete-time filtering and spectral analysis used throughout this work [23].

Digital downconversion and multirate processing

Once digitized, a band of interest is typically translated to baseband with a numerically controlled oscillator, filtered and decimated. Hogenauer's cascaded integrator-comb (CIC) filter performs large rate changes with no multipliers, using integrators at the high rate and comb sections at the low rate, which makes it well suited to hardware [24]. When many channels are needed simultaneously, the polyphase FFT filter bank is far more efficient than a bank of independent downconverters. Harris, Dick and Rice show how a single prototype low-pass filter, partitioned into polyphase components and followed by an FFT, forms a uniform channelizer whose cost grows only modestly with the number of channels [25,26]. This structure is the digital equivalent of the analog channelized receiver of Table 1 and underlies most modern wideband digital ES receivers [5,25].

Pulse detection and parameter encoding

In radar ES, the channelizer output feeds a detector that declares the presence of pulses and measures, for each pulse, its time of arrival, frequency, pulse width, amplitude and angle of arrival. These measurements are packed into a pulse descriptor word (PDW), which is the input to the deinterleaving and identification processes of Sections 7 to 9 [4,5]. Communications ES uses the analogous burst or signal descriptors [3].

5Software-defined radio

Mitola described the software radio as an architecture in which the analog functions are minimized and digitization occurs as close to the antenna as possible, so that channel selection, demodulation and waveform generation become programmable [27]. The ideal of digitizing directly at the antenna remains limited by converter bandwidth, dynamic range and power, the same constraints identified in Walden's survey [19,27]. Practical designs keep a short analog front end for filtering and gain and then sample either an intermediate frequency or, increasingly, the RF band directly.

Software-defined radio brings three properties that matter for EW. The first is agility: the same hardware can host many receive and transmit functions, and new waveforms or techniques can be loaded without a hardware change. The second is reuse: a common hardware platform can be shared across ES, EA and communications functions [3]. The third is portability, which depends on a standard software framework. In US military communications, the Software Communications Architecture (SCA) specifies how waveform applications are deployed and managed on heterogeneous processors [28].

Mitola and Maguire extended the software radio into the cognitive radio, a radio that senses its environment and adapts its behaviour according to learned models [29]. Haykin framed cognitive radio as a closed loop of radio-scene analysis, channel-state estimation and transmit-power control [30]. Haigh and Andrusenko apply the same ideas to cognitive EW, in which situation assessment, decision making and learning are built into ES and EA systems so that they can respond to emitters whose behaviour is not in a pre-programmed threat library [31]. The engineering consequence is that the SDR platform must support not only reconfiguration but also fast, safe, verifiable decision loops, which links this discipline to control (Section 10) and to verification (Section 11).

6FPGA and real-time embedded computing

Why FPGAs

Wideband EW processing has to keep pace with converter sample rates of billions of samples per second and must respond within microseconds, for example to repeat a radar pulse in an EA technique. General-purpose processors cannot sustain that throughput with deterministic latency, so the front-end processing of Section 4 is usually implemented in field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) or application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) [5,32]. Kuon and Rose quantified the cost of reconfigurability: for logic-only designs, FPGAs required roughly 35 times the area of a standard-cell ASIC, were 3.4 to 4.6 times slower, and consumed about 14 times the dynamic power. Hard multiplier and memory blocks narrowed the area and power gaps substantially [33]. FPGAs are nevertheless favoured in EW because production volumes are small and threat environments change faster than ASIC development cycles.

Arithmetic and architecture

FPGA signal processing relies on fixed-point arithmetic, pipelining and parallelism. Meyer-Baese covers the implementation of FIR and IIR filters, multirate filters, FFTs and related structures, including distributed arithmetic and the use of embedded multiplier blocks [32]. Volder's CORDIC algorithm computes rotations, phase and magnitude using only shifts and additions, which makes it a standard building block for numerically controlled oscillators and for converting I/Q samples to amplitude and phase in pulse-measurement logic [32,34]. Word lengths are a design decision with system consequences: each bit of truncation adds quantization noise and can raise spur levels, so the fixed-point design must be analysed as part of the dynamic-range budget of Sections 2 and 4 [23,32].

Real-time software

Behind the FPGA, embedded processors run the deinterleaving, identification, tracking and resource-management software. Here the requirement is bounded response time rather than raw throughput. Liu and Layland proved that a set of n independent periodic tasks with rate-monotonic priorities always meets its deadlines if the total processor utilization does not exceed n(21/n − 1), a bound that approaches ln 2 ≈ 69 percent for large n [35]. Kopetz describes the wider design discipline of distributed real-time systems, including time-triggered architectures in which communication and computation are scheduled against a global time base so that latency is predictable by construction [36].

Open architectures

The cost of bespoke processing hardware has driven the defence community toward modular open-systems standards. The Sensor Open Systems Architecture (SOSA) technical standard, published by The Open Group, defines a modular reference architecture and hardware profiles, based on the OpenVPX family, for radar, EW, communications and electro-optical sensors so that cards and software modules from different suppliers can interoperate [37]. For the EW systems engineer, such standards change the trade space: interfaces become fixed early, and performance must be achieved within standard form factors, power budgets and backplane bandwidths.

7Detection and estimation theory

Hypothesis testing

Every ES receiver must decide, many times per second, whether a signal is present. Neyman and Pearson showed that, for a fixed false-alarm probability, the test that maximizes detection probability compares the likelihood ratio with a threshold [38]. Van Trees and Kay develop the theory for known signals, signals with unknown parameters and random signals, and show how the receiver operating characteristic summarizes the attainable trade between probability of detection (PD) and probability of false alarm (PFA) [39,40].

An ES receiver rarely knows the waveform it is looking for, so the matched filter that is optimal for radar is not available. Urkowitz analysed the energy detector, which compares received energy over a time-bandwidth window with a threshold and requires no knowledge of the signal's structure [41]. It is the canonical detector for unknown signals and the baseline against which more specialised intercept receivers are compared. Pace applies detection theory to low-probability-of-intercept radar, whose waveforms are designed to spread energy so that a non-cooperative energy detector must integrate over long times or wide bandwidths [42]. Detectors that exploit cyclostationarity, the periodic correlation that modulated signals carry, can outperform energy detection and can discriminate between overlapping signals by their cycle frequencies [43].

Constant false-alarm rate

Real noise and interference levels vary, so EW detectors set their threshold adaptively from local estimates of the noise. In the cell-averaging constant false-alarm rate (CA-CFAR) detector introduced by Finn and Johnson, the threshold is a multiple α of the mean of N reference cells [44]. For square-law detection in exponentially distributed noise,

PFA = (1 + α/N)−N(8)

independent of the actual noise level [40,44]. Cell averaging degrades when other strong signals fall within the reference window, a common situation in dense EW environments. Rohling's ordered-statistic CFAR, which takes a ranked sample instead of the mean, is more robust in multiple-target situations [45].

Parameter estimation and its limits

Once a signal is detected, its parameters must be measured. The Cramér-Rao lower bound (CRLB) gives the minimum variance of any unbiased estimator and is the standard benchmark for EW measurement accuracy [39,46]. For the frequency of a single real sinusoid in white Gaussian noise observed for N samples at SNR η, the bound on the normalized frequency is

var(f̂) ≥ 12 / [(2π)2 η N(N2 − 1)](9)

so accuracy improves with the cube of the observation length [46]. Rife and Boorstyn showed that the maximum-likelihood estimator, approximated by locating the peak of a zero-padded FFT, reaches the bound above a threshold SNR [47]. Similar bounds apply to time of arrival, which improves with bandwidth, and to angle of arrival, which improves with aperture in wavelengths [4,46].

Emitter geolocation

Multi-platform ES systems geolocate emitters from time difference of arrival (TDOA) and frequency difference of arrival (FDOA). Stein showed how both are estimated jointly by locating the peak of the cross-ambiguity function between signals received at two sites [48]. Torrieri developed the statistical theory of passive location, including the use of the CRLB and of geometric dilution of precision to predict localization error from sensor geometry [49]. Chan and Ho gave a closed-form TDOA location estimator that approaches the CRLB at small noise levels [50]. These results allow the systems engineer to trade platform geometry, baseline and synchronisation accuracy against location error before any hardware exists.

8Statistical signal processing

Covariance-based direction finding

Statistical signal processing treats the received data as random vectors and builds estimators from their statistics, most often the spatial covariance matrix of an array. Schmidt's MUSIC algorithm separates the covariance eigenvectors into signal and noise subspaces and locates emitters where the array steering vector is orthogonal to the noise subspace. It can resolve sources closer than the conventional beamwidth and estimate several emitters simultaneously [51]. Roy and Kailath's ESPRIT exploits a translational invariance in the array geometry to obtain the angles directly, without a search over a calibrated array manifold [52]. Capon's minimum-variance distortionless-response beamformer, with weights

w = R−1a(θ) / [aH(θ) R−1 a(θ)](10)

passes the look direction undistorted while minimizing all other output power, and provides both a high-resolution spatial spectrum and an adaptive nulling beamformer [53,54]. Van Trees' Optimum Array Processing gives a unified treatment of these methods, their performance bounds and their sensitivity to calibration errors, which in practice often limit high-resolution DF more than noise does [54].

Adaptive filtering and spectral estimation

Adaptive filters such as least-mean-square and recursive-least-squares algorithms are used in EW for interference cancellation, equalization and adaptive nulling. Haykin gives their convergence and tracking properties, which determine how quickly an EP function can respond to a changing jammer [55]. For spectral surveillance, Welch's method averages periodograms of overlapped, windowed segments to reduce the variance of the power-spectrum estimate at the cost of resolution [56]. The choice of segment length and overlap is a direct trade between detection sensitivity and the ability to resolve closely spaced or short-duration signals.

Signal classification

Identifying what a detected signal is, by modulation, protocol or specific emitter, is a classification problem. Dobre and colleagues divide automatic modulation classification into likelihood-based methods, which are optimal when the signal models are known, and feature-based methods, which use statistics such as higher-order cumulants and cyclic features and are more robust and less costly [57]. More recently, O'Shea, Roy and Clancy showed that convolutional and residual neural networks trained on raw I/Q samples can outperform expert-feature classifiers on over-the-air data across a range of channel impairments [58]. Learned classifiers raise new systems engineering questions about training-data coverage, behaviour on unfamiliar signals and verification, which are discussed in Section 11.

Pulse deinterleaving

A radar ES receiver in a dense environment receives interleaved pulse trains from many emitters. Deinterleaving separates them, usually by clustering PDWs on frequency, angle and pulse width and then analysing time-of-arrival differences to find pulse repetition intervals. Mardia introduced cumulative difference histogram techniques for this purpose [59], and Wiley reviews the wider family of PRI analysis methods, including their behaviour with jittered and staggered PRIs [4]. The quality of deinterleaving bounds everything downstream: a mis-sorted pulse train produces a wrong identification and a corrupted track.

9Sensor fusion and tracking

State estimation

Tracking estimates the state of an emitter or platform over time from noisy measurements. Kalman's recursive filter gives the minimum mean-square-error estimate for linear Gaussian systems, alternating a prediction step, x̂k|k−1 = Fx̂k−1, with an update that weights the measurement innovation by the Kalman gain Kk = Pk|k−1HT(HPk|k−1HT + R)−1 [60]. Bar-Shalom, Li and Kirubarajan, and Blackman and Popoli, develop the filter's practical use in tracking, including motion models, filter tuning and consistency testing [61,62].

EW measurements are often nonlinear in the state. Bearings-only and TDOA/FDOA geolocation are typical cases. Extended Kalman filters linearize the measurement model; the unscented filter of Julier and Uhlmann propagates a deterministic set of sigma points through the nonlinearity and often handles strong nonlinearity better [63]. Particle filters represent the posterior with weighted samples and accommodate non-Gaussian errors and multimodal posteriors, at higher computational cost [64]. Targets that change behaviour, such as an aircraft that alternates between straight flight and manoeuvres, are handled by the interacting multiple model (IMM) estimator, which runs several motion models in parallel and mixes them according to Markov switching probabilities [65].

Data association

In dense environments the hardest tracking problem is deciding which measurement belongs to which track. Joint probabilistic data association (JPDA) weights all feasible measurement-to-track assignments by their probabilities [66], while multiple hypothesis tracking (MHT), introduced by Reid, maintains alternative association hypotheses over several scans and defers hard decisions until more data arrive [67]. Random finite set methods avoid explicit association altogether: Mahler's probability hypothesis density (PHD) filter propagates the first-order moment of the multitarget posterior, estimating the number of targets and their states jointly [68]. Blackman and Popoli compare these approaches and their computational demands for operational systems [62].

Multisensor fusion

EW sensors rarely work alone. ES data are fused with radar, electro-optical sensors and offboard data links to improve identification and localization. Hall and Llinas describe the Joint Directors of Laboratories (JDL) process model, which organises fusion into levels running from signal and object refinement through situation and threat assessment, and survey the associated algorithms [69]. A recurring architectural choice is between centralized fusion of raw measurements, which is optimal but bandwidth-hungry, and distributed track-to-track fusion, which is cheaper but must account for correlated errors between tracks [61]. When those cross-correlations are unknown, covariance intersection gives a consistent fused estimate without requiring them [70]. ES contributes particular strengths to fusion, including identification from emitter parameters and long-range passive detection, and particular weaknesses, including the lack of direct range from a single platform, so the fusion design must model each sensor's error characteristics honestly [3,62].

10Control systems

Feedback control appears throughout an EW system, often in places that are not labelled as control. The classical tools of loop gain, stability margins and frequency response, set out by Franklin, Powell and Emami-Naeini, apply to all of them [71].

Phase-locked loops and synthesizers

Frequency synthesizers and carrier-recovery circuits are built around phase-locked loops (PLLs). Gardner shows that a second-order type-2 loop, with a proportional-plus-integral loop filter, tracks a frequency step with zero steady-state phase error, and that its natural frequency and damping set the trade between acquisition speed, tracking of dynamics and rejection of reference and VCO noise [72]. In a frequency-agile EW receiver or exciter, synthesizer settling time directly limits revisit rate and technique response, while loop bandwidth shapes the phase-noise profile discussed in Section 2 [8,72].

Gain control and receiver protection

Automatic gain control is a feedback loop that keeps signal levels within the converter's dynamic range. Its attack and decay times must be chosen with care in EW, because an AGC that responds quickly to a strong pulse can suppress a weak signal that follows it, and an adversary can exploit a slow one [2]. Franklin and colleagues' treatment of loop dynamics applies directly to the design of these loops [71].

Adaptive nulling as a control loop

Applebaum's original adaptive array was an analog control loop in which correlators drive the element weights to the optimum Wiener solution [14]. Its convergence rate depends on the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix, so strong jammers are nulled quickly and weak ones slowly. This eigenvalue spread is a classic issue in adaptive-loop design [14,55].

Pointing, tracking and look-through

Mechanically steered antennas and positioners use servo loops whose bandwidth must exceed the angular dynamics of the target while rejecting measurement noise [71]. At system level, an EA system that must both jam and monitor its victim uses look-through, brief interruptions of transmission that let its own receiver observe how the victim responds. Look-through creates a closed loop around the adversary, with the trade that each look reduces jamming duty cycle [2,3]. Cognitive EW makes this loop explicit by adding learning and decision logic between observation and action [31]. Because the plant in that loop includes an adaptive adversary, its stability and effectiveness can only be assessed by modelling and test against representative threat behaviour (Section 11).

11Systems engineering and verification

Life-cycle frameworks

The disciplines above are integrated through systems engineering. ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 defines the system life-cycle processes in four groups: agreement processes, organizational project-enabling processes, technical management processes and technical processes [73]. The INCOSE handbook elaborates these processes and their practice, including model-based systems engineering [74], and the NASA handbook describes an equivalent engine of requirements definition, technical solution definition, design realization, verification, validation and transition, together with technical management processes such as risk management and technology readiness assessment [75].

Requirements and budgets

EW requirements flow from the mission and the threat. For an ES system, top-level needs such as "detect and locate a class of emitters at a given range within a given time" must be decomposed into frequency coverage, sensitivity, instantaneous bandwidth, probability of intercept, DF accuracy, throughput in pulses per second and false-alarm rate [3,4]. Each of these decomposes into budgets across the disciplines of this article. Sensitivity is budgeted using equation (1) across antenna gain, front-end loss, noise figure and detection threshold. Location accuracy is budgeted through the CRLB and geometry results of Section 7 [49]. Throughput is budgeted against converter rate, FPGA resources and real-time schedulability [35]. The NASA handbook recommends that such technical performance measures be tracked through the life cycle so that margin erosion is visible early [75].

Verification and validation

The NASA handbook distinguishes verification, which proves that a product meets its specified requirements, from validation, which proves that it accomplishes its intended purpose in its intended environment, and lists four verification methods: test, analysis, inspection and demonstration [75]. IEEE Std 1012 defines verification and validation activities across system, software and hardware life cycles and scales their rigour by integrity level, a useful framework for deciding how much independent assessment safety- or mission-critical EW software warrants [76].

Modelling, simulation and test

Because EW performance depends on interactions with adversary systems that cannot be fully reproduced, modelling and simulation carry an unusually heavy share of verification. Adamy describes the hierarchy of EW models from engineering-level models of individual components, through engagement-level simulations, to mission and campaign models, and discusses the fidelity each level requires [77]. In practice, verification progresses from digital simulation to hardware-in-the-loop testing, in which real receivers and processors are stimulated with simulated threat environments, then to anechoic chamber and installed-system testing and finally to open-air range testing [3,77].

Two families of standard environmental and electromagnetic tests apply to most military EW equipment. MIL-STD-461G sets requirements for conducted and radiated emissions and susceptibility, which is particularly important for systems that contain both sensitive receivers and high-power transmitters on the same platform [78]. MIL-STD-810H defines tailored laboratory tests for environmental stresses such as temperature, vibration, shock and humidity [79].

Verifying adaptive and learning systems

Cognitive EW and learned classifiers complicate verification because their behaviour depends on data and on experience in the field. Haigh and Andrusenko discuss the need to test such systems against representative and novel emitter behaviour, to constrain their decision space and to monitor them in operation [31]. In terms of the NASA framework, these systems shift weight from verification against fixed requirements toward validation of behaviour in the intended environment [75], and IEEE 1012's integrity-level scaling provides a principled way to decide how much independent V&V they need [76].

12How the disciplines couple

The most consequential EW design decisions sit at the interfaces between disciplines. Table 2 lists representative system-level parameters and the disciplines that jointly determine them, drawing on the relationships cited above.

Two examples show how these couplings drive architecture. Widening instantaneous bandwidth improves probability of intercept, but it lowers sensitivity by equation (1), lowers SFDR by equation (3), requires a faster ADC whose jitter limit by equation (7) becomes harder to meet and multiplies FPGA resources. The bandwidth decision therefore belongs to the system, not to any single discipline [5,19]. Similarly, moving from a phase-steered AESA to a digital array removes beam squint and enables simultaneous beams and adaptive nulling, but shifts cost and power into converters, data transport and processing and turns calibration into a statistical-signal-processing problem [13,54]. The systems engineering processes of Section 11 exist to make such trades explicitly, with budgets and verification evidence, rather than leaving them to emerge from subsystem design [74,75].

13Conclusion

Electronic warfare systems engineering is the practice of making a chain of specialised disciplines behave as one system against an adversary who is trying to make it fail. The governing relationships are well established in the open literature: thermal noise and Friis' cascade bound sensitivity; intercept points and converter physics bound dynamic range; array geometry bounds angular coverage and resolution; the sampling theorem and multirate filter banks determine how wide a band can be processed; FPGA and real-time scheduling theory determine how quickly; the Neyman-Pearson criterion and the Cramér-Rao bound set what can be detected and how accurately; and Kalman filtering and data association set how well emitters can be followed and fused. Software-defined and cognitive architectures increase what a single platform can do, but they also move more of the system's behaviour into software and learned models, which places greater weight on modelling, simulation and disciplined verification and validation.
