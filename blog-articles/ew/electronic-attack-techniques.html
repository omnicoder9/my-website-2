1Introduction and scope

Electromagnetic warfare, long called electronic warfare (EW), is military action that uses electromagnetic and directed energy to control the electromagnetic spectrum or to attack an adversary. US joint doctrine divides it into three parts: electronic attack (EA), electronic protection (EP), and electronic warfare support (ES)[1]. Electronic attack is the offensive part. It uses electromagnetic energy, directed energy, or anti-radiation weapons against personnel, facilities, or equipment with the intent of degrading, neutralizing, or destroying combat capability[1,2]. Electronic protection covers the measures that keep friendly systems working when an adversary, or friendly forces, use the spectrum against them. Electronic warfare support is the search, interception, and location of emitters that informs both[1].

This article surveys electronic attack against four classes of sensor and link: radar, communications, satellite navigation, and infrared and electro-optical (EO) systems. For each class it explains the physical principle of the attack and, with equal weight, the protective measures that answer it. Measure and countermeasure have evolved together for more than eighty years, and neither can be understood alone[3,4]. The treatment stays at the level of the open textbook literature. It explains principles and trade-offs rather than parameters of any fielded system.

Terminology

The 2020 edition of JP 3-85 replaced "electronic warfare" with "electromagnetic warfare" and folded it into joint electromagnetic spectrum operations (JEMSO)[1]. Most textbooks still use the older term and the abbreviations ECM (electronic countermeasures, roughly EA) and ECCM (electronic counter-countermeasures, roughly EP)[3,5]. This article uses both vocabularies where the source does.

2Radar-focused warfare

A monostatic radar detects a target by the echo of its own transmission. The echo power falls with the fourth power of range, because the signal spreads on the way out and again on the way back[6]. A jammer near the target only has to cover the one-way path to the radar, so its signal falls with the square of range. That asymmetry is the basic reason radar jamming works at all[3,4].

The jamming-to-signal ratio

The figure of merit is the jamming-to-signal ratio (J/S) at the radar receiver. For a self-protection jammer carried on the target, the textbook form, with both powers expressed as effective radiated power (ERP), is[4]:

J/S = 4π · ERPJ · R2  /  (ERPR · σ)

Here R is the range from radar to target and σ is the target's radar cross section (RCS). Two consequences follow directly. J/S falls as the target closes on the radar, which leads to burn-through (Section 2.6). And J/S is inversely proportional to RCS, so a smaller target needs less jamming power for the same effect, which is why signature reduction and jamming are designed together[3,4]. For a stand-off jammer, the jammer and target are at different ranges and the jammer usually enters through the radar's antenna sidelobes, which adds the ratio of sidelobe to main-beam gain to the equation[3,7].

2.1 Noise jamming

Noise jamming, also called masking or obscuration jamming, raises the noise floor of the victim receiver so that echoes cannot be detected against it[3,7]. It needs little knowledge of the radar beyond its frequency band, which makes it robust, but it also announces the jammer's presence and direction[4].

The central trade-off is bandwidth against power density. A jammer's output spread over a wide band delivers less power per hertz into any one receiver than the same output concentrated on one channel[3,4]. The literature distinguishes three forms:

  • Spot jamming concentrates power on the bandwidth of one radar. It gives the highest J/S but must know, and follow, the radar's frequency[7].
  • Barrage jamming covers a wide band at once. It defeats frequency agility and several radars together, at the cost of much lower power density[4,7].
  • Swept jamming moves a narrower spot rapidly across a band, trading continuous coverage for momentary high density[3].

Noise can also be pulsed or gated to cover only the range interval around the protecting platform (cover pulses), and it can be emitted from the protected platform (self-screening), from a dedicated aircraft outside weapon range (stand-off), from an escort, or from an expendable or unmanned platform close to the radar (stand-in)[4,7]. The radar's main answer is that noise reveals the jammer's bearing, so a radar can track the jammer itself, a mode known as home-on-jam, and netted radars can triangulate it[3,5].

2.2 Deceptive jamming

Deceptive jamming does not hide the echo. It supplies false information that the radar accepts as real: false targets, false ranges, false velocities, or false angles[3,7]. Because the false signal has to look like an echo of the radar's own waveform, deception jammers are usually repeaters that receive, modify, and retransmit the radar's pulses. The digital radio frequency memory (DRFM) made this practical for coherent radars. It samples an incoming pulse at high speed, stores it, and replays it with controlled delay, Doppler shift, and amplitude, so the replica keeps the coherence the radar's processing expects[8,9].

Deception needs far less power than masking, because it competes with the echo inside the radar's matched filter rather than with the whole receiver bandwidth. It demands much more knowledge of the radar, and its effect depends on the radar's tracking logic[3,10]. Classes of deception described in the open literature include:

  • False-target generation, which fills the display or tracker with plausible returns so that real targets are hidden among them or the processor saturates[7].
  • Range and velocity deception, described in Section 2.3.
  • Angle deception, which corrupts the direction a tracking radar measures. Techniques for conical-scan and track-while-scan radars, such as inverse-gain modulation, exploit the scan pattern. Monopulse radars measure angle from a single pulse and resist those methods, so the literature describes techniques such as cross-polarization, cross-eye (two coherent sources with a controlled phase difference that distort the wavefront), terrain bounce, and blinking between separated sources[3,7,10].

2.3 Range-gate and velocity-gate deception

A tracking radar follows a target with a range gate, a short time window centred on the expected echo delay, and in Doppler radars with a velocity gate, a narrow filter centred on the expected Doppler shift. The radar ignores returns outside its gates, which is what lets it track one target among clutter and noise[6,10].

Range-gate pull-off (RGPO) exploits this. The jammer first repeats the echo with no extra delay but greater strength, so that the range tracker's automatic gain control locks to the stronger replica. It then increases the delay of the replica progressively, and the gate follows it away from the real echo. When the gate has been walked away, the jammer stops. The radar is left with an empty gate and must return to search, losing track time[3,4,7]. Range-gate pull-in is the same idea with the replica advanced in time, which requires predicting the next pulse and is defeated if pulse timing is irregular[7].

Velocity-gate pull-off (VGPO) is the Doppler counterpart against pulse-Doppler and continuous-wave radars. The replica's Doppler shift is changed gradually to drag the velocity gate away from the true target Doppler[3,4]. A modern tracker can compare the rate of change of measured range with the measured Doppler, which should agree for a real target. Pulling only one gate creates a mismatch, so coordinated range and velocity pull-off has to keep the two consistent[5,10].

The standard protective measures are listed in Farina's ECCM catalogue[5]: leading-edge range tracking (a repeater cannot answer before the true echo arrives), guard gates that look for energy beside the tracked gate, range-rate/Doppler consistency checks, frequency agility and pulse-repetition jitter that deny the repeater a predictable waveform, and track-on-jam fallbacks.

2.4 Chaff and radar decoys

Chaff is the oldest radar countermeasure in service. It is a cloud of thin conducting fibres or foils cut to about half the wavelength of the threat radar, so each element behaves as a resonant dipole and the cloud returns a large echo from little mass[3,7]. Cutting a mix of lengths spreads the response across several bands. After release the cloud blooms, decelerates quickly to the local wind speed, and falls slowly[4,7].

Chaff is used in three main ways: laying corridors that mask aircraft flying inside them, creating false targets, and, for self-protection, presenting a large echo near the platform at the moment the platform manoeuvres so that a tracking radar transfers to the cloud[3,7]. Naval doctrine describes distraction (decoys deployed before a seeker locks on, to offer alternative targets), seduction (decoys within the seeker's resolution cell after lock-on, which pull the track centroid away from the ship), and dilution[7].

The physics that makes chaff work also limits it. The cloud quickly loses the platform's velocity, so radars that use Doppler processing, moving-target indication, or pulse-Doppler filtering can separate a moving target from chaff that moves with the wind[6,10]. For this reason chaff is most effective against non-coherent radars or in the brief interval before the cloud and the platform separate in velocity[4].

Decoys present a target-like signature in a separate location. Passive decoys use reflectors such as corner reflectors or Luneberg lenses to give a large RCS from a small object[7,11]. Active decoys carry a transmitter or repeater. Towed decoys trail behind an aircraft on a cable and attract a radar-guided missile's angle tracking away from the aircraft. Expendable active decoys are ejected or launched and fly or float on their own[8,9]. A decoy's credibility depends on how well its signature matches the target in every dimension the radar measures, including RCS, Doppler, and motion[3].

2.5 Low-observable design and signature management

Low-observable (LO) design reduces the energy a platform returns or emits, so it is detected later or not at all. It is a form of protection rather than attack, but it is inseparable from electronic attack because every jamming equation above depends on σ[3,4].

Knott, Shaeffer, and Tuley identify four ways to reduce RCS: shaping, radar-absorbing materials, passive cancellation, and active cancellation[11]. Shaping is usually the most effective. It aligns edges and surfaces so that specular reflections go to a few directions away from likely radars, and it removes corner reflectors such as junctions between surfaces. Radar-absorbing materials convert incident energy to heat and treat the regions shaping cannot fix. Passive and active cancellation create a second return that interferes destructively with the first, and they are narrow-band and hard to realise in practice[11].

The payoff is moderated by the radar equation. Detection range scales with the fourth root of RCS, so a 10 dB reduction in RCS cuts detection range to about 56 percent of its former value, and a 20 dB reduction to about 32 percent[6,11]. RCS reduction is also frequency dependent. Shaping works best when the platform is large compared with the wavelength, so low-frequency radars are less affected[11].

Signature management extends beyond RCS. It includes controlling the platform's own emissions through emission control (EMCON) and low-probability-of-intercept (LPI) radar waveforms, which spread energy in time and frequency so a radar can operate while remaining hard for an intercept receiver to detect[1,12].

2.6 Radar burn-through and counter-countermeasures

Because the echo grows with 1/R4 and the self-protection jamming with 1/R2, J/S falls as the target approaches. Burn-through range is the range at which the echo has recovered enough that the radar detects or tracks the target despite the jamming[3,4]. Setting J/S equal to the minimum value at which the jamming is still effective and solving the equation above gives the textbook expression[4]:

RBT = √[ ERPR · σ · (J/S)req  /  (4π · ERPJ) ]

The relation shows the radar designer's levers: more radiated power and antenna gain, longer coherent integration and pulse compression (which raise effective signal energy against noise jamming), and anything that denies the jammer its bandwidth or timing. Farina's chapter in the Radar Handbook organises ECCM by the part of the radar involved[5]:

Adaptive arrays deserve particular mention. An electronically scanned array with many receive channels can form nulls in the directions of several jammers at once, while keeping its main beam on the target. This directly erodes the stand-off jammer's ability to enter through sidelobes[5,13]. In return, electronic attack has moved toward greater agility and toward coherent DRFM techniques that are hard to separate from real echoes[8,9].

3Communications-focused warfare

Communications EA differs from radar EA in its geometry. A communication link is one-way: the wanted signal travels from transmitter to receiver once, and the jammer must reach the receiver, not the transmitter[14,15]. Both signals therefore follow similar propagation laws. In decibel form, Adamy writes the J/S at the receiver as the difference between jammer and transmitter ERP, corrected by the propagation losses on each path and by the receiving antenna's gain toward each source[14]. The practical consequence is that a jammer close to the receiver can be effective with modest power, while a receiver close to its own transmitter is hard to jam.

3.1 Communications interference and jamming

Digital receivers fail abruptly. Bit error rate rises steeply once signal-to-interference falls below the level the modulation and coding were designed for, so jamming does not need to overpower the signal by a wide margin to disrupt it[14,15]. Lichtman and colleagues classify communications jammers by what they know about the target and how they respond[16]:

Protocol-aware or "smart" jamming has drawn attention in the literature because it can be far more efficient than noise. Disrupting the small fraction of a waveform that carries synchronisation or control information can deny an entire link at low average power, which also makes the jammer harder to detect[16,17].

3.2 Signal denial and link disruption

Doctrine frames the purpose of communications EA in terms of effects: to deny, degrade, disrupt, deceive, or destroy an adversary's use of the spectrum[1,2]. Denial need not be complete to matter. A command-and-control network that delivers messages late, or a data link that loses enough packets to force retransmission, can lose tactical value well before it fails outright[15]. Remotely piloted systems are a clear case, because they depend on continuous command and telemetry links and are usually programmed with a lost-link behaviour that takes over when those links fail[9].

Link disruption also includes attacks at the layers above the physical signal. Pirayesh and Zeng review attacks on medium-access and network functions, such as jamming only acknowledgements so that senders back off or repeat traffic, and they note that such attacks often look like ordinary congestion to the victim[17]. The defensive implication is that link monitoring must distinguish deliberate interference from propagation loss and congestion.

3.3 Deception and spoofing

Electromagnetic deception is a recognised doctrinal task. Joint and Army doctrine describe manipulative deception (altering friendly emissions to mislead an adversary), simulative deception (simulating friendly forces or capabilities that do not exist), and imitative deception (introducing emissions into adversary channels that imitate the adversary's own)[1,2]. Imitative deception against communications includes injecting false messages into a network and spoofing the identity of a legitimate node[15,17].

The primary defence against imitative deception is cryptographic. Message authentication and transmission security make injected traffic detectable, because the intruder cannot produce valid authentication without the keys[17,18]. Procedural measures such as authentication challenges complement these technical controls[2].

3.4 Anti-jam communication techniques

Anti-jam design gives the receiver an advantage the jammer cannot easily match. The main tools are:

  • Direct-sequence spread spectrum (DSSS). The data are multiplied by a fast pseudo-random code, which spreads the signal over a much wider band. The receiver correlates with the same code, which collapses the signal back to the data bandwidth while spreading any uncorrelated jamming. The resulting processing gain is approximately the ratio of spread bandwidth to data bandwidth[15,18].
  • Frequency-hopping spread spectrum (FHSS). The carrier changes frequency in a pseudo-random sequence across a wide band. A jammer that does not know the sequence must either spread its power across the band or accept that it hits only some hops. Fast hopping also defeats follower jammers that need time to detect each hop[15,18].
  • Error-correcting codes and interleaving. Coding repairs the hops or symbols that jamming corrupts, and interleaving spreads a burst of errors across many code words so each sees only a few. Combined with hopping, coding is the standard answer to partial-band and pulsed jamming[18].
  • Adaptive and directional antennas. Antenna arrays can steer nulls toward jammers, and directional links reduce the receiver's gain toward the jammer, improving the J/S term on the receive side of the link equation[14,18].
  • Low probability of intercept and detection (LPI/LPD). Low power, short transmissions, directional antennas, and spreading make the signal hard to find, which denies the adversary the information that spot and follower jamming require[14,15].

3.5 Tactical network resilience

Resilience is a property of the network as a whole, not only of each link. A network built as a mesh can route around a jammed node, and one that offers several transmission paths of different types, such as line-of-sight radio, satellite communications, and wired links, does not depend on any single part of the spectrum[1,17].

Cognitive radio adds adaptation. Mitola introduced the idea of a radio that is aware of its environment and adjusts its own operation, and Haykin framed it as a closed loop of spectrum sensing, analysis, and reconfiguration[19,20]. In an anti-jam setting, that loop lets a network detect interference and move frequency, power, waveform, or route to avoid it. Pirayesh and Zeng survey such adaptive and learning-based defences, while noting that learning-based jammers can adapt in turn[17].

Joint doctrine treats this as spectrum management within electromagnetic spectrum operations: planning frequency use, controlling friendly emissions, and deconflicting friendly EA from friendly communications so that one's own jamming does not deny one's own links[1].

4Navigation and positioning

Global navigation satellite systems (GNSS), including GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, and BeiDou, supply position, navigation, and timing (PNT) to military and civil users worldwide. Their signals are transmitted from about 20,000 km and arrive at the Earth's surface far below the thermal noise floor of a receiver. The receiver recovers them only through the processing gain of their spreading codes[21,22]. That weakness makes GNSS one of the most exposed targets in the spectrum[23,24].

4.1 GNSS jamming and spoofing

Jamming raises the noise in the GNSS band until the receiver cannot acquire or track the satellites. Because the wanted signals are so weak, a transmitter of low power can deny reception over a wide area[21,23]. The US Volpe Center's 2001 assessment concluded that GPS was susceptible to unintentional and deliberate interference and recommended that critical transportation users keep backup systems[23]. In the United States, the operation, marketing, or sale of jamming equipment is illegal under federal law, and the government enforces this prohibition[25].

Spoofing is more insidious. A spoofer transmits counterfeit GNSS signals so that the receiver computes a false position or time without raising an obvious alarm[24,26]. Humphreys and colleagues distinguished three levels of sophistication[26]:

  • Simplistic spoofing broadcasts signals from a signal simulator with no synchronisation to the real constellation. It usually forces the receiver to lose lock and reacquire, which is detectable.
  • Intermediate spoofing synchronises the counterfeit signals with the real ones as seen at the target receiver, so the receiver's tracking loops can be captured smoothly and then drawn away.
  • Sophisticated spoofing uses multiple coordinated transmitters to also reproduce the spatial structure of the real signals, defeating single-antenna angle-of-arrival checks.

Meaconing, the delayed rebroadcast of real signals, is a related technique that does not require knowledge of the signal structure[21,24]. Kerns and colleagues later demonstrated in controlled tests that capture of a GNSS-dependent small unmanned aircraft's navigation could bring its flight path under external influence, which highlighted the safety stakes for autonomous systems[27].

4.2 Inertial navigation as a complementary system

An inertial navigation system (INS) measures specific force with accelerometers and angular rate with gyroscopes, then integrates them to track attitude, velocity, and position from a known starting point[22,28]. It emits nothing and receives nothing, so it cannot be jammed or spoofed by radio signals. Its weakness is drift. Sensor biases and noise are integrated over time, so errors grow without bound, and the rate of growth depends strongly on sensor grade[22,28].

GNSS and INS have complementary error characteristics. GNSS errors are bounded but the signal can be lost; INS errors grow but the output is continuous and smooth. Integration with a Kalman filter combines the two. Groves describes loosely coupled integration (combining GNSS position and velocity solutions with the INS), tightly coupled integration (combining raw pseudorange and range-rate measurements), and deep or ultra-tight integration (in which the INS aids the GNSS receiver's tracking loops)[22]. Tighter coupling keeps the navigation solution usable with fewer satellites and improves tracking under interference. During a GNSS outage, the INS carries the solution forward, and the quality of its sensors determines how long that solution stays useful[22,28].

The INS also helps detect spoofing. A spoofed GNSS solution that drifts away from the true trajectory produces measurement innovations that are inconsistent with the inertial prediction. Monitoring those innovations is one of the consistency checks discussed in Section 4.4[22,24].

4.3 Alternative positioning, navigation, and timing

Dependence on one source of PNT is now treated as a national-level risk. US Executive Order 13905 (2020) directed federal agencies to identify PNT systems and services and to develop PNT profiles that help critical-infrastructure owners use them responsibly[29]. Two documents followed from it. NIST IR 8323 applies the Cybersecurity Framework to PNT, organising the guidance under the functions identify, protect, detect, respond, and recover[30]. The DHS Resilient PNT Conformance Framework defines levels of resilience for PNT equipment, from basic protection against corrupted inputs to the ability to keep operating and recover when sources are compromised[31].

Alternative and complementary PNT sources reduce dependence on GNSS. Those discussed in the textbook and government literature include[22,32]:

  • Terrestrial radionavigation, such as enhanced Loran (eLoran), which uses high-power, low-frequency signals that are very different from GNSS and therefore not affected by the same interference.
  • Signals from other satellite constellations and orbits, including multi-constellation GNSS receivers and services from low Earth orbit, which arrive with higher power.
  • Terrain and feature-referenced navigation, which matches altimeter, imaging, or map data against stored references.
  • Vision and other environment-based sensors, such as visual odometry and signals of opportunity from existing transmitters.
  • Precision clocks that keep accurate time through a GNSS outage (holdover), and timing distributed over fibre networks, which matter for communications, power, and financial systems that use GNSS mainly for time.

The US Department of Transportation's 2021 demonstration report evaluated a range of such technologies and concluded that no single one can replace GPS for all users, and that a combination of sources suited to each application is required[32].

4.4 Detection of anomalous navigation signals

Psiaki and Humphreys provide a widely cited survey of spoofing defences, which they group by the signal property each one examines[24]. Detection works best when several independent checks are combined, because a sophisticated spoofer can defeat any single check[24,31].

Both NIST and DHS treat detection as a function that must be followed by response and recovery, such as excluding a suspect source and switching to a trusted alternative[30,31].

5Infrared and electro-optical countermeasures

Infrared (IR) sensors detect the thermal radiation of a target. Radiated power rises with the fourth power of absolute temperature, according to the Stefan-Boltzmann law, and the peak wavelength shifts shorter as temperature rises, so hot engine parts and exhaust plumes are strong sources in the mid-wave IR band[34]. Passive IR seekers emit nothing, so a target cannot detect them through ES as it can a radar, and IR-guided missiles have historically been a leading cause of aircraft losses[35,36]. EO countermeasures therefore combine warning, expendable decoys, active jamming, and signature reduction[35,36].

5.1 Flares and infrared decoys

A decoy flare is a pyrotechnic source whose IR output, in the seeker's band, exceeds the target's. Released at the right moment, it draws a seeker that tracks the brightest point in its field of view, or the centroid of the radiation, away from the aircraft[35,36]. Classic flares used magnesium-based compositions that burn very hot[36].

The development of seeker counter-countermeasures shows the measure and countermeasure cycle clearly[35,36]:

  • Spectral discrimination. A very hot flare has a different ratio of short to long wavelength energy than an aircraft. Two-colour seekers compare bands to reject it, and decoy designers answered with spectrally matched flares.
  • Kinematic discrimination. A flare decelerates and falls behind the aircraft as soon as it is released. Seekers can reject sources that separate in this way, and designers answered with kinematic decoys that keep moving with the aircraft for longer.
  • Imaging seekers. Imaging IR seekers form a picture of the target and can use shape and size to reject point-like decoys, which pushes decoys toward larger, distributed, or more realistic signatures.

Flares are most effective when they are dispensed in response to a warning of a missile launch, which is why missile warning systems that detect the missile's ultraviolet or IR emission are part of the same protective suite[9,35].

5.2 Laser warning and countermeasures

Lasers are used on the battlefield as rangefinders, target designators for laser-guided weapons, and guidance beams for beam-riding missiles[34,35]. A laser warning receiver detects the illumination, characterises the wavelength and pulse format, and estimates the direction of the source, so that the platform can respond[35,36]. Responses discussed in the literature include manoeuvre, obscurants such as smoke and aerosols that block the optical path in the relevant bands, and decoys or countermeasures directed against the guidance link[36].

Lasers are also used in countermeasures. Directed infrared countermeasures (DIRCM) point a modulated IR source, now usually a laser, at an incoming missile seeker. The modulation corrupts the seeker's tracking signal, and the narrow beam delivers much higher intensity at the seeker than earlier lamp-based jammers that radiated in all directions[35]. DIRCM depends on missile warning to detect the threat and on a fast, accurate pointing and tracking system to hold the beam on it[9,35].

5.3 Sensor dazzling

Dazzling is the temporary degradation of an optical sensor or of human vision by intense light, without permanent damage. In imaging sensors it shows up as saturation of pixels around the laser spot, blooming and crosstalk that spread the effect beyond the spot, and stray light scattered inside the optics, which together can hide large parts of the scene[35,37]. The effect depends on the wavelength, the laser's power and temporal format, the sensor's spectral response and dynamic range, and the design of the optics[37].

Protection measures are the electronic protection counterpart. Ritt and Eberle evaluated approaches including spectral filters that block known laser wavelengths, optical limiters that become less transmitting at high intensity, and sensor and processing designs with greater dynamic range. They found that each has limits, for example fixed filters do not protect against tunable or unexpected wavelengths[37].

The use of lasers against people is constrained by international law. Protocol IV to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (1995) prohibits laser weapons specifically designed to cause permanent blindness to unenhanced vision, and requires parties to take feasible precautions to avoid such blindness when using other laser systems[38].

5.4 Signature reduction and multispectral camouflage

IR signature reduction lowers the contrast between a platform and its background in the bands a seeker uses. Because radiated power depends on emissivity and on the fourth power of temperature, the main methods are to cool hot surfaces and exhaust (for example by mixing exhaust with ambient air), to shield hot parts from likely lines of sight, and to use coatings whose emissivity is low in the threat bands[34,36].

Multispectral camouflage aims to reduce a platform's signature across visible, IR, laser, and radar bands at once. The requirements conflict. Low IR emissivity implies high IR reflectance, while radar camouflage generally calls for absorption at microwave frequencies, and visual camouflage requires a particular colour and pattern[11,39]. A material that lowers emission in the IR bands where a seeker looks can also trap heat, unless it lets the surface radiate in the bands that seekers do not use. Zhu and colleagues demonstrated a layered structure that combines low emission in the mid- and long-wave IR detection windows, radiative cooling through the intermediate non-window band, low reflectance at common laser wavelengths, and microwave absorption, while remaining compatible with visible camouflage[39]. Work of this kind shows that signature management is moving from separate solutions for each band toward integrated designs[39].

6Conclusion

Across radar, communications, navigation, and EO systems, electronic attack exploits a small number of recurring principles. It overwhelms a receiver with energy, it feeds a receiver plausible but false information, it presents a false or reduced signature, or it removes a dependence on one channel[3,4]. Each technique has a matching protective measure: processing gain against noise, consistency checks and coherence against deception, Doppler discrimination against chaff, spread spectrum and adaptive antennas against communications jamming, sensor fusion and authentication against GNSS spoofing, and multispectral discrimination against IR decoys[5,18,24,35]. The policy literature on PNT reaches the same conclusion as the technical literature: resilience comes from diversity of sources, monitoring for anomalies, and planned response and recovery, rather than from any single defence[30–32].
