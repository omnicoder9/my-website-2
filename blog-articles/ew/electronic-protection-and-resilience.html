1Introduction and scope

US joint doctrine divides electromagnetic warfare (EW) into three divisions: electromagnetic attack, electromagnetic support, and electromagnetic protection[1,2]. This article uses the older and still common names electronic attack (EA), electronic support (ES), and electronic protection (EP). Electronic protection is defined as the actions taken to protect personnel, facilities, and equipment from any effects of friendly, neutral, or enemy use of the electromagnetic spectrum, and from naturally occurring phenomena, that degrade, neutralize, or destroy friendly combat capability[1]. The older engineering term for the same discipline is electronic counter-countermeasures (ECCM)[3,4].

Electronic protection is therefore broader than defeating a hostile jammer. It also covers unintentional interference from friendly and neutral emitters, spectrum management and deconfliction, and the operational discipline of controlling one's own emissions[1,2]. Civil guidance on positioning, navigation, and timing (PNT) frames the same goal as resilience. The US Department of Homeland Security (DHS) describes resilient PNT equipment in terms of three functions: prevent corruption of the solution, respond to detected anomalies, and recover to a proper working state[5]. That framing fits electronic protection well. Some measures make a system harder to attack, some detect and contain an attack, and some restore service when the first two fail.

The sections below cover nine families of measures: anti-jamming, anti-spoofing, frequency agility, spread-spectrum communications, adaptive antennas, filtering and signal processing, cryptography and authentication, redundancy, and emission control (EMCON). A closing section explains why cryptography protects the information a signal carries but does not by itself prevent radio-frequency (RF) jamming. The treatment stays at the level of published textbooks, peer-reviewed literature, and public doctrine.

Terminology

The 2020 edition of JP 3-85 renamed "electronic warfare" as "electromagnetic warfare" and placed it within joint electromagnetic spectrum operations (JEMSO)[1]. The acronyms EA, ES, and EP are unchanged. Most engineering literature, including the textbooks cited here, still uses "electronic."

2Anti-jamming

Anti-jamming (AJ) measures aim to keep communications and sensing working when an adversary deliberately radiates interference. A communications jammer does not need to overpower the transmitter. It needs only to deliver enough power at the receiver to push the error rate above what the link can tolerate[6,7]. The contest is therefore usually measured by the jamming-to-signal ratio (J/S) at the victim receiver.

The jamming-to-signal ratio

For line-of-sight propagation, and with all quantities in decibels, the J/S at a communications receiver can be written[8,6]:

J/S = ERPJ − ERPS − LJ + LS + GRJ − GR

Here ERPJ and ERPS are the effective radiated powers of the jammer and the wanted transmitter, LJ and LS are the propagation losses from each to the receiver, and GRJ and GR are the receiving antenna's gains toward the jammer and toward the wanted transmitter. Each term points to a family of protective measures. Higher transmit power and shorter links raise the signal. Directional and adaptive antennas lower GRJ relative to GR. Spread-spectrum processing and error-correcting codes raise the J/S the receiver can tolerate before it fails[6,9].

Jamming margin

Spread-spectrum texts express AJ capability through the jamming margin, the amount by which the jammer-to-signal power ratio can exceed the ratio the demodulator needs and still leave the link usable[9,10,11]:

MJ = Gp − (Eb/NJ)req − Lsys   (dB)

Gp is the processing gain (Section 5), (Eb/NJ)req is the bit-energy-to-jamming-density ratio needed for the required error rate, and Lsys collects implementation losses. Forward error correction and interleaving reduce the required Eb/NJ, especially against partial-band and pulsed jammers, which concentrate power to cause bursts of errors[9,10,12].

Layered anti-jam design

No single technique defeats every jammer. Barrage jamming spreads power over a wide band and is weakened by processing gain. Spot and narrowband jamming are countered by frequency agility and notch filtering. Directional jammers are countered by antenna nulling. Reactive jammers, which transmit only when they sense activity on the channel, call for low detectability and fast changes in the transmission pattern[7,13,14]. Practical AJ systems therefore combine waveform design, antenna processing, receiver signal processing, and network-level measures such as relaying and alternative routes[6,9]. Radar uses the same layering: frequency agility, sidelobe blanking and cancellation, pulse compression, and constant false-alarm rate (CFAR) processing each address a different class of electronic attack[4,3].

Detecting that jamming is under way is itself part of anti-jamming. It lets a network switch to a more robust mode rather than misreading interference as congestion or equipment failure. Studies of wireless networks show that signal strength or packet delivery ratio alone can misclassify jamming, and that combining measurements, for example delivery ratio with received signal strength, gives more reliable detection[14,13]. GNSS receivers similarly monitor automatic gain control (AGC) levels and correlator outputs to detect interference[15,16].

3Anti-spoofing

Spoofing, or deception, transmits counterfeit signals that a receiver accepts as genuine. Jamming produces an obvious outage. A successful spoof is more dangerous because the victim may continue to operate on false data without knowing it[17,7]. Anti-spoofing measures detect manipulated or counterfeit signals and stop them from corrupting the output.

GNSS spoofing defenses

Satellite navigation is the best-documented case because civil GNSS signals have published structures and arrive at very low power. Psiaki and Humphreys group the defenses into several families[17]:

  • Signal-processing checks in a single-antenna receiver. These include monitoring absolute received power, watching for distortion of the code correlation function, and searching continually for a second copy of each satellite signal. Most spoofers must overpower the authentic signal to capture the tracking loops, which leaves traces in power and correlation shape.
  • Cryptographic defenses. These include navigation message authentication (NMA) and spreading-code authentication, discussed in Section 8. They also include cross-correlating encrypted military codes between receivers.
  • Drift monitoring. The receiver checks whether the clock offset or position changes faster than a stable oscillator, an inertial sensor, or vehicle dynamics allow.
  • Signal-geometry defenses. Authentic signals arrive from widely separated directions. A single spoofing transmitter delivers all counterfeit signals from one direction. Multiple antennas, or a moving antenna, can reveal that inconsistency through carrier-phase measurements.

Psiaki and Humphreys stress that each defense has gaps and that layered, multi-pronged strategies are more robust[17]. Humphreys showed that cryptographic signals remain open to a security-code estimation and replay attack unless the receiver applies a careful statistical detection test[18]. Integrating GNSS with inertial sensors also helps. An inertial navigation system (INS) is self-contained, so a sudden disagreement between GNSS and inertial solutions flags a possible spoof[19,16].

Communications and radar deception

In communications, deception includes imitative messages, replay of recorded traffic, and injection of false control data[7,6]. The main defenses are cryptographic message authentication and replay protection (Section 8) together with procedural checks such as authentication challenges[2]. In radar, deceptive jammers create false targets or pull range and velocity tracking gates. ECCM responses include frequency agility, pulse-to-pulse waveform diversity, and consistency checks between measured range rate and Doppler[4,3].

4Frequency agility

Frequency agility means changing operating frequency to avoid interference, deny an adversary a fixed target, or both. It ranges from occasional manual channel changes to automatic, pseudorandom hopping many times per second[6,9].

Frequency hopping

In frequency-hopping (FH) systems the carrier jumps among many channels according to a pseudorandom pattern known to the transmitter and receiver. The idea is old. A 1942 US patent by Hedy Kiesler Markey (the actress Hedy Lamarr) and George Antheil described a radio guidance system whose transmitter and receiver changed frequency in synchronism[20]. A jammer that cannot predict the next hop must either spread power across the whole hopping band, which dilutes it, or detect and follow each hop[9,7].

A follower (or repeater) jammer has to intercept the hop, measure its frequency, and transmit on it before the dwell ends. Its delay includes processing time and the extra propagation path from transmitter to jammer to receiver. Faster hopping and wider separation between jammer and receiver therefore defeat followers that are too slow or too far away[9,7,6]. Fast hopping, with several hops per data symbol, also provides frequency diversity. With error-correcting codes, the link can tolerate a fraction of hops being jammed or faded[9,10].

Adaptive frequency selection and radar agility

Adaptive systems sense the spectrum and steer away from occupied or jammed channels instead of hopping blindly. This works against static interference but can be exploited if an adversary learns the adaptation rule[13,7]. Spectrum management doctrine treats frequency assignment, deconfliction, and reserve frequencies as part of electromagnetic protection[1,2]. In radar, pulse-to-pulse frequency agility forces a noise jammer to spread its power across the radar's whole agile bandwidth. It also defeats repeater jammers that rely on predicting the next pulse's frequency[4,3].

5Spread-spectrum communications

A spread-spectrum system occupies a bandwidth much wider than the minimum needed for its data, and the spreading is set by a code independent of the data. The receiver uses a synchronized copy of the code to despread the signal[11,9]. The two classical forms are direct-sequence spread spectrum (DSSS) and frequency hopping. Time hopping and hybrid forms also exist[10,9].

Processing gain

The key AJ property is processing gain, approximately the ratio of spread bandwidth W to data rate R[11,9]:

Gp ≈ W / R   or   Gp(dB) ≈ 10 log10(W/R)

When the receiver correlates with the spreading code, the wanted signal collapses back into the data bandwidth. Narrowband interference is spread across W, and only about 1/Gp of it falls in the data bandwidth after filtering. A system spreading 10 kbit/s over 10 MHz has a processing gain of about 30 dB[11,9]. Processing gain is gained against a jammer that does not know the code. It does nothing against a jammer that can synchronize to the code, which is why spreading sequences for military use are generated cryptographically (Section 8)[10,9].

Low probability of intercept

Spreading lowers power spectral density, which can push the signal below the noise floor of an unintended receiver and make detection harder. This low probability of intercept and detection (LPI/LPD) is an EP benefit closely tied to EMCON (Section 10)[6,9]. It is not absolute. Radiometers and feature detectors that exploit periodicities can still detect spread signals, given enough integration time and a favorable geometry[9,7].

Limits

Spread spectrum has known weaknesses. A strong nearby transmitter can overwhelm weaker distant ones in DSSS, the near-far problem[11,9]. A jammer with enough power to raise noise density across the whole spread band defeats any processing gain. Synchronization and acquisition are often the most vulnerable phase, because the receiver must find the code before it gains the full benefit of despreading[10,9]. GNSS illustrates the trade. Civil GPS signals already benefit from a coarse/acquisition code processing gain, yet their very low received power leaves them easy to jam[16,15].

6Adaptive antennas

An adaptive antenna, or adaptive array, combines the outputs of several antenna elements with adjustable complex weights. The array then shapes its own reception pattern, keeping gain toward the wanted signal and placing nulls toward interference[21,22,23]. In terms of the J/S equation in Section 2, it lowers the antenna gain toward the jammer, GRJ, by tens of decibels while largely preserving the gain toward the wanted signal.

Optimal and adaptive beamforming

The foundations were laid in the 1960s and 1970s. Widrow and colleagues applied the least-mean-squares (LMS) algorithm to antenna arrays[21]. Applebaum derived the weights that maximize signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio and built a sidelobe canceller that adapts through feedback loops[22]. Capon introduced the minimum-variance distortionless-response (MVDR) beamformer, which minimizes output power subject to unity gain in the look direction[24,25]:

w = R−1a(θ0) / [aH(θ0) R−1a(θ0)]

Here R is the covariance matrix of the array signals and a(θ0) is the steering vector toward the wanted direction. Frost generalized this to the linearly constrained minimum-variance (LCMV) beamformer and gave an adaptive algorithm for it[26,25]. Because jammers are usually far stronger than the wanted signal, they dominate R, and minimizing output power drives nulls toward them automatically[23,25].

Degrees of freedom and practical limits

An array with N elements has N − 1 degrees of freedom. In general it can null up to N − 1 independent narrowband interferers while keeping one constraint toward the wanted signal[23,25]. Wideband interference, multipath, and element mismatch consume extra degrees of freedom. Tapped-delay-line (space-time) processing restores performance over a wide band[23,26]. Null depth is limited by channel mismatch, calibration error, and finite averaging time when estimating R[23,25]. A jammer close in angle to the wanted signal cannot be nulled without also suppressing the wanted signal[23].

For GNSS, controlled reception pattern antennas (CRPAs) apply these principles. Because the satellite signals lie below the thermal noise, a CRPA can minimize total output power without knowing where the satellites are. Strong jammers are nulled while the satellites, buried in noise, pass through[16,19]. Radar sidelobe cancellers and sidelobe blanking are related techniques that use auxiliary antennas to remove interference entering through the sidelobes[4,3].

7Filtering and signal processing

Filtering and signal processing separate wanted signals from noise and interference wherever they differ in frequency, time, code, or statistical structure[27,28]. These techniques complement spread spectrum and adaptive arrays. They are often the cheapest protection to add, because many are implemented in receiver software.

Front-end selectivity and dynamic range

The first line of defense is the RF front end. Preselector filters reject out-of-band energy before it reaches amplifiers and converters, where it could cause saturation, intermodulation, or desensitization. Adequate linearity, automatic gain control, and analog-to-digital converter resolution keep strong interference from overwhelming the receiver before digital processing can act on it[15,16,8].

Interference excision

Milstein's review describes two broad classes of narrowband interference rejection for direct-sequence systems[27]. The first is adaptive time-domain prediction filtering. Because a wideband DSSS signal is nearly unpredictable from sample to sample while a narrowband interferer is highly predictable, a linear predictor estimates and subtracts the interference. The second is transform-domain processing. The receiver takes a fast Fourier transform or similar transform, removes or attenuates bins containing the interference, and transforms back[27,28]. GNSS receivers use the same principle in adaptive notch filters and frequency-domain excision[16,15]. Pulsed interference can be suppressed by pulse blanking, which zeroes samples during strong pulses at a small cost in signal energy[16].

Matched filtering, coding, and diversity

Matched filtering maximizes output signal-to-noise ratio for a known waveform in white noise and underlies correlation detection in both radar and communications[12,4]. Radar pulse compression combines a long transmitted pulse, for energy, with a matched filter, for range resolution. It also forces a jammer that does not reproduce the code to compete against the compression gain[4]. In communications, forward error correction, interleaving, and diversity combining turn bursts of jamming or fading into scattered errors that a decoder can correct. Receivers that estimate when symbols are jammed and erase them improve this further[9,12,10]. Radar receivers apply CFAR detection so that a rise in noise from jamming does not flood the display with false alarms. Detection range is reduced, but the false-alarm rate stays controlled[4,3].

8Cryptography and authentication

Cryptography provides confidentiality, data integrity, and authentication[29,30,31]. In electronic protection it serves two related roles. Communications security (COMSEC) protects the content of messages. Transmission security (TRANSEC) protects the transmission itself, for example by generating hopping patterns and spreading codes from secret keys so that an adversary cannot predict, follow, or imitate them[10,9,6].

Integrity and message authentication

Shannon's 1949 paper laid the theoretical foundations of secrecy systems[29]. Modern systems separate confidentiality from integrity and authenticity. A message authentication code (MAC) such as HMAC uses a shared secret key to let a receiver detect any change to a message and confirm that it came from a key holder[30]. A digital signature uses public-key cryptography, so anyone holding the public key can verify origin and integrity without being able to forge signatures[31]. Either mechanism, combined with sequence numbers or timestamps, defends against forged and replayed messages, which are the communications equivalent of spoofing[7,13].

Signal authentication in GNSS

Authentication can extend to the navigation signal itself. In navigation message authentication, the satellite broadcasts cryptographic data that let a receiver verify that the navigation message came from the system operator[17,32]. Galileo's Open Service Navigation Message Authentication (OSNMA) uses a TESLA (timed efficient stream loss-tolerant authentication) scheme. Delayed disclosure of keys from a one-way chain allows efficient authentication of broadcast data, and the root key is protected by a public-key digital signature[32,33]. EUSPA declared the OSNMA Initial Service operational on 24 July 2025, bringing signal-level data authentication to civil users[34,33].

Navigation message authentication is valuable but bounded. It authenticates the data in the message, not the timing of the ranging signal. An adversary can still record and rebroadcast authentic signals with a delay (meaconing), or estimate the security code on the fly and replay it, unless the receiver adds time checks and statistical tests[17,18]. Spreading-code authentication and encrypted military codes provide stronger protection at the ranging level. They require key management and, for symmetric schemes, delayed key release[17,18].

9Redundancy

Redundancy means having alternative communications paths, sensors, and navigation sources, so that loss or corruption of any one does not cause mission failure. It accepts that some electronic attacks will succeed and limits their effect[5,35,1].

Communications

Military and emergency communications planners use PACE plans, which designate primary, alternate, contingency, and emergency means of communication. They also set the conditions for moving between them[36,2]. Real diversity requires that the alternatives do not share a weakness. HF ground wave, VHF/UHF line-of-sight radio, satellite links, wired networks, and couriers differ in frequency band, propagation mechanism, and the jamming geometry needed to deny them[6,7]. Mesh and relay networks add path diversity, letting traffic route around a jammed node or link[13,6].

Navigation and timing

GNSS dependence is the most widely discussed single point of failure. US Executive Order 13905 directs federal agencies to identify and promote the responsible use of PNT services, and it led to the NIST Foundational PNT Profile[37,35]. The NIST profile applies the Cybersecurity Framework functions to PNT. It recommends identifying PNT dependencies, protecting and monitoring sources, and planning response and recovery, including the use of multiple independent sources[35].

The DHS Resilient PNT Conformance Framework defines cumulative resilience levels[5]. Level 1 requires recovery after the threat is removed. Level 2 provides a solution during the threat with degradation that may be unbounded. Level 3 bounds that degradation and requires cross-verification of solutions among sources. Level 4 provides a solution without degradation, and it requires diversity of PNT source technology so that a single common-mode threat cannot defeat every source[5]. Common complements to GNSS include inertial navigation, which cannot be jammed but drifts over time; stable local clocks for holdover; terrestrial radio navigation; and vision or terrain-referenced navigation[19,16]. Integrating these through a filter that weighs each source by its consistency gives both redundancy and a means of detecting a corrupted source[19,5].

Sensing

The same logic applies to sensors. A force that can cue engagements from radar, passive electronic support, and electro-optical sensors is harder to blind than one relying on a single radar band[3,1]. Multistatic and netted sensors spread the geometry an adversary must jam and allow fusion to reject a deceived sensor[4,3].

10Emission control (EMCON)

US doctrine defines emission control as the selective and controlled use of electromagnetic, acoustic, or other emitters to optimize command and control capabilities while minimizing, for operations security, detection by enemy sensors, mutual interference among friendly systems, and enemy interference with the ability to execute a military deception plan[1]. EMCON is the most operational of the protective measures. It relies on how equipment is used more than on how it is built.

Why emissions matter

Every emission is a potential intelligence source. Adversary electronic support can detect a transmitter, classify it by its signal parameters, locate it by direction finding or time-difference methods, and pass that information to targeting or jamming systems[8,6,7]. Emissions that reveal unit type, activity level, or a change in posture compromise operations security even when the content is encrypted[2,1].

EMCON measures

  • Limiting when emitters transmit. This includes graded EMCON conditions up to full radio silence, short transmissions, and burst messaging[1,2].
  • Limiting power. Commanders use the minimum power needed to close the link and so reduce the range at which the emission can be intercepted[6].
  • Limiting direction. Directional antennas and terrain masking place adversary receivers in sidelobes or behind obstacles[6,2].
  • Limiting detectability. LPI/LPD waveforms such as spread spectrum lower spectral density and make interception harder[6,9].
  • Remoting and controlled signatures. Units can move emitters away from command posts. Emissions can also be shaped deliberately in support of deception plans[2,1].

EMCON involves a trade-off between protection and capability. Silence protects against detection but denies the force its own sensors and communications. Doctrine therefore treats EMCON as a planned, adjustable posture coordinated through joint electromagnetic spectrum operations[1]. Wartime reserve modes, which are characteristics and procedures kept unused in peacetime so that an adversary cannot prepare countermeasures, follow the same reasoning[1].

11Why cryptography does not prevent jamming

Key point

Cryptography protects the information carried by a signal. It does not, by itself, stop RF jamming.

Jamming acts on the physical channel. A jammer raises the noise and interference power at the receiver until the demodulator can no longer recover symbols reliably[7,6]. The jammer does not need to read, understand, or alter the message to succeed. It needs only enough power at the right frequency and time to drive the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio below the demodulator threshold[9,13]. Encryption transforms the bits being sent. It does not change their power, bandwidth, or timing, so an encrypted signal is as easy to jam as an unencrypted signal on the same waveform[7,13].

Cryptography does contribute to anti-jamming in three indirect ways, and each depends on something other than the cipher:

  • Keyed waveforms (TRANSEC). When a secret key generates the hopping pattern or spreading code, the jammer cannot predict or synchronize to the signal. The resulting protection comes from the processing gain of the wide bandwidth or the hop set. The key only makes sure the adversary cannot obtain that gain too[10,9].
  • Authentication against deception. MACs, digital signatures, and signal authentication let a receiver reject forged or spoofed messages[30,31,17]. This turns a deception attack into, at worst, a denial attack. The receiver loses service rather than accepting false data. It does not restore service.
  • Hiding traffic patterns. Encrypting headers and control traffic hides which packets matter. That makes selective or protocol-aware jamming harder, although a jammer can still deny the whole channel by brute force[13,14].

Authentication also offers no help once the authentic signal itself is jammed. A receiver that correctly rejects unauthenticated data still has nothing to use, which is why authenticated GNSS must still be paired with interference mitigation and alternative sources[17,5]. For these reasons, design guidance treats cryptographic protection as one layer to be combined with the physical-layer and operational measures in Sections 2 to 7, 9, and 10. The DHS and NIST frameworks express the same principle as defense in depth across prevent, respond, and recover functions[5,35].

12Conclusion

Electronic protection is a layered discipline. Spread spectrum, frequency agility, and filtering raise the power an adversary must spend to deny a link. Adaptive antennas exploit the jammer's direction. Anti-spoofing and authentication stop false signals from being accepted. Redundancy limits the effect of the attacks that still succeed. EMCON reduces the information the adversary has to aim with in the first place[1,6,9,17]. Cryptography is essential for confidentiality and authenticity, but it addresses the information rather than the channel. Resilience against jamming comes from combining it with physical-layer, spatial, and operational protection, and from planning to keep working when some protection fails[5,35,7].
