Modelling, Simulation and Testing in Electronic Warfare

Electronic warfare systems are hard to evaluate in real operational environments: the conditions that matter are dangerous, expensive, unlawful to create casually, or simply unavailable on demand. This article describes the models and controlled tests that fill the gap, from link budgets and propagation predictions to hardware-in-the-loop facilities, calibrated instruments, authorized ranges and the metrics used to judge the results.

1Introduction and scope

US joint doctrine treats electronic warfare (EW) as military action that uses electromagnetic energy to control the electromagnetic spectrum or to attack an adversary, and places it within joint electromagnetic spectrum operations [1]. Every one of its activities, whether electronic attack, electronic protection or electronic support, depends on interactions that are hard to observe directly: a jammer's effect on a radar's detection statistics, a receiver's ability to find one pulse train among thousands, or a navigation set's behaviour when its satellite signals are drowned out. Those interactions are shaped by geometry, terrain, weather, antenna patterns, signal density and the adversary's own choices, and they are rarely available for measurement on demand.

Operational evaluation is therefore difficult. The NATO handbook on EW test and evaluation describes EW performance as strongly dependent on scenario, so that combat-representative conditions usually require complex models and simulations [2]. Open-air ranges hold only a limited number of threat simulators, which constrains realism in dense threat or signal environments [2]. The US Director, Operational Test and Evaluation (DOT&E) reported in its FY 2025 assessment of test resources that modern threat and friendly capabilities exceed the airspace available on open-air ranges, that more emitters and surrogates are needed to represent realistic signal density and congestion, and that adversary development is outpacing the development of validated, high-fidelity models [3].

The response has been a layered test process. The NATO handbook groups EW test resources into six categories: modelling and simulation (M&S), measurement facilities, system integration laboratories (SILs), hardware-in-the-loop (HITL) facilities, installed system test facilities (ISTFs) and open-air ranges (OARs) [2]. M&S runs through the whole process in a predict, test, compare cycle: results are predicted before each test, compared with what was measured, and the models are then used to extrapolate beyond the conditions that could be tested [2]. Adamy's text on EW modelling and simulation develops the same idea from the engineer's side, treating link equations, propagation and engagement models as tools for predicting what a test should show [4]. Figure 1 sketches the resulting ladder.

Formal acquisition policy reflects the same logic. DoD Instruction 5000.89 requires that test tools and infrastructure supporting acquisition decisions, including M&S, be verified, validated and accredited (VV&A) by the intended user, and states that initial operational test and evaluation must rest on more than an evaluation based exclusively on computer modelling and simulation [5]. DoD Instruction 5000.61 defines verification as determining that a model accurately represents its developer's conceptual description and specifications, validation as determining the degree to which it is an accurate representation of the real world from the perspective of its intended use, and accreditation as the official certification that it is acceptable for a specific purpose [6].

This article follows the chain of tools in the order an engineer meets them: link budgets and propagation models (Sections 2 and 3), radar and communications simulation (Section 4), Monte Carlo methods (Section 5), signal processing testbeds (Section 6), software-in-the-loop and hardware-in-the-loop testing (Sections 7 and 8), RF test equipment (Section 9), controlled RF environments and authorized ranges (Section 10) and the metrics used to judge results (Section 11). Section 12 shows how VV&A ties them together. Companion articles in this series cover electronic attack, protection and support in their own right.

2Link budgets

A link budget is the bookkeeping of gains and losses between a transmitter and a receiver. It is the first model built for almost any EW question, because it answers whether a signal will be detectable, whether a jammer can overcome a wanted signal, and how much margin remains for everything the simple model leaves out.

Free-space transmission

Friis gave the free-space transmission formula in 1946 [7]. With transmit power Pt, antenna gains Gt and Gr, wavelength λ and range d, the received power is

Pr = Pt Gt Gr (λ / 4πd)2(1)

The factor (4πd/λ)2 is the free-space basic transmission loss. ITU-R Recommendation P.525 gives it in engineering units as

Lbf = 32.4 + 20 log10 f + 20 log10 d   dB(2)

with f in MHz and d in km [8]. Loss therefore grows by 6 dB for each doubling of distance or frequency. In decibel form the budget becomes a sum: received power equals transmitter power plus antenna gains minus path loss and minus any feeder, polarization, pointing or atmospheric losses.

Noise and sensitivity

The other side of the budget is noise. Johnson measured, and Nyquist explained, the thermal noise generated in any conductor, whose available power in bandwidth B is kTB [9,10]. At the standard reference temperature of 290 K this is about −174 dBm in each hertz of bandwidth. Friis defined the noise figure F of a receiver as the ratio of input to output signal-to-noise ratio, and showed that for a cascade of stages

F = F1 + (F2 − 1)/G1 + (F3 − 1)/(G1G2) + …(3)

so the first stage dominates when its gain is high [11]. Receiver sensitivity, the smallest signal that meets a required signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), then follows as

Smin = −174 + 10 log10 B + NF + SNRreq   dBm(4)

with B in Hz and the noise figure NF and required SNR in dB [12,13]. Link margin is the received power minus this sensitivity. Sklar presents the communications form in terms of the ratio of bit energy to noise density, Eb/N0, which links the budget directly to bit error rate [12].

Atmospheric gases and rain add frequency-dependent attenuation that ITU-R gives in P.676 and P.838 respectively, and these terms become significant at millimetre-wave frequencies [14,15].

Radar and jamming budgets

For a monostatic radar the same bookkeeping applies twice, once on the way out and once on the way back. The radar range equation in SNR form is

SNR = Pt G2 λ2 σ / [(4π)3 R4 k T0 B F L](5)

where σ is the target radar cross section, R the range and L the system losses [16,17]. The fourth-power dependence on range is the root of most EW engagement arithmetic. A self-protection jammer on the target only suffers one-way spreading loss, so the jamming-to-signal ratio for a main-beam, matched-bandwidth case is

J/S = (PjGj / PtGt) · 4πR2 / σ(6)

which falls as the target closes; the range at which the echo overcomes the jamming is the burn-through range [13,18]. For communications jamming, Adamy and Poisel give the corresponding expressions in terms of the jammer-to-receiver and transmitter-to-receiver distances, with the propagation exponent chosen to match the path (2 for free space, 4 for the two-ray model) [13,19]. A jamming budget therefore inherits every uncertainty in the propagation model, which leads to the next section.

3Propagation models

Propagation models replace the free-space term in a link budget with a prediction that accounts for the Earth, the atmosphere, terrain and obstacles. They range from closed-form formulas to numerical solutions of the wave equation, and the right choice depends on frequency, path geometry and the question being asked.

Physical mechanisms

Over a flat, reflecting Earth the direct and ground-reflected rays interfere. At ranges well beyond the antenna heights, the two-ray model gives

Pr ≈ Pt Gt Gr ht2 hr2 / d4(7)

so received power falls by 40 dB per decade of distance and becomes independent of frequency [20]. Atmospheric refraction bends rays towards the Earth; the usual engineering treatment uses an effective Earth radius of four-thirds the true radius, which gives a radio horizon of about 4.12(√h1 + √h2) km for antenna heights in metres [13,16]. Beyond the horizon, or behind obstacles, energy arrives by diffraction.

ITU-R Recommendation P.526 is the reference for diffraction. It characterizes an obstacle by the dimensionless parameter ν = h√[(2/λ)(1/d1 + 1/d2)], where h is the height of the obstacle above the direct path and d1, d2 are the distances to each terminal, and gives the single knife-edge loss as

J(ν) = 6.9 + 20 log10[√((ν − 0.1)2 + 1) + ν − 0.1]   dB(8)

for ν greater than about −0.78 [21]. At grazing incidence (ν = 0) the loss is about 6 dB, and it grows quickly as the obstacle intrudes further into the first Fresnel zone. P.526 also gives methods for multiple obstacles and for diffraction over a smooth spherical Earth [21].

Empirical and semi-empirical models

Okumura and colleagues measured VHF and UHF field strength in and around Tokyo and published the results as curves of median field strength against distance, frequency and antenna height [22]. Hata fitted closed-form expressions to those curves; for urban areas the median path loss is

L = 69.55 + 26.16 log f − 13.82 log hb − a(hm) + (44.9 − 6.55 log hb) log d(9)

with f in MHz (150 to 1500 MHz), base-station height hb in metres (30 to 200 m), distance d in km (1 to 20 km) and a correction a(hm) for mobile antenna height [23]. The European COST 231 action extended the Hata formulation to 1500 to 2000 MHz for personal communications bands [24]. These models capture urban clutter statistically, but they are tied to the environments in which they were measured and they do not use path-specific terrain.

Terrain-based and statistical prediction

The Longley-Rice model, now known as the Irregular Terrain Model (ITM), was developed at the Institute for Telecommunication Sciences to predict tropospheric transmission loss over irregular terrain using either a terrain profile (point-to-point mode) or statistical terrain parameters (area mode) [25,26]. Its guide covers frequencies from 20 MHz to 20 GHz and expresses its results in terms of time, location and situation variability, so that a prediction carries an explicit statement of reliability and confidence [26]. That feature makes ITM well suited to coverage and interference studies in which the question is not “what is the loss” but “how often will the loss be less than this”.

ITU-R provides internationally agreed methods. Recommendation P.1546 predicts field strength for point-to-area terrestrial services from 30 MHz to 4000 MHz from curves for given percentages of time and location, with corrections for terrain clearance and clutter [27]. Recommendation P.452 evaluates interference between stations on the Earth's surface at frequencies above about 0.1 GHz, and explicitly models the mechanisms that dominate for small time percentages, such as ducting and layer reflection, alongside diffraction and tropospheric scatter [28]. For an EW planner P.452 answers the question of how far an emitter's energy can reach under anomalous conditions, which matters both for intercept and for fratricide.

When the atmosphere is strongly stratified, for example over the sea where evaporation ducts form, ray methods break down. Parabolic equation methods solve a paraxial approximation of the wave equation by marching in range over a refractivity and terrain profile, and Levy's monograph is the standard treatment [29]. For short-range urban and indoor links, geometry-based stochastic channel models such as 3GPP TR 38.901 define path loss, shadowing, delay spread and angular spread for standardized scenarios from 0.5 to 100 GHz [30]. Small-scale fading in such models follows the Rayleigh or Rician statistics and the Doppler spectrum treated by Jakes [20,31].

Two practical points follow. First, propagation is usually the largest single source of uncertainty in an EW prediction, so model outputs should be treated as distributions rather than numbers, which is why ITM and the ITU-R methods report time and location percentages [26,27]. Second, the choice of model is itself an assumption that must be validated for the intended use, a point taken up in Section 12.

4Radar and communications simulation

Simulation extends the link budget from a single number to a description of system behaviour. It is useful to distinguish three levels of fidelity. Power-domain or budget-level models track signal, noise and interference powers and convert them into performance through analytical curves. Waveform-level or baseband simulations generate complex samples of the actual signals, pass them through channel and receiver models, and run the real processing algorithms. Mission-level or engagement simulations place many platforms with sensors, communications and weapons in a time-stepped or event-driven scenario. Jeruchim, Balaban and Shanmugan describe the methodology for waveform-level communications simulation, including the choice of sampling rate, the representation of bandpass signals by complex envelopes and the modelling of nonlinear devices [32]; Mahafza gives the equivalent treatment for radar [33].

Radar detection

The statistical theory of radar detection was laid down by Marcum, who computed detection probability for a steady target in Gaussian noise, and by Swerling, who extended it to targets whose cross-section fluctuates from scan to scan or pulse to pulse [34,35]. The resulting Swerling cases remain the standard target models in radar simulation [17]. For quick budget-level work, Albersheim gave a closed-form approximation to the SNR required for a given probability of detection PD and false-alarm probability PFA with a non-fluctuating target [36]:

SNRdB = −5 log10 N + [6.2 + 4.54/√(N + 0.44)] log10(A + 0.12AB + 1.7B)(10)

where N is the number of noncoherently integrated pulses, A = ln(0.62/PFA) and B = ln[PD/(1 − PD)]. For a single pulse with PD = 0.9 and PFA = 10−6, Equation (10) gives about 13.1 dB. Combining this with Equation (5) gives detection range; adding the jamming power of Equation (6) to the noise term gives the degraded detection range under jamming, which is the most common EW effectiveness calculation [4,18].

A waveform-level radar simulation goes further: it synthesizes the transmitted waveform, applies range delay, Doppler shift and fluctuating amplitude for each scatterer, adds clutter, noise and jamming, and runs the pulse compression, Doppler processing and constant false-alarm rate (CFAR) detection that the real radar uses [17,33]. This is the level at which deceptive techniques can be studied, because their effect depends on how the victim's processing responds to the false echoes rather than on their power alone.

Communications link performance

For communications, the corresponding curves are bit or symbol error rate against Eb/N0. For coherent binary phase-shift keying in additive white Gaussian noise the bit error probability is Q(√(2Eb/N0)), and Proakis and Salehi give the expressions for other modulations and for fading channels [37]. In a waveform-level simulation the error rate is measured by counting errors, which requires the Monte Carlo techniques of Section 5. Under jamming, the simulation must also represent the processing gain of spread-spectrum or frequency-hopping waveforms and the effect of the jamming waveform on synchronization, not merely the average jamming power [19].

Interference

Interference simulations combine many emitters with propagation models to find where and when the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) at each receiver falls below its requirement. ITU-R Recommendation SM.337 sets out the method for determining the frequency and distance separations needed between transmitters and receivers, accounting for receiver selectivity and transmitter spectra [38]. P.452 provides the propagation term for such studies when long-range or anomalous paths matter [28]. In EW planning the same machinery answers two questions at once: whether a jammer will reach an adversary receiver, and whether it will also disrupt friendly ones.

Mission-level and distributed simulation

Mission-level frameworks place sensor, communications and EW models on moving platforms in a shared scenario. The US Air Force Research Laboratory's Advanced Framework for Simulation, Integration and Modeling (AFSIM) is described by its developers as a C++ object-oriented, multi-domain, multi-resolution framework used mainly at engagement and mission level, in which each platform carries component models for movement, sensing, information processing, communications and weapons [39]. When simulations from different organizations must run together, interoperability standards such as the High Level Architecture (IEEE 1516) and Distributed Interactive Simulation (IEEE 1278.1) define how entity state and interactions are exchanged [40,41]. DOT&E's 2025 assessment noted that the joint simulation environment used for high-end air combat evaluation needs additional blue and red platforms, emitters and weapon types, and that non-kinetic effects should be built into accredited simulation environments [3].

5Monte Carlo simulation

EW performance depends on quantities that are uncertain or random: emitter power and antenna pointing, terrain and atmospheric conditions, target fluctuation, receiver noise and the timing of pulse collisions. Monte Carlo simulation handles these by drawing random samples of the uncertain inputs, running the model for each sample and treating the outputs as a statistical sample of performance. Metropolis and Ulam introduced the name and the method in 1949 [42]; Rubinstein and Kroese give a modern treatment [43].

Estimating probabilities

Most EW metrics are probabilities: of detection, of false alarm, of bit error or of successful jamming. If K of N independent trials succeed, the estimate p̂ = K/N has variance p(1 − p)/N. Its relative standard error is therefore approximately

σp̂ / p ≈ 1 / √(N p)   for small p(11)

so roughly 100 events must be observed to achieve 10 per cent relative precision. For a false-alarm probability of 10−6 this means about 108 trials. Jeruchim and colleagues discuss this cost in the context of error-rate simulation and describe variance-reduction techniques, chiefly importance sampling, which biases the input distribution towards the rare events of interest and then reweights the results to remove the bias [32,43].

Results should be reported with confidence intervals. Clopper and Pearson gave the exact interval for a binomial proportion [44]. A useful special case follows directly from it: if no events are observed in N trials, the one-sided 95 per cent upper bound on p satisfies (1 − p)N = 0.05, which gives p ≈ 3/N. A test that shows zero false alarms in 1000 opportunities therefore supports a false-alarm probability below about 3 × 10−3, not zero.

Sampling the input space

Simple random sampling can leave parts of a multi-dimensional input space unexplored. McKay, Beckman and Conover introduced Latin hypercube sampling, which stratifies each input into equal-probability intervals and samples each interval once, and showed that it can estimate output distributions more efficiently than simple random sampling for many computer models [45]. The quality of the underlying pseudo-random number generator also matters for very long runs; the Mersenne Twister of Matsumoto and Nishimura, with its period of 219937 − 1, is a common default [46]. Seeds should be recorded so that any trial can be reproduced exactly.

The metrology community has standardized the same approach for measurement uncertainty. Supplement 1 to the Guide to the expression of uncertainty in measurement (JCGM 101) specifies propagation of distributions by Monte Carlo, in which probability distributions for each input are propagated through the measurement model to give a distribution for the output, and notes that 106 trials can often be expected to give a 95 per cent coverage interval correct to one or two significant digits [47]. The method transfers directly to EW predictions such as geolocation error, where an analyst propagates sensor timing, position and heading errors through the location algorithm to obtain an error distribution.

From Monte Carlo to test design

Monte Carlo explores what the model says; design of experiments decides which live conditions to test. DOT&E's 2010 guidance asks test plans to identify the factors that affect effectiveness, to vary them systematically across developmental and operational testing, and to state the statistical power and confidence for each response variable [48]. Montgomery's text is the standard reference for the factorial, fractional factorial and response surface designs used for this purpose [49]. The two methods meet in validation: Monte Carlo runs at the designed test points provide the predicted distributions against which live results are compared (Section 12).

6Digital signal processing testbeds

A DSP testbed is a controlled environment for developing and scoring the algorithms at the heart of EW receivers: detection, parameter estimation, deinterleaving, classification and geolocation. Its value lies in three things a live environment cannot easily provide: known ground truth, repeatability and the ability to vary one condition at a time.

Architecture

A typical testbed has four parts. A signal source produces test signals, either synthetically in software or from vector signal generators and arbitrary waveform generators. A channel applies noise, interference, multipath and Doppler, either numerically or through a hardware channel emulator. A receiver, often a software-defined radio (SDR), digitizes the result. The algorithm under test processes the samples and its outputs are scored against truth. GNU Radio, an open-source toolkit in which signal processing blocks written in C++ are connected into flow graphs, usually from Python, is widely used to build such chains [50]. Recorded data are most useful when they are stored with metadata describing sample rate, centre frequency, hardware and annotations; the Signal Metadata Format (SigMF) specifies a JSON metadata file alongside each binary dataset for this purpose [51].

Detection and classification

Detection algorithms are scored by their probability of detection and probability of false alarm, and the trade between them is shown as a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve; Kay gives the theory for the Neyman-Pearson detector and its practical variants [52]. Fawcett's review explains how to construct and compare ROC curves from finite test sets and warns that class imbalance can make accuracy alone misleading [53]. Classification algorithms are scored with confusion matrices across SNR.

Automatic modulation classification illustrates the testbed's role. Dobre and colleagues surveyed the classical likelihood-based and feature-based approaches [54]. O'Shea, Roy and Clancy evaluated deep convolutional and residual networks on a large dataset of 24 modulation types, using both synthetic signals with simulated channel impairments and over-the-air captures, and studied how performance depends on SNR and on impairments such as carrier offset and multipath [55]. Their work highlighted a recurring issue for EW testbeds: a classifier trained only on synthetic data may not perform as well on real captured signals, so test sets must include data from the hardware and channels the system will actually face.

Scaling up

Some facilities extend the testbed concept to many radios at once. Colosseum, a large-scale wireless emulation facility, connects 256 software-defined radios through an FPGA-based channel emulator that applies finite-impulse-response filters representing the taps of the desired wireless channels to every radio-to-radio path [56]. Such systems blur the line between a DSP testbed and the hardware-in-the-loop facilities of Section 8, and they make it possible to reproduce the same congested spectrum scenario for every algorithm under comparison.

7Software-in-the-loop testing

In software-in-the-loop (SIL) testing, the actual operational software, or a compiled build of it, runs against a simulated environment rather than against real sensors and RF signals. The simulation supplies the inputs the software would see in service, such as pulse descriptor words from a receiver model, track reports from other sensors or navigation data, and accepts its outputs, such as threat declarations, technique selections or display messages. Because nothing physical is in the loop, SIL runs can be faster or slower than real time, can be repeated exactly and can be automated in large numbers.

The NATO handbook's system integration laboratory category covers the laboratory environments in which EW hardware, software and mission data are integrated and checked, and notes that programmes often require several SIL entries as software matures [2]. Mission data files, the threat libraries that tell a radar warning receiver or self-protection suite what each emitter is and how to respond, are a particularly important SIL subject: a SIL can replay thousands of emitter scenarios to confirm that every library entry produces the intended identification and response before any flight test [2,57].

Fathy and colleagues describe how the in-the-loop paradigm grew from a control validation tool into a broader approach that blends virtual and physical prototyping, in which components move from simulation to hardware as they become available [58]. SIL is the first step on that path. Its strengths are coverage and repeatability; its weaknesses are that timing, numerical precision and interface behaviour may differ from the target hardware. The simulated environment is also a model, so the accreditation requirements of DoD Instruction 5000.89 apply to it as they do to any other M&S tool used for decisions [5]. DOT&E has called for synthetic test ranges that support both hardware-in-the-loop and software-in-the-loop evaluation under operationally representative conditions [3].

When separate simulations must be linked, for example an EW suite model with a flight simulator and a threat integrated air defence model, the interfaces are usually built on HLA or DIS so that entity positions, emissions and engagement events stay consistent across the federation [40,41].

8Hardware-in-the-loop testing

Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing places real hardware in a closed loop with a simulation of the rest of the system and its environment. Bacic describes HIL as including the real components where they are available, especially those that are hard to model accurately, and stresses that the simulation must run in real time so that the hardware experiences realistic timing [59]. He also identifies the fundamental limitation: perfect transparency is unattainable because the interfaces between real and simulated parts have their own dynamics, and because the simulated parts are themselves uncertain [59]. Mihalič, Truntič and Hren review the engineering challenges of HIL across its history, including the real-time computing and interface requirements that limit achievable fidelity [60].

HIL in EW

In the NATO categorization, HITL facilities test real hardware against simulated inputs, typically earlier and more cheaply than flight test [2]. For an EW receiver, the simulated inputs are RF threat signals. Schulte's application note on radar warning receiver testing illustrates the practice: a scenario of moving platforms and emitters is rendered as pulse descriptor words, which save memory compared with storing full IQ waveforms for every pulse, and one vector signal generator with several baseband channels can produce overlapping pulses from different emitters so that pulse-on-pulse conditions are reproduced rather than dropped [57]. The note stresses that power must be calibrated at the device under test, for example with a power sensor to correct splitter and cable loss, and that the generators used to simulate angle of arrival must perform better than the system under test [57].

For a radar or for a jammer that must interact with one, the loop is closed through a target or echo generator. A radar echo generator captures the radar's transmitted signal, delays it to simulate range, shifts it in frequency to simulate radial velocity and attenuates it to represent radar cross-section, then returns it to the radar [61]. The same application note measured a system latency of about 11.1 µs, corresponding to a minimum simulated range of about 0.9 nautical miles, and warned that the latency depends on configuration and must be re-measured whenever it changes [61]. Latency of this kind is a defining parameter of any HIL facility, because it limits the closest targets and the fastest responses that can be represented.

HIL is where many effects that a model cannot predict first appear: receiver saturation, spurious responses, timing jitter between subsystems and software that behaves differently under real interrupt loads. The handbook notes that inadequate ground evaluation of installed EW performance usually leads to higher flight test cost and longer schedules [2]. ISTFs extend HIL to the complete aircraft in a large anechoic chamber, where the installed antennas, cabling and interactions with other avionics are all present [2].

9RF test equipment

Every test in the preceding sections ultimately depends on instruments that generate and measure RF signals accurately. The main instrument families, and the properties that matter for EW, are summarized in Table 3 and discussed below.

Spectrum and signal analyzers

A swept superheterodyne spectrum analyzer mixes the input with a swept local oscillator and measures the power passing through an intermediate-frequency filter whose bandwidth is the resolution bandwidth (RBW). Rauscher shows that the minimum sweep time is approximately

Tsweep = k · Δf / BIF2(12)

where Δf is the span, BIF the RBW and k about 2.5 for analog filters or about 1 for digital Gaussian filters, so halving the RBW quadruples the sweep time [62]. Reducing the RBW by a factor of ten lowers the displayed noise floor by 10 dB [63]. Detector choice matters for EW signals: peak detectors over-weight noise, while an RMS detector reports true power regardless of the signal's time structure [62]. Keysight's application note 150 covers these trade-offs and the related limits set by phase noise and intermodulation [63].

Swept analysis can miss short or intermittent signals because the analyzer is tuned elsewhere when they occur. Real-time spectrum analyzers sample a band continuously and compute discrete Fourier transforms without gaps, so that every event within the real-time bandwidth is processed; they add frequency-mask triggers and persistence displays for finding intermittent interference [64]. For pulsed radar signals, vector signal analyzers with pulse measurement options capture IQ data and report pulse width, repetition interval, intrapulse modulation and amplitude trends [57].

Signal generators

Vector signal generators produce arbitrary modulated waveforms from IQ data, and arbitrary waveform generators synthesize wideband signals directly. In EW testing they act as threat simulators, communications emitters and jammers. The important parameters are frequency range, modulation bandwidth, switching speed, phase noise, spurious content and, for multi-emitter work, the number of independent basebands and the accuracy with which several generators can be phase- and time-aligned [57].

Vector network analyzers

A vector network analyzer (VNA) measures the magnitude and phase of a device's reflection and transmission, expressed as scattering parameters [65]. Its accuracy rests on calibration. Keysight's note on error correction distinguishes systematic errors, which are repeatable and can be removed by calibration, from random errors and drift, which cannot; a full two-port correction uses a twelve-term error model covering directivity, crosstalk, source and load match, and reflection and transmission tracking [66]. Calibration uses known standards: short, open, load and through (SOLT) in coaxial systems, or through, reflect and line (TRL), introduced by Engen and Hoer, where good impedance standards are hard to make [66,67]. In EW, VNAs characterize antennas, filters, cables, limiters and the transfer functions of complete RF front ends, and verify the cable losses that enter every conducted-test link budget.

Noise figure and power measurement

Noise figure meters and analyzers measure the quantity in Equations (3) and (4), usually by the Y-factor method with a calibrated noise source [68]. Power meters with thermal or diode sensors provide the absolute power reference against which generators and analyzers are checked, and Keysight's application note 1449 explains how power measurements are traced through a hierarchy of laboratory standards to national metrology institutes [69].

Calibrated receivers and traceability

For electromagnetic compatibility and spectrum compliance, measurements are made with measuring receivers whose bandwidths, detectors and accuracy are specified in CISPR 16-1-1 [70]. More generally, a measurement is useful as evidence only when it is metrologically traceable, which NIST, following the International Vocabulary of Metrology, defines as the property of a result that can be related to a reference through a documented unbroken chain of calibrations, each contributing to the measurement uncertainty [71]. ISO/IEC 17025 sets the requirements for the competence of testing and calibration laboratories that provide that chain [72].

10Controlled RF environments

EW systems deliberately radiate energy that can disrupt other users of the spectrum, and they must also be measured without interference from those users. Controlled environments solve both problems. They range from a coaxial cable to a range covering thousands of square kilometres, and the choice is a trade between control and realism.

Conducted testing

The most controlled environment is no radiation at all. In conducted testing, generators, the system under test and measuring instruments are connected by cables, attenuators, couplers, combiners and, where needed, channel emulators. Power levels at the system's ports are set by calibrated attenuation rather than by distance, so a link budget such as Table 1 can be reproduced exactly at the bench. Conducted setups are the norm for receiver sensitivity, threat library and pulse density testing [57], and large-scale network emulators such as Colosseum use the same principle with FPGA-based channel models [56]. The limitation is that the antennas, their patterns and their installation on the platform are bypassed.

Shielded enclosures and anechoic chambers

Radiated tests need an enclosure that keeps external signals out and test signals in. IEEE Std 299 provides uniform procedures for measuring the shielding effectiveness of such enclosures [73]; its revision project approved in 2025 covers a core range of 9 kHz to 40 GHz with possible extension from 50 Hz to 100 GHz, and leaves the test frequencies and pass/fail limits to the enclosure owner [74]. Lining a shielded room with absorber turns it into an anechoic chamber that approximates free space for antenna and radiated-system measurements; Hemming's guide covers chamber design, absorber performance and the specification of quiet-zone quality [75]. At the largest scale, ISTFs are anechoic chambers big enough to hold a whole aircraft [2].

Electromagnetic compatibility tests

MIL-STD-461G defines the standard laboratory tests for electromagnetic interference at the subsystem and equipment level [76]. Several are directly relevant to EW equipment, as summarized in Table 4. MIL-STD-464D sets the system-level electromagnetic environmental effects requirements for complete platforms, including intra-system compatibility and margins, and now references MIL-STD-461 for subsystem requirements [77].

Authorized test ranges

Some questions can only be answered in the open air, but radiating jamming signals outside a controlled facility is tightly regulated. In the United States, the Federal Communications Commission states that federal law prohibits the operation, marketing or sale of jamming equipment, citing Section 302(b) of the Communications Act, which prohibits the manufacture, importation, marketing, sale or operation of signal jammers, and Section 333, which prohibits wilful or malicious interference with radio communications; limited exceptions apply to authorized federal use [78]. Federal agencies obtain frequency assignments under the procedures in NTIA's Manual of Regulations and Procedures for Federal Radio Frequency Management [79].

Live GNSS interference testing illustrates the coordination involved. The FAA publishes flight advisories and notices to air missions for military GPS interference tests; a January 2024 advisory for the Nevada Test and Training Range, for example, listed test windows of one to three hours, warned that GPS-dependent services including WAAS, GBAS and ADS-B could be unreliable, and defined an affected area extending 262 nautical miles from the test centre at flight level 400 and above, shrinking at lower altitudes [80]. Open-air ranges also face the limits noted in Section 1: restricted airspace, limited numbers of threat simulators and growing difficulty in representing modern signal density [2,3].

11Performance metrics

Models and tests are only as useful as the measures used to compare them. EW metrics fall into a small number of families, each with its own definitions and pitfalls. Table 5 summarizes them.

Probability of detection and false-alarm rate

Probability of detection PD and probability of false alarm PFA are the basic detection metrics, linked by the detector threshold and displayed together as a ROC curve [52,53]. Operational users usually care about the false-alarm rate, the expected number of false alarms per unit time, which equals PFA multiplied by the number of independent detection decisions per second; for a wideband receiver that number can be very large, so an apparently small PFA can still produce frequent false reports [16,17]. Because both quantities are proportions, test results must carry confidence intervals (Section 5) [44].

Geolocation error

Emitter location accuracy is described by the error covariance of the position estimate. Torrieri's statistical theory of passive location systems derives that covariance for direction-finding, time-difference and related systems and relates it to the circular error probable (CEP), the radius of the circle centred on the true position that contains half of the estimates [81]. A widely used approximation from that work is CEP ≈ 0.75√(σ12 + σ22), where σ12 and σ22 are the eigenvalues of the two-dimensional error covariance [81]. Error ellipses describe the same information with orientation. In test, geolocation error is measured against surveyed or GNSS-referenced emitter positions, and Monte Carlo propagation of sensor errors (Section 5) provides the predicted distribution for comparison [47].

Signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio

SINR is the ratio of wanted signal power to the sum of interference and noise powers at a defined point in the receiver. It is the quantity that determines bit error rate in Section 4 and detection performance under jamming, and it is the natural figure of merit for adaptive arrays, whose optimum weights maximize output SINR [37,82]. Measuring it requires care about where in the processing chain it is defined, since processing gain from despreading, pulse compression or beamforming changes it substantially.

Latency

Latency is the delay between a stimulus and the system's response: from a threat's first pulse to its declaration, from declaration to the start of a jamming technique, or from a navigation disruption to a valid alternative solution. It is a time distribution rather than a single number, and it must be measured with instrumentation time-stamped to a common reference. Latency is also a property of the test facility: HIL simulations must run in real time for timing results to mean anything [59], and the measured latency of a radar echo generator sets the minimum range it can present [61].

Resilience

Resilience describes how well a system maintains or recovers performance under attack. The DHS Resilient PNT Conformance Framework defines four cumulative levels for navigation and timing equipment: Level 1 requires full recovery after the threat is removed; Level 2 requires a solution during the threat, possibly with unbounded degradation; Level 3 requires bounded degradation and cross-verification of sources; and Level 4 requires operation through the threat without degradation, using diverse sources [83]. A complementary quantitative view comes from Bruneau and colleagues, who measured resilience by the loss of system quality integrated over the time taken to recover, the so-called resilience triangle [84]. Applied to EW, the same idea compares performance-versus-time curves with and without electronic attack, capturing both how far performance falls and how quickly it returns.

12Bringing it together: validation and the test process

The tools in this article are linked by the predict, test, compare cycle of Figure 1 [2]. A link budget and propagation model predict signal levels; waveform-level and Monte Carlo simulations convert them into predicted distributions of detection, error rate or location accuracy; laboratory, HIL and range tests measure the same quantities under controlled conditions; and the comparison either builds confidence in the models or shows where they must change.

DoD policy formalizes this. Under DoD Instruction 5000.61, models used for decisions must be verified, validated for their intended use and accredited by the responsible authority [6]. MIL-STD-3022 provides templates for the four core VV&A documents, the accreditation plan, verification and validation plan, verification and validation report and accreditation report, so that the evidence is recorded consistently [85]. DOT&E's guidance on M&S used in operational test and live-fire assessments states that a model should not be accredited until a rigorous comparison of live data with the model's predictions has been made, using the same statistical rigour as the design of live tests [86]. DoD Instruction 5000.89 adds that operational evaluation must not rest on M&S alone [5].

Two practical lessons follow. First, plan tests so that their results can validate models, not just verify requirements: choose test points with design-of-experiments methods, record truth and environmental conditions, and compare results with predicted distributions rather than single numbers [48,49]. Second, keep the metrics consistent from simulation to range, so that a PD, CEP or latency computed in a Monte Carlo run means exactly the same thing as one measured in a HIL facility or on an open-air range.

13Conclusions

Because EW performance depends on scenario, geometry and an adversary's behaviour, it cannot be established by a handful of live trials. Modelling and controlled testing fill the gap. Link budgets and propagation models give the first, physically grounded estimates and show where the margins are; radar and communications simulations turn those estimates into detection and error-rate predictions; Monte Carlo methods carry uncertainty through to distributions with confidence intervals; DSP testbeds, SIL and HIL put real algorithms, software and hardware into reproducible environments; calibrated, traceable instruments and controlled RF environments make the measurements trustworthy and lawful; and well-defined metrics let every stage be compared with every other. Open-air testing remains essential for final evaluation, but its cost, its limited signal density and the regulatory constraints on radiating make it the last rung of the ladder rather than the first [2,3,78]. The common thread is validation: each model is only as credible as its documented comparison with measured data [6,86].
