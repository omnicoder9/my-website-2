Radar, Sensing and Electronic Warfare Interaction

A radar announces itself every time it transmits and listens to anything that arrives in its receiver. That makes radar engineering and electronic warfare two sides of one problem. This article reviews the radar techniques that shape the contest, from pulse compression to cognitive radar, and for each one sets out what electronic countermeasures exploit and what electronic counter-countermeasures recover.

01Introduction and scope

Radar and electronic warfare (EW) are two views of the same physics. A radar transmits electromagnetic energy, waits for an echo and extracts information about range, velocity, angle and shape from what returns. Every one of those steps leaves the radar exposed. Its transmissions can be intercepted and characterized by an electronic support receiver, and its receiver will accept energy that did not come from its own transmitter. Because the radar signal travels to the target and back while a jammer's signal travels only one way, the energy balance between a radar and an opposing jammer is set by geometry long before any clever signal processing is applied [1,2,3].

This article reviews the radar techniques that matter most to that contest: waveforms and pulse compression, detection and tracking, Doppler processing, synthetic aperture radar (SAR), phased arrays, multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) radar, passive radar, and cognitive or adaptive radar. For each, it explains the principle, what an electronic countermeasure (ECM) can exploit, and what the radar can do in return. The final section draws those threads together as electronic counter-countermeasures (ECCM). The treatment stays at the level of the open textbook and peer-reviewed literature.

Key distinction

Electronic countermeasures (ECM) seek to disrupt or deceive sensing systems: to deny the radar the information it needs (for example by noise jamming) or to give it false information (for example with false targets or track-stealing deception). Electronic counter-countermeasures (ECCM) are the techniques a sensing system uses to preserve its effectiveness when it is subjected to such measures [2,3,4].

Current US joint doctrine uses electronic attack (EA) and electronic protection (EP) as divisions of electromagnetic warfare, alongside electromagnetic warfare support (ES) [5]. EA includes, but is broader than, ECM, and EP includes, but is broader than, ECCM. The older ECM and ECCM terms remain standard in the radar engineering literature and are used here [2,6].

The distinction is functional rather than technological. The same adaptive array that a radar uses to null a jammer (an ECCM) is closely related to the array a jammer uses to steer power at a radar (an ECM), and a digital radio frequency memory (DRFM) that stores and replays a radar pulse is a deception tool in one system and a test instrument in another [3,2]. What decides the label is whose sensing is being degraded and whose is being preserved.

02The radar and jamming equations

The starting point for every radar–EW engagement is the radar range equation. For a monostatic radar with transmit power Pt, antenna gain G, wavelength λ, target radar cross section σ, range R and system losses L, the received echo power is [1,7]:

S = Pt G2 λ2 σ / [(4π)3 R4 L](1)

A noise jammer carried on the target itself (a self-screening jammer) with power Pj, antenna gain Gj and noise bandwidth Bj delivers into a radar receiver of bandwidth Br the power [2,3,6]:

J = Pj Gj G λ2 Br / [(4π)2 R2 Bj Lj](2)

The ratio of the two, ignoring losses, gives the jamming-to-signal ratio:

J/S = (Pj Gj / Pt G) · (4π R2 / σ) · (Br / Bj)(3)

Three features of equation (3) organize most of what follows. First, the echo falls as R−4 while the jamming falls as R−2, so J/S falls as R2 as the target closes. The range at which the radar's signal-to-jamming ratio reaches the value it needs for detection is the burn-through range [2,6]:

RBT = √[ Pt G σ Bj / (4π Pj Gj Br (S/J)req) ](4)

Second, the bandwidth ratio Br/Bj rewards a radar that forces the jammer to spread its power: frequency agility over a wide band compels barrage jamming and dilutes jammer power spectral density. Third, the radar's gain G appears in the signal once more than in the jamming for a main-beam jammer, but a jammer that enters through a sidelobe sees the sidelobe gain instead, which is why low sidelobes and sidelobe cancellation are among the most important ECCMs (section 8) [3,4]. Equation (3) also omits the processing gain that coherent radar receivers apply to their own echo but not to uncorrelated noise, and that gain is the subject of the next section.

03Radar waveforms and pulse compression

The matched filter

The receiver filter that maximizes output signal-to-noise ratio for a known signal in white noise is the matched filter, whose impulse response is the time-reversed complex conjugate of the transmitted waveform [8,9]. Its peak output SNR depends only on the signal energy E and the noise spectral density N0, not on the waveform's shape [10]:

SNRmax = 2E / N0(5)

This result decouples detection from resolution. Detection depends on energy, which a long pulse provides at modest peak power. Range resolution depends on bandwidth B:

ΔR = c / (2B)(6)

Pulse compression modulates a long pulse of duration τ so that its bandwidth greatly exceeds 1/τ, then compresses it in the matched filter to a width of about 1/B. The compression ratio, and the SNR gain relative to an unmodulated pulse of the same compressed width, is the time–bandwidth product Bτ [1,10,11].

Waveform families

Linear frequency modulation (LFM, or "chirp"), analysed in detail at Bell Laboratories [12], is the most widely used pulse-compression waveform. It is tolerant of Doppler shift but exhibits range–Doppler coupling: a Doppler-shifted echo compresses to a peak displaced in range [13,10]. Its compressed range sidelobes (about −13 dB for an unweighted pulse) are normally reduced by amplitude weighting, at the cost of a small SNR loss and mainlobe broadening [14,10].

Phase-coded waveforms divide the pulse into chips and assign each a phase. Binary Barker codes have a peak autocorrelation sidelobe of 1/N; the longest known has length 13, giving a peak sidelobe of about −22.3 dB [15,13]. Polyphase codes such as the Frank code, derived from the phase steps of a stepped-frequency approximation to a chirp, achieve lower sidelobes for long codes [16,13]. Frequency-hopped Costas codes give an ambiguity function close to an ideal "thumbtack", with a narrow central peak and low, evenly spread sidelobes [17].

The ambiguity function

Woodward's ambiguity function describes the matched-filter response to an echo with delay mismatch τ and Doppler mismatch fD [18,13]:

|χ(τ, fD)| = | ∫ u(t) u*(t + τ) ej2πfDt dt |(7)

The total volume under |χ|2 is fixed by the signal energy, so waveform design can only move ambiguity around the delay–Doppler plane, not remove it [18,13]. This "conservation" constrains the radar designer and is equally useful to the EW analyst, who can predict from the ambiguity function where a Doppler-shifted false echo will appear after compression.

EW interaction

Pulse compression is an inherent ECCM. Noise that is uncorrelated with the transmitted code is not compressed, so the radar recovers a processing gain of about Bτ against it that the jammer does not share [2,3]. A wide instantaneous bandwidth also forces a noise jammer to spread its power, as equation (3) shows. Pulse compression is also an ECM opportunity. A coherent repeater, typically built around a DRFM, samples the radar pulse, stores it and retransmits a modified copy. Because the replica carries the radar's own modulation, it receives the full compression gain and can produce convincing false targets at any delay or Doppler offset that the jammer chooses [3,2].

The radar's answer is waveform diversity: changing the code, centre frequency or chirp parameters from pulse to pulse or within a coherent processing interval so that a replica derived from one pulse does not match the filter of the next [19,3]. Low probability of intercept (LPI) radar pushes the same idea further, using wide bandwidths, low peak power and continuous-wave modulations such as FMCW, so that the radar's own matched filter enjoys a processing gain that a non-cooperative intercept receiver, lacking the reference waveform, cannot match [20].

04Target detection and tracking

Detection as hypothesis testing

Radar detection is a binary hypothesis test between noise alone and target plus noise. The Neyman–Pearson criterion maximizes the probability of detection PD for a fixed probability of false alarm PFA, which in practice means comparing the matched-filter output envelope with a threshold [21,10]. Marcum derived detection probabilities for non-fluctuating targets with pulse integration [22], and Swerling extended the analysis to the four fluctuating-target models that still bear his name [23].

Constant false-alarm rate processing

Because noise and clutter power are unknown and vary across the scene, practical radars estimate the interference level from reference cells around the cell under test. For the cell-averaging CFAR (CA-CFAR) detector with N reference cells and a square-law detector in exponentially distributed interference, the threshold multiplier that maintains the design false-alarm probability is [10,7]:

T = α · (1/N) Σ xi,   α = N(PFA−1/N − 1)(8)

CA-CFAR is optimal in homogeneous interference but degrades when interfering targets or clutter edges fall in the reference window. Ordered-statistic CFAR (OS-CFAR), which sets the threshold from a ranked sample rather than the mean, is far more robust in multiple-target situations [24].

Tracking

Detections are associated over time into tracks by recursive state estimators, most commonly the Kalman filter and its extensions [25,26]. A track filter predicts where the target should appear next and opens a gate around that prediction; measurements inside the gate are candidates for update, and the gate size follows from the predicted measurement covariance [26,27]. Angle tracking in modern radars relies on monopulse, which forms sum and difference beams simultaneously and estimates the angle from their ratio on a single pulse [28].

EW interaction

Every stage of this chain offers an ECM entry point. Noise jamming raises the CFAR threshold and suppresses detection; many false targets saturate detection and track-initiation logic. Deceptive ECM attacks the tracker directly. In range-gate pull-off (RGPO), a repeater first captures the radar's range gate with a strong replica coincident with the skin return, then walks the replica away in delay so that the gate follows it and the true target is lost; velocity-gate pull-off (VGPO) does the same in Doppler [3,2]. Monopulse resists the amplitude-modulation techniques that defeat older conical-scan trackers because it measures angle on a single pulse, so angle deception against monopulse uses more demanding methods such as cross-eye (two coherent sources with a controlled phase relationship that distort the wavefront) and terrain bounce [3,28].

The corresponding ECCMs include robust CFAR schemes, leading-edge range tracking (a repeater's delay means the true skin return arrives first), consistency checks between the range rate derived from successive range measurements and the Doppler measured directly, and track-management logic that models the jammer as well as the target [3,4]. Blair and colleagues published a well-known tracking benchmark in which a phased-array radar had to allocate its resources and maintain tracks in the presence of stand-off and self-screening jamming and RGPO, which provides a reference for comparing algorithms under ECM [29].

05Doppler processing

Principles

A target with radial velocity vr shifts the frequency of a monostatic echo by [1]:

fD = 2vr / λ(9)

A pulsed radar samples this Doppler shift at its pulse repetition frequency fr. That sampling creates a trade-off between unambiguous range and unambiguous velocity [1,30]:

Ru = c / (2fr),    vb = n λ fr / 2(10)

where vb are the blind speeds at which a moving target's echo is indistinguishable from stationary clutter. Moving-target indication (MTI) cancels returns that do not change from pulse to pulse; staggered PRFs move the first blind speed well above the velocities of interest [1,31]. Pulse-Doppler radar instead applies a bank of Doppler filters, usually an FFT over the pulses of a coherent processing interval, with window weighting to control Doppler sidelobes [10,14]. Airborne radars choose between low, medium and high PRF modes, each with different ambiguity and clutter properties, and resolve ambiguities using several PRFs in succession [30].

Space-time adaptive processing

For a moving airborne radar, ground clutter is spread in Doppler according to its angle, so clutter occupies a ridge in the joint angle–Doppler domain. Space-time adaptive processing (STAP) applies a two-dimensional adaptive filter across array elements and pulses to suppress this ridge and any jamming, which appears as a narrow line in angle spread across all Doppler frequencies [32,33,34,35]. The adaptive weights are estimated from training data, and the Reed–Mallett–Brennan result shows that about twice as many independent training samples as adaptive degrees of freedom are needed to keep the average SNR loss within 3 dB of the optimum [36].

EW interaction

Doppler processing is a strong ECCM against noise jamming and chaff. Noise spreads across every Doppler bin, so each bin sees only a fraction of the jammer's power, while the target's energy accumulates coherently in one bin; chaff decelerates rapidly after release and soon separates from a moving aircraft in Doppler [2,3]. Against coherent deception, Doppler processing is a target rather than a defence: a DRFM can add a precise frequency offset to its replica and pull the velocity gate, or populate the Doppler–range map with false targets [3]. The ECCM response is consistency checking: a genuine target's range rate, Doppler, and angle history must agree, while a replica generated without detailed knowledge of the radar's processing will usually violate at least one of those relations [3,4].

06Synthetic aperture radar

Principles

Synthetic aperture radar forms a long effective antenna by coherently combining echoes received along the platform's flight path. Carl Wiley's 1951 observation at Goodyear that the Doppler spread of returns across the beam encodes cross-range position is generally credited as the origin of the technique [37]. Range resolution is set by bandwidth, as in equation (6). In stripmap mode, the azimuth resolution achievable is approximately half the real antenna length D, independent of range and wavelength, because a longer range lengthens the synthetic aperture in proportion [38,39]:

δaz ≈ D / 2(11)

Spotlight SAR steers the beam to keep a scene illuminated over an angular interval Δθ, achieving cross-range resolution of about λ/(2Δθ) [40]. Image formation algorithms include the range–Doppler, chirp scaling and omega-k algorithms for stripmap data and the polar-format algorithm for spotlight data [39,40]. Moreira and colleagues give a modern tutorial review that extends to interferometric, polarimetric and multistatic SAR [41].

EW interaction

The long coherent integration that gives SAR its resolution also gives it a large processing gain against noise jamming. A noise jammer illuminating the radar does not focus in the image; its energy is spread across the image in a characteristic stripe or smear, and the jammer must therefore deliver substantial power to mask a scene [42]. Deceptive jamming against SAR attempts to insert focused false scatterers or entire false scenes by retransmitting replicas of the radar's pulses with the delay and phase history of a scatterer at a chosen location, which requires accurate knowledge of the SAR geometry and waveform [42,3]. SAR ECCMs therefore draw on the same toolbox as other coherent radars: wideband and pulse-to-pulse diverse waveforms, low antenna sidelobes, and adaptive array nulling where the SAR has multiple receive channels [42,4].

07Phased-array and electronically scanned radar

Principles

A phased array steers its beam by applying a progressive phase (or true time delay) across its elements. For a linear array with element spacing d, the phase increment needed to steer to angle θ0 is [43,1]:

Δφ = 2π d sin θ0 / λ(12)

Grating lobes are avoided over a scan range to θmax if d/λ ≤ 1/(1 + |sin θmax|) [43]. Electronic scanning removes mechanical inertia, so a single array can interleave search, track and other functions on a dwell-by-dwell basis [44,4]. Architectures have evolved from passive electronically scanned arrays (PESA), with a central transmitter and phase shifters at each element, to active arrays (AESA) with a transmit/receive module behind every element, and now to digital arrays that digitize at the element or subarray level and form beams in software [45,44].

EW interaction

Electronic scanning cuts both ways. An agile beam with short, irregular dwells and many simultaneous functions is harder for an ES receiver to characterize than a rotating antenna with a regular scan, and an AESA's ability to spread energy across frequency and time supports LPI operation [20,3]. At the same time, an array's sidelobes are a permanent entry path for jamming from directions other than the target's. This makes the array the natural place for spatial ECCM:

  • Low and ultra-low sidelobes reduce sidelobe jamming in direct proportion to the sidelobe level [4,43].
  • Sidelobe blanking compares the main channel with a broad-beam guard channel and blanks returns that are stronger in the guard channel, rejecting pulsed sidelobe interference [46,47].
  • Sidelobe cancellation and adaptive nulling weight auxiliary channels or array elements to place nulls on jammers. The Howells–Applebaum loop maximizes the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio given a steering direction [48], and sample-matrix inversion converges in roughly twice as many samples as degrees of freedom [36]. Kelly's generalized likelihood ratio test provides an adaptive detector that keeps a constant false-alarm rate when the interference covariance is estimated [49].

Adaptive nulling is most effective against a small number of noise jammers outside the main beam. A jammer inside the main beam cannot be nulled without also nulling the target, which is why main-beam jamming, and particularly self-screening jamming, remains the hardest case for spatial ECCM [47,4]. Home-on-jam, in which a missile seeker or radar tracks the jammer's angle, turns that weakness into an angle-only track [3].

08Multiple-input, multiple-output radar

Principles

A MIMO radar transmits different waveforms from multiple antennas and separates them at each receiver, so that the echo from every transmitter–receiver pair can be processed individually [50,51,52]. Two branches of the literature have developed. With colocated antennas, the transmitters and receivers are close enough that the target is seen from essentially one aspect. Waveform separation then creates a virtual array whose elements are the pairwise sums of transmit and receive element positions, up to M·N virtual elements from M transmitters and N receivers, which improves angular resolution, parameter identifiability and flexibility in transmit beampattern design [53,51,54]. With widely separated antennas, each pair views the target from a different aspect, so the fluctuations of radar cross section become partly independent across pairs. This spatial diversity reduces the deep fades that limit detection of fluctuating targets [55,50].

EW interaction

MIMO radar interacts with EW in several ways described in the open literature. Its transmit beampattern can be shaped by designing the cross-correlation of the transmitted signals, including broad patterns that do not concentrate energy in one direction [54], which complicates interception by an ES receiver that relies on main-beam illumination. The larger virtual aperture provides more spatial degrees of freedom for adaptive nulling of jammers [52]. For the jammer, a MIMO radar presents several waveforms rather than one, so a repeater that replicates a single waveform will match only part of the radar's receive processing. The widely separated configuration also forces a jammer to cover multiple receivers from different geometries [55,52]. These benefits depend on the orthogonality of the transmitted waveforms, which is imperfect in practice; residual cross-correlation raises sidelobes and is a central topic in MIMO waveform design [52,19].

09Passive radar

Principles

Passive radar, also called passive coherent location or passive bistatic radar, transmits nothing. It uses signals already in the environment, such as FM radio, digital audio and video broadcasting and cellular transmissions, as illuminators of opportunity [56,57]. A reference channel receives the direct signal from the illuminator; one or more surveillance channels receive target echoes. Targets are detected by computing the cross-ambiguity function between the surveillance signal s(t) and the reference r(t) [56,58]:

ψ(τ, fD) = ∫ s(t) r*(t − τ) e−j2πfDt dt(13)

The geometry is bistatic: contours of constant measured delay are ellipses with the transmitter and receiver at the foci, and the bistatic Doppler depends on the target's velocity relative to both [59]. The direct-path signal and its clutter multipath are typically many tens of decibels stronger than target echoes and must be cancelled before detection; adaptive cancellation such as the extensive cancellation algorithm addresses this problem [58,56]. Howland and colleagues demonstrated detection and tracking of aircraft using FM broadcast transmissions [60].

EW interaction

Passive radar's most important EW property is that it has no emission of its own for an ES system to detect, locate or target, which makes it covert [56,57]. Conventional self-protection jammers that respond to a detected radar signal have no trigger. Broadcast illuminators also operate at frequencies, often VHF and UHF, where radar-absorbent shaping optimized for microwave radar is less effective [56]. The trade-offs are that the system does not control its waveform, the illuminators are not designed for radar so their ambiguity functions vary with programme content, and the transmitters are fixed, known and potentially deniable. An adversary can therefore attack the illuminator or the reference channel rather than the receiver, and the bistatic geometry is set by where broadcasters happen to be [56,57].

10Cognitive and adaptive radar

From adaptive to cognitive

Classical adaptive radar adapts its receiver: CFAR thresholds, adaptive arrays and STAP all estimate the environment from received data and adjust processing accordingly [32,33]. Haykin proposed the term cognitive radar for a system that also adapts its transmitter in a closed perception–action cycle. He identified three ingredients: intelligent signal processing that learns from interaction with the environment, feedback from the receiver to the transmitter, and preservation of the information content of radar returns through Bayesian tracking [61,62]. Guerci developed the closely related knowledge-aided, fully adaptive approach, in which prior knowledge of the environment (such as terrain databases) and adaptive transmit and receive processing are combined to approach optimal performance [63].

Bell and colleagues formalized a cognitive radar framework for detection and tracking in which the radar chooses its next waveform or dwell parameters to optimize a performance metric conditioned on what it has learned so far [64]. Greco and colleagues review the state of the field and its remaining open problems [65]. Experimental work includes software-defined cognitive radars that sense the spectrum and move their transmission into unoccupied bands to avoid time-varying radio-frequency interference [66].

EW interaction

Cognitive radar formalizes a long-standing ECCM principle: sense the threat, then change what the radar does. Spectrum sensing followed by frequency selection generalizes frequency agility; adapting waveforms, dwell and power to an inferred interference environment generalizes waveform diversity and power management [65,61,66]. The same feedback loop creates new considerations. A radar whose behaviour depends on its environment can in principle be steered by an adversary that manipulates that environment, and its adaptations are themselves observable to an ES receiver [65]. Verifying the behaviour of a closed-loop, learning sensor is also more difficult than verifying a fixed one [65,63].

11Electronic counter-countermeasures

The preceding sections each closed with an ECM–ECCM exchange specific to one technique. ECCM as a discipline brings those exchanges together. The standard references classify ECCM by where in the radar it acts: the antenna, the transmitter, the receiver and signal processor, and the way the radar is operated [4,2,3]. Three general principles run through the table. The first is processing gain: any operation that integrates the true echo coherently while treating jamming as uncorrelated noise (pulse compression, Doppler filtering, SAR focusing) buys signal-to-jamming ratio that the jammer must pay for in power [2,10]. The second is diversity in frequency, code, time, space and polarization: a jammer must match the radar in every dimension the radar exploits, and the radar wins whenever it can change faster than the jammer can learn [19,3]. The third is consistency: real targets obey physics, so measurements of range, range rate, Doppler, angle and amplitude must agree with each other and across sensors, while a replica generated by a jammer is likely to violate at least one constraint unless the jammer reproduces the radar's processing in full [3,4].

ECCM is never absolute. Every technique in Table 1 has a costed counter on the ECM side, and every ECM has a corresponding ECCM; the literature treats the relationship as a continuing cycle of measure and countermeasure rather than a problem with a final solution [2,3]. Equation (4) captures the underlying arithmetic. As range shrinks, the radar eventually burns through, so the practical value of an ECM is measured by how much it shortens the radar's useful range and how long it delays detection and track, and the value of an ECCM by how much of that range and time it recovers [2,6].

The key distinction stated at the outset holds across every technique: ECM seeks to deny or falsify what a sensor perceives, and ECCM seeks to preserve that perception. The two co-evolve. Each advance in radar waveforms, arrays and processing has created both a new ECCM and a new ECM target, and the trend toward digital, software-defined and cognitive radars is making both sides adaptive at the same time [65,3,45].
