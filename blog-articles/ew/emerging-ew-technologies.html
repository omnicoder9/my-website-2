1Introduction and scope

US joint doctrine defines electromagnetic warfare (EW) as military action that uses electromagnetic and directed energy to control the electromagnetic spectrum or to attack an adversary, and places it within the wider practice of joint electromagnetic spectrum operations[1]. The Congressional Research Service describes the same field as military activity that uses electromagnetic energy to control the spectrum, and records the conclusion of the National Defense Strategy Commission that the United States was "losing its advantages in electronic warfare"[2]. The Government Accountability Office reached a similar view in 2020. It found that China and Russia had improved their ability to contest US use of the spectrum, and that earlier Department of Defense spectrum strategies had not been fully carried out[3].

Several technologies are now being developed in response. Some are well established in other fields and are being adapted to EW; others are still at an early research stage. This article reviews ten of them: cognitive EW, AI-assisted signal classification, autonomous spectrum management, digital twins, distributed sensing, multifunction RF systems, photonic RF processing, advanced antennas, unmanned systems, and quantum sensing. For each it explains the underlying principle, summarises what has been shown in the open literature, and states how mature the evidence is.

How maturity is described

Each section ends with a short status note. Fielded means the technique is in routine operational or commercial use. Demonstrated means it has been shown in field, flight, or over-the-air tests reported in open sources. Laboratory means results come from controlled experiments or emulation. Research means the evidence is mainly theoretical or from early experiments. The notes summarise published sources only and make no claim about classified programmes.

2Cognitive EW

2.1 From cognitive radio to cognitive EW

The idea of a cognitive system in the radio domain began with Mitola and Maguire, who described a software radio that is aware of its environment and its user, and that adapts its own behaviour through a cycle of observing, orienting, planning, deciding, and acting[4]. Haykin later framed cognitive radio as a closed loop: the radio senses the spectrum, analyses it to identify unused bands and interference, and reconfigures its transmission in response, learning from the outcome over time[5].

Haykin applied the same reasoning to radar. In his account, a cognitive radar learns about its environment from experience, uses that knowledge to adapt its transmitted waveform, and closes a feedback loop from receiver to transmitter, rather than using a fixed waveform designed in advance[6]. Bell and colleagues later gave a formal framework for this loop in target detection and tracking, in which the radar chooses its next transmission to optimise a performance measure that depends on what it has already observed[7].

Cognitive EW extends this logic to electronic support, attack, and protection. Haigh and Andrusenko define it in terms of the artificial intelligence functions that an EW system needs: situation assessment, decision making, and learning, all running in a loop within the time available in an engagement[8]. They also treat electronic attack and electronic protection as two sides of one problem, since the same reasoning that picks a jamming response can pick an anti-jam response[8].

2.2 Why adaptive threats drive the need

Conventional EW systems rely on threat libraries. An emitter is recognised by matching its parameters to a database, and a countermeasure is chosen from a pre-programmed list. BAE Systems, describing the DARPA Adaptive Radar Countermeasures (ARC) programme, noted that this approach limits adaptability against radars whose behaviour is not in the database[9]. Software-defined radars and radios can change waveform, frequency, and timing quickly, so a library compiled from earlier observations may not describe what a system meets in practice[10,8].

2.3 Published programmes and results

Two DARPA programmes give the clearest open record. ARC aimed to let airborne EW systems detect and characterise a radar with unknown waveform properties, synthesise a countermeasure, and assess its effect, in a tactically relevant time and as a software upgrade to existing systems[11]. In 2016 the programme manager stated that the system had characterised and adaptively countered advanced threats in a closed-loop test environment, and Phase 3 work covered readiness testing and transition planning[9].

The Behavioral Learning for Adaptive Electronic Warfare (BLADE) programme addressed communications. Its stated aims were to detect and characterise new radio threats, dynamically synthesise new countermeasures, and provide battle damage assessment from changes in the threat that can be observed over the air[12]. In 2016 Lockheed Martin reported a flight demonstration in which BLADE software hosted on an airborne EW system detected, characterised, and jammed test signals, including military radios, mobile phones, and data links, in "minutes, not months"[13]. DARPA lists both programmes as complete[12].

On the radar side, Greco and colleagues reviewed progress toward practical cognitive radar. Their review covers adaptive waveform design and spectrum sharing among the approaches that have moved furthest toward practice, and sets out the work that remains before cognitive radar, in the full sense of learning and memory across engagements, becomes routine[10].

2.4 Open issues

  • Assessment of effect. A cognitive jammer must judge whether its action worked without direct access to the target system. BLADE treated over-the-air battle damage assessment as a core task for this reason[12].
  • Training data and novelty. Learning methods perform best on conditions similar to those seen in training. Haigh and Andrusenko stress the need to handle emitters not seen in training and to keep learning during a mission[8].
  • Verification. An adaptive system can behave differently each time it runs, which complicates conventional test and certification. This links cognitive EW to the digital twin and emulation methods described in Section 5.

Status Demonstrated. Closed-loop laboratory and flight demonstrations have been reported for adaptive radar and communications countermeasures[9,13]. Learning across engagements and assured behaviour are still research topics[10,8].

3AI-assisted signal classification

3.1 Learned modulation recognition

Automatic modulation classification, which identifies how a signal is modulated, is a basic electronic support task. Classical methods compute expert-designed features, such as higher-order statistical moments, and feed them to a classifier. O'Shea, Corgan, and Clancy showed in 2016 that a convolutional neural network trained directly on raw sampled in-phase and quadrature (I/Q) data could outperform classifiers built on such expert features, especially at low signal-to-noise ratio[14].

O'Shea, Roy, and Clancy extended this work in 2018. They compared deep networks against a baseline of higher-order moments with boosted gradient trees across a range of channel impairments, including carrier frequency offset, symbol rate variation, and multipath fading, and they measured performance over the air with software-defined radios as well as in simulation[15]. The public RadioML datasets released by this group became common benchmarks for later research[14,15]. O'Shea and Hoydis set this work in a broader context, arguing that deep learning could replace or complement hand-designed blocks across the radio physical layer[16].

3.2 Specific emitter identification

A harder task is to tell apart individual transmitters of the same type. Small hardware imperfections, such as oscillator offsets, amplifier non-linearity, and I/Q imbalance, give each transmitter a signature that is difficult to forge. Riyaz and colleagues trained a convolutional network on raw I/Q samples to separate a set of nominally identical software radios by these impairments[17]. DARPA's Radio Frequency Machine Learning Systems (RFMLS) programme made RF fingerprinting one of its four core tasks, together with fingerprint enhancement, spectrum awareness across wide bandwidths, and autonomous configuration of RF hardware[18]. The programme stated the problem directly: RF systems are "still designed using models and equations based on idealized assumptions," whereas learned models could reflect real hardware and channels[18].

3.3 Robustness and limits

Learned classifiers have specific weaknesses. Sadeghi and Larsson showed that very small, carefully designed perturbations, known as adversarial examples, could sharply reduce the accuracy of a deep-learning modulation classifier, and that such attacks could be far more effective than conventional jamming of the same power[19]. In an EW context this means a classifier can itself become a target for electronic attack. Performance can also fall when the channel, hardware, or signal mix differs from the training data, which is why over-the-air testing, and not only simulation, was a focus of the 2018 work[15].

Status Laboratory to demonstrated. Deep-learning classifiers outperform feature-based baselines on benchmark data and in over-the-air laboratory tests[14,15]. Robustness to adversarial signals and to conditions outside the training set remains an active research problem[19].

4Autonomous spectrum management

4.1 Dynamic spectrum access

Traditional spectrum management assigns fixed frequencies to users in advance. Dynamic spectrum access instead lets radios find and use spectrum as conditions change. Zhao and Sadler grouped the approaches into three models: dynamic exclusive use, open sharing among peers, and hierarchical access, in which secondary users operate in bands held by primary users without causing them harmful interference[20]. Cognitive radio supplies the sensing and decision functions such schemes need[5].

A single radio may fail to detect a primary user because of fading or shadowing. Akyildiz and colleagues surveyed cooperative spectrum sensing, in which several radios share their observations so that the group decides more reliably than any one member[21]. A simple fusion rule declares a band occupied if any of N independent sensors detects a signal, so the cooperative detection probability is:

Qd = 1 − ∏i=1N (1 − Pd,i)

The same rule raises the false-alarm rate, so practical schemes weigh both measures and the cost of reporting over a control channel[21].

4.2 Collaborative autonomous radios

DARPA's Spectrum Collaboration Challenge (SC2), run from 2016 to 2019, asked teams to build radio networks that used artificial intelligence to share spectrum with other, unknown networks without central coordination. In the final event in October 2019, ten finalist teams competed in scenarios that included shrinking bandwidth, sudden surges in demand, and legacy systems sensitive to interference. The winning team, GatorWings from the University of Florida, used reinforcement learning to exploit each available portion of spectrum[22].

The competition ran on the Colosseum, a channel emulator built for the challenge. It supported 256 wireless devices with 128 software-defined radios and computed more than 65,000 channel interactions in real time using 64 field-programmable gate arrays. After the finale it moved to Northeastern University as a research testbed under the National Science Foundation's Platforms for Advanced Wireless Research programme[23].

4.3 Automated sharing in regulated spectrum

Automated spectrum coordination is already in civil use. In 2015 the Federal Communications Commission created the Citizens Broadband Radio Service in the 3550 to 3700 MHz band. Access is divided into three tiers (incumbent, priority, and general authorised access) and is managed by a Spectrum Access System, which the rules describe as a highly automated frequency coordinator. An Environmental Sensing Capability detects federal radar signals so that the Spectrum Access System can move or stop commercial users to protect the incumbent[24]. This arrangement is an example of sensing-driven, automated spectrum management protecting military radar while sharing the band.

4.4 Contested environments

Military networks must manage spectrum while an adversary is trying to deny it. Pirayesh and Zeng review anti-jamming strategies, including channel hopping, power control, and learning-based methods, and they note that a learning jammer can adapt in turn, so neither side's strategy is fixed[25]. Joint doctrine places such functions within electromagnetic spectrum management and the deconfliction of friendly emissions[1].

Status Fielded in civil spectrum sharing[24]. AI-based collaborative radios were demonstrated in large-scale emulation[22,23]. Autonomous spectrum management under deliberate jamming is still mainly a research topic[25].

5Digital twins

5.1 Definitions

Grieves and Vickers describe the digital twin as a virtual representation of a physical product that is linked to it through the product's life, and propose using it to find unpredictable and undesirable behaviour in complex systems before it appears in the physical system[26]. Glaessgen and Stargel, writing for NASA and the US Air Force, described it as an integrated, multiphysics, multiscale, probabilistic simulation of an as-built vehicle that uses the best available models, sensor data, and fleet history to mirror the life of its physical twin[27].

The US National Academies gave a more general definition in 2024: a digital twin is a set of virtual information constructs that mimics the structure, context, and behaviour of a physical system, is dynamically updated with data from that system, has a predictive capability, and informs decisions. The report treats the two-way interaction between virtual and physical as central, and states that "a digital twin without serious considerations of VVUQ" (verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification) "is not trustworthy"[28].

5.2 Relevance to EW

The Department of Defense digital engineering strategy aims to make models an "authoritative source of truth" across a system's life and to use them for virtual testing and sustainment[29]. In EW, three uses follow from the definitions above:

  • Equipment twins. A model of a receiver, jammer, or array, updated with calibration and health data, can predict how the real unit will perform as it ages or as its software changes[27,30].
  • Environment twins. A model of the electromagnetic environment, including emitters, propagation, and interference, lets adaptive systems be tested against many scenarios. The Colosseum, which emulated RF channels among 256 devices in real time, shows the scale of emulation that is now practical[23].
  • Assurance of adaptive systems. Because cognitive EW systems change their behaviour, they need large numbers of repeatable test cases. A validated environment model can supply these more cheaply than open-air testing alone[8,28].

5.3 Limits

The National Academies found that the maturity of digital twins "varies significantly across problem spaces", that many current examples are custom builds, and that one briefer described the evidence base for their value as "seriously lacking"[28]. Fuller and colleagues point to similar challenges in data integration, model fidelity, and standards[30]. For EW the hardest part is often the threat model, since the behaviour of an adversary's adaptive system is uncertain by nature.

Status Established as an engineering practice[29]; emerging for EW. Mature RF channel emulation exists[23], but full, continuously updated twins of EW systems and their environments are not documented in open sources, and VVUQ methods are still developing[28].

6Distributed sensing

6.1 Multisensor data fusion

Hall and Llinas define multisensor data fusion as combining data from several sensors, and related information, to achieve more accurate and more specific inferences than one sensor could provide. They describe a layered model running from detection and identification of single objects, through assessment of situation and threat, to management of the sensors themselves[31]. Distributed EW applies this model across platforms that may be far apart.

6.2 Emitter geolocation from separated receivers

Separated receivers can locate an emitter from the time difference of arrival (TDOA) of its signal and, if the platforms are moving, from the frequency difference of arrival (FDOA) caused by differing Doppler shifts. For receivers at positions si and s1, and an emitter at u, each TDOA measurement places the emitter on a hyperbolic surface:

c · τi1 = ‖u − si‖ − ‖u − s1‖

Ho and Xu gave an algebraic solution that combines TDOA and FDOA to estimate both the position and the velocity of a moving source, and showed that it approaches the Cramér-Rao lower bound when noise is small[32]. Accuracy depends on geometry, on time and frequency synchronisation between receivers, and on the bandwidth available to move data between them.

6.3 Networked and cooperative sensing

Distribution also helps detection. Haimovich, Blum, and Cimini showed that radar with widely separated antennas views a target from several aspects, so fluctuations in its radar cross-section average out, giving a diversity gain over a single co-located radar[33]. In the spectrum domain, cooperative sensing among several receivers reduces the chance that fading or shadowing at one site hides an emitter[21]. The cost in each case is coordination: sensors must share data, timing, and position over links that may themselves be jammed[31,25].

Status Fielded. Multi-platform TDOA/FDOA geolocation and data fusion are long-established methods[31,32]. What is emerging is fusion across many small, low-cost, or unmanned nodes in contested conditions (Section 10).

7Multifunction RF systems

7.1 Shared apertures

Ships and aircraft have traditionally carried a separate antenna and electronics chain for each function, which causes clutter, electromagnetic interference, and higher radar cross-section. The US Naval Research Laboratory's Advanced Multifunction RF Concept (AMRF) tested the alternative: shared wideband transmit and receive arrays, supported by common signal and data processing, that perform radar, EW, and communications at the same time[34]. The Office of Naval Research's Integrated Topside (InTop) programme built on this work, with the stated goals of reducing the number of topside antennas, easing interference and compatibility problems, and creating a scalable, open-architecture family of EW, radar, and communications systems for several ship classes[35].

7.2 Joint radar and communication waveforms

Sharing can also occur in the waveform itself. Hassanien and colleagues describe dual-function radar-communication systems, in which a radar embeds information in its transmissions, for example by varying sidelobe levels or waveform choice, so that one emission serves both functions and less spectrum is needed[36]. Liu and colleagues survey joint design more broadly, covering applications from vehicles to defence and the trade-offs between sensing accuracy and data rate[37].

7.3 Enabling technologies and limits

Multifunction operation depends on wideband apertures and on digital control of each element. Herd and Conway trace the move toward highly digitised arrays and note that low-cost commercial manufacturing methods are reducing cost[38]. The main limits are scheduling (the aperture's time and power must be shared among functions), isolation between simultaneous transmit and receive, and wideband element design[34,39].

Status Demonstrated. Shared-aperture radar, EW, and communications were demonstrated on testbeds and carried into naval programmes[34,35]. Integrated joint radar-communication waveforms are mostly at the research and early prototype stage[36,37].

8Photonic RF processing

8.1 Microwave photonics

Microwave photonics uses optical components to generate, transport, and process radio-frequency signals. Capmany and Novak list its advantages as very wide bandwidth, low loss over optical fibre, immunity to electromagnetic interference, and low size and weight, and they describe applications including photonic filtering, true-time-delay beamforming for arrays, and signal generation[40]. Yao gives a comparable review, covering photonic generation of microwave and millimetre-wave signals and photonic processing of microwave signals[41].

8.2 Functions relevant to EW

  • Wideband measurement. Zou and colleagues review photonic instantaneous frequency measurement, channelisation, spectrum analysis, and angle-of-arrival detection, and state that instantaneous frequency measurement is critical for radar, EW, and cognitive radio. They report photonic methods with instantaneous bandwidth above 20 GHz, against roughly 500 MHz of real-time bandwidth for electronic signal analysers of the time, but also note that photonic systems generally trail electronics in resolution, stability, and sensitivity[42].
  • Sampling. Valley reviewed photonic analogue-to-digital converters, which use the low timing jitter of mode-locked lasers to sample high-frequency signals more precisely than electronic clocks allow[43].
  • Signal generation and radar. Ghelfi and colleagues built a coherent radar in which a single mode-locked laser both generated the transmitted signal and sampled the echo. They reported better performance than state-of-the-art electronics above 2 GHz and detected non-cooperating aircraft in a field trial[44]. Pan and Zhang review later photonic radars, including wideband and multiband designs[45].

8.3 Integration

Early microwave photonic systems used discrete fibre components, which limited their size, cost, and stability. Marpaung, Yao, and Capmany describe progress in integrated microwave photonics, where light sources, modulators, and detectors are combined on one chip to make reconfigurable signal processors[46]. Noise figure, dynamic range, and conversion loss across the electro-optic interfaces are still the main engineering limits[40,42].

Status Demonstrated for selected functions. Fibre transport of RF signals is widely used, and photonic radar and wideband measurement have been demonstrated in field and laboratory tests[44,42]. Integrated photonic processors for EW are still at the laboratory stage[46].

9Advanced antennas

9.1 Electronically steered arrays

A phased array steers its beam by setting the relative phase or time delay of each element. For a uniform linear array with element spacing d and wavelength λ, the phase step between adjacent elements needed to steer the beam to angle θ0 is[47,48]:

Δφ = 2π d sin θ0 / λ

Because the phase step depends on wavelength, a phase-steered array points in a slightly different direction at each frequency across a wide band, an effect called beam squint. True time delay avoids this, which is one reason for interest in photonic delay lines for wideband EW arrays[47,40]. Element spacing must also stay below about half a wavelength at the highest frequency to avoid grating lobes, which makes wideband arrays harder to design[48].

9.2 Digital beamforming

In a fully digital array, each element or small subarray has its own receiver and digitiser, so beams are formed in software. Fulton and colleagues describe the benefits as multiple simultaneous beams, adaptive nulling of interference, and flexible use of the aperture, and the challenges as power, heat, data rates, and calibration[39]. Herd and Conway expect next-generation arrays to be highly digitised, helped by the rapid progress of commercial digital processors[38]. For EW, digital arrays allow wide-area search and precise direction finding at the same time as directed jamming.

9.3 Reconfigurable antennas and metasurfaces

Reconfigurable antennas change their frequency, pattern, or polarisation using switches, varactors, or tunable materials. Costantine and colleagues review the main design approaches and their uses in cognitive radio and other adaptive systems[49]. A related line of work uses programmable metasurfaces: Cui and colleagues introduced coding metamaterials, in which each cell is set to one of a few digital states so that the surface's scattering pattern can be programmed by an electronic controller[50].

Status Fielded for active electronically scanned arrays; demonstrated to fielded for digital beamforming, depending on band. Reconfigurable antennas and programmable metasurfaces are mainly at the laboratory and early prototype stage for EW[38,49,50].

10Unmanned systems

10.1 Distributed electronic support and spectrum awareness

Unmanned platforms can carry EW payloads closer to emitters than crewed aircraft, and in larger numbers. The US Army's EW plans have included long-endurance unmanned airborne EW systems[2]. A group of unmanned sensors also forms a natural distributed array for the geolocation and cooperative sensing methods of Section 6. Chung and colleagues survey aerial swarm robotics and identify the core problems: coordinated planning and control, state estimation and localisation, and communication within the swarm[51].

10.2 Resilient control and data links

Unmanned systems depend on their command, control, and data links. Gupta, Jain, and Vaszkun review UAV communication networks and discuss the routing, handover, and energy problems of keeping links alive when nodes move fast and topology changes[52]. DARPA's Collaborative Operations in Denied Environment (CODE) programme developed software to let groups of unmanned aircraft "work together under a single person's supervisory control" in "contested electromagnetic environments", with an architecture that tolerates weak communications links[53].

Recent combat has shown how exposed these links are. The Royal United Services Institute reported in 2023 that Ukrainian forces were losing about 10,000 unmanned aircraft a month, that Russian EW remained potent, and that Russia fielded roughly one major EW system per 10 km of front[54]. This experience has increased interest in drones that can navigate without satellite navigation, change frequency, or complete tasks with intermittent links[54,25].

10.3 Autonomy and human judgement

Greater on-board autonomy reduces dependence on links but raises questions of control. DoD Directive 3000.09 requires that autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems be designed to allow commanders and operators "to exercise appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force", and it covers non-kinetic as well as kinetic force[55]. CODE's model, in which a mission supervisor approves or rejects team actions, is one way of meeting such requirements[53].

Status Fielded. Unmanned EW and spectrum-sensing payloads are in widespread operational use[2,54]. Coordinated swarms performing distributed EW with high autonomy under jamming have been demonstrated in programmes and remain an area of active development[53,51].

11Quantum sensing

11.1 Principles

Degen, Reinhard, and Cappellaro define quantum sensing as the use of a quantum system, quantum properties, or quantum phenomena to measure a physical quantity. Their examples include long-established devices such as atomic clocks, atomic vapour magnetometers, and superconducting quantum interference devices, as well as newer platforms such as spin qubits and trapped ions[56]. The Congressional Research Service reports the Defense Science Board's view that quantum sensing is the most mature military application of quantum technology, and lists alternative navigation, submarine detection, and location of concealed forces by their emissions among its possible uses[57].

11.2 Rydberg-atom RF receivers

The most direct link to EW is the Rydberg-atom electric-field sensor. Atoms excited to very high energy states are extremely sensitive to electric fields, and lasers read out the field's effect through electromagnetically induced transparency in a small glass vapour cell. Sedlacek and colleagues demonstrated this method for microwave electrometry in 2012[58], and Holloway and colleagues showed that it gives broadband field measurements traceable to SI units through atomic constants, without the calibration a conventional antenna probe requires[59]. Later work showed reception of AM and FM radio signals[60] and an atomic superheterodyne receiver, which measured phase and frequency with a reported sensitivity of 55 nV cm−1 Hz−1/2[61].

These results need careful interpretation. Meyer and colleagues compared Rydberg sensors with conventional electronics from 1 kHz to 1 THz and found that, in their low-frequency comparison, a dipole antenna with passive electronics was significantly more sensitive than the free-space atoms. They also found instantaneous bandwidth limited by the optical readout, typically to the order of 10 MHz[62]. Fancher and colleagues similarly conclude that atomic sensors are unlikely to replace conventional receivers in common applications, but could serve niche uses, such as very wide tuning from one small sensor, calibration-free measurement, and vector field and angle-of-arrival measurement[63].

11.3 Other quantum approaches

  • Navigation. Atom interferometers can measure acceleration and rotation with low drift, which could reduce dependence on satellite navigation. Bongs and colleagues describe the engineering needed to move these sensors from laboratory to field, including reductions in size, weight, and power[64].
  • Quantum illumination. Lloyd showed in theory that using entangled photons can improve detection of a weakly reflecting object in a noisy, lossy environment, even though the entanglement does not survive[65]. Barzanjeh and colleagues reported a microwave quantum illumination experiment in a cryogenic laboratory set-up[66]. Sorelli and colleagues review this "quantum radar" literature for radar engineers[67]. Reported advantages so far apply only at very low signal powers and in laboratory conditions[66,67].

Status Research to laboratory for RF sensing. Rydberg receivers work in the laboratory and in early prototypes but do not generally beat conventional receivers in raw sensitivity or bandwidth[62,63]. Quantum radar remains at the research stage[66,67]. Quantum navigation sensors are the nearest to field use[64,57].

12Cross-cutting themes and outlook

The ten technologies depend on each other. Cognitive EW needs reliable classification and spectrum awareness, so it relies on the learned methods of Section 3. Adaptive systems need digital twins and large-scale emulation for testing. Distributed sensing and unmanned systems need resilient links and autonomous spectrum management. Multifunction systems need wideband digital arrays and, in some bands, photonic components. Three common challenges appear across the literature:

  • Assurance. Learning and adaptive systems are hard to verify, and the National Academies warn that models without verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification are not trustworthy[28,8].
  • Adversarial adaptation. Learned classifiers can be fooled by small perturbations, and learning jammers can respond to learning defences[19,25].
  • Governance and transition. GAO found that DoD spectrum strategies were limited by governance and oversight rather than by technology alone[3].

The common direction is toward EW systems defined more by software, models, and data than by fixed hardware and threat libraries. The open evidence supports that direction for classification, spectrum sharing, arrays, and unmanned platforms. It is thinner for full cognition in combat, for digital twins of EW systems, and for quantum RF sensing, where claims should still be read as research results.
