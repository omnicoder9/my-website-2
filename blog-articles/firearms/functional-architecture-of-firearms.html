What says when?

A firearm has no clock, no sensor and no feedback path. Every event in it is scheduled by a physical consequence of the event before. Asking what fixes each instant sorts the machine — and finds the hazard in the four events nothing schedules at all.

§ 1What says when?

A firearm has no clock. It has no sensor, no controller, and no feedback path of any kind between the instant it is fired and the instant it is ready to be fired again. Everything that happens inside it during that interval happens because something else happened first, and for no other reason.

This is a stranger fact than it looks. A self-loading rifle performs eleven distinguishable operations in about 75 milliseconds, in a fixed order, at temperatures and pressures that would destroy most machinery, with a failure budget of roughly one event in a thousand. It does this with no electrical system, no timing shaft, no cam tree, and nothing that measures anything. An internal combustion engine, which faces a comparable timing problem, solves it with a crankshaft: a single rotating datum that every valve, every injector and every spark is geared to. A firearm has no crankshaft. Each of its events is triggered directly by a physical consequence of the event before it.2,9

The usual way to describe a firearm is as a list of subsystems — barrel, chamber, receiver, bolt, firing mechanism, trigger, feed, extractor, ejector, recoil system, safeties, sights, furniture, mounting interfaces — followed by a list of cycle steps: load, chamber, lock, fire, unlock, extract, eject, cock, feed. Both lists are correct and neither explains anything. They describe a machine as an inventory, when the thing that actually makes it a machine is the schedule that binds the inventory together.

So this article asks one question of every part and every event:

The organising question

What says when?

What fixes the instant at which this happens? Not what causes it — causes are easy, and in a firearm almost everything is caused by expanding gas. What schedules it. What would have to change for this event to occur at a different moment, and what is holding that thing steady.

Asking it turns out to sort the machine cleanly. Some events are timed by geometry: they occur at a position, and a position cannot drift. Some are timed by an interlock: one part physically cannot move until another has. Some are timed by the pressure event itself. Some by a spring running down. Some by a person. And a small, important handful are timed by nothing at all.

The finding this article ends on is that the last category is not a miscellany. It is the whole hazard.

§ 2Four changes to the schedule

For four centuries the interesting problem in firearms was not power but latency: the delay between deciding to fire and the charge lighting. The matchlock closed a smouldering cord onto a priming pan. The wheellock spun a serrated wheel against pyrite. The flintlock scraped flint down steel to shower sparks into a pan of priming powder, which then had to flash through a touch-hole into the main charge.50,49 Each was an improvement in reliability, and each is documented in detail in the standard survey literature;52,21 but all shared the same architecture: a two-stage ignition with a variable and perceptible delay between them. Contemporary accounts of flintlock shooting are full of advice about holding through the lock time, because the lock time was long enough to matter to the aim.

The first genuine change in the schedule came in 1807, when Alexander John Forsyth patented the use of a detonating compound struck by a hammer rather than a flame conducted through a hole.15,51 The percussion principle collapsed a two-stage ignition into one and made the delay short enough to stop being a shooting problem. It also, less obviously, moved timing authority: ignition was now scheduled by the arrival of a hammer at a position, which is a geometric fact, rather than by the propagation of a flame, which is a chemical one.

The second change was the unitary metallic cartridge, developed through the nineteenth century from Pauly, Lefaucheux, Flobert and others into the drawn brass case with a centrally located primer.48,31,32 Its usual description — that it made loading faster — undersells it. The brass case solved the sealing problem in a way no mechanical breech had managed. A gas seal made of metal parts must be tight enough to hold pressure and loose enough to open, and those requirements fight. A brass case is neither: it is a deformable liner that is inflated into sealing by the very pressure it has to contain, and relaxes when the pressure goes.1,4 This is the single most consequential idea in the architecture, and we will come back to it.

The third was smokeless propellant, from Vieille's nitrocellulose powder of the 1880s onward.41,13,14 Beyond the obvious gains in energy density and visibility, it gave designers a burn rate they could shape. Black powder deflagrates at a rate largely fixed by its composition. A nitrocellulose grain can be made with a chosen web thickness, perforated to increase its burning surface as it is consumed, or coated with a deterrent to slow its initial burn. That control over the pressure–time curve is what made the modern schedule possible: the pressure event became something a designer could place.

The fourth was self-loading. Hiram Maxim's insight in the 1880s was that the recoil impulse, previously a nuisance to be absorbed, was a free power source large enough to run the entire reloading cycle.16,17 Browning's later gas-operated designs tapped the same energy from a port in the barrel instead.18,2,43 From that point the machine had to schedule itself, and the timing question stopped being about lock time and became about something much harder: how a mechanism running on borrowed energy can know that the pressure event is over.

Each of those four changes moved a critical event from a weaker timing authority to a stronger one, or created a new event that had to be scheduled. The vocabulary in §3 is an attempt to make that movement precise.

The vocabulary is my own, but the underlying decomposition is the conventional one used in armament engineering, where a weapon is treated as an energy source, a ballistic system and a mechanism, each with its own schedule.45,46,47

Two things about the coding need stating plainly, because everything later depends on them.

The rung is the weakest link, not the strongest. Most events are scheduled by a chain of several things. Chambering, for instance, requires a spring to push the bolt, a cam to strip the round, and a chamber shoulder to stop it. The rung is the weakest member of that chain, because the variance of a chain is set by its worst member. Chambering is coded T5 because the instant is fixed by the shoulder — the spring only has to be strong enough to get there, and a stronger spring does not make the round stop somewhere else. Where the weakest member does set the instant, as with the carrier's rearward run, the event drops down the ladder.

Rung is not importance. T0 does not mean unimportant; it means unscheduled. The four T0 events in this vocabulary are among the most consequential things a firearm can do.

Crossed with the rung is a second, independent question: what happens if the event occurs at the wrong moment. Four classes, again ordered: K0 self-correcting, K1 a stoppage, K2 damage to the mechanism, K3 uncontained — pressure or fragments leaving the arm by a path nobody designed. The two axes are orthogonal by construction, and the whole argument of this article is about how they turn out to be distributed.

§ 4The two clocks

Everything in this section comes from a lumped-parameter interior-ballistic integration run at build time, listed in full in §15. Its constants are stated illustrative round numbers for a hypothetical intermediate cartridge. They are chosen to put the model in the right decade and nothing more; the point of the exercise is a set of ratios, and the ratios are robust to the constants in a way the absolute figures are not.

The model is the standard one: a Noble–Abel equation of state for the propellant gas, a form function relating burnt fraction to a geometric burn variable, a pressure-dependent burn rate, and a Lagrange approximation for the pressure gradient between breech and projectile base.12,5,8,6 It integrates to muzzle exit and then models the residual gas emptying through the bore as a choked isentropic blowdown.

The result is a machine with two clocks running at different speeds.

The propellant's clock runs for 1.63 milliseconds. The mechanical clock runs for 75. The ratio is 45.9 to one, and the projectile — the entire purpose of the device — has left the building during the first 2.18 per cent of the cycle.

Two consequences follow immediately, and between them they explain most of the architecture.

The first is that nothing mechanical can respond to the pressure event while it is happening. At a mean acceleration of about 61,123 g, no linkage, no spring, no mass in the action can travel a useful distance in the time available. The action does not react to the shot; it reacts to the aftermath of the shot. Everything the mechanism does is scheduled by events that are already over.

The second is that the projectile spends 41 per cent of the in-bore period not moving at all. Shot start does not occur until pressure has climbed high enough to force the projectile into the rifling. Until then the chamber is a closed vessel, and the pressure rise is governed only by burn rate and the free volume left between the grains.

Spin is imposed over the same interval and by the same pressure. The rifling accelerates the projectile rotationally as well as linearly, and the twist rate needed for stability has been estimated since the nineteenth century by rules of thumb relating it to projectile length and diameter.39,34 What happens to the projectile after it leaves the muzzle — exterior and terminal behaviour — is a separate literature and outside this article entirely.42

The shape of that curve is the compromise the whole cartridge is designed around. Peak pressure is bounded by what the chamber and case can survive, which is why the pressure ceilings for every commercial cartridge are published as standards rather than left to designers: SAAMI in the United States, the C.I.P. in most of Europe, whose 1969 convention on the reciprocal recognition of proof marks is why a proof stamp applied in one member state is accepted in the others.23,24,25,26 Velocity, meanwhile, is the integral of pressure over travel. A designer wants area under the curve without height, which is exactly what progressive propellant geometry buys: a grain whose burning surface grows as it is consumed releases its energy later, into a larger volume, flattening the peak for the same total impulse.5,13,8

§ 5Where the energy goes

The energy accounting is worth doing carefully, because it explains why several subsystems exist at all.

Roughly 32 per cent of the chemical potential ends up as projectile kinetic energy, which is close to what real guns achieve and is a respectable figure for a heat engine of any kind.4,8 A further 4.0 per cent is kinetic energy in the propellant gas itself, which is moving fast and has mass. Bore resistance takes 1.0 per cent. The remainder — 63 per cent — is internal energy still in the gas at muzzle exit. This model is adiabatic, so the heat that a real tube absorbs comes out of that last line rather than appearing separately; §10 treats it on its own terms.

Now the part that matters for architecture. Momentum, unlike energy, is conserved in a way the shooter feels directly. The projectile carries 3.916 N·s. The gas carries more, and continues to carry it after muzzle exit, when the emptying bore acts briefly as a rocket nozzle; the conventional accounting puts total recoil impulse at about 1.56 times the projectile's alone.1,40

Divide that impulse into a 3.0 kg arm and free recoil velocity is 2.04 m/s, carrying 6.24 J — about 0.33 per cent of the muzzle energy. The asymmetry is entirely a mass ratio: the same momentum in a body some 750 times heavier carries a proportionally smaller share of the energy.

That number is why the recoil-management subsystem looks the way it does. Stocks, buffers, recoil pads and muzzle devices are not fighting a large quantity of energy. They are fighting a rate. The impulse is delivered over roughly 1.6 ms in the bore, and the arm then moves for the remainder of the cycle. Every part of recoil management is a device for lengthening the time base of a fixed impulse, or for redirecting some of the gas momentum, or for putting the resultant force closer to the line of the shooter's shoulder so that it produces less rotation.11,33 None of them reduces the impulse itself, because nothing can. Muzzle devices in particular trade recoil against blast and noise directed back at the shooter, which is why the acoustic consequences are themselves a specified design constraint.36

§ 6Containment, and the dwell problem

Here is the central problem of the whole architecture, and the one that separates a firearm from every other machine that manages a combustion event.

The breech must be shut, and stay shut, while there is enough pressure behind the case to matter. Then it must open. Nothing tells it when. The mechanism must schedule its own opening against a curve it cannot observe, using energy it took from that same curve, with no ability to check its work.

Obturation: the seal that pressure makes

Start with the seal, because the whole problem inherits its shape. When the charge ignites, the brass case expands radially against the chamber wall and seals the breech from the inside.1,4,7 This is worth pausing on. The gas seal in a firearm is not a designed interface between two rigid parts. It is a thin deformable liner that is inflated into contact by the pressure it is containing, and which relaxes — mostly — when the pressure goes away.

The elegance is that the seal gets tighter exactly when it needs to. The cost is that the same mechanism grips the case to the chamber wall with enormous force at peak pressure, and that grip does not vanish instantly. This is why primary extraction exists as a distinct stage: a slow, high-mechanical-advantage initial movement, usually produced by a cam ramp of deliberately shallow slope, whose only job is to break a case free that the architecture itself stuck there.3,7,9 The machine must mechanically undo a seal it made pneumatically.

Dwell

If the breech opens while residual pressure is still high, several things happen, none of them good: the case is pulled against friction it cannot survive and tears, sometimes leaving its forward half in the chamber; gas escapes rearward into the action along the only path now available; the case head, no longer supported, may fail outright. So the action must wait. The interval it waits is dwell, and it is the only requirement anywhere in the machine that is naturally stated as an interval rather than as a position.2,9

I built the third experiment expecting to find that dwell was hard because the pressure curve is variable — that ammunition lot, temperature and barrel condition would move the requirement around, and the mechanism would be chasing a moving target. The experiment says the opposite, clearly enough that it reorganised this section.

Blowdown from a fixed volume through a choked orifice is close to logarithmic in initial pressure. Raising muzzle pressure by a quarter does not delay the crossing by a quarter; it delays it by the log of a quarter, which is almost nothing. Across a half-decade swing the safe-opening instant moves 42 microseconds. The propellant's clock is the precise one.

The mechanism is not. Model a carrier of stated mass receiving a stated impulse and running a stated free travel against a return spring before the unlocking cam picks up, and a ±20 per cent variation in that impulse — a thoroughly ordinary spread across ammunition, fouling, temperature and wear — moves the unlocking instant by 1953 microseconds.

The finding this article turns on

The requirement is stable to within 42 microseconds and the mechanism that must meet it wanders by 1953 — a ratio of 46 to one. Locked breeches are therefore designed with margin rather than precision, because nothing in the action can be trusted to hit a microsecond.

At nominal the model unlocks 0.89 ms after the earliest safe instant. At +20 per cent impulse that margin falls to 0.17 ms — 19 per cent of what it was. The direction is what makes it dangerous: a hotter round both raises the pressure the breech must contain and shortens the delay before the breech opens. The two errors compound.

This is the reason locking systems exist, and the reason their variety is so much narrower than the variety of other firearm subsystems. Rotating bolts with multiple lugs, tilting blocks, falling blocks, toggle links, rollers and rotary bolt heads are all answers to the same question: how to hold a load path closed for a couple of milliseconds and then get out of the way quickly, using a cam whose profile fixes where unlocking happens even though the impulse that drives it fixes when the cam gets there.3,7,19,22 The cam converts a badly-timed input into a well-defined output, which is the single most characteristic move in the whole architecture.

Delayed and retarded blowback designs make the trade explicit by removing the lock entirely and substituting mass, friction or a mechanical disadvantage.2,22 They work, at modest impulses, and they are instructive precisely because they show what a lock is buying: not strength, but a way of making the opening instant a function of position rather than of momentum.

§ 7A chain that never resets

Once the breech is open the machine still has 71 milliseconds of work to do, and it does all of it on a single impulse taken at the beginning. No further energy arrives. The carrier runs rearward carrying the case, the case is extracted and thrown clear, the hammer or striker is re-cocked, the return spring absorbs what momentum is left, reverses it, drives the carrier forward, a fresh round is stripped and chambered, and the action locks. Nine stages, one power stroke, no clock.3,9,7

The consequence of having no clock is that timing error accumulates. Each event's arrival time is the sum of every duration before it, so the variances add. Nothing anywhere in the cycle resets the reference.

At the unlocking boundary the accumulated standard deviation is 0.311 ms. Against a dwell margin of 0.891 ms that is 2.87 standard deviations — comfortable but not lavish, and a reminder that real designs carry considerably more margin than this illustrative one, for exactly this reason. By the end of the cycle the spread has grown 7.7-fold to 2.40 ms, which is 3.2 per cent of the cycle and worth about 51 rounds per minute of variation in cyclic rate.

Where the tolerance is spent

The architecture puts its only tight requirement at the point where the least error has yet accumulated, and spends the rest of the cycle on events that do not care when they happen.

Feeding is a good example of the second kind. A round must be stripped from the feed device and chambered, and the sequence has to be right, but there is no instant at which it must occur; the requirement is entirely one of position and relative order. The feed lips must release the case at a point in the bolt's travel where the bolt face has already captured it; the chamber shoulder stops the round wherever the round happens to arrive. The timing of feeding is therefore T5 even though the energy delivering it is a spring running down, because the spring's variance changes when the round is chambered, not whether it is chambered correctly.3,7,38,44

The cocking stage is the interesting hybrid. Its instant is fixed by geometry — the carrier rides over the hammer at a position — but it is paid for out of a momentum budget that is nearly spent by the time it is asked for. This is why short-stroking, where the carrier travels far enough to extract and eject but not far enough to re-cock, is a recognisable and specific failure rather than a general malaise.28,20

Reversal, at the rear of the stroke, is the softest-timed event in the whole machine. It has no position and no interlock: the carrier stops when the spring has absorbed its energy, and it starts forward when the spring says so. It is also, uncomfortably, adjacent to some of the worst consequences in the vocabulary, because a carrier that bounces at the rear can present itself to the feed device at a moment nothing intended.

§ 8The order is the machine

Treat the cycle as a partial order rather than a sequence and ask how much freedom the constraints leave. The answer is startling.

Eleven steps admit 39,916,800 orderings. The precedence constraints admit 3. That is a fraction of 7.52e-08, and the only freedom remaining is where cocking falls among extraction, ejection and feeding — which is precisely the design decision that differs between real actions and precisely the one that has no safety consequence.

More useful than the count is what happens when a constraint is violated. Take each reversible precedence, reverse it, and name the result. Of the 10 that can meaningfully be reversed, 4 produce uncontained events, 1 produce damage, and 5 produce a stoppage. And the uncontained ones are not scattered: their mean distance from the firing step is 1.50 steps against 4.58 for everything else. Every uncontained ordering error lies within 2.5 steps of ignition.

Locking before chambering; firing before locking; unlocking before the projectile has left. These are the same failure wearing three costumes, and the costume is proximity to the pressure event. Move an event across the ignition boundary and it becomes catastrophic; move one at the far end of the cycle and you get a stoppage and a bad afternoon.

This is what the safety subsystem is actually for. A manual safety, a disconnector, an out-of-battery interlock and a firing-pin block are not general-purpose protections. Each one enforces a specific precedence in the immediate neighbourhood of ignition — the neighbourhood the experiment identifies as the only one where reversal is uncontained.3,7,35 The disconnector in particular exists to answer a question the pressure event cannot: has the previous shot finished before the next one starts. It is one of the only two events in the whole vocabulary coded as a mechanical interlock, and its companion is the manual safety.

§ 9Failure, in series

The cycle is nine stages in series with no redundancy anywhere. There is exactly one path through it, every stage is a single point of failure, and the system reliability is the product.

With the stated figures the arm completes a cycle with probability 0.999350, which is a mean of 1,539 rounds between stoppages, a 98.1 per cent chance of finishing a thirty-round magazine untroubled, and 87.2 per cent for a seven-magazine load. Those are plausible service figures rather than measured ones, but the structure they sit in is exact. Real figures come from instrumented endurance firing under standardised environmental conditions — dust, mud, icing, salt fog — because every one of those conditions attacks a different stage of the same series chain.37

Two things fall out. First, effort is worth spending only where the unreliability is. Improving the worst stage tenfold takes the mean from 1,539 to 2,128. Improving the best stage tenfold takes it to 1,554 — a gain of well under one per cent, for the same engineering effort. Second, the arithmetic of series reliability is brutal at the top end: to reach a mean of 10,000 rounds between stoppages, every one of the nine stages must run at 0.9999889, a budget of 1.11e-05 failures per stage per round.

The historical record bears this out in an unusually well-documented way. The early service history of the M16 in Vietnam is the standard case study: a combination of a propellant change that altered the pressure curve and the fouling behaviour, chambers that were not initially chromium lined, and a supply of cleaning equipment that had been deemed unnecessary, produced extraction failures at rates that a congressional subcommittee investigated in 1967.29,20,30 The instructive part is not the scandal but the mechanism: the failures concentrated in extraction, which is exactly where a series chain with a case-gripping stage would be expected to break when the gas curve and the chamber surface both move at once.

What a stoppage tells the operator

Almost nothing, is the answer, and this turns out to be a structural property rather than a training problem. §11 takes it up.

§ 10Two loops that never close

Two loops in the system never close, and they are different in kind.

Heat

A firearm is an open thermal system with no measurement anywhere in it. Each shot deposits energy in the tube; the tube sheds heat to the air by convection and radiation; nothing in the architecture compares the two.

On the stated assumptions the tube retains 1200 J per shot and gains 2.55 K with it. At 200 °C it rejects heat fast enough to sustain 10.4 rounds per minute. The mechanism will deliver 800. The gap is a factor of 77.

Nothing in the machine knows this. There is no thermostat, no interlock, no rate governor tied to temperature. The consequences are real: chamber temperature is what sets the risk of cook-off, in which a chambered round is ignited by conducted heat with the trigger untouched, and barrel temperature drives the thermo-chemical erosion that eventually ends a barrel's accurate life.27,10,2 Quick-change barrels, heavy profiles, fluting, and the doctrinal rate limits printed in operator manuals are all responses to a loop the architecture declines to close. The last of those is the most telling: the governor is a person reading a table.

The subsystems the cycle never touches

The sighting system, the furniture and the accessory interfaces appear on every list of firearm subsystems and appear nowhere in the functional cycle. Nothing schedules them. Nothing in the cycle can cause them to fail, and their failure cannot stop the cycle.34,35

They belong to a different loop — the only closed loop in the whole system. The shooter observes the result, corrects, and fires again. That loop has a sensor (the eye), a comparator (the sight picture), an actuator (the hands) and a feedback path (the fall of shot). It is the only part of the arrangement that deserves the word control at all, and it runs at human speed: several hundred milliseconds, against the mechanism's 75.

Which means that in a self-loading arm the machine is routinely ready before the person is. Recoil recovery is the one place where the two clocks are compared, and the slow one governs. That is a design fact with consequences for stock geometry, grip angle, muzzle devices and the entire discipline of practical marksmanship — all of which are, in the terms of this article, attempts to shorten the only loop in the system that has any feedback in it.35,34,11

The accessory interface is the odd one out even here. A mount holds a zero by geometry and preload, which is a strong timing authority, but nothing verifies it and nothing announces when it slips. A drift that develops over weeks is, from behind the arm, indistinguishable from the shooter's own error. It is the only subsystem in the vocabulary combining strong authority with zero observability, and it is worth noticing that the rest of the machine avoids that combination.

§ 11What the machine cannot tell you

I coded every event in the vocabulary for whether a person operating the arm can tell that it happened: directly, by inference from something else, or not at all. The experiment was built to test a specific hypothesis — that the machine hides its most dangerous moments. That hypothesis is not supported.

33.3 per cent of the damaging and uncontained events are invisible; 29.4 per cent of the benign ones are. Those are the same number. Severity does not predict blindness, and I am reporting the hypothesis as refuted rather than quietly reframing it.

What does predict blindness is speed. The propellant's clock is 71.4 per cent invisible; everything else averages 21.4 per cent. A factor of 3.33. The machine is blind exactly where it is fast, which on reflection is not a design choice at all but a consequence of human perception: an event lasting a fraction of a millisecond, inside a steel tube, behind a closed breech, cannot be perceived, and no amount of design intent changes that.

The practical consequence shows up in diagnosis. A stoppage presents as a symptom — the trigger moves and nothing happens, the bolt will not close, a case is trapped in the port — and each symptom is consistent with many causes.

Mean ambiguity is 4.67 causes per symptom and the minimum is 3. The machine emits, in effect, about one bit where nine stages failed.

This is why immediate-action drills are stage-agnostic. The standard response taught everywhere — reseat the feed device, cycle the action, try again — makes no attempt to diagnose. On this coding it addresses 11 of the 20 causes, or 55 per cent, without the operator ever learning which one it was.28,35 That is not a crude drill. It is the correct response to an information-theoretic fact about the machine: when the signal cannot distinguish the causes, the efficient action is the one that covers the most causes at once. The remaining 9 require an armourer, and the drill's real function is to sort quickly between the two populations.

§ 14What the architecture is doing

The question was: what says when? Having asked it of 35 events, four answers stand out, and together they amount to a description of what the architecture is for.

It converts timing problems into position problems wherever it can. This is the characteristic move, and the cam is its instrument. An impulse of uncertain magnitude arrives, drives a mass through an uncertain interval, and reaches a cam surface. From that point the outcome is geometric: the lugs disengage through the angle the cam cuts, in the order the cam dictates, regardless of how long the mass took to get there. 15 of 35 events end up on fixed geometry, and almost all of them got there this way.

It spends its tolerance where variance is low. The only interval requirement in the machine — dwell — sits at the second stage of the cycle, where accumulated timing error is 0.311 ms. By the end of the cycle that error has grown 7.7-fold, and by then every remaining event is a position or an order rather than an instant.

It uses interlocks only next to the pressure event. Two events in the whole vocabulary are timed by a mechanical interlock: the manual safety and the disconnector reset. Both sit within one step of ignition, which is precisely the neighbourhood where the ordering experiment says a reversal becomes uncontained. Interlocks are expensive — a part that must move when another part has moved is a part that can fail to — and the architecture buys exactly as few of them as the hazard requires.

What it cannot schedule, it hands to a person. Cook-off has no thermostat, so the schedule is a rate limit in a manual. Case failure has no predictor, so the answer is a vent path and a shooter's face kept off the line of it. Whether to fire at all has no mechanical answer, so it is a finger. The sighting loop is the only closed loop in the system, and the person is in it.

Which is the uncomfortable conclusion, and I think the true one. A firearm is a machine that schedules everything it possibly can, with the strongest authority available, in an order it enforces almost totally — and then leaves, untimed and unmeasured, the small set of events that are the reason the whole thing is dangerous. That is not a failure of design. Cook-off cannot be scheduled by a machine with no thermometer, and adding a thermometer changes what the object is. But it means that the safety of a firearm is not, and architecturally cannot be, a property of the firearm alone. The schedule ends where the hazard begins, and a person is standing in the gap.

A_BORE  = math.pi / 4 * D_BORE ** 2
L_TRAV  = 0.457        # m     projectile travel in the bore
V0      = 1.90e-6      # m^3   initial chamber volume
IMPETUS = 1.00e6       # J/kg  propellant force constant
COVOL   = 1.00e-3      # m^3/kg covolume
RHO_P   = 1600.0       # kg/m^3 propellant density
GAMMA   = 1.25
T_FLAME = 2800.0       # K
R_GAS   = IMPETUS / T_FLAME
P_START = 15.0e6       # Pa    shot-start (engraving) resistance
P_FRIC  = 5.0e6        # Pa    equivalent constant bore resistance
BURN_N  = 0.90         # Vieille exponent
P_REF   = 100.0e6      # Pa    burn-rate reference pressure
BURN_B  = 790.0        # 1/s   lumped burn-rate coefficient
FORM_L  = 0.20         # form-function progressivity
FORM_X  = 1.0 / (1.0 + FORM_L)
DT      = 2.0e-8       # s     integration step

def form(z):
    z = min(max(z, 0.0), 1.0)
    return FORM_X * z * (1.0 + FORM_L * z)

def state_pressure(z, x, v, wfric):
    psi = form(z)
    vfree = V0 + A_BORE * x - M_C * (1.0 - psi) / RHO_P - COVOL * M_C * psi
    ekin = 0.5 * (M_P + M_C / 3.0) * v * v
    e = IMPETUS * M_C * psi - (GAMMA - 1.0) * (ekin + wfric)
    return max(e / vfree, 1.0), psi

LAG_BASE   = 1.0 / (1.0 + M_C / (3.0 * M_P))
LAG_BREECH = (1.0 + M_C / (2.0 * M_P)) / (1.0 + M_C / (3.0 * M_P))

def integrate_bore():
    """Lumped-parameter interior ballistics to muzzle exit."""
    t = x = v = wfric = 0.0
    z = 0.0
    trace = []
    moving = False
    t_start = t_allburnt = None
    while x < L_TRAV and t < 0.01:
        p, psi = state_pressure(z, x, v, wfric)
        pb = p * LAG_BASE
        if not moving and p >= P_START:
            moving = True
            t_start = t
        if z < 1.0:
            z = min(1.0, z + DT * BURN_B * (p / P_REF) ** BURN_N)
            if z >= 1.0 and t_allburnt is None:
                t_allburnt = t
        if moving:
            a = A_BORE * (pb - P_FRIC) / M_P
            if a < 0 and v <= 0:
                a = 0.0
            v += a * DT
            dx = v * DT
            x += dx
            wfric += A_BORE * P_FRIC * dx
        t += DT
        if len(trace) == 0 or t - trace[-1][0] >= 1.0e-6:
            trace.append((t, p, pb, p * LAG_BREECH, x, v, psi))
    p, psi = state_pressure(z, x, v, wfric)
    trace.append((t, p, p * LAG_BASE, p * LAG_BREECH, x, v, psi))
    return dict(t=t, x=x, v=v, p=p, wfric=wfric, z=z, psi=psi,
                t_start=t_start, t_allburnt=t_allburnt, trace=trace)

def blowdown(p_e, v_e, dur=0.030):
    """Choked isentropic emptying of the residual gas through the bore."""
    m = M_C
    T = p_e * v_e / (m * R_GAS)
    T_e, m_e = T, m
    coef = (2.0 / (GAMMA + 1.0)) ** ((GAMMA + 1.0) / (2.0 * (GAMMA - 1.0)))
    dt = 1.0e-7
    t = 0.0
    out = [(0.0, p_e)]
    while t < dur and m > 1e-9:
        T = T_e * (m / m_e) ** (GAMMA - 1.0)
        p = m * R_GAS * T / v_e
        if p <= 1.0e4:
            out.append((t, 1.0e4)); break
        dm = p * A_BORE * math.sqrt(GAMMA / (R_GAS * T)) * coef
        m -= dm * dt
        t += dt
        if t - out[-1][0] >= 2.0e-6:
            out.append((t, p))
    return out

def cross(series, thr_pa):
    """Linearly interpolated time at which a (t, p) series first falls below thr."""
    prev = series[0]
    for cur in series[1:]:
        if cur[1] <= thr_pa:
            (t0, p0), (t1, p1) = prev, cur
            if p0 == p1:
                return t1
            return t0 + (p0 - thr_pa) * (t1 - t0) / (p0 - p1)
        prev = cur
    return None

def run(rate_rps, nmax=100000, tmax=1200.0):
    T = T_AMB
    t = 0.0
    dt = 0.002
    nxt = 0.0
    fired = 0
    hit = None
    hist = [(0.0, T)]
    while t < tmax:
        while nxt <= t and fired < nmax:
            T += q_shot / (M_BARREL * C_STEEL)
            fired += 1
            nxt += 1.0 / rate_rps

        if hit and t > hit[1] + 2:
