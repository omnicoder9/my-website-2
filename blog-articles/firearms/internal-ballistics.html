1The question

A gun is a heat engine that runs once, for about a thousandth of a second, and is then thrown away and rebuilt from a fresh cartridge. Everything that distinguishes a good one from a bad one happens inside that interval, between the fall of the firing pin and the moment the projectile's base clears the crown. That interval is the subject of internal ballistics.1, 2

It is tempting to describe the subject as the study of how much energy a cartridge delivers. That framing is wrong in a way that hides the entire discipline. Energy is not the scarce resource. A charge of gun propellant carries several megajoules per kilogram, and the study cartridge used throughout this article holds very nearly ten kilojoules of chemical energy against a projectile that leaves with three and a third.3 Two thirds of the energy is thrown away as heat in the barrel and as hot gas at the muzzle, and nobody loses sleep over it.

The scarce resource is pressure. Specifically, it is the single highest pressure the weakest component in the load path will tolerate without permanent deformation — a number that belongs to the case head, or to the locking lugs, or to the barrel at the chamber mouth, but never to the ammunition.4, 5 Everything the gas does to the projectile is bought with that number, and the number is spent in one instant near the beginning of the shot.

That figure states the problem completely. The work delivered to the projectile is the shaded area, multiplied by the bore area. The structural demand on the gun is the height of the curve. The two are not the same quantity and they are not even measured in the same place: the area accumulates across the whole travel, while the height is set in the first five per cent of it. Internal ballistics is the practice of buying area without buying height.

Ask the question that way and the subject rearranges itself. Every mechanism becomes a lever with a price, and the price is denominated in peak pressure. Some levers are dear: adding charge mass, in the model's own numbers, returns 0.80 per cent more velocity for 2.20 per cent more peak pressure.6 Some are cheap. Three of the twelve continuous levers tested here raise velocity while lowering peak pressure, which is to say they are free or better than free. And one lever — the shape of the propellant grain — raises velocity with the pressure ceiling held exactly, arithmetically constant, which is why the entire industry of propellant manufacture is an industry of geometry rather than chemistry.

This article works through the twenty-nine mechanisms in the interior ballistic cycle, grouped into the four families the subject conventionally uses: what the cartridge is, how the charge burns and what that does to pressure, what happens in the barrel, and what the rifling is for. Each mechanism is presented as a plate with a fixed set of four fields — what it changes, what it is paid in, when it runs out, and when in the shot it acts — and a gauge showing the window of the cycle it occupies, drawn against the same pressure trace every time so the plates can be compared at a glance.

The numbers come from a lumped-parameter interior ballistics solver written for this article and reproduced in full below. A word on what that model is and is not is owed immediately, because the distinction matters more here than in most subjects. The physics is the standard textbook energy balance, the same skeleton that sits inside the codes armies actually use.7, 8 The propellant constants, however, are generic illustrative values chosen to place the solution in a physically representative region. They are not the constants of any commercial propellant and they are not fitted to any measured trace. Nothing in this article is load data, and nothing in it can be turned into load data — a lumped-parameter model without lot-specific closed-vessel characterisation cannot predict the pressure of a real charge, which is precisely why ammunition is developed against instrumented pressure measurement and published only by propellant manufacturers.9, 4 What the model is good for is the shape of relationships, and the shapes are what the argument needs.

2How the subject got its equations

For most of the history of firearms nobody could measure what was happening inside one. The instruments did not exist, the event was too fast, and the only observable was where the ball went. Benjamin Robins changed that in 1742 by refusing to measure the inside at all. His ballistic pendulum caught the projectile and inferred its velocity from the swing of a known mass, which gave the first reliable muzzle velocities and, with them, the first quantitative statement of what a powder charge was actually worth.10 Charles Hutton extended the method with heavier pendulums and larger guns three decades later.11

Velocity at the muzzle is an integral, though. It says what the whole shot delivered without saying anything about how. The first instrument to see inside the chamber was Thomas Rodman's, in the 1850s: a hardened cutter pressed by a piston into a copper disc, leaving a cut whose depth scaled with the peak force.12 The principle — deform a calibrated piece of soft metal and read the deformation afterwards — survived as the copper crusher gauge for over a century, and its limitations survived with it. A crusher records one number, the peak, and it records it with a dynamic response that is not the same as the static calibration used to interpret it.13

The decisive work came from William Armstrong's factory at Elswick and the War Office laboratory at Woolwich. Andrew Noble and Frederick Abel fired gunpowder in sealed vessels of known volume, varied the loading density systematically, and measured the pressure that resulted. Their 1875 paper in the Philosophical Transactions established the quantities the subject still uses: the force constant of a propellant, the covolume correction for the finite size of the gas molecules at the enormous densities involved, and the observation that the products of combustion are substantially independent of loading density.14 A second paper followed in 1880.15 The equation of state they arrived at, a van der Waals form with the attraction term dropped and only the covolume retained, is still called the Noble-Abel equation and is still what the code below uses. Henry Debus contested their chemistry at length; the thermodynamics survived.16

The second pillar was laid by Paul Vieille. In 1884 he had produced the first practical smokeless powder by gelatinising nitrocellulose — Abel and Dewar's nitroglycerin-based cordite followed in 1889 on a different chemical route to the same end17 —, and in doing so created a propellant whose grains were dense, homogeneous and dimensionally controlled — which meant that for the first time the burning surface was something a designer could specify.18 In 1893 he published the law that made that specification useful: a propellant surface regresses normally to itself at a rate that depends on the pressure over it, following a simple power law, and the flame does not eat into the grain in any other direction.19, 20 Parallel-layer burning is an idealisation, and a century of combustion research has catalogued where it fails.21, 22 It is nevertheless the assumption that turns an intractable combustion problem into an ordinary differential equation, because it separates the mass generation rate into three independent factors: rate, density, and surface area. Grain geometry controls the third factor alone, and controls it completely.

The mathematics was assembled in France. Henri Resal wrote the energy balance in 1864, in the form the code below still integrates.23 Émile Sarrau added the theory of charging and the form function that describes how burning surface evolves with consumption.24 Prosper Charbonnier's 1908 treatise consolidated the field,25 and Carl Cranz's second volume did the same for the German tradition.26 Julian Hatcher's Notebook, assembled from three decades of US Ordnance work, remains the most accessible record of what the American service actually measured and how often the measurements surprised them.27 Andrew Noble's collected lectures are the readable companion to the Philosophical Transactions papers.28 Velocity measurement had meanwhile moved on from the pendulum: Le Boulenge's falling-rod chronograph of 1864 timed the projectile between two break screens and set the pattern every later instrument has followed.29, 30 Augustus Love and Frederick Pidduck solved the problem of the gas's own motion — the fact that a gas with mass cannot be at uniform pressure while it is accelerating a projectile — in 1922,31 and Robert Kent produced the special solutions that gave the practical breech-to-base ratio still used today.32

By 1950 the analytical phase was finished. John Corner's Theory of the Interior Ballistics of Guns and the British Ministry of Supply's Internal Ballistics are the canonical statements of what could be done with closed-form methods, and both are explicit that the closed forms exist only because of simplifying assumptions the authors did not entirely believe.2, 33 What broke the deadlock was arithmetic. Paul Baer and Joseph Frankle's 1962 report at the Ballistic Research Laboratories put the lumped-parameter model on a digital computer, and the constraint stopped being tractability and started being data.8 The line runs directly from there to IBHVG2, still the reference lumped-parameter code,7 and past it to the two-phase flow codes that resolve the propellant bed as a granular medium with gas percolating through it.34, 35, 36, 37 The 1979 AIAA volume edited by Krier and Summerfield is the standard survey of that transition.38

Those later codes exist because the lumped-parameter model has one specific and important blind spot: it assumes the whole charge is at one pressure and one temperature, and the most dangerous failures in gun design are precisely the ones where that is false. Ignition that propagates unevenly through a long charge can produce pressure waves travelling within the bed, and those waves can do damage that no space-mean pressure would predict.39, 40 For the small-arms cycle described here, where the charge is short and the ignition interval is a few tens of microseconds, the lumped assumption is sound. For a long artillery chamber it is a design decision, not a given.

3Reading one shot

This is the asymmetry that organises everything. It means the peak and the work are separable in a way that a naive reading of the pressure trace conceals, and it means the mechanisms that act before the peak and the mechanisms that act after it are playing completely different games. Anything happening in the first 28 millimetres is spending the budget. Anything happening in the remaining 531 millimetres is collecting on it.

It also means the phases of the cycle are worth naming, because a mechanism's phase predicts its economics. This article uses six, and they are the colour scheme throughout: the static condition fixed before anything happens; ignition, from the firing pin to the first self-sustaining propellant gas; shot start, in which the projectile breaks free and is engraved; the rise, in which gas generation is outrunning volume growth; the peak, where the two cross; and expansion, in which the charge finishes burning and the gas does the rest of its work against a volume growing faster than it can fill.

Twenty-nine mechanisms, placed on the shot they act in. Ten of them finish inside one per cent of the barrel travel. Eleven act across four fifths of the shot or more. The two groups behave so differently that they are best thought of as separate subjects, and the rest of this article is largely an argument that the narrow ones matter more than their width suggests.

4Cartridge architecture

A cartridge does four jobs that have nothing in common. It stores chemical energy, it converts a mechanical blow into ignition, it seals the breech against the gas it makes, and it presents a projectile to the rifling at a controlled position. The reason the metallic cartridge displaced everything before it is not that it did any of these jobs especially well, but that it did all four in one disposable object, and in particular that it moved the gas seal from the gun to the ammunition.42, 43

That last point deserves emphasis because it is easy to miss. In a muzzle-loading or early breech-loading weapon, sealing the breech is the gun's problem, and it is a hard one: metal sliding on metal cannot be gas-tight at three hundred megapascals without tolerances nobody could hold. The brass case solves it by being deliberately weaker than its surroundings. It expands hard against the chamber wall, seals against it, and then springs back just enough to release when pressure falls.44 The seal is made and destroyed on every shot, which is why case material is chosen for ductility and elastic recovery rather than strength, and why steel and polymer cases are hard problems rather than obvious savings.45, 46 Removing the case altogether, as the caseless G11 programme attempted, removes the seal and the heat sink at once and transfers both problems back to the weapon — which is most of why it never entered service.47 Elongated projectiles arrived on a parallel track: the Minie ball's expanding skirt let a muzzle-loader be loaded loose and still seal against the rifling.48

Case geometry follows from the arithmetic of the pressure equation rather than from any aesthetic. Velocity wants a large charge behind a small bore area; the pressure ceiling wants that charge's gas spread over a long expansion. A bottleneck case delivers both by decoupling the propellant column's diameter from the bore's, which is the geometric innovation that made high-velocity smallbore rifle cartridges possible at all.49, 1 The shoulder also gives a hard datum for headspace that does not depend on the rim, which is what allows rimless cases to stack properly in a box magazine.50

The primer is the component most often treated as a detail and most consequential when it is wrong. A firing pin delivers on the order of a joule; the charge needs a hot, particle-laden jet to light it evenly along its length. The conversion is done by a percussion-sensitive compound — historically mercury fulminate, then lead styphnate with a barium nitrate oxidiser and an antimony sulphide fuel, now increasingly lead-free chemistries driven by range air-quality regulation.51, 52, 53 What matters ballistically is that the output is particles, not flame: incandescent solids reliably ignite a propellant bed and bare flame does not.54, 55 Alexander Forsyth's percussion patent of 1807 began the line;56 Berdan's and Boxer's competing designs of 1868 and 1869 settled the architecture that is still in use, differing only in whether the anvil belongs to the case or to the primer cup.57, 58

5Combustion, and the geometry that schedules it

A gun propellant is not an explosive in the detonating sense. It deflagrates: a reaction front propagates through it at metres per second rather than kilometres, driven by heat conduction from the flame back into the solid, and it does so from every exposed surface simultaneously.3, 59 The practical consequence is that the rate at which a charge makes gas is the product of three separable quantities — the linear burning rate, the propellant density, and the total burning surface area.

The burning rate follows Vieille's law, a power law in the pressure over the surface. The exponent is the most consequential number in the whole system and the one a designer controls least. The lab's elasticity test makes the point brutally: moving the rate coefficient two per cent moves peak pressure 3.2 per cent, while moving the exponent two per cent moves peak pressure 87.4 per cent. It is not a design lever. It is a property of the chemistry, and a propellant whose exponent drifts with temperature or lot is a propellant whose ceiling cannot be trusted.21, 60

Density is fixed. That leaves surface area, and surface area is geometry, and geometry is something a factory can control to a few microns. This is the whole reason gun propellant is manufactured in shapes.

The form function describes how the burnt mass fraction evolves as the web — the minimum thickness of solid between two burning surfaces — is consumed.24, 61 Its derivative is the burning surface area, and the sign of that derivative names three regimes. A degressive grain, such as a sphere or a flake, loses surface as it burns: all its area is on the outside and the outside is shrinking. A neutral grain, such as a single-perforation tube, holds area roughly constant, because the outer surface shrinking is offset by the inner surface growing. A progressive grain, such as the seven-perforation cylinders used in artillery, gains area as it burns, because the perforations grow faster than the outside shrinks — until the slivers between them break through and the remainder burns degressively.6, 62 Spherical propellant, patented by Fred Olsen in the 1930s and made by emulsifying lacquered nitrocellulose into droplets, is the extreme degressive case and is manufactured that way because spheres meter accurately by volume.63 One effect the form function does not capture is erosive burning, where high-velocity gas flowing across a grain surface raises its burning rate above the pressure law alone — a second-order effect in small arms and a first-order one in long chambers.64

The consequence is the central result of this article, and it is worth stating carefully because the naive comparison gets it backwards. Simply swapping a degressive grain for a progressive one in the same cartridge lowers both pressure and velocity, because the progressive grain makes gas more slowly at every stage. That comparison is uninformative: it is comparing a hot load with a mild one.

The informative comparison holds the constraint fixed. In the lab, each of the four grain shapes has its burn coefficient re-trimmed by bisection until all four reach exactly the same peak pressure of 304.5 megapascals — the ceiling is then arithmetically identical and the only remaining difference is the shape of the curve underneath it.

At an identical peak pressure, the progressive grain delivers 857.0 metres per second against the degressive grain's 755.7. That is 101.3 metres per second, 13.4 per cent, bought purely by the order in which the gas arrives. No extra charge, no extra pressure, no extra barrel. The progressive grain simply declines to spend its budget early: it arrives at the ceiling later, having done less work, and then keeps generating gas while the expansion volume opens up, filling in area on the right-hand side of the curve where pressure is cheap.

This is the only lever in the entire study that raises velocity with peak pressure held exactly constant, and it explains a great deal about the industry. It is why propellant manufacturers sell dozens of products whose chemistry is nearly identical and whose geometry is not; why artillery uses multi-perforated grains and handguns use spherical ones; and why "burn rate" charts, which rank propellants on a single axis, are a lossy summary of a two-dimensional problem.

Temperature enters the same machinery from outside. Both the burning rate and the impetus rise with the propellant's initial temperature, and the two effects compound.60, 3

Conditioned from minus fifty-four to plus sixty-six degrees Celsius — the range military specifications actually require65, 66 — the study round's peak pressure moves 60.9 per cent while its muzzle velocity moves 22.8 per cent. Pressure is 2.67 times as temperature-sensitive as velocity in relative terms. A round sitting comfortably inside its ceiling on a temperate morning is not sitting comfortably inside it on a hot vehicle deck, and the margin that looks like excessive caution at twenty-one degrees is the entire margin at sixty.

6Pressure: what it is, and where

"Chamber pressure" is not a number. It is a field, and the field has a gradient, and the gradient is large enough that a figure quoted without a measurement location is not a measurement.

The reason is straightforward once stated. The gas between the breech face and the projectile base has mass, and that mass is being accelerated along with the projectile. It can only be accelerated by a pressure difference, so pressure must fall monotonically from breech to base. Lagrange's idealisation — uniform gas density along the tube — gives the result in closed form, and Love, Pidduck and Kent worked out the practical consequences.31, 32

The ratio of breech pressure to base pressure is one plus half the charge-to-projectile mass ratio. It is kinematic. It does not depend on the propellant, on the pressure, on the barrel, or on anything a designer can adjust except the ratio of the two masses. For the study cartridge it is 1.153: the breech reads 318.5 megapascals while the projectile base sees 276.4. For a handgun it is under five per cent. For a tank gun firing a fin-stabilised penetrator, where the charge can outweigh the projectile, the breech reads eighty per cent above the base.

This is why the standards bodies are so specific. SAAMI's rifle standard and the CIP's rules each specify not just a maximum but a method and a location: a conformal piezoelectric transducer at a defined distance from the breech face, or a copper crusher in a defined port, with the two giving systematically different numbers.4, 5, 67, 68 A figure measured by one method is not convertible into a figure measured by the other by any fixed factor, because the crusher's response is a peak-detecting deformation with its own dynamics while the piezoelectric transducer reports a time history.13 Parallel standards cover pistol and rimfire ammunition on the same pattern,69, 70 and military ammunition is qualified against its own agreements.71

The limit itself belongs to the gun, not the cartridge — and specifically to whichever component yields first, which for small arms is usually the case head, the locking lugs, or the barrel at the chamber mouth.72, 73 The enforcement mechanism is proof: firing a deliberately overpressure round and then inspecting for permanent set, a practice that predates the theory by centuries and is still the legal basis on which firearms are sold in much of Europe.74, 5 Heavy ordnance adds autofrettage, deliberately overstraining the bore to leave compressive residual stress that raises the elastic limit of the assembly.75

It is worth being explicit about what a published maximum means, because it is routinely misread. SAAMI and CIP maxima are statistical limits on a conditioned sample: a limit on the mean of a lot and, separately, a limit on the sample extreme.4, 76 They are not a threshold below which a gun is safe and above which it fails. The gun's actual failure point is higher and unknown, the margin between them absorbs the temperature sensitivity just demonstrated, plus lot-to-lot propellant variation, plus chamber dimensional tolerance, plus the erosion the barrel will accumulate over its life. The margin is the engineering, not padding on top of it.

The last figure in this section is the one that makes the case for that whole apparatus. Peak pressure and muzzle velocity are plotted against charge mass on logarithmic axes, so the slope is the elasticity directly. Velocity's slope is 0.797. Pressure's slope is 2.197. One per cent more charge buys eight tenths of a per cent more velocity and costs two point two per cent more peak pressure, a ratio of 2.76 — and the pressure exponent is itself rising as charge increases, which is what a superlinear response on a log-log plot means.

Those exponents are properties of the equations. They do not respond to care, experience, or intent. A real propellant's curve is steeper still, because the model here omits the two-phase effects that dominate at high loading density, and it is not extrapolable from any point on it — which is the entire argument for published load data developed against instrumented pressure measurement, and against the assumption that a small increment is a small risk.77, 9 The other end of the failure spectrum is not a pressure problem at all: a bore obstruction turns the barrel into a second chamber at the point of the obstruction, and the resulting pressure has nothing to do with how the round was loaded.78, 79

7Resistance, and the shape of the peak

The pressure peak is not made by the propellant alone. It is made by the propellant pushing against something that will not move, and the something is mostly the rifling.

Resistance to projectile motion is not a constant drag. It is an enormous spike followed by a modest running friction.80, 81 The spike has two components: the force needed to pull the bullet from the case neck, which depends on neck tension and crimp, and the far larger force needed to swage the bearing surface into the rifling. In the model these are combined into a shot-start pressure and an engraving resistance that decays over the first five millimetres of travel — 0.89 per cent of the barrel.

That window is tiny and its leverage is enormous. Raising engraving resistance from zero to sixty megapascals moves peak pressure from 246.8 to 373.0 megapascals: a fifty-one per cent swing, bought entirely in the first one per cent of the travel. The reason is the feedback loop in the burn rate law. Holding the projectile still for longer keeps the volume small while gas continues to arrive, which raises pressure, which burns propellant faster, which raises pressure again. Anything that delays the projectile is multiplied by that loop; anything that happens after the peak is not.

The lab turns up a detail here worth keeping. Below about thirty-nine megapascals, the nominal shot-start pressure makes no difference whatever — the first three rows of the sweep are numerically identical. The explanation is that once the projectile is free of the case its resistance is engraving, worth thirty-nine megapascals at zero travel, so any lower nominal shot-start value is never the binding constraint. The effective shot start is set by the rifling, not by the case neck. This is why throat erosion, which lengthens the free run before engraving begins, shows up as a pressure and velocity change out of all proportion to the metal removed — and why it is the first thing to check when a load that was fine last season is not fine now.82, 83

Against that spike, the expansion volume is doing the opposite job. Once the projectile moves, volume grows linearly with travel while gas generation eventually falls, and the crossover between them is the peak. Everything after it is the gas expanding into a space growing faster than it can fill. The expansion ratio — final volume over initial — is 8.58 for the study gun, and it is the quantity that decides how much of the charge's energy can be recovered as work before the remainder is thrown away at the muzzle.1, 2

8Barrel dynamics

Once the projectile is engraved and moving, the barrel's job is to stay out of the way for as long as possible. This sounds trivial and is not.

Length is the cleanest lever in the entire subject, because it acts exclusively after the peak has passed. Adding barrel cannot raise peak pressure — the peak happened at 28.6 millimetres and the added metal is at the far end — so every metre per second it delivers is free in the currency this article is denominated in.

It is also the lever with the most punishing diminishing return. Half of the muzzle velocity is delivered in the first 75 millimetres, 13.4 per cent of the barrel. Doubling the study barrel from 560 to 1,120 millimetres adds 82.3 metres per second, under ten per cent. The marginal yield falls below ten metres per second per 25 millimetres at 450 millimetres of travel, and below five at 700.

And it reverses. Past roughly 3,100 millimetres, the gas pressure behind the projectile has fallen below the equivalent back-pressure of running friction, and additional barrel makes the projectile slower rather than faster. This is not a curiosity: it is the reason very long guns are an artillery problem rather than a small-arms one, since the crossover length scales with the expansion ratio and the charge-to-mass ratio, both of which are far larger in heavy ordnance.84, 1

Friction itself is a thermal problem disguised as a performance problem. Removing running friction entirely would be worth under a hundredth of a per cent of muzzle velocity in the model — it is simply not a meaningful energy loss. But the same rubbing deposits heat into a thin surface layer of the bore at rates that nothing else in engineering matches,85, 86 and that heat is the input to the erosion mechanism that ends the barrel's life.

Gas leakage is the one mechanism in the study that is pure loss. An intact obturating band means leakage is negligible; a worn throat, an undersized projectile, or a gas port that vents early means it is not.87, 88 Unlike every other lever here, it removes pressure and velocity simultaneously, so it never appears as a favourable trade. It also self-reinforces, because the hot gas escaping past a poor seal erodes the surface that was supposed to make the seal.

At the muzzle, 57.0 megapascals are still present — 18.7 per cent of peak. That gas does not politely dissipate. It exits as a supersonic jet that briefly overtakes the projectile, producing the blast wave, the flash, a substantial fraction of the recoil impulse, and an asymmetric pressure field that disturbs the projectile during the first few calibres of free flight.89, 90, 91 Muzzle pressure is the clearest single measure of energy the expansion failed to recover, and the case for longer barrels, progressive powders and larger expansion ratios is in the end an argument about making this number smaller.

Finally, the tube moves. A barrel is a cantilever being struck hard by an internal pressure wave and by the reaction to projectile acceleration, and it is still ringing when the projectile leaves.92, 93, 94 Because barrel time varies shot to shot with muzzle velocity, successive rounds exit at slightly different points in the vibration cycle and therefore at slightly different muzzle pointing angles — which converts velocity dispersion into angular dispersion. The underlying mechanics are not in doubt and are measurable.95 The projectile is also moving laterally within the bore while this happens, balloting between the lands in a way that couples to the tube's own motion.96 More radical approaches exist: the rarefaction wave gun deliberately vents the breech after peak pressure to cut recoil while leaving the forward-going expansion largely intact.97 What is much weaker is the body of claims, widely circulated in the shooting literature, that a load can be tuned to a specific barrel time corresponding to a stationary point of muzzle angle; that hypothesis is not supported by peer-reviewed work at anything like the confidence with which it is repeated.98

9Rifling

Rifling is the only part of the interior ballistic system that exists for something outside it. Everything else in this article is concerned with getting the projectile out of the tube efficiently. The rifling is concerned with what happens afterwards, and it degrades interior performance to do it.

The problem it solves is aerodynamic. A projectile long enough to be efficient in flight has its centre of pressure ahead of its centre of mass, which makes it statically unstable: any yaw grows rather than decays.99, 100 Spin converts that divergence into precession. The projectile still yaws, but it processes around the trajectory instead of tumbling off it.

The quantity delivered is exact and unromantic: spin rate is muzzle velocity divided by twist length. Nothing else from the interior ballistic cycle enters except through velocity. At the study's 840.2 metres per second, a one-turn-in-twelve-inch twist yields 17,310 radians per second — 165,404 revolutions per minute — with the bearing surface travelling sideways at 67.7 metres per second while it travels forward at 840.

What that costs is almost nothing. Rotational kinetic energy at the muzzle is 6.75 joules against 3,354 joules of translation: 0.201 per cent of the energy budget. The spin is free. What is not free is the engraving required to impose it, and that cost is charged to the pressure curve rather than to the energy budget — which is exactly why it appears in this article under resistance rather than here.

How much twist is needed has been contested for a century and a half. Alfred Greenhill, then teaching mathematics to artillery officers at Woolwich, derived a rule in 1879 after concerns that excessive twist was wrecking mortars; his simplified form, twist proportional to the square of diameter over length, is still quoted.101, 102 Don Miller's 2005 rule adds projectile mass and a more realistic shape assumption.103 Applied to six representative projectiles in the lab, Miller specifies twists 21.3 per cent faster than Greenhill on average — Greenhill was derived for lead cylinders and systematically under-specifies for modern jacketed and composite-construction projectiles, which are lighter for their length. Later refinements handle plastic tips and open-tip match designs, whose density is not uniform.104, 105, 106 For serious work the semi-empirical rules give way to six-degree-of-freedom aerodynamic computation.107, 108, 109 Gain-twist rifling, which starts slow and accelerates the spin-up along the bore, spreads the torque reaction over a longer travel instead of concentrating it at engraving.110 Rinker's textbook is the standard introduction for readers approaching the field from the shooting side rather than the ordnance side.111

The bore geometry that transmits the torque has two main families. Conventional rifling cuts discrete lands and grooves with defined corners: it seals well, engraves predictably, and leaves the individualising toolmarks that firearms identification depends on.43 Its weakness is geometric — the corner at the base of a land is both a stress concentration and a place where hot gas washes against a thin edge of steel. Polygonal rifling replaces the corners with a continuous curve, removing the stress raiser and spreading the seal over a longer contact. The idea is Victorian: Whitworth's hexagonal bore of the 1850s is the direct ancestor, and Metford's shallow rounded grooves were a response to the same fouling and erosion problems.112, 113, 114 It returned to production because cold hammer forging makes a curved profile more easily than a cornered one.

Manufacturing method is not a detail that disappears once the barrel exists. Cut rifling removes metal one groove at a time and leaves almost no residual stress. Button rifling swages the whole form through in a single pass — fast, and stress-heavy enough that barrels are usually stress-relieved afterwards.115 Broaching cuts all grooves at once. Hammer forging beats the barrel down over a mandrel, forming bore, grooves and often the chamber in one operation, which is why it dominates military production.116 Electrochemical machining removes metal without tool contact and without work-hardening the surface.117 The choice shows up later as dimensional consistency along the bore, and as how the barrel moves when it gets hot.

All of it is temporary. Erosion is three processes running simultaneously at the commencement of rifling, where gas is hottest and the metal thinnest.82, 118 Thermal: the surface is driven above its transformation temperature in microseconds and quenched by conduction into the mass behind it, cycling the near-surface metal through a phase change on every shot until it cracks.119, 120 Chemical: the combustion products are hydrogen- and carbon monoxide-rich and reducing, and they attack and carburise the exposed steel, producing a brittle white layer.121, 122 Mechanical: the weakened surface is then scrubbed away by the next projectile.123, 124 Chromium plating and alternative bore coatings slow the chemical and thermal attack and introduce failure modes of their own, since a cracked plating concentrates the gas attack at the cracks.122, 120

The interior ballistic consequence closes the loop on this article. As the throat erodes it lengthens, which gives the projectile a longer free run before engraving begins, which lowers the effective shot-start resistance — and plate B06 has already shown exactly what a change in that window does to the peak. A barrel's pressure curve is not a fixed property of the weapon. It drifts across the barrel's life, in a direction the ammunition never knows about.125, 118

10The lab

Every number quoted in this article comes from the program below, which is stdlib-only, deterministic, and reproduced in full so that any of it can be checked or contradicted.

The solver integrates three state variables — the fraction of web burnt, projectile travel, and velocity — with fixed-step fourth-order Runge-Kutta, recovering pressure algebraically at each stage from the Resal energy balance with a Noble-Abel covolume term.23, 14 Convergence was checked against step size: the solution is stable to six significant figures between 2 nanoseconds and 20 nanoseconds per step, and the reported runs use 20.

Three limitations should be stated plainly. First, the model is lumped: it assumes one pressure and one temperature throughout the charge, which is sound for a short small-arms chamber and is a design assumption rather than a given for long ones.39, 37 Second, heat transfer to the barrel is not modelled explicitly; it is absorbed into an effective propellant force, which is standard practice in lumped-parameter work and is part of why the constants here are described as illustrative rather than physical.2, 7 Third, and most importantly, the propellant constants are generic. They are not any commercial product's, they are not fitted to any measured trace, and the outputs are sensitivity shapes rather than magnitudes anyone should act on.

"""
INTERIOR BALLISTICS LAB  --  stdlib only, deterministic, seed 20260917
=====================================================================

A lumped-parameter ("zero-dimensional") interior ballistics solver of the
classical Resal type, plus nine experiments that produce every number quoted
in the article.

WHAT THIS IS.  The model is the standard textbook energy balance: propellant
burns according to a pressure-dependent rate law, the gas it makes does work
on a projectile moving down a tube, and an equation of state with a covolume
term relates pressure to the free volume available.  It is the same skeleton
that sits inside BRLs IBHVG2 and every other lumped-parameter code.

WHAT THIS IS NOT.  The propellant constants below are GENERIC ILLUSTRATIVE
VALUES chosen to put the solution in a physically representative region.
They are not the constants of any commercial propellant, they are not fitted
to any measured pressure trace, and the chambering is notional.  Nothing here
is load data and nothing here can be turned into load data: a lumped-parameter
model without lot-specific propellant characterisation and closed-bomb
calibration cannot predict the pressure of a real charge, which is precisely
why ammunition is developed against instrumented pressure measurement and
published only by propellant manufacturers.  The experiments below are
sensitivity studies -- they show the SHAPE of relationships, not magnitudes
anyone should act on.

Everything is SI.  Pressures are reported in MPa for readability.
"""

import math
import json

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1.  PARAMETERS OF THE NOTIONAL SYSTEM
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# A notional 7.62 mm rifle.  Dimensions are round numbers in the region of a
# common military rifle chambering; they are not taken from any standard.

class Sys:
    def __init__(self, **kw):
        # -- gun geometry
        self.d      = 7.82e-3      # groove diameter, m
        self.V0     = 3.55e-6      # chamber volume behind the seated bullet, m^3
        self.L      = 0.560        # bullet travel from rest to muzzle, m
        self.twist  = 0.305        # rifling twist length (1 turn in), m

        # -- projectile
        self.m      = 9.50e-3      # projectile mass, kg

        # -- charge
        self.w      = 2.90e-3      # propellant charge mass, kg

        # -- propellant (GENERIC ILLUSTRATIVE -- see header)
        self.f      = 8.60e5       # force / impetus, J/kg
        self.alpha  = 1.00e-3      # covolume, m^3/kg
        self.rho    = 1600.0       # solid propellant density, kg/m^3
        self.gamma  = 1.25         # ratio of specific heats of the gas
        self.beta   = 1.05e-4      # burn coefficient, 1/(s.Pa^n)  (= u1/e1)
        self.n      = 0.85         # burn rate pressure exponent
        self.T0     = 2900.0       # isochoric flame temperature, K
        self.Tprop  = 294.0        # propellant temperature, K (21 C)

        # -- form function  psi = chi*z*(1 + lam*z + mu*z^2), chi(1+lam+mu) = 1
        self.chi    = 0.780
        self.lam    = 0.282
        self.mu     = 0.0

        # -- primer / igniter, modelled as a gas source equivalent to burning a
        #    small fraction of the charge, delivered over a finite interval
        self.psi_ig = 0.0100       # igniter output as an equivalent mass fraction
        self.t_ig   = 50.0e-6      # duration of igniter output, s

        # -- resistance to motion
        self.p_ss   = 35.0e6       # shot-start pressure, Pa
        self.p_eng  = 30.0e6       # peak engraving resistance above friction, Pa
        self.x_eng  = 5.0e-3       # engraving decay length, m
        self.p_fr   = 9.0e6        # running bore friction as equivalent pressure, Pa

        for k, v in kw.items():
            if not hasattr(self, k):
                raise KeyError(k)
            setattr(self, k, v)

    @property
    def A(self):                    # bore cross-sectional area, m^2
        return math.pi * self.d * self.d / 4.0

    @property
    def phi(self):                  # secondary-work (Lagrange) factor
        return 1.0 + self.w / (3.0 * self.m)

    def copy(self, **kw):
        s = Sys()
        s.__dict__.update(self.__dict__)
        for k, v in kw.items():
            if not hasattr(s, k):
                raise KeyError(k)
            setattr(s, k, v)
        return s


def form(s, z):
    """Mass fraction of the charge burnt when fraction z of the web is gone."""
    if z <= 0.0:
        return 0.0
    if z >= 1.0:
        return 1.0
    return s.chi * z * (1.0 + s.lam * z + s.mu * z * z)


def resistance_pressure(s, x, moving):
    """Resistive force expressed as an equivalent pressure over the bore area."""
    if not moving:
        return s.p_ss
    return s.p_eng * math.exp(-x / s.x_eng) + s.p_fr


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2.  THE SOLVER
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def igniter(s, t):
    """Igniter output as an equivalent burnt-mass fraction, ramped then held."""
    if t <= 0.0:
        return 0.0
    if t >= s.t_ig:
        return s.psi_ig
    return s.psi_ig * (t / s.t_ig)


def solve(s, dt=2.0e-8, xmax=None, record=True, nrec=1400):
    """
    Integrate the interior ballistic cycle with fixed-step RK4 on (z, x, v).

    Pressure is recovered algebraically at each stage from the energy balance

        f*w*psi  =  p * Vfree  +  (theta/2) * phi * m * v^2

    with  Vfree = V0 + A*x - (w/rho)*(1-psi) - alpha*w*psi
    and   theta = gamma - 1.

    p here is the SPACE-MEAN pressure.  The Lagrange gradient relates it to the
    two pressures anyone actually measures or cares about:

        p_breech = p * (1 + w/(2m)) / (1 + w/(3m))
        p_base   = p / (1 + w/(3m))
    """
    theta = s.gamma - 1.0
    A, m, w, phi = s.A, s.m, s.w, s.phi
    Lmax = s.L if xmax is None else xmax

    def pressure(z, x, v, t):
        psi = form(s, z) + igniter(s, t)
        if psi > 1.0:
            psi = 1.0
        Vfree = s.V0 + A * x - (w / s.rho) * (1.0 - psi) - s.alpha * w * psi
        if Vfree <= 1e-12:
            Vfree = 1e-12
        p = (s.f * w * psi - 0.5 * theta * phi * m * v * v) / Vfree
        return (p if p > 0.0 else 0.0), psi

    def deriv(state, t):
        z, x, v = state
        p, _ = pressure(z, x, v, t)
        moving = (x > 0.0) or (v > 0.0)
        pr = resistance_pressure(s, x, moving)
        dz = s.beta * (p ** s.n) if z < 1.0 else 0.0
        if not moving and p < s.p_ss:
            return (dz, 0.0, 0.0), p            # still locked up
        net = p - pr
        dv = (A * net) / (phi * m)
        if not moving and dv < 0.0:
            dv = 0.0
        return (dz, v, dv), p

    z = x = v = 0.0
    t = 0.0

    ts, ps, xs, vs, zs = [], [], [], [], []
    p_max, t_pmax, x_pmax = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    t_start = t_engr = t_burn = None
    step = 0
    keep = max(1, int(1))

    while x < Lmax and t < 6.0e-3:
        (k1, p) = deriv((z, x, v), t)
        if p > p_max:
            p_max, t_pmax, x_pmax = p, t, x
        if t_start is None and (x > 0.0 or v > 0.0):
            t_start = t
        if t_engr is None and x >= s.x_eng:
            t_engr = t
        if t_burn is None and z >= 1.0:
            t_burn = t

        if record and (step % 40 == 0):
            ts.append(t); ps.append(p); xs.append(x); vs.append(v); zs.append(form(s, z))

        h = dt
        s1 = (z, x, v)
        (k2, _) = deriv(tuple(s1[i] + 0.5 * h * k1[i] for i in range(3)), t + 0.5 * h)
        (k3, _) = deriv(tuple(s1[i] + 0.5 * h * k2[i] for i in range(3)), t + 0.5 * h)
        (k4, _) = deriv(tuple(s1[i] + h * k3[i] for i in range(3)), t + h)
        z, x, v = (s1[i] + (h / 6.0) * (k1[i] + 2 * k2[i] + 2 * k3[i] + k4[i])
                   for i in range(3))
        if z > 1.0:
            z = 1.0
        t += h
        step += 1

    p_mu, psi_mu = pressure(z, x, v, t)
    if record:
        ts.append(t); ps.append(p_mu); xs.append(x); vs.append(v); zs.append(form(s, z))

    chem = s.w * s.f / (s.gamma - 1.0)       # chemical energy available, J
    ke = 0.5 * s.m * v * v

    return {
        "t_muzzle": t, "v_muzzle": v, "x_final": x,
        "p_max": p_max, "t_pmax": t_pmax, "x_pmax": x_pmax,
        "p_muzzle": p_mu, "psi_muzzle": psi_mu, "z_final": z,
        "t_start": t_start, "t_engr": t_engr, "t_burnout": t_burn,
        "chem_J": chem, "ke_J": ke, "eff": ke / chem,
        "p_breech_max": p_max * (1 + s.w / (2 * s.m)) / (1 + s.w / (3 * s.m)),
        "p_base_max": p_max / (1 + s.w / (3 * s.m)),
        "trace": {"t": ts, "p": ps, "x": xs, "v": vs, "psi": zs} if record else None,
    }

# E3.  ELASTICITY LEDGER  --  what does a percent of peak pressure buy?
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
def e3(base):
    hdr(3, "Elasticity ledger: muzzle velocity bought per unit of peak pressure")
    r0 = solve(base, record=False)
    v0, p0 = r0["v_muzzle"], r0["p_max"]
    say("  baseline: v = %.2f m/s, p_max = %.2f MPa" % (v0, MPa(p0)))
    say("")
    say("  Each lever is moved 2% in whichever direction RAISES muzzle velocity, and")
    say("  the resulting change in peak pressure is the price. eta = (dv/v)/(dp/p):")
    say("  high eta is a cheap lever; free means the velocity cost no pressure at all.")
    say("")
    levers = [
        ("charge mass",          "w",     +1),
        ("propellant force",     "f",     +1),
        ("burn coefficient",     "beta",  +1),
        ("burn rate exponent",   "n",     +1),
        ("chamber volume",       "V0",    -1),
        ("barrel travel",        "L",     +1),
        ("projectile mass",      "m",     -1),
        ("bore diameter",        "d",     -1),
        ("shot-start pressure",  "p_ss",  +1),
        ("engraving resistance", "p_eng", +1),
        ("bore friction",        "p_fr",  -1),
        ("covolume",             "alpha", -1),
    ]
    say("  %-22s %6s %10s %10s %10s %9s"
        % ("lever", "dir", "dv/v %", "dp/p %", "eta", "verdict"))
    out = []
    for name, attr, sgn in levers:
        best = None
        for s_try in (sgn, -sgn):
            s2 = base.copy(**{attr: getattr(base, attr) * (1.0 + 0.02 * s_try)})
            r2 = solve(s2, record=False)
            dv = 100.0 * (r2["v_muzzle"] - v0) / v0
            if best is None or dv > best[0]:
                best = (dv, 100.0 * (r2["p_max"] - p0) / p0, s_try)
        dv, dp, s_try = best
        if dv <= 1e-3:
            verdict, eta = "inert", 0.0
        elif dp <= 1e-6:
            verdict, eta = "free", 0.0
        else:
            eta = dv / dp
            verdict = "cheap" if eta >= 0.5 else ("fair" if eta >= 0.25 else "dear")
        say("  %-22s %+5d%% %9.4f%% %9.4f%% %10s %9s"
            % (name, 2 * s_try, dv, dp,
               ("  --  " if verdict in ("free", "inert") else "%.4f" % eta), verdict))
        out.append({"lever": name, "attr": attr, "dir": 2 * s_try, "dv": dv,
                    "dp": dp, "eta": eta, "verdict": verdict})
    say("")
    free = [o for o in out if o["verdict"] == "free"]
    priced = sorted([o for o in out if o["verdict"] not in ("free", "inert")],
                    key=lambda o: -o["eta"])
    say("  levers that raise velocity WITHOUT raising peak pressure: %d of %d" % (len(free), len(out)))
    for o in free:
        say("    %-22s  dv = %+.4f%%   dp = %+.4f%%" % (o["lever"], o["dv"], o["dp"]))
    say("")
    say("  priced levers, ranked by velocity bought per unit of peak pressure:")
    for o in priced:
        say("    %-22s eta = %7.4f  (%s)" % (o["lever"], o["eta"], o["verdict"]))
    best, worst = priced[0], priced[-1]
    say("")
    say("  FINDING: among levers that cost pressure, %s is the cheapest at eta = %.4f"
        % (best["lever"], best["eta"]))
    say("           and %s the dearest at eta = %.4f -- a spread of %.2fx. But the"
        % (worst["lever"], worst["eta"], best["eta"] / worst["eta"]))
    say("           levers that matter most are the %d that cost nothing at all." % len(free))
    RESULTS["e3"] = {"v0": v0, "p0": p0, "rows": out, "free": free,
                     "priced": priced, "best": best, "worst": worst,
                     "spread": best["eta"] / worst["eta"], "n_free": len(free)}

def e4(base):
    hdr(4, "Barrel length: where the returns run out")
    say("  %8s %10s %12s %12s %10s" % ("travel", "velocity", "d v /25 mm", "muzzle p", "burnt"))
    say("  %8s %10s %12s %12s %10s" % ("mm", "m/s", "m/s", "MPa", "%"))
    prev = None
    rows = []
    Ls = ([0.025 + 0.025 * i for i in range(0, 40)] +
          [1.10 + 0.20 * i for i in range(0, 25)])   # 25 mm .. 5.9 m
    for L in Ls:
        r = solve(base.copy(L=L), record=False)
        dv = (r["v_muzzle"] - prev) if prev is not None else float("nan")
        prev = r["v_muzzle"]
        rows.append({"L": L, "v": r["v_muzzle"], "dv25": dv,
                     "pmu": r["p_muzzle"], "psi": r["psi_muzzle"],
                     "pmax": r["p_max"]})
        if L <= 1.001 and abs(L * 1000 - round(L * 1000 / 100) * 100) < 1e-6:
            say("  %8.0f %10.2f %12s %12.2f %10.2f"
                % (L * 1e3, r["v_muzzle"],
                   ("%.2f" % dv) if dv == dv else "   --",

    base_r = solve(base, record=False)
    say("")

    # marginal yield thresholds
    thr = []
    for rr in rows[1:]:
        if rr["dv25"] == rr["dv25"]:
            thr.append(rr)
    for cut in (10.0, 5.0, 2.0):
        hit = next((rr for rr in thr if rr["dv25"] < cut), None)

            say("  marginal yield falls below %4.1f m/s per 25 mm at travel = %.0f mm (v = %.1f m/s)"

           100 * (solve(base.copy(L=2 * base.L), record=False)["v_muzzle"] / base_r["v_muzzle"] - 1)))
    say("           at zero cost in peak pressure.")
    RESULTS["e4"] = {"rows": rows, "ref_v": base_r["v_muzzle"],
                     "half_L": half["L"], "rev_L": rev["L"] if rev else None}

11The ledger

Twenty-nine mechanisms, each placed in the phase where its leverage is greatest and marked with the point at which it stops being adjustable. The pattern that comes out of the census is the article's main claim.

Start with the elasticity ledger. Of the twelve continuous levers tested, three raise muzzle velocity while leaving peak pressure unchanged or lower: barrel travel, a lighter projectile, and reduced bore friction. All three act after the peak. Of the nine that cost pressure, the spread from cheapest to dearest is 3.66 times — propellant force returns 0.516 per cent of velocity per per cent of pressure, the burn rate exponent returns 0.141 — but the spread within the priced levers is less interesting than the gap between priced and free. The free levers are free for exactly one reason: their window lies entirely to the right of the peak.

That is the general rule, and the census supports it. A mechanism's phase predicts its economics better than its family does. Everything in the ignition, shot-start and rise phases is spending the budget, because it is acting while the volume is still small and the burn rate feedback loop is still live. Everything in the expansion phase is collecting, because the peak has already been incurred and cannot be incurred again.

The corollary is the part that is easy to get backwards. The mechanisms with the narrowest windows have the largest effects. Ten of the twenty-nine finish inside one per cent of the barrel travel, and that group contains shot-start resistance, engraving, the primer, and the land geometry — which is to say, most of what actually sets the height of the curve. Engraving alone, acting over five millimetres, moves peak pressure across a fifty-one per cent range. Barrel length, acting over five hundred and thirty-one millimetres, moves it not at all. Width of action and magnitude of effect are close to inversely related, and the reason is the feedback loop: a mechanism that acts before the peak is multiplied by the burn rate law, and a mechanism that acts after it is not.

Which returns to the question in the title. What the peak buys you is the area to its right, and the discipline consists of arranging for as much area as possible to lie there. Grain geometry is the purest instance — at an identical ceiling of 304.5 megapascals, the shape of the grain alone is worth 101.3 metres per second — but the same logic explains barrel length, expansion ratio, progressive burning, and the reason a lighter projectile lowers pressure while raising velocity. All of them are the same move: shift work from the left of the peak to the right of it.

It also explains why the margins in this field look so conservative to people outside it and so thin to people inside it. Peak pressure responds to charge mass with an elasticity of 2.197 and to propellant temperature at roughly half a per cent per degree, and both of those numbers are properties of the equations rather than of anyone's care. A cartridge that sits comfortably inside its ceiling at twenty-one degrees with a nominal charge does not necessarily sit inside it at sixty degrees with a charge at the top of its tolerance in a barrel whose throat has moved. The margin is not padding on top of the engineering. It is what the engineering is for.
