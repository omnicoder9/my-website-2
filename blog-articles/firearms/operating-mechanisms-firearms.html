Who Decides When the Breech Opens?

Firearm operating mechanisms — manual actions, self-loading principles and breech locking — read as a single problem of timing

(57)ABSTRACT

Every firearm mechanism answers the same question: what is allowed to open the breech after the shot, and when. This article sorts the manual actions, the four self-loading principles and the families of breech lock onto a six-rung ladder by how far the unlocking signal sits from the cartridge head, from the rising pressure itself to a human hand. A six-experiment normalised lab shows that one angle, measured against the friction angle, separates a lock from a delay from a blowback; that a self-loader cannot change its recoil impulse, only its shape, and lowers the peak only when its moving parts store and shed energy slowly; and that reliability under fouling is bought with surplus energy the mechanism must then absorb.

01The question every action answers

A cartridge firearm is a heat engine of an unusual kind: it turns the energy of a propellant burned in a fraction of a second into the kinetic energy of a projectile1. For that fraction of a second the combustion chamber is the cartridge case, and the only wall behind it is the breech face. Every mechanism described here is, at bottom, an arrangement for deciding when that wall is allowed to move.

The shot itself is the same in every design. Its mechanical sequence is conventionally divided into eight steps: feeding, chambering, locking, firing, unlocking, extracting, ejecting and cocking2. In a manually operated arm a person supplies the energy for nearly all of them. In an automatic arm every action after the first chambering and the trigger pull is driven by the energy of the propellant gas, and automatic arms are classified by how that energy is applied: blowback, recoil, gas, and smaller families such as inertia and blow-forward1,3. Figure 1 sets the two cases side by side.

Read the eight steps with one question in mind and the families sort themselves. The question is not where the energy comes from, which is the usual way to classify, but what is allowed to open the breech, and when. In a simple blowback arm the answer is the rising pressure itself. In a bolt-action rifle the answer is a person, a second or more later. Between those ends sit the delayed blowbacks, the recoil-operated and the gas-operated systems, each of which places the decision at a different distance from the cartridge head. This article arranges every mechanism on a six-rung ladder by that distance:

The ladder matters because the breech must not move far while the bore is at high pressure. A blowback bolt does move while the bullet is still in the barrel, and that early travel has to satisfy what Morawski and Zahor call the safety condition — it may be no longer than the thickness of the case head1. Arms with a locked breech need the motion of a separate component, a slide or carrier, to unlock; delayed-blowback arms are described as half-locked1. Where on the ladder a mechanism sits therefore fixes what the cartridge can do on its own, how much mass must move, what happens when friction or ammunition changes, and how the shot feels at the shoulder.

Scope. This is an article about principles. It describes how mechanisms work at the level of museum labels, patents and engineering handbooks, and it gives no dimensions, tolerances, spring rates, masses, gas-port positions or other construction data for any real firearm, and nothing about converting or modifying one. The lab that accompanies it works entirely in normalised units: masses are fractions of a reference mass, times are multiples of the pressure-pulse duration. Its constants illustrate mechanics — conservation of momentum, wedge friction, parallel springs, overlapping distributions — and are not fitted to any product. The results are statements about classes of mechanism, which is exactly what a normalised model can support and all it can support.

Each mechanism appears as a numbered plate with the same four fields — where the energy comes from, what holds the breech shut, when it unlocks, and what the design pays — and a small gauge: the eight-step cycle drawn as a ring, each step coloured by what powers it, with the unlocking step in the colour of the plate's rung. The plates are collected into a ledger in section 6.

02A short history of who decided

The history of operating mechanisms is a history of moving the unlocking decision away from the hand and, later, of moving it back towards the cartridge once materials and geometry made that safe. Figure 2 sets out the dated events this article relies on.

The breech opens to the hand. The Parisian gunmaker Casimir Lefaucheux patented a pin-fire system in 1833 that combined powder, shot and primer in one cartridge loaded from the breech into a hinge-action gun4. Samuel Colt received a United States patent for a revolving gun on 25 February 1836, the document later renumbered X94305,6. In Prussia, Nikolaus von Dreyse followed his muzzle-loading needle gun with a bolt-action breechloader in 1836, which the army began to buy in 18417. Repeating rifles fed from magazines followed: Christopher Spencer's patent of 1860 and Benjamin Tyler Henry's lever-action magazine rifle, patented on 16 October 18608,9. Spencer returned with Sylvester Roper to patent the first commercially successful pump-action shotgun in 1882, and John Browning's Winchester designs of 1893 and 1897 made the type ubiquitous10,11. Browning's 1886 lever rifle for Winchester replaced the toggle link with two vertical locking blocks so that the bolt could not move under the thrust of the cartridge12. Paul Mauser patented the design that became the Gewehr 98 in 189513. The earlier volumes of Chinn's survey trace the same arc through the manually operated machine guns that preceded automatic fire14.

The cartridge is given the decision. Hiram Maxim's gun, patented in 1884 and improved in 1885, was the first true automatic weapon, worked by the energy of its recoiling barrel15; between 1883 and 1885 he also patented gas operation16. Hugo Borchardt's C-93, the first commercially viable semi-automatic pistol, was the direct ancestor of Georg Luger's short-recoil, toggle-locked redesign, first adopted by Switzerland in 190017. Browning filed his patent for a recoil-operated shotgun on 6 May 1899; it became the Auto-5, the first successful semi-automatic shotgun18. His pistol patent US 984,519, issued on 14 February 1911, carried the single-link tilting barrel into the M191119,20.

The decision moves back towards the case head. Between the wars designers learned to let the case head open the breech slowly. Paul von Király patented a lever-delayed blowback in 1913; Andreas Schwarzlose's toggle-delayed machine gun and John Blish's friction lock in the Thompson submachine gun date from the same period1. The M1 rifle was adopted on 9 January 1936 with a gas trap at the muzzle; field use exposed its weaknesses, and production rifles with a drilled gas port came off the line in 194021. Late in the Second World War the roller-locked, recoil-operated MG 42 was simplified at Mauser into a roller-delayed blowback, the StG 45(M); its designers carried the idea to Spain and the CETME rifle, and from there to the Heckler & Koch G3, manufactured from 19591,22.

Refinement, not new principles. Post-war rifles chose among principles that already existed. The FN FAL, introduced in 1951, pairs a short-stroke gas piston with a tilting breechblock23. Eugene Stoner's gas-operated bolt and carrier system was filed in 1956 and granted on 6 September 196024. Bruno Civolani's patent of 1967 revived the inertia principle for Benelli, whose first inertia shotguns were produced in 196825. Every one of these sits on a rung that existed before 1920.

03Manually operated actions

In a manual action the operator is the unlocking signal, the energy source and the timer. The cartridge has no vote. That single fact explains why the manual actions could adopt the strongest possible locks — square shoulders the case head cannot push open — and why their engineering is dominated by the operator: how much force he must apply, how far he must move, and how many separate motions each shot costs. Figure 3 abstracts the five families to the motions the hand makes.

Break action

The hinged breechloader is the oldest of the five. In the Lefaucheux pattern the barrel tilted on a hinge, a hook at the base of the barrel lay on a pivot in the stock, and a lever was turned to the side to open it; in later actions a combination of three bolts made the lockup very strong26. The earliest British pin-fire guns held their barrels by an interrupted screw acting on a single bite on the barrel lump4.

Bolt action

The turn-bolt is the purest form of the rung-five idea: the bolt is a cylinder the operator turns into lugs and draws back. The Mauser Gewehr 98 locks by two large, opposed lugs at the bolt head that engage recesses in the receiver ring, with a smaller safety lug at the rear; a non-rotating claw extractor holds the case rim from magazine to ejection, which is what is meant by controlled-round feed27. A 90-degree rotation frees the lugs, and their position at the head greatly reduced the tendency of the bolt body to stretch under firing loads28.

The Lee action makes the opposite choice. Its lugs sit at the rear of the bolt and bear on the receiver bridge, which allows a 60-degree bolt lift and a shorter stroke30; the cost is that the thrust passes through the length of the bolt to the back of the receiver, a weaker arrangement, and the return is a bolt that cocks on closing and gives very powerful primary extraction31. Rear lugs also save the stroke that a front-locking bolt needs to clear its lugs from the receiver ring29.

Lever action

Henry's 1860 rifle used a toggle link: two arms braced end to end behind the bolt and broken by the lever9. The pattern was carried into Winchester's 1873 and 1876 models, whose toggle geometry limited cartridge length32, and Browning's 1886 design dropped it for a pair of vertically sliding steel blocks on either side of the bolt that fully restrain the bolt against the cartridge's thrust12. The change is a move from a lock that depends on links staying near dead centre to one whose bearing surfaces are square to the thrust — the distinction that section 5 turns into a single number.

Pump action

The slide action moves the operating handle to the support hand. Spencer and Roper's 1882 shotgun was the first commercial success10; Browning's Winchester Model 1897 made the type standard11. In the Remington 870, a rocking locking block inside the bolt is cammed up by twin action bars into a recess in a hardened barrel extension, so the thrust never reaches the receiver directly33,34.

Revolver

The revolver solves a different problem. Its chambers are loaded in advance and never unlock during firing; what the mechanism must do each shot is index a fresh chamber to the bore and hold it there. A pawl, or hand, driven by the trigger or hammer turns the cylinder by a ratchet on its rear face, and a cylinder stop rises into a notch to lock it35. The order is critical: the stop must drop, the hand must turn the cylinder just far enough, and the stop must rise before the hammer falls, or the bullet meets the bore off-centre36. The thrust is taken by the frame behind the cylinder, and some gas, flame and residue always escape at the gap between cylinder and barrel37. Colt's 1836 patent covered the revolving breech itself5.

Engineering considerations

Mechanical advantage. Every manual action converts a large, slow hand motion into a small, forceful motion of the bolt — above all at primary extraction, when a fired case must be broken free of the chamber wall, and at final closing. A cam or helix does that conversion, and its advantage depends on the cam angle and on friction. For a surface inclined at α to the plane square to the motion and a friction coefficient of 0.10, the lab's experiment E4 gives a forward advantage of 10.00 at α = 0°, 3.56 at 10° and 1.39 at 30° (figure 8). Low cam angles give large forces and require long hand travel; that is the trade the Lee's camming primary extraction exploits31,38.

Locking strength. Strength is set less by how much steel bears than by how short and direct the path is from bolt face to the part that resists. Front lugs bearing on the receiver ring keep the stressed length short28; rear lugs load the whole bolt31; a pump's block bearing on a barrel extension keeps the load out of the receiver altogether33. Section 5 returns to stress distribution.

Operator workload. The motions per shot in figure 3 are the cost the operator pays in time and attention: four for a turn-bolt, two for a lever or a slide, one trigger stroke for a double-action revolver. Bolt lift, bolt throw and where the knob sits relative to the hand all change the speed, and a rear-locking bolt with a 60-degree lift and short throw is fast for exactly those reasons29,30.

Simplicity and reliability. A manual action has no timing problem to solve because the operator times it. Its remaining reliability questions are mechanical — whether the extractor controls the case through the whole stroke, as the Mauser claw does27, and whether the operator completes each stroke. Its strength margin can also be revised by redesign without changing the principle: Winchester replaced the 1893 pump with the stronger 1897 when smokeless powder arrived11.

04Self-loading operating principles

A self-loading arm takes the operator off the timing problem and hands it to the cartridge. The four principles — blowback, delayed blowback, recoil and gas — differ in where they take energy from, but more usefully in how far from the cartridge head they place the unlocking signal, how much mass they set in motion, and how they react when friction or ammunition changes. 4.1 Energy source: the impulse is fixed before the mechanism moves

Every principle draws on the same momentum. The projectile and the propellant gas leave the muzzle with a forward momentum that the firearm must match in the opposite direction; that is the recoil impulse, and it depends on the ejecta, not on the mechanism41,42. The recoil energy of a free firearm is the square of that impulse divided by twice its mass. Experiment E1 tabulates the consequence in normalised form: raising the gas share of the ejecta momentum from 0 to 40 per cent multiplies recoil energy by 1.96, and doubling the firearm's mass divides it by 2.00 (figure 4). A self-loading mechanism cannot alter either number. What it can alter is when, and how fast, the impulse reaches the shooter.

4.2 Blowback and its delays

In simple blowback the barrel is fixed and not locked to the bolt; the breech is closed only by the inertia of a sufficiently heavy bolt and its recoil spring, and the cycle starts with the case head pushing on the bolt face while the bullet is still moving down the bore1. The engineering design handbook for automatic weapons develops the recoil and counter-recoil timing of exactly this case first3. Its limitation is clear from E1: a heavier cartridge needs a heavier bolt or a stiffer spring, which raises the weapon's weight and recoil, so simple blowback is largely confined to pistol cartridges1.

Delayed blowback keeps the case head as the unlocking signal but slows the bolt during the first, high-pressure part of its motion, by adding a decelerating force, an apparent increase in bolt mass, or both1. Morawski and Zahor classify the delays as gas, friction and mechanical1; this article puts the mechanical delays on rung U1 and the gas and friction delays on U2, because the second group does not merely multiply inertia but sets a force against the case head that itself fades with bore pressure.

The mechanical delays share one trick: a two-piece bolt in which the heavy rear part is made to move faster than the light front part. The bolt head moves a little; the carrier moves a lot1. Experiment E3 shows what that ratio buys. With a velocity ratio n between carrier and head and no friction, the inertia the case head feels is the head's mass plus n² times the carrier's. A carrier four times the head's mass behind a 4:1 ratio looks 13 times as heavy as a plain bolt of the same total mass; to present the same effective mass as that plain bolt, a 4:1 system needs only 7.7 per cent of its mass and a 2:1 system 26.2 per cent (figure 5). The square is the whole reason delayed blowback can work with rifle cartridges.

The gas and friction delays work differently. In the gas-delayed pistol a port just ahead of the chamber feeds a cylinder below the barrel, and a piston fixed to the slide is pushed forward while the case head pushes the slide back; when the bullet clears the muzzle the cylinder pressure falls and the slide is free1. In the Blish lock of the Thompson, a bronze insert on inclined abutments held because friction between mating parts depended on the load they carried; once pressure dropped, the insert lifted and released the bolt1.

4.3 Recoil operation

Recoil operation moves the decision one rung further away. The breech is positively locked, and the lock is released by the travel of a recoiling mass — the barrel and bolt together in short and long recoil, the whole gun past an inertial bolt body in the inertia system. Maxim's gun used the energy of the recoiling barrel15. Browning's single-link pistol used a short joint travel of barrel and slide, after which the link drew the barrel down20. The Luger's barrel and toggle recoil together about a quarter of an inch before the toggle knobs strike an inclined surface and the toggle breaks like a knee joint17. In long recoil the barrel and breech-block travel together the full length of the stroke, and the lock can tip free only when both are fully back43.

The inertia system is recoil operation turned inside out. The bolt body, more than five times the mass of the bolt head, tends to stay where it is while the gun recoils, compressing a very rigid spring between body and head; the spring then throws the body back about two millimetres, and inclined planes release the head45. A cam pin in the head turns it out of the barrel extension46. Because it depends on the gun recoiling relative to the bolt body, an inertia gun held against something immovable may fail to cycle47.

4.4 Gas operation

Gas operation takes the decision furthest from the case head that a self-loader can. A small part of the propellant gas is taken from the bore after the bullet has passed a port — or, in the gas trap, at the muzzle — and used to drive a piston, rod or carrier that unlocks the bolt only after it has travelled some distance. The variants differ in how much mass the gas moves. In the Kalashnikov pattern the long-stroke piston is permanently joined to the bolt carrier, the carrier rides on rails with significant clearances that let the gun run when fouled, and a cam track turns a two-lug bolt48. In the FAL a short-stroke, spring-loaded piston above the barrel strikes the carrier, and a regulator lets the user adjust for fouling, ammunition and conditions23. Stoner's system carries gas back through a tube into a chamber between bolt and carrier; the patent describes it as a true expanding gas system rather than an impinging one24. The original M1 trapped gas at the muzzle and was changed to a drilled port21; its piston drives an operating rod whose cam turns the bolt49.

4.5 Timing, moving mass and recoil character

The rung determines the timing. On U0 the bolt moves from the first instant; on U1 and U2 it moves from the first instant but slowly; on U3 and U4 it does not move relative to the barrel at all until a separate signal has arrived. The handbook literature treats each family's timing separately for exactly this reason: the recoil time, counter-recoil time and gas-filling period are different calculations3,51.

The moving mass determines the character of the recoil, and here the lab produced the result least expected at the outset. Experiment E2 holds the impulse fixed and asks only how its delivery to a compliant shoulder changes when part of the firearm's mass — a bolt group or a recoiling barrel — is allowed to move on a buffer spring before it reaches the frame. With a lightly damped buffer, the peak shoulder force is not lower than for a rigid firearm but higher, 170.7 per cent of it, because the spring stores the moving mass's momentum and returns it to the frame all at once; the frame's peak velocity reaches 1.45 times that of the rigid gun. Heavier damping brings the peak down to 108.2 per cent, still above the rigid case. Only when the internal exchange is also slow compared with the shoulder's own response does the peak fall below rigid: 85.8 per cent with a buffer period of 32 pulse-durations, 65.4 per cent at 64. And if the travel is too short for that slow exchange, the moving mass hits its stop and the peak rises to 176.1 per cent of rigid (figure 6). In every one of the fifteen cases the integral of the shoulder force is 1.0000: the impulse is conserved exactly, and only its shape changes.

Three practical readings follow. A self-loader feels softer only if its moving parts absorb energy along the way and give it back slowly: Browning's long-recoil shotgun carried friction rings set for the load43, and Browning at one time advertised that the long-recoil system spread the recoil over a longer period52. A buffer that simply bounces the moving mass back adds a second sharp impulse. And any design whose moving parts bottom against a stop on every shot re-creates the rigid gun's spike with interest. None of this changes the impulse the operator receives, which E1 showed is fixed by the ejecta.

4.6 Reliability and fouling sensitivity

Self-loading arms fail most simply when the energy delivered to the operating parts falls short of what the cycle needs. That is a load–strength interference problem of the kind reliability engineering treats with overlapping distributions53. Experiment E6 models the delivered energy as normally distributed around a margin m over the clean requirement, with the requirement growing as fouling accumulates. With 6 per cent shot-to-shot spread, the margin must exceed 1.162 just to keep the short-stroke probability below 1 per cent in a clean gun (1.075 at 3 per cent spread). At a margin of 1.3 and a moderate fouling drag the per-shot hazard reaches 1 per cent after 790 rounds; raising the margin to 2.0 extends that to 4,806 rounds — but the clean gun then delivers a full requirement's worth of surplus energy on every shot, which buffers and stops must absorb (figure 7). A Monte Carlo check of 400 simulated guns at margin 1.3 put the median first short-stroke at round 638, earlier than the 1-per-cent point, because the cumulative chance of one failure grows before the per-shot hazard is large.

Where fouling goes matters as much as how fast it grows. In Stoner's system the gas enters the carrier, and later patentees cite fouling of the carrier's gas ports and the bolt's gas rings as a disadvantage50. The Kalashnikov's long-stroke design accepts a heavy moving mass in return for clearances that tolerate dirt48. The FAL gives the user a regulator to raise the margin when fouled23 — E6's trade made adjustable. The gas trap, which concentrated fouling at the muzzle, was abandoned21. The delayed blowbacks are sensitive in a different way: they are more affected by variation in the same ammunition type, because pressure history, not a separate signal, sets their timing1.

4.7 Mechanical complexity

Complexity tracks the rung. U0 needs a bolt and a spring. U1 adds a two-piece bolt and its coupling, and requires precise manufacture of the delay surfaces1. U2 adds a gas path or a friction element whose behaviour depends on surface condition. U3 adds a moving barrel or a separate inertial mass. U4 adds a port, a cylinder or tube, a piston or carrier, and in some designs a regulator. Every added element is another part that must work every cycle, and in a series system the probabilities of success multiply53. The question for a designer is never whether complexity is bad but whether it buys a decision that sits far enough from the cartridge head for the cartridge in use.

05Breech locking

5.1 Purpose of locking

Locking keeps the breech closed while bore pressure is high, so that the case is supported by the chamber and the bolt face rather than by its own walls. Without a lock, the only resistance is the bolt's inertia; with one, the case head cannot move the bolt at all until something else releases it1. Locking also sets headspace — the fit between case head and bolt face — and the stiffness of that joint decides how much the case stretches each shot28,42.

5.2 Locking geometry: one angle decides

Every lock in this article bears the cartridge's thrust on some surface. What decides whether that surface locks, delays or merely resists is its inclination to the plane square to the bore axis, compared with the friction angle of the contact. This is the classical self-locking condition of the wedge and the screw38, and Morawski and Zahor state it directly for firearms: a bolt can be unlocked automatically by inclining the mating surfaces at an angle higher than the angle of friction, and the Pedersen hesitation lock works precisely because its surfaces are inclined at less than that angle, so the slide must move to unlock it1.

Experiment E4 computes it. Per unit of axial thrust, a surface at α pushes the locking element sideways with a force proportional to tan(α − φ), where φ is the friction angle. For friction coefficients of 0.05, 0.10, 0.15 and 0.20 the friction angles are 2.86°, 5.71°, 8.53° and 11.31°. Below φ the drive is zero and the surface locks; above it the thrust itself opens the breech (figure 8). A square shoulder is a lock in any condition. A roller bearing on a steep recess is a delay. The same roller between straight surfaces is a lock — which is why rollers appear on rung U1 in the G3 and on U3 in the MG 421,22.

The same calculation explains the fouling sensitivity of friction-margined designs. If friction rises from 0.10 to 0.15 — oil burned off, grit in the recess — the sideways drive at α = 10° falls by 65.8 per cent, at 20° by 20.3 per cent, and at 45° by only 9.7 per cent. A delay that sits just above its friction angle is a delay whose timing belongs to the lubricant. That is the mechanical root of the Blish lock's dependence on materials1.

5.3 Rotating systems

A rotating lock turns lugs into recesses whose bearing faces lie square to the thrust. The Mauser's two front lugs engage the receiver ring27; the Kalashnikov's two lugs lock into the receiver48; in Stoner's system a pin riding in a helical slot in the carrier turns the bolt out of engagement with its abutment24; the Benelli head turns out of the barrel extension46. Rotation separates two motions cleanly — a turn that unlocks and a pull that extracts — and the turn can be driven by a cam with a high mechanical advantage at its start, which is where primary extraction needs it (E4).

5.4 Tilting systems

A tilting lock rocks a block, a bolt or a barrel into a shoulder. The FAL's breechblock drops into a shoulder in the receiver23; the Remington 870's block rocks up into the barrel extension33; the Auto-5's block tips into a recess in the barrel extension43; the M1911's barrel rocks its ribs into the slide20. In each of these the thrust is carried on a single bearing face rather than a ring of lugs, which removes the question of sharing load between lugs and places that face to one side of the bore axis.

5.5 Linear and radial locking

Linear and radial locks move an element straight across the thrust line: the vertical blocks of the 1886 Winchester12, the rollers of the MG 42 and its descendants1, the bronze insert of the Blish lock1. Their bearing faces can be made square or inclined at will, which is why this family spans the most rungs in the census. Whether a roller locks or delays is not a property of rollers but of the angle against which they bear.

5.6 Stress distribution

Two choices govern how the thrust is spread: where the locking surfaces sit along the bolt, and how many share the load. On the first, front lugs keep the stressed length between bolt face and lock short and limit bolt stretch28, while rear lugs carry the thrust through the whole bolt body31; designs that lock into a barrel extension keep the receiver out of the load path entirely33. On the second, more lugs reduce the rotation needed to unlock but only help if they all bear. E5 treats the lugs as parallel springs with random initial gaps — the elementary model of members sharing a load in parallel54. When the gap scatter equals the elastic deflection of one lug at its share of the load, two lugs carry a worst-lug load 1.17 times the perfect share and eight lugs 1.39 times. When the scatter is four times the deflection, a two-lug bolt bears on a single lug in 25.1 per cent of builds, and an eight-lug bolt's worst lug carries 2.42 times its share on average and 3.10 times at the 95th percentile (figure 10). Adding lugs without tightening the fit makes the worst lug's overload larger, not smaller.

5.7 Unlock timing

Unlock timing is the point where the ladder and the lock meet. On U0 to U2 there is no separate unlocking event; the breech is opening from the start, and the design question is whether its travel stays within the safety condition until pressure has fallen1. On U3 the lock holds for a set recoil travel — the Luger's quarter inch, the Benelli body's two millimetres17,45. On U4 it holds until gas has done work on a piston or carrier, and the gas-delayed pistol makes the dependence on bullet exit explicit: the slide opens once the projectile clears the barrel and pressure falls1. On U5 the operator decides, long after the pressure has gone. A design is safe when its unlock signal cannot arrive before the bore pressure has fallen far enough for the case and the lock it has to support.

06The ledger: what the census shows

Coding the twenty-one plates onto the ladder and the lock axis produces figure 11 and the ledger below. Three findings stand out.

Lock geometry does not predict the rung. All 5 lock geometries that appear in more than one plate appear on more than one rung; rotating locks span 3 rungs and linear-radial locks 4. Of the 31 pairs of plates that share a lock geometry, 81 per cent sit on different rungs. The rung is a property of what releases the lock, not of its shape — the census restatement of E4's single angle.

Only rungs U0 to U2 run without a positive lock, and they have to. All 6 plates on the three lowest rungs carry either no lock or a delaying element the thrust can eventually move: inclined roller recesses, a lever or toggle that the bolt can turn, a gas piston, or a friction contact whose hold fades with pressure. None bears on a surface square to the thrust. By E4 such a surface would leave the cartridge unable to open the breech, so wherever the case head decides, the design must give it a surface it can push open.

The principles are old; the choices within them are not. Rungs U1 to U5 were each occupied by a design patented before 1916 — Colt in 1836, Maxim's recoil and gas patents of 1883–1885, Király's lever delay in 1913 and Blish's friction lock in 1915 (section 2). The newest principle in the census is the gas delay, first used in the German Volkssturmgewehr at the end of the Second World War1. What changed after 1945 was where on each rung designers chose to sit, and the materials and manufacturing that made the delays and the lighter locked systems practical.

07Appendix: the lab

The lab is one Python file using only the standard library. It runs six experiments in about thirty seconds, prints the report reproduced below and writes the same numbers to a JSON file, from which every figure in this article and every number quoted in its prose were generated. Experiment E5 uses a seeded generator; the others are deterministic computations. The output was checked to be byte-identical under several values of Python's hash seed. Every unit in it is normalised, and none of its constants describes a real firearm.

E1 tabulates free-recoil impulse and energy from momentum conservation. E2 integrates a two-body model — a recoiling group on a damped buffer inside a frame resting on a spring-damper shoulder — with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta step of one-thousandth of a pulse duration over 400 pulse durations, which is long enough for the shoulder to absorb the whole impulse. E3 evaluates reflected inertia through a massless lever. E4 evaluates wedge friction. E5 solves parallel springs with random initial gaps exactly, 20,000 trials per cell. E6 evaluates normal-distribution margins analytically and checks one case by simulation.

Two choices in E2 were changed after the first run and are disclosed here. The first run used a lightly damped buffer only and found every configuration worse than rigid; rather than report that as the behaviour of self-loaders in general, the sweep was extended over damping, buffer period and travel, which is what produced the finding in section 4.5. The simulated interval was also lengthened from 200 to 400 pulse durations so that the slowest buffer had settled before the impulse integral was taken.

    """Standard normal CDF."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def phi_inv(p):
    """Inverse standard normal CDF by bisection (deterministic, stdlib)."""
    lo, hi = -10.0, 10.0
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if phi(mid) < p:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi)

def simulate_e2(mu, L, rigid=False, dt=0.001, t_end=200.0, zb=0.05, Tb=8.0):
    """Two-body recoil model against a spring-damper 'shoulder'.

    Total mass 1. A half-sine force pulse of duration 1 and integral 1 acts on
    the recoiling group (mass mu). The group is tied to the frame (mass 1-mu)
    by a spring of period 8 and limited to relative travel L by a stiff stop;
    a second stiff stop prevents it going forward of battery. The frame bears
    on a shoulder of period 40 and damping ratio 0.5. With rigid=True the
    pulse acts on the whole mass directly.
    Returns peak shoulder force, its time, integral of shoulder force and the
    peak relative travel reached.
    """
    tau = 1.0
    amp = math.pi / (2.0 * tau)          # integral of amp*sin(pi t/tau) = 1
    M = 1.0
    ks = (2 * math.pi / 40.0) ** 2 * M
    cs = 2 * 0.5 * math.sqrt(ks * M)
    if rigid:
        mr, mf = 0.0, M
    else:
        mr, mf = mu, M - mu
    kb = mr * (2 * math.pi / Tb) ** 2 if not rigid else 0.0
    red = (mr * mf / (mr + mf)) if not rigid else 1.0
    cb = 2 * zb * math.sqrt(kb * red) if not rigid else 0.0
    kstop = 400.0

    def pulse(t):
        return amp * math.sin(math.pi * t / tau) if t < tau else 0.0

    def deriv(t, s):
        xf, vf, xr, vr = s
        fsh = ks * xf + cs * vf
        if rigid:
            return (vf, (pulse(t) - fsh) / mf, 0.0, 0.0)
        d = xr - xf
        dv = vr - vf
        fint = kb * d + cb * dv
        if d > L:
            fint += kstop * (d - L)
        elif d < 0.0:
            fint += kstop * d
        ar = (pulse(t) - fint) / mr
        af = (fint - fsh) / mf
        return (vf, af, vr, ar)

    s = (0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0)
    t = 0.0
    n = int(round(t_end / dt))
    peak, tpk, integ, dmax = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    vfmax = 0.0
    fprev = 0.0
    for _ in range(n):
        k1 = deriv(t, s)
        k2 = deriv(t + dt / 2, tuple(s[i] + dt / 2 * k1[i] for i in range(4)))
        k3 = deriv(t + dt / 2, tuple(s[i] + dt / 2 * k2[i] for i in range(4)))
        k4 = deriv(t + dt, tuple(s[i] + dt * k3[i] for i in range(4)))
        s = tuple(s[i] + dt / 6 * (k1[i] + 2 * k2[i] + 2 * k3[i] + k4[i])
                  for i in range(4))
        t += dt
        fsh = ks * s[0] + cs * s[1]
        integ += 0.5 * (fprev + fsh) * dt
        fprev = fsh
        if fsh > peak:
            peak, tpk = fsh, t
        vfmax = max(vfmax, s[1])
        if not rigid:
            dmax = max(dmax, s[2] - s[0])
    return peak, tpk, integ, dmax, vfmax

# E5  Lug load sharing under tolerance
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
def share_once(gaps, P):
    """Parallel unit springs with initial gaps; return loads (same order)."""
    order = sorted(range(len(gaps)), key=lambda i: (gaps[i], i))
    g = [gaps[i] for i in order]
    s = 0.0
    u = None
    for j in range(len(g)):
        s += g[j]
        cand = (P + s) / (j + 1)
        nxt = g[j + 1] if j + 1 < len(g) else float("inf")
        if g[j] <= cand <= nxt:
            u = cand
            break
    loads = [max(0.0, u - gi) for gi in gaps]
    return loads


def e5():
    hdr("E5  How many lugs actually carry the load?")
    print("N lugs, each a unit spring. Perfect fit: each deflects delta = 1 under")
    print("total load P = N. Initial gaps uniform on [0, t]; ratio r = t/delta.")
    print("Overload = N x (largest single-lug share); 1.00 is perfect sharing.")
    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    trials = 20000
    Ns = [2, 3, 4, 6, 8]
    rs = [0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0]
    rows = []
    print(f"{'r':>5}{'N':>4}{'mean over':>11}{'p95 over':>10}{'mean engaged':>14}{'P(one lug)':>12}")
    for r in rs:
        for N in Ns:
            overs, engaged, ones = [], 0.0, 0
            for _ in range(trials):
                gaps = [rng.uniform(0.0, r) for _ in range(N)]
                loads = share_once(gaps, float(N))
                mx = max(loads)
                overs.append(N * mx / N)  # P = N, so share*N = mx
                e = sum(1 for x in loads if x > 1e-12)
                engaged += e
                if e == 1:
                    ones += 1
            overs.sort()
            mo = sum(overs) / trials
            p95 = overs[int(0.95 * trials) - 1]
            me = engaged / trials
            po = ones / trials
            rows.append({"r": r, "N": N, "mean_over": mo, "p95_over": p95,
                         "mean_engaged": me, "p_one": po})
            print(f"{r:5.2f}{N:4d}{mo:11.3f}{p95:10.3f}{me:14.2f}{po:12.4f}")
    # load carried by the worst lug, in units of the perfect-fit lug load,
    # i.e. how much stronger each lug must be than the naive N-way split
    OUT["E5"] = {"rows": rows, "trials": trials, "seed": SEED}

========================================================================
E4  Inclined locking surfaces against friction
========================================================================
alpha = inclination of the bearing surface from the plane square to
the bore axis (alpha = 0: square shoulder). mu = Coulomb friction
coefficient, phi = atan(mu). Per unit axial thrust the surface can
push the locking element sideways with tan(alpha - phi); if that is
<= 0 the surface self-locks and something other than the thrust
must move the element. Forward advantage (lateral drive -> axial
force, e.g. a cam closing or extracting) is 1/tan(alpha + phi).
