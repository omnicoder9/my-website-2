01

How Long Does It Have to Be Wrong?

Interior ballistics ends at the muzzle. Everything after it is subtraction, and the discipline is the bookkeeping of what is being subtracted and for how long.

A rifle is a machine for making one decision and then becoming irrelevant to it. For a millisecond or two the shooter, the cartridge and the barrel are all still parties to the outcome; at the instant the projectile clears the crown they stop being parties to anything. Nothing can be added to the projectile after that. It has a velocity, a spin rate, a mass, a shape and an attitude, and for the rest of its existence those five quantities are only ever spent.

That makes external and terminal ballistics a strange kind of engineering. In most fields you improve a result by intervening in it. Here the intervention is over before the interesting part begins, and the only thing still under anyone's control is information: what was known, before the shot, about the forces the projectile was about to encounter. Every downrange discipline is therefore a discipline of prediction under a deadline that has already passed.

The organising question of this article is a consequence of that. If a force cannot be resisted but only endured, what matters about it is not how strong it is but how long it gets to act. Gravity is a constant acceleration of about ten metres per second squared; at the muzzle, the reference projectile in this article's lab is decelerating at 56.8 g from drag, roughly 57 times gravity's contribution, and yet it is gravity that ruins the shot. The difference is exposure. Drag acts on the velocity that produces the exposure; gravity acts on the exposure itself, quadratically. So the question every term on the list has to answer is: how long does it have to be wrong?

The article catalogues thirty mechanisms, from gravity to ricochet, and places each on a six-rung ladder that measures something other than size. The rung records what recourse exists against the error: whether it can be computed from published constants, corrected by a coefficient measured once for a projectile type, removed by zeroing a particular rifle, estimated from an incomplete sample, absorbed into a statistical budget, or not handled at all. Crossed with that ladder is a second, continuous axis measured directly in the lab: the exponent with which each mechanism grows with range.

Two results come out of the crossing, and they are worth stating before the physics that supports them. The first is that the mechanisms with the best recourse are not the small ones. Gravity and Coriolis deflection sit at the top of the ladder, fully computable, because they depend on nothing about the projectile at all; and gravity is the largest single displacement in the whole catalogue. The second is that the mechanisms with no recourse cluster at the end of the flight. Of the thirty, the four placed on the bottom rung are the transonic transition, projectile deformation, material interactions and yaw at impact. Three of those four happen after the projectile has stopped being a ballistic object and become a mechanical one.

Scope. This article is about the physics of projectiles in flight and at impact. It contains no load data, no propellant or primer information, no charge weights, no projectile construction specifications and no procedures for defeating protective materials. The reference body in the lab is a generic mass, diameter and length with a published standard drag function attached; it is not a product and is not fitted to one. The terminal-ballistics models are first-order retardation laws into a homogeneous inert simulant, of the kind used in forensic and trauma teaching, and their limitations are stated where they are used rather than waved at in a disclaimer. Where the models stop being informative, the article says so and stops.
02

What leaves the muzzle, and what arrives

A projectile is issued a fixed budget of energy and momentum. The first thing to establish is how little of it survives the trip.

The lab's reference body is a 10.90 g projectile of 7.82 mm diameter and 32.0 mm length, which is 4.09 calibres, launched at 800 m/s. Its form factor referred to the G7 standard shape is 1.16, giving a G7 ballistic coefficient of 0.2186 lb/in2 and a sectional density of 178.24 kg/m2. At the muzzle it is travelling at Mach 2.351 in the standard atmosphere1 and carries 3488.0 J.

A thousand metres later it carries 497.5 J. The air has taken 85.74% of the energy the cartridge supplied, and the projectile has delivered none of it to anything anyone cares about. This is not a defect of the design; it is the design. A projectile is a device for spending almost all of its energy on the medium in order to arrive somewhere with a usable remainder, and the remainder is small.

The second column of that ledger is more consequential than the first. Flight time to a thousand metres is 2.1546 s. In a vacuum the same launch would arrive in 1.2500 s. Drag has added 72.4% to the exposure, and because gravity's contribution goes as the square of the exposure, the drop grows from 7.661 m to 16.709 m — a factor of 2.181. Rather more than half of the drop that a shooter dials out is not gravity's doing at all. It is drag's, laundered through gravity.

This is the mechanism the whole article turns on, so it is worth being precise about it. Drag does not deflect the projectile. It removes velocity, velocity is what converts range into time, and time is what every other perturbation is integrated over. Drag is therefore not one error among several: it is the multiplier on all of them. A ten per cent increase in flight time is a ten per cent increase in the wind's opportunity, the Earth's rotation's opportunity and gravity's opportunity all at once.

Gravity's placement on the top rung of the ladder will look perverse to anyone who has spent an afternoon dialling elevation. It is by far the largest displacement in the catalogue — 16.709 m at a thousand metres against 0.90 m for a one-metre-per-second crosswind — and it is nonetheless the mechanism about which there is least to say, because predicting it requires knowing nothing whatever about the projectile. It needs a constant from a table and a flight time. Size and tractability are independent, and the ladder exists to keep them apart.

The measurement is then expressed in a form that transfers to every copy of that shape, which is the subject of the next section, and the transfer is where most of the practical trouble lives.

03

Drag, and the number that stands in for it

A ballistic coefficient is not a property of a projectile. It is a ratio between a projectile and a fictional reference body, and it is meaningless without it.

The subject has an unusually clean intellectual history, because for four centuries the answer was known to be wrong and nobody could say by how much. Tartaglia's Nova scientia of 1537 gave the first printed account of a curved trajectory and correctly identified the maximum-range elevation, while retaining an impetus theory that made the path a circular arc joined to a straight line2. Galileo replaced it with the parabola, and in the same breath conceded that the parabola holds only for motion in a vacuum and that air would spoil it3. Everything since has been the quantification of that concession.

Newton's treatment of resisted motion in Book II of the Principia4 established that resistance in a fluid rises with the square of speed, which is right for the inertial term and silent about everything else. Robins, who built the ballistic pendulum and was the first person to measure projectile velocities rather than argue about them, discovered in 1742 that the resistance on a musket ball near the speed of sound was some three times what the square law predicted5. Euler translated and annotated him within three years6, and Hutton's whirling-arm and pendulum work extended the measurements7; but the explanation had to wait for compressible aerodynamics, for Mach and Salcher's spark photographs of the shock cone in 18878, for Boys applying the same technique in England six years later9, for Prandtl's boundary layer10, and eventually for the wave-drag theories of Ackeret and of von Karman and Moore11,12.

What the nineteenth century did instead was extremely practical. If resistance cannot be predicted, it can be tabulated. Mayevski and Siacci reduced firing data to standard resistance functions and to tabulated integrals that turned trajectory computation into arithmetic13,14, and Didion had already produced the result that still governs wind correction15. Cranz systematised the whole subject16. The method survives intact: modern practice still refers a projectile to a standard body, and the two that matter are G1, a flat-based reference of the 1880s, and G7, a boat-tailed reference that resembles a modern long-range projectile17.

The two curves have different shapes, not merely different scales. That is the whole problem with the ballistic coefficient as a communication device. A coefficient is the ratio of the projectile's sectional density to its form factor against a named reference; a projectile whose drag curve resembles G7 will produce a consistent G7 coefficient across its velocity band and a G1 coefficient that drifts as it slows, because the G1 curve is the wrong shape to divide by. Quoting a single number without the reference function is not an abbreviation but an error, and quoting a G1 number for a boat-tailed projectile guarantees that the number is only correct at one velocity.

How a drag curve gets measured is worth a paragraph, because the answer changed twice. The earliest method was to fire through screens and difference the times, which gives a mean coefficient over an interval and nothing about its shape. Spark photography made the flow itself visible, and Cranz and Schardin's high-speed camera turned single images into sequences18. Aeroballistic ranges then made it possible to extract the full set of aerodynamic coefficients from the yawing motion of a free-flying body, using the reduction methods of Nicolaides and of Chapman and Kirk. Continuous-wave Doppler radar finally made it possible to measure the deceleration over the whole flight rather than between stations, and McCoy's published characterisations of service projectiles are the standard worked examples of what the technique yields19. Alongside the measurements sit prediction codes — SPINNER and its descendants, and the semi-empirical aeroprediction family — which estimate coefficients from geometry and are accurate enough for design iteration and not for fire control20,21.

One consequence of that history is that the Reynolds number, which governs so much of low-speed aerodynamics, is nearly irrelevant here22. A projectile at these speeds and scales sits in a regime where the viscous contribution is small beside pressure and wave drag, which is why a single Mach-indexed curve works at all and why the same curve serves across a wide range of sizes.

The lab's drag table gives the G7 peak coefficient as 0.4043 at Mach 1.05, which is 3.10 times the Mach 0.85 value and 1.45 times the value at the muzzle. A projectile's drag coefficient, in other words, is worst not at its fastest but on the way down, at a point it reaches when it is already low on energy. We return to that in the section on stability.

The practical question a shooter or a designer asks is which of the two inputs to buy: more velocity or less drag. The lab answers it by matching them. Holding one constant and perturbing the other, then bisecting for the muzzle-velocity increase that reproduces the effect of one per cent more ballistic coefficient, gives an exchange rate that varies with range and — more interestingly — varies with the question.

At a thousand metres, one per cent of ballistic coefficient buys what 0.44% more muzzle velocity buys against drop, and what 1.02% more buys against wind: a factor of 2.31 between the two answers. Velocity is the efficient purchase against gravity, because gravity is paid for in flight time and velocity is the direct route to flight time. Ballistic coefficient is the efficient purchase against wind, because wind is paid for in lag time — the flight time in excess of what the muzzle velocity alone would have produced — and lag time is exactly what a high coefficient suppresses. The two are not substitutes. They are answers to different questions that happen to share a unit.

One caution about that figure, which the lab surfaced rather than hid: the thousand-metre wind column is not monotone with the eight-hundred metre column, and the reason is that the baseline projectile has gone transonic before the target while the faster one has not. Exchange rates computed across the transition are comparing two different aerodynamic regimes. The figure is honest about where it stops meaning anything.

04

Time is the currency

Eight perturbations, one integrator, and a control that has to come out at exactly one.

If exposure is the thing that matters, it should be possible to measure it. The lab does so directly: each perturbation is applied to a zeroed trajectory, the resulting displacement at the target is recorded at ten ranges, and a log-log fit gives the exponent with which it grows. A pure angular error — a scope misaligned by a tenth of a milliradian — is carried through the same machinery as a control, because it must come out at exactly the first power of range, and if it does not, the method is broken.

It comes out at 1.000. Every fit in the set has R2 of at least 0.99458. And every mechanism that touches the projectile in flight is steeper than the control: Coriolis at 2.140, crosswind at 2.208, gravity at 2.306, muzzle velocity error at 2.384, and drag-coefficient error at 3.537.

The ordering is the practical content of the figure. An aiming error is the cheapest kind of error there is: it is the only one whose relative importance does not grow. Double the range and it doubles, while everything else more than doubles. This is why a rifle that shoots well at a hundred metres tells you so little about how it will shoot at a thousand: the term you measured on the short range is the one term that does not compound.

Before the integrator was a subroutine it was a building full of people. Siacci's tabulated integrals existed because numerical integration of a trajectory by hand took a skilled computer the better part of a day, and a firing table needed thousands. The mathematics of turning that labour into something tractable was set out by Bliss, who had run the ballistics computation effort at Aberdeen in the First World War, and comprehensively by McShane, Kelley and Reno after the Second23,24. The modern position is that the approximations those books exist to justify are no longer needed and the books are still worth reading, because they are explicit about which physical effects were being discarded and why — a discipline that software makes it easy to lose.

The identity nobody mentions

Two rows of the lab's table agree to exactly zero at every range — not to within a tolerance, but bit-identically, every digit the same. A one per cent error in the ballistic coefficient and a one per cent error in the air density are not merely similar in their effects; they are the same floating-point computation. A point-mass model contains the two only as a product, so no quantity of downrange data, however precise, can separate them. The figure above shows seven curves for eight perturbations because two of them are one curve.

The consequence is worth spelling out, because it explains a practice that otherwise looks like superstition. When a shooter "trues" a solver by adjusting the drag scaling until predicted and observed drops agree at distance, the adjustment absorbs a density error, a coefficient error, a velocity error and a measurement error indiscriminately, and produces a number that is correct for the day and misleading thereafter. The truing worked. What it fixed is unknown.

Wind, and why it is the hard one

It cannot be measured where it matters. Instruments read the wind at the firing point; the deflection is produced along the whole path.
grows as
As the square of range and a little more, because lag time itself grows faster than flight time.
fails when
Whenever the wind between the shooter and the target differs from the wind at the shooter, which is almost always.

Didion's result is that crosswind deflection equals the wind speed multiplied not by the flight time but by the lag time, the difference between the actual flight time and the time a drag-free projectile would have taken15,17. The lab confirms it to better than a tenth of a millimetre: at a thousand metres the lag is 0.9046 s and the integrated deflection for a one-metre-per-second crosswind is 0.9046 m. The agreement is exact to first order because in a frame translating with the wind, the trajectory is simply the no-wind trajectory.

What makes wind the governing term is not its magnitude. At a thousand metres a full-value ten-mile-per-hour crosswind moves the impact about four metres, against sixteen for gravity. It is that wind is the only term on the list whose value cannot be measured where it acts. A meter at the firing point reads the wind at the firing point; the deflection is generated along the whole path, through terrain, thermals and the boundary layer over whatever the ground happens to be doing half a kilometre away. The shooter is estimating a line integral from a single sample at one endpoint.

The vertical consequence of a horizontal wind deserves its own plate, because it is counter-intuitive and is routinely misdiagnosed. A spinning projectile that meets a crossflow at the muzzle responds gyroscopically, and the resulting aerodynamic jump puts the impact high or low depending on the direction of the wind and the direction of the twist17,25. Vertical stringing that correlates with wind direction is not a load problem.

Coriolis deflection and spin drift are frequently confused because both are small lateral terms that grow with flight time, and they are entirely different in kind. Coriolis is a consequence of describing motion in a rotating frame26; it depends on latitude and azimuth and on nothing about the projectile, which is why it sits at C5 alongside gravity. It also reverses sign between hemispheres. Spin drift is the slow yaw of repose of a stable spinning body, an aerodynamic effect that depends on the projectile's stability factor, does not reverse with hemisphere, and is not produced by a point-mass solution at all27,28. Its immediate cause is the side force a spinning body develops when its axis is not aligned with the airflow, described by Magnus in 1852 after artillerymen had spent decades complaining about unexplained lateral deviation29; the magnitude of that force on a supersonic body at angle of attack has since been measured directly30. At 13.0 cm for the reference body at a thousand metres, Coriolis is roughly a seventh of the deflection from a one-metre-per-second wind error — which is a useful calibration for how much attention it deserves.

05

Staying point-first

A ballistic coefficient assumes the projectile is pointing where it is going. Two mechanisms decide whether that assumption holds.

Everything so far has treated the projectile as a point with a drag coefficient attached. The assumption buried in that treatment is that the projectile is flying nose-first, so that the drag coefficient measured in a range or a tunnel is the one actually in effect. A projectile is statically unstable in this attitude — the centre of pressure of an elongated body in supersonic flow lies ahead of its centre of mass — and is held nose-first only by gyroscopic action. The rigorous treatment is the six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body problem set out by Fowler, Gallop, Lock and Richmond in 192027, refined by Nielsen and Synge31 and by Murphy28,32, with the practical aeroballistic-range reduction methods of Nicolaides and of Chapman and Kirk33,34. Between the point mass and the full rigid body sits the modified point-mass model standardised as STANAG 4355, which carries enough of the yaw behaviour to be useful for fire control without solving the whole problem35,36.

For selecting a twist rate the field uses an algebraic rule. Greenhill produced one at Woolwich in 1879, working on mortars that were being damaged by excessive spin; Miller traced its provenance and showed what it actually assumed37,38, and published a replacement that handles modern projectile shapes properly39. The lab implements Miller's rule, and reproduces a trap worth recording: the twist term in the bracket is in calibres per turn, not inches. Taking it as inches for a 1:11 inch barrel gives 11 rather than 35.73, and the stability factor comes out 10.6 times too large — comfortably enough to certify an unstable combination as stable. The corrected rule puts the reference body at Sg = 1.826 in a 1:11 barrel and says that the usual design floor of Sg = 1.4 requires 1:12.70 inches or faster.

The right-hand panel is the part that surprises people. Stability is proportional to the square of the spin rate divided by air density times the square of velocity. Over a thousand metres the spin falls by 23.6% while the velocity falls by 62.2%, so the ratio rises: Sg goes from 1.826 to 7.469, a factor of 4.09. A marginally stable projectile does not become unstable downrange. It becomes more stable, which is why instability announces itself as keyholing at close range rather than as a long-range mystery.

The same relation explains the altitude effect. Since stability is inversely proportional to density, the lab finds Sg rising 37.6% between sea level and three thousand metres. A combination that keyholes at a coastal range on a winter morning may be entirely well-behaved in the mountains in summer — which makes cold, dense, low-altitude air the correct place to test a marginal one.

The band, not the line

Poorly. Standard drag functions are tabulated through the transition but individual bodies depart from them there.

It is not a growth at all but a band, several hundred metres wide, that the projectile is inside for part of its flight.

The transonic transition is the only mechanism in the external family placed on the bottom rung, and the reason is not that the drag rise is unknown. Standard drag functions are tabulated straight through it. The reason is that individual projectiles depart from the standard functions there in ways that are specific to the individual projectile, and that the yawing behaviour in the transition is not captured by any coefficient a shooter has access to17,40. Some projectiles pass through it cleanly; some do not; the difference is not predictable from the catalogue data.

It is worth noting that the aircraft industry met the same wall at the same time and answered it by reshaping the body, which is what the area rule amounts to41. A projectile cannot be reshaped in flight, and the transition arrives when it has already spent most of its energy, so the only available response is to arrive at the target before the transition does.

The figure makes a further point that the vocabulary obscures. People speak of a projectile "going transonic" as though it crossed a line. For the five form factors in the lab, the span between Mach 1.2 and Mach 0.9 is between 197 and 397 metres wide, which for the reference body is 663 to 970 metres — roughly a fifth of the distance at which it arrives. The projectile is not crossing anything. It is inside the transition for a substantial fraction of its useful flight, and any solution computed across that span inherits whatever the individual body does there.

The atmosphere is an input, not a background

Four environmental variables, three of them easy to read and one of them not, and a persistent confusion about which way humidity goes.

The standard atmosphere is a convention, not a place. ISO 2533 fixes sea-level conditions at 288.15 K and 101 325 Pa with a tropospheric lapse rate of 6.5 K per kilometre1,42, with addenda extending it below sea level and into feet43,44. Every published ballistic coefficient is referred to some such convention, and every real shot is fired somewhere else.

The lab holds the zero at its standard-atmosphere value and asks what the drop at a thousand metres becomes under conditions a shooter meets in a single season. The answer is that altitude dominates: moving from sea level to three thousand metres removes 382 cm of drop, which is 25.5% of the total. Temperature is next, at 83 cm for a twenty-degree swing and 194 cm for a winter morning at minus twenty. These are not corrections; at a thousand metres they are the difference between a hit and a miss by several body widths.

Humidity is the interesting one, because almost everyone has the sign backwards. Water vapour has a molar mass of about 18 against roughly 29 for dry air, so replacing air with vapour makes the mixture lighter. Saturating the air at 35 °C reduces the drop by 24 cm — the same direction as heating it, not the opposite. The effect is 29.3% of the size of a twenty-degree temperature change, which makes it smaller than temperature but not the negligible term it is usually called; at very warm saturated conditions it is worth roughly a quarter of a milliradian at long range. The physics is standard moist-air thermodynamics45,46,47 and has been settled for a century; the folklore has not caught up.

Air density belongs on rung C2 rather than C4 for a reason that has nothing to do with how hard it is to measure at the firing point, where it is the easiest quantity on the list. It belongs there because the density that matters is the density along the path, and over any shot with meaningful elevation change or over any terrain with a thermal structure, that is a field and not a number. Artillery practice has long acknowledged this by transmitting a layered meteorological message rather than a single reading, which is the convention this article's masthead borrows.

07

Accuracy and precision are different quantities

One is a distance from the truth and the other is a distance from yourself, and almost every argument about rifles confuses them.

Metrology settled this vocabulary before shooting borrowed it. ISO 5725 distinguishes trueness, the closeness of the mean of many results to a reference value, from precision, the closeness of results to one another, and reserves accuracy for the combination of both48; the international vocabulary of metrology draws the same line and adds the language of measurement uncertainty that makes it operational49,50. Applied to a rifle: a group that is tight and two hands to the left is precise and untrue, and it is fixed by turning a turret. A group that is centred and the size of a dinner plate is true and imprecise, and no turret helps at all.

The distinction is not pedantry, because the two failure modes have completely different remedies and completely different costs. Bias is cheap: one adjustment removes it permanently, and the adjustment does not need to know why the bias existed. Dispersion is expensive: reducing it requires finding and fixing a physical cause, and — as the next section shows — even detecting that you have reduced it requires an amount of ammunition that almost nobody spends.

This is why seven of the thirty plates sit at rung C3, "zeroed per installation". It is the most populated rung in the catalogue. A large part of what a rifle does wrong is repeatable for that rifle, unknowable in advance, and removable by the single cheap act of firing at a known distance and adjusting. The C3 rung is where engineering surrenders gracefully: the mechanism is not modelled, it is measured away.

A01Mechanical accuracyC3 · zeroed per installation
what it moves
The direction the bore points, shot to shot, through bedding, crown condition, fastener torque and thermal state.
measured by
By firing. There is no bench measurement of a rifle that predicts its group size.
grows as
Purely with range: it is an angular quantity and stays one.
fails when
As the barrel heats, at which point it stops being repeatable and becomes a slow drift.
A03Barrel harmonicsC3 · zeroed per installation
what it moves
The angle of the muzzle at the instant of exit, which oscillates during the few milliseconds the projectile is in the bore.
measured by
Indirectly, by firing a ladder of charges and looking for a region where vertical position is insensitive to velocity.
grows as
With range, as an angle, once it has been converted into a departure angle.
fails when
When the sample behind the ladder is too small to distinguish a node from a run of luck.

Barrel harmonics is the clearest case of a C3 mechanism being mistaken for something more tractable. A barrel is a cantilever that rings; the muzzle is describing a small arc during the millisecond or so the projectile is inside it, and the departure angle therefore depends on exactly when in that oscillation the projectile leaves. Because time in the bore correlates with velocity, departure angle correlates with velocity, and there exist charge levels at which the two correlations partly cancel so that faster shots leave at a slightly lower angle and land at the same height. Finding such a node is a legitimate and well-attested practice. Certifying that you have found one from the small samples normally used is not, and the statistics of the following section apply to it with full force.

A04Lockup and return to batteryC3 · zeroed per installation
what it moves
The relationship between the barrel and the sighting system from one shot to the next.
measured by
By repeated measurement of point of impact after deliberate disassembly and reassembly.
grows as
With range, as an angle.
fails when
On tilting-barrel and take-down actions, where it is a step change rather than a spread.
A05Sight alignment and trackingC3 · zeroed per installation
what it moves
The relationship between where the shooter is looking and where the bore is pointing.
measured by
On a fixture, by commanding known adjustments and measuring the movement actually delivered.
grows as
With range, as an angle, and additionally as a proportional error on every correction dialled.
fails when
When a turret's stated click value and its delivered click value differ by a few per cent and nobody has checked.

Sight alignment earns a plate of its own because it is the one mechanical term that is also a multiplier. A misaligned scope is a fixed angular offset and is removed by zeroing. A scope whose adjustment delivers ninety-seven per cent of its nominal click value is not removed by zeroing at all: it introduces a three per cent proportional error on every correction dialled thereafter, which is invisible at the zero range by construction and grows linearly with the size of the correction. The test is mechanical, requires no shooting, and is skipped almost universally.

08

What a group estimates

A group is a sample. Samples have sampling distributions, and the one attached to the most common measurement is unusually bad.

Model a rifle's dispersion the way everyone implicitly does: impacts scattered around a centre by independent normal errors of equal variance in both axes. The radial miss distance is then Rayleigh distributed51,52,53, with median radius 1.1774σ, ninetieth percentile 2.1460σ and mean radius 1.2533σ. Everything a shooter says about a rifle is, formally, a statement about the single parameter σ, and every group is an estimator of it. The theory of those estimators was worked out for exactly this application by Grubbs54, with the distribution of extreme spread treated directly by Taylor and Grubbs55 and the radial-error and circular-error literature by Weil, Moranda and Harter56,57,58.

A07Statistical dispersionC1 · random, distribution known
what it moves
Nothing. It is the model, not a mechanism: a circular bivariate normal whose radial miss distance is Rayleigh distributed.
measured by
By fitting a scale parameter to a sample of impacts, which is what every group is whether or not anyone says so.
grows as
With range, as an angle, by assumption.
fails when
When the true distribution is elliptical or has a flier component, both of which the one-parameter model absorbs silently.
A02Ammunition consistencyC1 · random, distribution known
what it moves
Muzzle velocity primarily, and through it the vertical position of every shot beyond a few hundred metres.
measured by
Directly, as the standard deviation of a chronographed string; twenty rounds is the minimum that means anything.
grows as
With range through the velocity term, so faster than linearly.
fails when
When a small sample is used to certify a small standard deviation, which a small sample cannot do.

The lab simulates sixty thousand groups at each of five shot counts and compares the two estimators in common use. The results are unkind to the popular one.

Expected extreme spread is 2.411σ at three shots, 3.814σ at ten and 4.452σ at twenty: an 84.7% increase for a rifle that has not changed in any respect. This is obvious once stated — the maximum of a larger sample is larger — and it is ignored constantly. A three-shot group and a ten-shot group are not measurements of the same quantity expressed with different confidence; they are measurements of different quantities. Comparing them favours whichever was fired with fewer rounds, systematically, by about a third.

None of this is new mathematics. The problem of inferring a population parameter from a handful of observations, and of being honest about how little a handful supports, was the founding problem of modern small-sample statistics59. Shooting inherited the problem and, for the most part, not the solution.

The run-to-run variability is worse than the bias. A three-shot extreme spread has a coefficient of variation of 37.3%. Two identical rifles will routinely produce three-shot groups differing by a factor of two, and a shooter who fires three groups and reports the best has produced a number with no information content at all.

A08Group-size measurementC1 · random, distribution known
what it moves
Nothing physical. It is an estimator, and estimators have properties that the quantity being estimated does not.
measured by
Extreme spread, mean radius, figure of merit or circular error probable, each with a different efficiency.
grows as
Extreme spread grows with the number of shots even when nothing about the rifle changes, which no other term on this list does.
fails when
Whenever groups of different shot counts are compared, which is most of the time.

Mean radius is better at every sample size, and the lab's Monte Carlo confirms its own arithmetic at the boundary: at two shots, extreme spread is exactly twice the mean radius, and the measured variance ratio comes out at 1.000. By twenty shots the ratio is 1.564, meaning extreme spread needs about fifty-six per cent more ammunition than mean radius to achieve the same precision from the identical holes. The information is in the paper either way; one estimator throws most of it out.

The price of knowing

The question that matters commercially is not how to measure dispersion but how many rounds it takes to establish that one thing is better than another. Since the sum of squared radii about the group centre is chi-square distributed, the comparison is an F test, and the power calculation is standard60,61,62.

To detect a genuine 50% improvement at eighty per cent power takes 10 shots per rifle. A 30% improvement takes 33. A 20% improvement takes 81. A 10% improvement — which is about the size of difference that sells ammunition and barrels — takes 356 shots per rifle, and a 5% improvement takes 1494.

Essentially no published comparison of loads, barrels or projectiles fires anything like those quantities. The consequence is not that the comparisons are wrong; it is that they are uninformative in a specific and predictable way. An underpowered study that reports a positive result has necessarily observed an effect larger than the true one, because only large observed effects clear the threshold — the type M error of Gelman and Carlin63, which is the same mechanism that produces inflated effect sizes across the empirical sciences64,65. The shooting literature is a small, unusually candid instance of a general problem.

A06Shooter-induced errorC1 · random, distribution known
what it moves
Everything. Hold, trigger release, breathing, position and anticipation all act on the departure angle.
measured by
Only statistically, and only by separating the shooter from the rifle with a rest or a machine mount.
grows as
With range, as an angle, and it usually dominates the angular terms at every range.
fails when
Under recoil anticipation, which produces a bias rather than a spread and therefore hides inside the zero.

The shooter's own contribution sits at C1 rather than C3 because it is not repeatable in the way a bedding fault is. It is worth noting the one exception, which the ladder handles badly and which is therefore worth stating in prose: recoil anticipation produces a bias, not a spread, and biases hide inside the zero. A shooter who flinches consistently will zero the flinch into the rifle and then discover it again the first time someone else shoots the same gun.

The error budget

One standard deviation of each input, pushed through the integrator and added in quadrature. The ranking is the practical conclusion of the external half of the subject.

Assembling a budget is the point at which the whole external apparatus becomes usable. Each input gets a realistic one-sigma value, is carried through the trajectory model, and the resulting displacements are combined as independent contributions in the ordinary metrological way50. The inputs modelled are a five-metre-per second muzzle-velocity standard deviation, a one per cent ballistic coefficient uncertainty, a one and a half per cent unmeasured density error, a half per cent range estimate error, a one and a half metre-per-second crosswind estimate error, a 0.15 milliradian hold and sight error and a 0.30 milliradian intrinsic dispersion.

The vertical budget stays mixed at every range. At a thousand metres the intrinsic dispersion of the rifle and load holds 28.4% of the variance, the unmeasured density and the muzzle-velocity spread hold 18.9% each, range estimation holds 18.3%, drag coefficient 8.4% and the shooter's hold 7.1%. No single term dominates, which means vertical accuracy is an engineering problem with six handles.

The horizontal budget is not like that at all. The wind estimate holds 41.0% of the horizontal variance at three hundred metres, 79.1% at six hundred and 94.2% at a thousand. By long range the horizontal problem is the wind problem, and the wind term is the one input in the budget that no instrument carried on the rifle can measure. Everything else on the list — velocity, coefficient, density, range — has an instrument. The dominant term does not.

A09Zeroing and truingC3 · zeroed per installation
what it moves
The correspondence between the solver's prediction and the rifle's behaviour, by adjusting inputs until the two agree.
measured by
By firing at two or more ranges and adjusting velocity or drag scaling until the predicted and observed drops match.
grows as
It removes growth rather than producing it, and it removes it wherever the truing ranges were chosen.
fails when
When a single scaling factor is used to absorb two errors, most commonly drag and density, that happen to be indistinguishable.

This asymmetry explains the shape of the sport and of the military discipline that preceded it. Effort spent on better velocity measurement, better coefficient data or better atmospheric input has a known and bounded payoff, and beyond a certain point the payoff is small, because the terms are already minor contributors. Effort spent on reading wind has an unbounded payoff and no instrumental shortcut, which is why it remains the part of long-range shooting that is taught as a craft rather than as a procedure. The error budget does not merely rank the terms; it explains why one of them has a folklore.

Arrival: two conserved quantities, two different questions

Momentum is the time integral of the retarding force and energy is the distance integral. Quoting one where the other belongs is the commonest error in writing on the subject.

At impact the projectile stops being a ballistic object and becomes a mechanical one, and the governing quantities change. Two are conserved and both get quoted, usually interchangeably and usually wrongly.

T01MomentumC5 · computed from law
what it moves
Nothing by itself. It is the time integral of the retarding force, so it answers how long the medium must push to stop the projectile.
measured by
Directly, as mass times velocity, with no property of the target involved.
grows as
It does not grow with range; it decays with it, and what arrives is what is available.
fails when
When it is offered as a measure of effect rather than as a bookkeeping quantity.
T02Kinetic energyC5 · computed from law
what it moves
Nothing by itself. It is the distance integral of the retarding force: the work available, not the work done.
measured by
Directly, as half the mass times the square of velocity.
grows as
It decays with range faster than anything else here, because it goes as the square of a decaying quantity.
fails when
When the number quoted at the muzzle is used to describe an event that happens several hundred metres later.

The distinction is elementary and consequential. Integrating the retarding force over time gives the change in momentum; integrating it over distance gives the work done, which is the change in kinetic energy. So momentum answers "how long must the medium push to stop this", and energy answers "how much work is available to be done". Depth of penetration is a distance, but the distance the projectile travels depends on how much mass is behind how much area — which is momentum-like — far more than on how much work is in the bank.

The lab makes this concrete with a first-order retardation model of the Poncelet type, in which the resisting force on a projectile in a dense medium has a quasi-static strength term and an inertial term proportional to the square of velocity66,67. That form has been the backbone of penetration analysis for two centuries and underlies the modern reviews of the field68,69,70, including the empirical forms fitted for specific media71,72. Integrating it gives a closed form for the depth, and the closed form is where the teaching is.

Depth is linear in sectional density — mass divided by presented area — and logarithmic in impact velocity. The two panels of that figure are the same model plotted against its two inputs, and they could hardly look less alike. Halving the impact velocity from 800 to 400 m/s removes 75% of the kinetic energy and 21.3% of the depth. Doubling the sectional density exactly doubles the depth, with no logarithm involved anywhere.

The cleanest demonstration is a matched pair. A body of four times the mass at a quarter of the velocity has the same momentum as the reference body, a quarter of its kinetic energy, and penetrates 131% further. A quantity that can fall by seventy-five per cent while the outcome it is supposed to describe improves by half is not the right quantity.

What this model is and is not. The constants are chosen so that the non-deforming reference body lands in the published range for a homogeneous ten per cent ordnance-gelatin block, not fitted to any projectile. The model has no yaw, no cavitation history, no material failure and no projectile deformation in it. It reproduces the two scaling laws correctly and it is not a wound model; the clinical and forensic literature cited below is, and it is considerably more careful than this arithmetic is.

Deformation, expansion and the one thing nobody controls

Two ways to lose depth. One is designed in and behaves like a budget; the other is not designed at all and dominates.

Ordnance gelatin exists because heterogeneous targets are not reproducible. Fackler's work at the Letterman Army Institute established both the preparation protocol and the "wound profile" method of recording what a projectile does along its path73,74,75; Jussila later reviewed the preparation methods and proposed a standard76, and the mechanical characterisation of the simulant itself — strain rate, temperature and ageing dependence — has been worked out since77,78,79. The point of a simulant is not that it is tissue. It is that it is the same on Tuesday as it was on Monday, which no real target is. Kneubuehl's handbook and DiMaio's text are the standard syntheses of what the technique has established80,81.

T03Projectile deformationC0 · no usable model
what it moves
The presented area, which is the denominator of the penetration equation, and therefore the depth directly.
measured by
By recovery and measurement after firing into a simulant, which is the only way the final geometry is known at all.
grows as
It has no range growth. It is set in the first few centimetres of the medium.
fails when
At the low end of its design velocity band, where the projectile may not deform at all.
T04PenetrationC4 · measured coefficient
what it moves
Depth, as the linear product of sectional density and the logarithm of impact velocity.
measured by
In a standardised homogeneous simulant, because heterogeneous targets are not reproducible.
grows as
It has no range growth, and it falls with range far more slowly than energy does.
fails when
As soon as the medium stops being homogeneous, which is immediately in every real target.

Expansion behaves exactly like a budget, and the model makes the accounting explicit. Because depth is inversely proportional to presented area, expansion to twice the original diameter quadruples the area and leaves 25.0% of the depth, while the swept volume stays fixed at 0.341 cm2 m across the whole column. That constancy is an identity of this model rather than a discovery — depth goes as one over area by construction, and a real expanding projectile also changes the resistance constants as it deforms. What the identity does establish is the shape of the trade. Expansion does not create effect; it moves a fixed quantity from depth into width. Any claim that a design achieves both must rest on physics this model does not contain, and should be asked to produce it.

T07Yaw at impactC0 · no usable model
what it moves
The presented area by a factor of five for this body, and therefore the retardation by the same factor.
measured by
By witness cards immediately ahead of the target, or by the shape of the entry in a simulant.
grows as
It has a range dependence, but not a monotone one: yaw is largest close in and after the transonic band.
fails when
It is not controllable at all. This is the largest single term in terminal behaviour and the shooter has no access to it.

Yaw is the other way to lose depth, and it is larger than any plausible expansion. The reference body presents 0.480 cm2 nose-on and 2.502 cm2 side-on, a factor of 5.21. Modelling a yaw onset five centimetres into the medium and full side presentation thereafter leaves 24.9% of the non-yawing depth. A projectile that turns sideways early is subject to five times the retardation for the rest of its travel, and no difference in impact energy within any realistic range comes close to mattering as much.

The reason this belongs on the bottom rung is that the shooter has no access to it. Whether a projectile yaws on entry, and how early, depends on its attitude at the moment of impact, which depends on where in its epicyclic motion it happens to be, which depends on the range, the stability history and whether it has been through the transonic transition. The single largest term in terminal behaviour is one that nobody selects, measures or controls, and the honest statement of the state of the art is that its variability is why terminal performance is described statistically rather than predicted.

T05Energy transferC1 · random, distribution known
what it moves
The distribution of work along the path, which is not the same question as how much work was available.
measured by
By measuring the cavity left in a simulant, or by differencing impact and exit velocities.
grows as
No range growth. The transferred fraction depends on whether the projectile stops, and a projectile that exits transfers less.
fails when
When a high transferred fraction is treated as a virtue independent of where along the path it occurred.

Energy transfer is included as a plate mainly to mark the place where the popular literature goes wrong. A projectile that stops inside a target transfers all its remaining energy and one that exits transfers less, so "energy transferred" can be maximised by a projectile that stops early — which is also the projectile that penetrates least. Transferred fraction is not a figure of merit independent of where along the path the transfer happened, and treating it as one has produced a long history of confident nonsense. The review literature has been patiently correcting this for decades75,82,83,84, against the countervailing force of a marketplace with an interest in single numbers. MacPherson's treatment and the injury-criteria work of Sturdivan and of Bir are the more careful alternatives85,86,87.

Running the physics backwards

Forensic ballistics asks the inverse question, and inverse problems have error structures of their own.

Everything to this point has propagated forward: given a shot, predict where it lands and what it does. Forensic work inverts the problem. Given what was left behind, recover the shot. Two distinct inverse problems travel under the same heading and they have nothing in common except the word ballistics.

T06Material interactionsC0 · no usable model
what it moves
Everything downstream of the first interface: direction, yaw, integrity and remaining velocity.
measured by
Empirically, target by target. There is no coefficient that transfers between bone, glass, sheet steel and wallboard.
grows as
No range growth, and no useful generalisation across materials either.
fails when
Always, in the sense that the result is specific to one geometry and one obliquity and does not transfer.

The first is identification: attributing a recovered projectile or cartridge case to a particular firearm from the marks its surfaces bear. This is not ballistics at all — it is toolmark comparison, and the physics in this article is irrelevant to it. It is included here because its epistemic situation is the sharpest illustration available of the ladder's bottom rung.

T08Forensic identification and reconstructionC1 · random, distribution known
what it moves
Nothing about the shot. It runs the physics backwards to recover the shot from what it left behind.
measured by
By comparison microscopy for identification, and by geometry for trajectory, each with its own error structure.
grows as
The reconstruction error grows with the distance projected back and shrinks with the length of the baseline measured.
fails when
On short baselines, where an angular uncertainty of a fraction of a degree becomes tens of metres at the firing position.

The discipline's own statement of method, the AFTE theory of identification, rests on "sufficient agreement" of individual characteristics as judged by a trained examiner88. The National Research Council examined it twice, in the ballistic imaging study and in the broader forensic science report, and both times found the underlying premise of uniqueness unproven and the decision procedure subjective89,90. The 2016 PCAST report went further, holding that the discipline had at that point only one appropriately designed black-box study to its name91. Large studies have since been conducted — the consecutively-rifled-barrel work of Hamby and colleagues92, the Ames cartridge-case study93, and the much larger FBI/Ames accuracy study reporting false-positive rates below one per cent94.

The dispute that followed is instructive for anyone who works with error rates, because it is not about the arithmetic. It is about what counts as an error. A large share of the comparisons in these studies return "inconclusive", and the reported error rates treat those as non-errors. Treat an inconclusive response on a known non-matching pair as a failure to eliminate and the headline figures change by more than an order of magnitude. Dorfman and Valliant set out three defensible statistical framings that give materially different answers95, and a separate critique argues that the comparison sets themselves are easier than casework96. Courts have moved in the same period from admitting unqualified identification to limiting it: Maryland barred unqualified testimony in Abruquah in 202397, then held in 2026 that the ruling had been case-specific rather than categorical98, and the broader pattern across US courts has been catalogued99,100. The instrumental response has been to replace the judgement with a measurement — three-dimensional surface topography and correlation-cell matching that produce a number rather than an opinion101,102.

Trajectory reconstruction

The second inverse problem is geometric, and this one is ballistics. Given perforations in known surfaces, recover the path and hence the firing position103,104. The error structure is simple enough to compute and is routinely underestimated. Two perforations separated by a baseline define a direction; each is located with some uncertainty; the angular uncertainty is roughly the positional uncertainty divided by the baseline, and it is then multiplied by the distance projected back.

With each perforation located to two millimetres, a 5 cm baseline — two holes in a single car door — locates a firing position a hundred metres away to no better than ±11 m. That is a street, not a window. A 1.5 m baseline on the same geometry gives ±0.37 m. The controlling variable is the separation between the reference points, not the care taken over either of them, which is precisely the conclusion of the published accuracy work105.

There is a second error that is systematic rather than random, and the lab quantifies it because it is the point at which external ballistics re-enters. Back-projection assumes a straight line; the path is a curve. Taking the local slope at an impact six hundred metres downrange and extrapolating back 150 m places the origin 0.51 m from where the projectile actually was, against 0.060 m over fifty metres. The error grows roughly as the square of the distance projected, and half a metre is the difference between a standing shooter and a kneeling one. Curvature is not a refinement in reconstruction. Past about fifty metres it is the dominant term, and a reconstruction that ignores it is not conservative — it is wrong in a known direction.

Ricochet is the third thing that breaks reconstruction, and it breaks it badly, because a deflected projectile arrives from a direction that no back-projection will ever recover. The empirical literature establishes that critical angles depend on the surface and that deflected projectiles depart at angles substantially different from the mirror reflection106,107,108. A single intermediate contact turns a soluble geometry problem into an insoluble one.

Ethics, safety, and where the models stop

The parts of the subject where being approximately right is not good enough, and where the honest answer is a refusal to extrapolate.

Everything in this article is a model, and models fail at their edges. The places where they fail are, inconveniently, the places where the consequences are largest.

T09Backstop, ricochet and overpenetrationC2 · sampled, never complete
what it moves
Where the projectile goes after it has done whatever it was intended to do, which is the part of the problem with no upper bound on consequences.
measured by
By range design and by measurement of penetration into the materials actually present behind the target.
grows as
It has no range growth, but its consequences have no range limit either: a ricochet redistributes the remaining energy in a direction nobody computed.
fails when
At low obliquity on hard or water surfaces, where deflection is the rule rather than the exception.

The first is that a projectile does not stop existing when it has done whatever it was fired to do. Overpenetration and ricochet redistribute the remainder in directions nobody computed, and the remainder is not small: the reference body still carries 497 J at a thousand metres, which is more than most handgun cartridges deliver at the muzzle. A projectile fired at high elevation returns at its terminal velocity, and Hatcher's firing trials in the 1920s established both that figure and the fact that it is entirely capable of causing serious injury109. The clinical literature on celebratory gunfire has confirmed it repeatedly and at cost110,111. This is the only mechanism in the catalogue whose consequences have no range limit, which is why range design treats the backstop as the primary engineered safety element rather than an afterthought112.

The second is that terminal-ballistics models are validated on simulants and applied to things that are not simulants. Every quantitative statement in the penetration sections of this article is a statement about a homogeneous block. Real targets contain interfaces, and each interface is an opportunity for the projectile to change direction, change attitude, deform or break up. The material-interactions plate sits at C0 not out of modesty but because the published position is that results do not transfer between materials, geometries or obliquities80,82.

The third is that quantitative terminal ballistics has legitimate applied purposes, and they are the ones that discipline the field. In wildlife management the criterion is time to insensibility, and the research asks whether a given projectile reliably achieves it — which is an empirical animal-welfare question, not a physics question113. The comparative studies of lead-free and lead-based hunting projectiles were run because the welfare answer had to be established before the toxicological argument could proceed114,115. And the toxicological argument is substantial: lead projectiles fragment, the fragments disperse well beyond the visible wound channel, and they enter both scavenging wildlife and the human food chain116,117,118. A reader who came to this article for the aerodynamics should notice that the strongest empirical literature on projectile design in the last two decades was driven by a question about consequences rather than about performance.

The fourth is the one this article has been circling from the start. The single largest term in terminal behaviour — yaw at impact — is uncontrolled, and the second largest — what happens at an interface — is not transferable between targets. Any confident, quantitative claim about what a projectile will do to a specific thing at a specific distance is therefore claiming knowledge of two quantities that the field does not have. The appropriate register for terminal ballistics is statistical and comparative, and the appropriate response to a single confident number is to ask which of those two it has assumed away.

Standards work exists precisely to stop the numbers drifting. Pressure and velocity standards fix what a cartridge may do119; the standard atmosphere fixes what the air is assumed to be1; simulant protocols fix what the target is assumed to be76. Each is a convention that makes numbers comparable, and each is occasionally mistaken for a description of reality. The distinction between a convention and a fact is the last thing an article like this should blur.

The ledger

Thirty mechanisms, two axes, and what the crossing shows.

The catalogue is collected below. The rung is a judgement about recourse, made against the published literature and stated in the same four fields for every plate; the exponent is measured, where the lab could measure it, and assigned from the mechanism's form where it could not.

Three things fall out of the crossing. The first is the distribution across the ladder: 4 plates at C0, 6 at C1, 4 at C2, 7 at C3, 5 at C4 and 4 at C5, for a mean rung of 2.50. The most populated single rung is C3 — repeatable, unknowable in advance, removed by zeroing — which is a fair description of what a rifle actually is: a machine with a large, stable, unexplained offset that is cheaper to measure than to understand.

The second is that the three families occupy three different parts of the ladder. The flight family averages rung 3.17, the scatter family 2.11 and the arrival family 2.00. Tractability falls monotonically along the projectile's own timeline. Everything that happens while the projectile is a point moving through a fluid is either computable or measurable; everything that happens once it touches something is neither.

The third is what occupies the extremes. All four plates at the top of the ladder — E01 gravity, E11 coriolis deflection, T01 momentum, T02 kinetic energy — are computable precisely because they depend on nothing about the projectile. All four at the bottom — E09 transonic behaviour, T03 projectile deformation, T06 material interactions, T07 yaw at impact — are places where an individual body's behaviour departs from any coefficient that describes its class. The ladder is, in effect, measuring how much of an outcome is a property of the situation and how much is a property of the object. Situations are computable. Objects have to be measured, and some of them will not hold still long enough.

Taken as a classification, rung and growth class together separate 93.8% of the 435 pairs of plates, against 68.3% for subject-matter family and 85.3% for rung alone, occupying 16 of the thirty available cells. The axis that was chosen for its explanatory value also happens to be the more discriminating one, which is at least weak evidence that it is carving the subject somewhere near a joint.

The lab

Ten deterministic experiments, stdlib only, run at build time. Every number quoted in this article comes from the output below.

The lab is a single Python file with no dependencies outside the standard library. It implements an ISO 2533 atmosphere, the tabulated G1 and G7 drag functions17, a fourth-order Runge-Kutta point-mass integrator with wind and Coriolis terms, Miller's stability rule39, a Monte Carlo of group statistics, an F-test power calculation built on a hand-rolled incomplete beta function, and a Poncelet retardation model66. It is 1426 lines and produces 483 lines of output.

Three checks are built in rather than asserted. Step-size convergence is verified before any sweep: the integrator agrees with a step twenty times smaller to 4.4 × 10-10 m in drop at a thousand metres. The growth-exponent method is validated against a pure angular error, which must come out at exactly the first power of range and does. And the group-statistics Monte Carlo is checked at two shots, where extreme spread is analytically twice the mean radius and the measured variance ratio is 1.000.

Determinism was verified rather than assumed. The whole lab was run under three different values of PYTHONHASHSEED and the output was byte-identical each time, which is the check that catches the failure mode where a fixed random seed coexists with an iteration order that depends on the process hash seed.

On the constants. The reference body is a mass, a diameter, a length and a form factor. It is not a product and no product was consulted in choosing it; the values were selected to land in a physically ordinary region so that the scaling laws would be legible. The penetration constants were chosen to put the baseline depth in the published range for a homogeneous simulant, and the article treats them as illustrative throughout.

    x downrange, y up, z right.  wind = (head/tail, vertical, cross) in m/s,
    positive cross = left-to-right.  Returns a list of sample dicts."""

def fit_power(xs, ys):
    """Least-squares slope and intercept of log(y) against log(x)."""
    lx = [math.log(v) for v in xs]
    ly = [math.log(v) for v in ys]
    n = len(lx)
    mx = sum(lx) / n
    my = sum(ly) / n
    num = sum((lx[i] - mx) * (ly[i] - my) for i in range(n))
    den = sum((lx[i] - mx) ** 2 for i in range(n))
    slope = num / den
    # coefficient of determination
    pred = [my + slope * (lx[i] - mx) for i in range(n)]
    ss_res = sum((ly[i] - pred[i]) ** 2 for i in range(n))
    ss_tot = sum((ly[i] - my) ** 2 for i in range(n))
    r2 = 1.0 - ss_res / ss_tot if ss_tot > 0 else 1.0
    return slope, math.exp(my - slope * mx), r2

def miller_sg(mass_gr, twist_in, dia_in, len_cal, v_fps=None,
              T_F=59.0, P_inHg=29.92):
    """Miller's stability rule.  NOTE the bracketed twist t is in CALIBRES
    per turn, not inches -- twist_in / dia_in."""
    t = twist_in / dia_in
    l = len_cal
    sg = 30.0 * mass_gr / (t * t * dia_in ** 3 * l * (1.0 + l * l))
    if v_fps is not None:
        sg *= (v_fps / 2800.0) ** (1.0 / 3.0)
    sg *= ((T_F + 460.0) / 519.0) * (29.92 / P_inHg)
    return sg


def miller_twist(mass_gr, dia_in, len_cal, sg_target=1.4):
    """Inverse rule: returns required twist in INCHES per turn."""
    l = len_cal
    t2 = 30.0 * mass_gr / (sg_target * dia_in ** 3 * l * (1.0 + l * l))
    return math.sqrt(t2) * dia_in

def extreme_spread(pts):
    best = 0.0
    for i in range(len(pts)):
        for j in range(i + 1, len(pts)):
            dx = pts[i][0] - pts[j][0]
            dy = pts[i][1] - pts[j][1]
            d = math.sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy)
            if d > best:
                best = d
    return best


def mean_radius(pts):
    cx = sum(p[0] for p in pts) / len(pts)
    cy = sum(p[1] for p in pts) / len(pts)
    return sum(math.sqrt((p[0] - cx) ** 2 + (p[1] - cy) ** 2)
               for p in pts) / len(pts)


def stats(vals):
    n = len(vals)
    mu = sum(vals) / n
    var = sum((v - mu) ** 2 for v in vals) / (n - 1)
    return mu, math.sqrt(var)

def _betacf(a, b, x):
    MAXIT, EPS, FPMIN = 300, 3.0e-14, 1.0e-300
    qab, qap, qam = a + b, a + 1.0, a - 1.0
    c = 1.0
    d = 1.0 - qab * x / qap
    if abs(d) < FPMIN:
        d = FPMIN
    d = 1.0 / d
    h = d
    for m in range(1, MAXIT + 1):
        m2 = 2 * m
        aa = m * (b - m) * x / ((qam + m2) * (a + m2))
        d = 1.0 + aa * d
        c = 1.0 + aa / c
        if abs(d) < FPMIN:
            d = FPMIN
        if abs(c) < FPMIN:
            c = FPMIN
        d = 1.0 / d
        h *= d * c
        aa = -(a + m) * (qab + m) * x / ((a + m2) * (qap + m2))
        d = 1.0 + aa * d
        c = 1.0 + aa / c
        if abs(d) < FPMIN:
            d = FPMIN
        if abs(c) < FPMIN:
            c = FPMIN
        d = 1.0 / d
        de = d * c
        h *= de
        if abs(de - 1.0) < EPS:
            break
    return h


def betai(a, b, x):
    """Regularised incomplete beta function I_x(a,b)."""
    if x <= 0.0:
        return 0.0
    if x >= 1.0:
        return 1.0
    lb = (math.lgamma(a + b) - math.lgamma(a) - math.lgamma(b)
          + a * math.log(x) + b * math.log(1.0 - x))
    if x < (a + 1.0) / (a + b + 2.0):
        return math.exp(lb) * _betacf(a, b, x) / a
    return 1.0 - math.exp(lb) * _betacf(b, a, 1.0 - x) / b


def f_cdf(f, df1, df2):
    return betai(df1 / 2.0, df2 / 2.0, df1 * f / (df1 * f + df2))


def f_quantile(df1, df2, p):
    lo, hi = 1.0e-6, 1.0e6
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = math.sqrt(lo * hi)
        if f_cdf(mid, df1, df2) < p:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return math.sqrt(lo * hi)


def power_for(n, ratio):
    """Power of the two-sided 5% F test on the ratio of two group variances,
    n shots each.  Sum of squared radii about the group centre is
    chi-square on 2(n-1) degrees of freedom."""
    df = 2 * (n - 1)
    crit = f_quantile(df, df, 0.975)
    return 1.0 - f_cdf(crit / (ratio * ratio), df, df)

sigma_p, and projected back a distance D to a candidate firing position.
