Correlation and Linear Regression

Association, prediction, residuals, and the boundary between a useful linear model and an unjustified causal story.

Published October 27, 2026 · 12 minute read

← Back to all articles

Correlation summarizes linear co-movement. Regression describes how an outcome’s conditional mean changes with predictors. Both are useful, but neither automatically explains why variables move together.

1. Correlation

Pearson correlation r is standardized covariance. It lies between −1 and 1, is unchanged by shifting or positively rescaling either variable, and measures the direction and strength of linear association. A value near zero means little linear association, not necessarily no relationship.

r = covariance(X,Y) / (SD(X)·SD(Y))

Correlation is sensitive to outliers and can be misleading for curved patterns, mixtures of groups, restricted ranges, and time trends. Always inspect a scatterplot. Spearman rank correlation measures monotonic association and is less tied to a linear scale, but it does not solve confounding or dependence.

Aggregation can reverse an association. A positive trend within every subgroup can become negative after groups are combined, or vice versa. This is commonly called Simpson’s paradox and reflects how group composition enters the comparison.

2. The simple regression line

Simple linear regression models the conditional mean of outcome Y as β₀ + β₁X. The intercept β₀ is the modeled mean when X = 0; it may lack a useful interpretation when zero is outside the observed range. The slope β₁ is the modeled change in mean Y for a one-unit increase in X.

Yᵢ = β₀ + β₁Xᵢ + εᵢ   ·   fitted value Ŷᵢ = b₀ + b₁Xᵢ

Ordinary least squares chooses coefficients that minimize the sum of squared residuals. Squaring prevents positive and negative errors from canceling and strongly penalizes large misses. It also makes the fit sensitive to influential observations.

Interpret the unit.

If b₁ = 2.4 minutes per kilometer, the model associates each additional kilometer with 2.4 more minutes in mean travel time over the studied range. It does not say every trip gains exactly 2.4 minutes.

3. Residuals and diagnostics

A residual is observed minus fitted: eᵢ = Yᵢ − Ŷᵢ. Residual plots reveal structure the coefficient table hides. Curvature suggests the mean function is not linear. A funnel shape suggests nonconstant variance. Clusters may reveal omitted groups, and sequential runs may reveal time dependence.

Diagnostic patternPossible issuePossible response
Curved residual bandNonlinear meanTransform variables or model curvature
Increasing spreadHeteroskedasticityRobust standard errors or variance model
One high-leverage pointFit depends on unusual XVerify it and report sensitivity
Runs over timeAutocorrelation or driftUse a time-series structure

Normal residuals are mainly relevant to small-sample reference distributions for conventional tests and intervals. Linearity, independence, and appropriate variance structure are usually more fundamental.

4. Multiple regression

Multiple regression adds predictors: E[Y|X] = β₀ + β₁X₁ + ··· + βₚXₚ. A coefficient describes the modeled association with one predictor while holding the included others fixed. That phrase is mathematical, not necessarily a feasible intervention.

Adding a variable can reduce confounding, improve precision, introduce bias by controlling for a collider, or obscure the total effect by controlling for a mediator. Variable selection therefore needs a scientific or causal rationale, not only automated significance rules.

Interactions allow one predictor’s slope to depend on another. Polynomial or spline terms allow curved relationships. Categorical predictors require a reference coding, making each coefficient a comparison with a baseline group.

5. Prediction and extrapolation

A confidence interval for the mean response at X is narrower than a prediction interval for a new individual outcome, because the latter includes both coefficient uncertainty and individual residual variation. Confusing the two produces overconfident predictions.

Evaluate predictive models on data not used to fit or select them. Cross-validation estimates performance under an exchangeability assumption; a chronological split is often more honest when deployment predicts the future. Extrapolation beyond the observed predictor range relies on the functional form continuing where it has not been checked.

6. Association is not causation

A regression coefficient can arise from a causal effect, reverse causation, confounding, selection, measurement error, or some mixture. Regression adjustment supports a causal claim only with assumptions about the data-generating graph, treatment assignment, measured confounders, and model specification.

  1. State whether the goal is description, prediction, or causal estimation.
  2. Draw or describe the assumed causal structure before selecting controls.
  3. Inspect support and overlap; avoid comparing populations the data do not connect.
  4. Report diagnostics and sensitivity to plausible specifications.
  5. Translate coefficients into domain units and decisions.
Takeaway. Fit the relationship, inspect what the fit missed, and keep the purpose explicit. A good predictive line can still be a poor causal explanation.
