Probability Distributions

Random variables, parameters, expectation, and the common model families that turn uncertain processes into answerable questions.

Published October 27, 2026 · 11 minute read

A probability distribution is a model of possible values and how probability is allocated among them. It is not a picture fitted for decoration: its assumptions determine which events are common, how tails behave, and which calculations are legitimate.

1. Random variables and their distributions

A random variable maps outcomes of an uncertain experiment to numbers. A discrete variable has countable possible values and is described by a probability mass function, P(X = x). A continuous variable is described by a density f(x); probability is area under the density over an interval, and the probability at any single exact point is zero.

Density is not probability. A continuous density can exceed 1. Only its integral over a range must lie between 0 and 1, and the total area must equal 1.

The cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X ≤ x) works for both kinds. It answers threshold questions directly and fully determines the distribution.

2. Expectation and variance

The expected value is a probability-weighted average over repeated realizations. It need not be a value the variable can take: a fair die has expectation 3.5. Variance measures expected squared distance from the mean, and standard deviation expresses that spread in the original unit.

E[X] = Σ x·P(X=x)   or   ∫x f(x)dx   ·   Var(X) = E[(X−E[X])²]

Linearity of expectation is unusually powerful: E[aX+bY] = aE[X]+bE[Y], even when X and Y are dependent. Variances add only under independence or zero covariance; otherwise the covariance term matters.

Binomial example.

If 20 independent components each fail with probability 0.05, the number that fail is Binomial(20, 0.05). The expected failure count is np = 1, while P(no failures) = 0.95²⁰ ≈ 0.358.

The binomial assumptions matter: a fixed number of trials, two outcomes per trial, constant p, and independence. Sampling many items without replacement violates independence; a hypergeometric model is then exact.

4. Common continuous families

The uniform distribution assigns equal density across a bounded interval. The normal distribution is symmetric and controlled by mean μ and standard deviation σ; it often models accumulated small effects or sampling error, but it is not a universal default. The exponential distribution models waiting time between events in a constant-rate Poisson process and has a memoryless property.

5. Choose from the process, not the silhouette

Start with support: can values be negative, bounded, integer-valued, or zero-inflated? Then ask how observations arise. Is the variable a count, waiting time, maximum, proportion, or sum? Finally check independence, stationarity, and whether parameters vary across units.

Several models can resemble the same histogram in a small sample. Use subject knowledge, diagnostic plots, predictive checks, and sensitivity analysis. A distribution is useful when it predicts relevant features of new data, not merely when its center follows the old data.

Parameters are not observations. The population mean μ is a fixed but usually unknown model quantity. The sample mean x̄ is a random statistic before data are collected and an estimate afterward.

6. Simulation as a check

When a formula is difficult, simulate the proposed process many times and estimate the target frequency or average. Simulation also exposes misunderstood assumptions. For example, simulating dependent failures produces more all-good and all-bad batches than an independent binomial model with the same marginal failure rate.

  1. Specify the data-generating steps precisely.
  2. Generate many independent repetitions.
  3. Compute the statistic of interest in each repetition.
  4. Compare the simulated distribution with the analytic result or observed data.
Takeaway. Distribution choice encodes a story about how variation is produced. Check the story before trusting the formula.
