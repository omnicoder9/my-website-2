From Samples to Populations

Sampling distributions, standard error, the law of large numbers, and what the central limit theorem does—and does not—promise.

Published October 27, 2026 · 11 minute read

← Back to all articles

Statistical inference works because a sample statistic has its own probability distribution under repeated sampling. That distribution connects the observed sample to uncertainty about the population. The central limit theorem is one part of this connection, not a license to ignore how the sample was obtained.

1. Population, sample, parameter, statistic

The population is the collection about which a question is asked. A parameter, such as population mean μ or proportion p, describes that population. A sample is the observed subset, and a statistic such as x̄ or p̂ is computed from it. Parameters are fixed within a model; statistics vary from sample to sample.

A target population might be all current users, all units a process could manufacture under defined conditions, or all days in a future operating period. A convenient dataset is not automatically a sample from that target. The sampling mechanism determines which generalizations are defensible.

2. The sampling distribution

Imagine repeatedly taking samples of size n by the same method and calculating the sample mean each time. The distribution of those means is the sampling distribution of x̄. It is conceptually distinct from the distribution of individual observations.

Three layers.

Individual delivery times vary around a population mean. One sample produces one sample mean. Repeating the entire sample produces many means; those means vary less than individual deliveries.

Bias describes whether the sampling distribution is centered on the target parameter. Variability describes how widely the statistic moves across samples. An estimator can have low bias but high variability, or be stable but systematically wrong.

3. Standard error

A standard deviation describes variation among observations. A standard error describes variation of an estimator across repeated samples. For independent observations with population standard deviation σ, the sample mean has standard error σ/√n. In practice σ is usually replaced by sample standard deviation s.

SE(x̄) = σ / √n   ≈   s / √n

Because precision improves with the square root of sample size, halving a standard error requires roughly four times as many independent observations. Ten times the data does not produce ten times the precision.

Independence matters. Repeated measurements on the same person, clustered classrooms, adjacent time points, and spatial neighbors carry less independent information than the same number of unrelated observations. The naive σ/√n formula can be much too optimistic.

4. Law of large numbers

Under suitable conditions, the sample average converges toward the expected value as n grows. This is the law of large numbers. It explains why long-run frequencies stabilize and why Monte Carlo averages can approximate expectations.

The law says where the average goes, not what its finite-sample distribution looks like. It also does not remove systematic bias. A large sample from the wrong population converges precisely to the wrong target.

5. Central limit theorem

For independent, identically distributed observations with finite variance, the standardized sample mean approaches a standard normal distribution as sample size grows. Consequently, x̄ is approximately normal with mean μ and standard error σ/√n, even when the original observations are not normal.

(x̄ − μ) / (σ/√n)   →   Normal(0,1)

How large n must be depends on the population shape and the statistic. Symmetric light-tailed data may behave well at modest n. Strong skew, rare events, heavy tails, or dependence can require far more data or a different method. The theorem concerns sums and means; it does not make the raw data normal.

Practical reading.

If transaction values are right-skewed but have finite variance, averages over many independent transactions tend to have a more symmetric sampling distribution. This supports normal-based uncertainty for the mean, not a claim that individual transaction values are normally distributed.

6. What a larger sample cannot fix

ProblemDoes larger n fix it?Needed response
Random sampling noiseUsually reduces itIncrease effective sample size
Coverage or selection biasNoRedesign recruitment or weighting
Measurement biasNoImprove or calibrate measurement
ConfoundingNoRandomize or model the causal structure
DependenceNot at the nominal rateUse cluster, time-series, or spatial methods
Takeaway. Sampling theory quantifies uncertainty created by random selection. It cannot rescue a sample whose relationship to the target population is undefined.
