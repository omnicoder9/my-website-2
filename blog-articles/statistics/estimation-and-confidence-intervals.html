Estimation and Confidence Intervals

What point estimates leave out, how intervals are constructed, and what a 95% confidence statement actually commits you to.

Published October 27, 2026 · 11 minute read

← Back to all articles

A point estimate gives one plausible value for an unknown parameter. An interval estimate adds the estimator’s sampling uncertainty. The interval is useful only when its construction matches the sampling design, model, and target.

1. What makes an estimator good?

An estimator is a rule applied to sample data, such as the sample mean used to estimate a population mean. Bias is the difference between the estimator’s expected value and the target. Variance measures how much the estimate changes across samples. Mean squared error combines both: variance plus squared bias.

MSE(estimator) = variance + bias²

Unbiasedness is attractive but not absolute. A small, stable bias may be preferable to a wildly variable unbiased estimate. Consistency asks whether the estimator approaches the true parameter as sample size grows. Efficiency compares variability among estimators aimed at the same target.

2. The interval recipe

Many confidence intervals have the form estimate ± critical value × standard error. The estimate locates the interval. The standard error measures sampling variability. The critical value sets the long-run coverage level under the reference distribution.

confidence interval = estimate ± critical value × SE(estimate)

This compact form hides assumptions. The estimator may need an approximately normal sampling distribution; the standard error may rely on independence; and the critical value may come from a normal, t, chi-square, or resampling distribution.

3. A confidence interval for a mean

For independent observations with unknown population standard deviation, a common interval for μ is x̄ ± t*·s/√n. The t critical value depends on confidence level and degrees of freedom n−1. It is wider than the corresponding normal interval for small samples because σ is estimated.

Worked example.

A sample of 25 measurements has mean 72 and standard deviation 10. The standard error is 10/√25 = 2. With 24 degrees of freedom, the two-sided 95% critical value is about 2.064. The interval is 72 ± 2.064×2, or approximately 67.9 to 76.1.

This calculation assumes observations are independent and that the t procedure is reasonable for the data. With a small, strongly skewed sample or influential outliers, those conditions deserve close inspection.

4. What 95% confidence means

A 95% confidence procedure is designed so that, over repeated samples generated under its assumptions, about 95% of the intervals contain the fixed true parameter. After one interval is computed, the frequentist procedure does not assign a 95% probability to that fixed parameter being inside this particular interval.

Avoid three common translations.
  • It is not the range containing 95% of observations.
  • It is not a guarantee that 95% of future estimates fall inside it.
  • It does not include measurement, selection, or model bias unless those sources were built into the procedure.

A confidence interval also is not a decision threshold. Values near either boundary are not categorically different from values just outside it. Report the estimate and interval as continuous evidence.

5. Bootstrap intervals

The nonparametric bootstrap repeatedly resamples n observations with replacement from the observed sample, recomputes the statistic, and uses the resulting empirical distribution to estimate uncertainty. It is helpful when analytic standard errors are difficult.

  1. Draw a resample of size n from the observed rows with replacement.
  2. Compute the target statistic.
  3. Repeat many times.
  4. Construct an interval using an appropriate bootstrap method.

The bootstrap reproduces the empirical sample, including its weaknesses. It cannot repair selection bias, and naive row resampling fails for clustered or time-dependent data. Small samples and boundary parameters may require specialized methods.

6. Precision and sample-size planning

For a mean under a simple independent design, margin of error scales approximately as 1/√n. To cut the margin in half, multiply sample size by about four. Planning also needs a plausible standard deviation, the desired confidence level, expected attrition, clustering, and the smallest precision that changes a decision.

Wider intervalNarrower interval
Smaller effective sampleLarger effective sample
More variable observationsLess variable observations
Higher confidence levelLower confidence level
Design clusteringEfficient paired or blocked design
Takeaway. Interval width measures precision under a procedure’s assumptions. It is not a complete measure of truthfulness: a precisely estimated biased quantity remains biased.
