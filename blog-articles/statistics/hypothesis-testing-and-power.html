Hypothesis Testing, Effect Size, and Power

How tests control error, what a p-value measures, and why practical importance cannot be reduced to a threshold.

Published October 27, 2026 · 12 minute read

← Back to all articles

A significance test compares observed data with what a specified null model would tend to produce. It is a calibrated procedure for controlling certain long-run errors. It does not calculate the probability that a scientific claim is true, and it does not determine whether an effect matters.

1. The logic of a significance test

Begin with a parameter and a null hypothesis, such as no mean difference or no association. Choose a test statistic that becomes more extreme as data depart from the null. Derive or approximate its distribution assuming the null and the model conditions. The observed statistic is then located in that reference distribution.

A two-sided test counts values at least as incompatible with the null in either direction. A one-sided test examines a prespecified direction. Choosing the direction after seeing the estimate invalidates the nominal calibration.

test statistic = (estimate − null value) / standard error

2. What a p-value says

The p-value is the probability, under the null model, of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one. A small p-value says the data are unusual under that particular null model. It is not P(null is true | data), the probability the result occurred “by chance,” or the probability a replication will succeed.

Conditioning runs one way. A p-value concerns P(data as or more extreme | null and assumptions). Reversing it to P(null | data) is a base-rate error. The latter requires a Bayesian model with prior probabilities.

The conventional 0.05 threshold is a policy choice, not a natural boundary. Results at 0.049 and 0.051 are almost indistinguishable as evidence. Report the estimate, uncertainty, exact p-value, design, and analysis choices.

Failing to reject is not proof of no effect. It may reflect little information, noisy measurement, or an effect too small for the design to detect. If the goal is to show effects are practically equivalent, use an equivalence or non-inferiority design with a justified margin.

4. Effect size and uncertainty

Statistical significance depends on both effect magnitude and precision. With enough observations, a negligible effect can produce a very small p-value. With few observations, an important effect can remain uncertain. An effect estimate and confidence interval separate magnitude from precision.

Decision framing.

A treatment that shortens a process by 0.2 seconds may be precisely nonzero but operationally irrelevant. A plausible reduction of 8 to 40 seconds may miss a threshold while still warranting a larger study. The decision needs costs, benefits, and the interval—not the p-value alone.

Standardized effects can help compare measurements on different scales, but raw-unit effects are usually easier to connect to real decisions. For binary outcomes, distinguish risk difference, relative risk, and odds ratio.

5. Power and sample-size planning

Power is the probability a prespecified test rejects the null when a particular alternative is true. It rises with larger effects, larger effective sample size, lower variability, more efficient design, or a larger α. Power is not a fixed property of a study; it is a function of the assumed effect.

  1. Define the smallest effect that would change practice.
  2. Choose the primary outcome and analysis before data collection.
  3. Estimate variability and design effects realistically.
  4. Set acceptable Type I and Type II error rates.
  5. Allow for missingness, exclusions, and multiplicity.

Observed post-hoc power calculated from the same estimate adds little: it is largely a transformation of the p-value. Use the interval to describe what effect sizes remain compatible with the data.

6. Multiple testing and researcher flexibility

If 20 independent null hypotheses are each tested at α = 0.05, the expected number of false rejections is 1, and the probability of at least one false rejection is about 64%. Family-wise methods such as Bonferroni control the probability of any false rejection. False-discovery-rate procedures control the expected proportion of false discoveries among rejections.

Multiplicity also comes from trying outcomes, subgroups, transformations, stopping rules, and model specifications. Preregistration, held-out confirmation, transparent reporting, and correction strategies protect the distinction between exploration and confirmation.

Takeaway. A test is one component of an evidence argument. Design quality, effect size, uncertainty, multiplicity, prior evidence, and replication determine whether the argument holds.
