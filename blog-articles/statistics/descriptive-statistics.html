Reading a Distribution

Descriptive statistics without losing the shape: how to summarize a dataset while preserving what its averages conceal.

Published October 27, 2026 · 10 minute read

← Back to all articles

A dataset is not a number. It has a shape: concentrations, gaps, long tails, repeated values, and observations that may belong to a different process entirely. Descriptive statistics compress that shape into quantities people can compare. Good compression preserves the features that matter; bad compression makes unlike datasets appear identical.

1. Start with the variable

Before calculating anything, identify what one observation represents and how the variable is measured. A categorical variable records membership, such as browser type or blood group. An ordinal variable has an order but not necessarily equal gaps, such as a five-point rating. A quantitative variable represents an amount for which differences are meaningful.

Quantitative variables may be discrete, such as a defect count, or continuous, such as duration. This distinction affects useful displays and models. A histogram is natural for continuous measurements; a bar chart is usually clearer for categories. Treating category codes such as 1, 2, and 3 as measurements creates averages with no defensible meaning.

Unit of analysis. If rows are transactions but the question concerns customers, transaction-level summaries overweight frequent customers. Decide whether an observation is a person, device, visit, batch, or event before interpreting the numbers.

2. See the shape first

Plot the data before choosing a summary. Look for modality, symmetry, skew, truncation, heaping, and impossible values. A single peak suggests one dominant process; multiple peaks may indicate distinct subpopulations. A long right tail is common for income, latency, and file size. Rounded spikes can reveal measurement or reporting behavior.

FeatureWhat it may indicateUseful next check
Two peaksMixed groups or operating modesColor or facet by a relevant category
Long tailRare but influential large valuesCompare mean with median; inspect a log scale
Sharp boundaryCensoring, truncation, or a physical limitReview the data-generating and collection rules
Unexpected gapsRounding, filtering, or missing rangesInspect raw values and collection metadata

3. Measures of center

The arithmetic mean uses every value and is the balancing point of a distribution. It is appropriate when totals matter and when extreme values genuinely belong to the process. The median is the middle ordered value and is resistant to tail observations. The mode identifies the most frequent value or region and can be useful for discrete or multimodal data.

mean = (x₁ + x₂ + ··· + xₙ) / n
Worked example.

For response times 40, 43, 44, 46, and 327 ms, the mean is 100 ms while the median is 44 ms. Neither is wrong. The median describes a typical request; the mean incorporates the rare slow request and is more relevant to total waiting time or capacity cost.

A trimmed mean discards equal fractions from both tails before averaging. It sits between the efficiency of the mean and the robustness of the median. Always state the trimming percentage.

4. Measures of spread

Center without spread is incomplete. The range is easy to read but depends entirely on two observations. The interquartile range, IQR = Q3 − Q1, covers the middle half of the ordered data and is robust to extremes. Variance averages squared deviations from the mean; standard deviation returns that quantity to the original unit.

sample variance: s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1)   ·   standard deviation: s = √s²

Standard deviation is most intuitive for roughly symmetric, single-peaked distributions. It should not automatically be paired with every mean. For a strongly skewed distribution, report the median with the IQR or selected quantiles such as the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles.

5. Outliers and robust summaries

An outlier is a prompt to investigate, not permission to delete. It may be a data error, a rare valid event, or evidence that the assumed population is actually several populations. The common boxplot rule flags values below Q1 − 1.5·IQR or above Q3 + 1.5·IQR. It is a screening convention, not a proof that a value is invalid.

Robust statistics change little when a small fraction of observations is extreme. Medians, IQRs, median absolute deviations, and trimmed means are valuable when contamination is plausible. Domain bounds and audit trails are still more informative than a purely numerical rule.

6. A reliable descriptive workflow

  1. Define the observation, variable, unit, and missing-value meaning.
  2. Check counts, ranges, duplicates, and impossible values.
  3. Plot the full distribution and important subgroups.
  4. Choose center and spread to match the shape and decision.
  5. Report sample size and missingness beside every summary.
  6. Preserve the raw scale or explain transformations.
Takeaway. A compact report often needs only a plot, n, missing count, appropriate center and spread, and a sentence about shape. More decimals do not recover information discarded by the wrong summary.
