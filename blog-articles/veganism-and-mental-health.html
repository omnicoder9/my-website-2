Veganism and Mental Health

An honest reading of what the evidence supports, what it merely suggests, and what it cannot yet say

Few nutrition questions are argued about with more confidence and less warrant. Meta-analyses report that people who avoid meat are twice as likely to be depressed; other meta-analyses, drawing on overlapping studies, find nothing. This is an attempt to work through the actual literature — study design by study design — without deciding the answer first.

01 The question, and why it resists a clean answer

Ask whether veganism is good or bad for mental health and you will get a confident answer from almost anyone with a stake in the matter. The confidence is not warranted. The literature is large, active, and unusually contradictory, and the contradictions are not noise around a stable signal: they are structural, arising from how the exposure is defined, who chooses it, and what kind of study can be run on a diet that people adopt for moral reasons.

Start with the exposure. “Plant-based” does not always refer to the same thing. A vegan diet excludes all animal products; lacto-ovo vegetarianism permits dairy and eggs; pescatarian and “semi-vegetarian” patterns permit fish or occasional meat; and self-identification does not reliably track behaviour in any of these groups [1]. Nor is the motivation uniform. People adopt these diets for ethics, religion, environmental concern, health, cost, or disgust, and those motivations predict different psychological profiles before any food is eaten [2]. A study that lumps a lifelong Seventh-day Adventist, a Taiwanese Buddhist, a 21-year-old who went vegan after watching a documentary, and someone cutting red meat on their cardiologist's advice into a single “meat abstainer” category is not measuring one exposure. It is measuring a heterogeneous social category and reporting the average.

Then there is the design problem. Diet cannot be blinded, ethical veganism cannot be randomised in any meaningful long-term sense, and the people who choose it differ from those who do not on almost every variable that also predicts depression. A pre-registered PRISMA review that searched Medline, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library found 23 eligible studies on vegetarian or vegan diets and depression — of which 18 were cross-sectional, three were prospective cohorts, and two were randomised trials [3]. That composition is the single most important fact about this field, and it constrains every conclusion drawn below.

02 What the pooled estimates actually say

Four quantitative syntheses of broadly the same literature have been published since 2020, and they do not agree.

The most widely cited is a meta-analysis in Nutrition Reviews covering 13 studies and 17,809 individuals. It reported that vegans and vegetarians were at increased risk of depression (OR 2.14, 95% CI 1.11–4.15) but had lower anxiety scores (mean difference −0.85, 95% CI −1.68 to −0.02), with no significant association for continuous depression scores, stress, well-being, or cognitive impairment [4]. The odds ratio of 2.14 is the number that travels; the null findings for the continuous outcomes in the same paper rarely do, and the confidence interval — spanning a 10% to a 315% increase — is almost never quoted alongside it.

A second Nutrition Reviews meta-analysis pooled nine effect sizes and found a 53% greater risk of depression among vegetarians (OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.14–2.07), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 69.1%). Its authors were candid that the finding was not robust, and that the result depended on the type of vegetarian diet and the country of the study — with the strongest association appearing, awkwardly, in semi-vegetarians (OR 1.86) [5].

A third, in Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition, pooled cohort data and found no significant association between vegetarian diets and depression [6]. A fourth, in the Journal of Affective Disorders, analysed 13 studies covering 49,889 participants and reported significantly higher depression scores among vegetarians, while noting high between-study heterogeneity and geographical variation [7]; it subsequently required a published corrigendum [8].

Finally, a meta-analysis framed around meat consumption rather than meat avoidance pooled 20 studies and 171,802 participants (157,778 consumers, 13,259 abstainers) and concluded that meat abstention was associated with higher levels of both depression and anxiety, with the association strengthening as study quality increased [9]. Its authors had previously published a systematic review reaching the same qualitative conclusion [10]. The effect sizes were described as small.

Note what has just happened. Five syntheses of substantially overlapping primary literature have produced: a doubled risk, a 53% increase, a null, a positive continuous effect, and a small positive effect that grows with rigour — alongside an inverse association with anxiety in the largest of them. This is not the signature of a well-characterised effect. It is the signature of a fragile one being measured by different instruments through different filters.

03 Why the same studies yield opposite conclusions

Four mechanisms explain most of the divergence, and none of them is a matter of one team being careless.

Inclusion criteria do most of the work

The syntheses differ in whether they admit a posteriori dietary patterns derived by factor analysis, whether semi-vegetarians count as exposed, whether they pool odds ratios or standardised mean differences, and whether they include intervention studies at all. The narrative PRISMA review that declined to pool at all made this explicit: earlier reviews were limited by including fewer observational studies and by admitting diets identified by a posteriori methods [3]. When the estimand is this sensitive to definitional choices, the pooled number is partly a report about the reviewers.

The primary studies point in three directions

Across the 25 study outcomes in that review, 11 (44%) indicated higher rates of depression among vegetarians and vegans, seven (28%) indicated a beneficial effect, and the remainder showed no association [3]. More pointedly: three of the four studies the reviewers graded as high quality found vegetarian diets associated with lower depression risk. Another research group, using its own quality appraisal, reported the opposite relationship between rigour and direction [9]. Two teams looking at the same shelf of papers have concluded that better studies favour opposite answers, which should temper anyone's certainty about what “the evidence shows.”

Geography flips the sign

Where meat avoidance is culturally normative, the association tends to reverse. A prospective Taiwanese cohort followed from 2005 to 2014 found vegetarians roughly 30% less likely to develop depression [11]; among middle-aged and older South Asians in the United States, the odds of depression were 43% lower among vegetarians [12]. Meanwhile, cross-sectional studies in Australia [13], Brazil [14], Peru [15] and Germany [16] have generally found the reverse. A dietary practice carries different social weight in Taipei than in Texas, and a variable whose effect changes sign with cultural context is behaving less like a nutrient and more like a social position.

Cross-sectional data cannot tell you which way the arrow points

This is the deepest problem, and it deserves its own treatment.

04 Which comes first?

A handful of studies can speak to temporal order, either because they recorded ages of onset, because they followed people over time, or because they used genetic instruments. They are the most informative work in the field, and they are strikingly less alarming than the cross-sectional literature.

The most methodologically distinctive is a German study drawing on the representative German Health Interview and Examination Survey and its Mental Health Supplement. It is the only study in this literature to use standardised clinical diagnostic interviews rather than self-report questionnaires, and it matched vegetarians to non-vegetarians on socio-demographic characteristics. Vegetarians showed elevated prevalence rates of depressive, anxiety and somatoform disorders — and, critically, the retrospective age-of-onset data showed that adoption of the vegetarian diet tended to follow the onset of the mental disorder rather than precede it [16]. The authors' own reading was that experiencing a mental disorder may raise the probability of choosing a vegetarian diet, whether through heightened health-orientation, health anxiety, or increased sensitivity to the suffering of other creatures.

The pattern recurs. In the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children, vegetarian men scored higher on depressive symptoms than non-vegetarian men — but the association weakened substantially once baseline health status and anxiety were accounted for [17]. In the French Constances cohort, depressive symptoms were associated with the exclusion of any food group, including meat, which points toward restriction as such rather than anything specific to animal products [18].

The cleanest longitudinal test to date used the 1970 British Cohort Study. At age 30, 4.5% of participants were vegetarian. In unadjusted cross-sectional models they reported more psychological distress (b = 0.30, 95% CI 0.09–0.52). In the fully adjusted model that difference disappeared (b = 0.02, 95% CI −0.17 to 0.21). Followed to ages 46–48, there were no differences in within-person change in distress between vegetarians and non-vegetarians (p = 0.72); sensitivity analyses using red meat consumption gave the same result [19]. The authors' framing is worth restating plainly: most studies in this area are methodologically weak, and when a strong design is applied, the cross-sectional signal does not survive.

Shorter longitudinal work in German and Chinese student samples similarly found no substantive association between vegetarianism and one-year changes in wellbeing or mental health [20, 21]. And where the sample is one in which vegetarianism is embedded in a broader supportive lifestyle — Seventh-day Adventists — vegetarians reported better mood states than omnivores [22], while one large convenience sample of vegans reported lower stress and anxiety than omnivores [23].

Against these sit the cross-sectional findings from Brazil [14], France [24] and Peru [15], which continue to report elevated depressive and anxiety symptoms among meat avoiders. The honest summary is that the association is real in many Western cross-sections and does not reliably survive designs capable of ordering the events.

The meat-reducer signal

One further wrinkle deserves attention. An analysis of North American data found that the apparent link between vegetarian diet and depression may be driven substantially by meat-reducers — people currently attempting to cut down — rather than by settled vegetarians [25]. This is consistent with the semi-vegetarian anomaly noted earlier [5]: the group with the strongest association is the group in transition, not the group at the endpoint. If a diet's biology were doing the damage, one would expect the reverse.

05 The experiments

Randomised evidence is thin, and reading it fairly requires attention to what was actually randomised and to what was actually measured.

The earliest trial randomised 39 omnivores to continue eating meat, to eat fish three to four times weekly, or to eliminate meat, fish and poultry for two weeks. Mood scores improved in the vegetarian arm [26]. Two weeks, 39 people, no blinding, and a self-report mood inventory: this is a pilot, and its authors said so.

The most-cited intervention is the GEICO study, in which employees at ten corporate sites with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² or type 2 diabetes received either weekly instruction in following a low-fat vegan diet or no instruction, for 18 weeks. The intervention group showed significant improvements in depression, anxiety, fatigue, emotional wellbeing and work productivity as measured by SF-36 and WPAI instruments [27]. Three caveats are load-bearing. First, despite the title's language, the design is described in the paper's own abstract as quasi-experimental, with worksites pair-matched and clusters assigned [27, 28]. Second, the control arm received nothing — no attention, no group sessions, no cafeteria changes — so the contrast is between an intensive supported lifestyle programme and ordinary life, not between two diets. Third, the outcomes were general health-survey subscales in a population selected for overweight and diabetes, not depression scales in a population selected for depression. An earlier uncontrolled worksite programme in the same research lineage reported similar quality-of-life gains [29].

The most informative trial arrived recently. The Nutritious Eating with Soul (NEW Soul) study randomised 159 African American adults with overweight or obesity to culturally adapted soul-food vegan or omnivorous diets, with matched behavioural support in both arms — weekly classes for six months, then tapering — over two years [30, 31]. Perceived stress fell in both groups. Mental wellbeing improved in the omnivorous group at 24 months. There were no between-group differences in change in stress or mental wellbeing at any timepoint, and none among participants adherent to their assigned diet [30]. When the supportive scaffolding is equalised across arms, the diet-specific effect vanishes. That is the single most instructive result in the interventional literature, and it points squarely at the GEICO design as the explanation for the GEICO finding.

A ten-week cluster-randomised trial comparing moderate red meat intake with plant-based meat alternatives in healthy young adults similarly examined psychological wellbeing as an outcome [32]. And the broader dietary-psychiatry literature offers a useful calibration: the SMILES trial found that a modified Mediterranean diet improved symptoms in adults with major depression [33], and a meta-analysis of dietary-improvement RCTs found small but real reductions in depressive symptoms across trials [34]. Diet quality plausibly matters for mood. Nothing in that body of work identifies the exclusion of animal products as the active ingredient.

06 The genetic evidence, and its limits

Mendelian randomisation offers a way around confounding and reverse causation: because genotypes are assigned at conception and fixed for life, associations between a genetic predictor of an exposure and an outcome are less vulnerable to the usual observational problems [35, 36]. One bidirectional two-sample MR study has been applied to vegetarianism, using a UK Biobank GWAS of vegetarianism (n = 442,589) [37, 38] against consortium GWAS of depressive symptoms, neuroticism and subjective wellbeing [39].

Its headline results were that genetically predicted vegetarianism was associated with depressive symptoms (OR 3.26, 95% CI 1.03–10.31, p = 0.044), neuroticism (OR 6.72, 95% CI 2.29–19.74, p = 5.31 × 10⁻⁴) and lower subjective wellbeing (OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.05–0.77, p = 0.019), with depressive symptoms also showing a causal influence on vegetarianism (OR 1.01, p = 0.0069) [40]. Sensitivity analyses found no significant heterogeneity or pleiotropy [41].

This is a genuine methodological advance over the cross-sectional pile, and it should not be dismissed. It should also not be over-read, for five reasons the authors themselves largely disclose. Because fewer than three SNPs met genome-wide significance for vegetarianism, the instrument threshold was relaxed from p < 5 × 10⁻⁸ to p < 5 × 10⁻⁶. The confidence intervals are extremely wide — an odds ratio whose interval runs from 1.03 to 10.31 is compatible with a trivial effect. Applying the study's own Bonferroni threshold of p < 0.0167, the depressive-symptoms result is “suggestive” rather than significant. Sample overlap with UK Biobank reached 24% for depressive symptoms and neuroticism, a known source of bias toward the observational estimate [42]. And the vegetarianism phenotype derives from touchscreen food-frequency questions in a cohort aged 40–69 that is unrepresentative of the general population [37, 38].

Most importantly, the result is bidirectional. Even taken at face value, it says that depressive symptoms make vegetarianism more likely as well as the converse — which is precisely what the age-of-onset data suggested a decade earlier [16]. An MR study is not a licence to read a one-way arrow into a two-way finding.

07 Nutritional mechanisms: plausible, largely unproven

The nutritional story is the one most often told, and it is worth separating its two halves. The first half — that plant-based diets carry specific nutrient risks — is well established. The second half — that those risks translate into mood pathology, and that correcting them fixes it — is much weaker than the first half's solidity implies.

A systematic review of 141 studies found that intake and status of vitamin B12, vitamin D, iron, zinc, iodine and calcium were generally lower in plant-based dietary patterns than in meat-eaters, with vegans lowest for B12, calcium and iodine, and with EPA and DHA intake lower in both vegetarians and vegans. The same review found meat-eaters at risk of inadequate fibre, PUFA, α-linolenic acid, folate, vitamin D, vitamin E, calcium and magnesium — nutrient inadequacy is not a vegan monopoly [43]. B12 deficiency in particular is common among unsupplemented vegetarians and vegans [44], and untreated deficiency produces documented neuropsychiatric consequences including irritability, memory impairment and personality change [45].

So the ingredients of a mechanism are present. What is missing is the demonstration. Reviews of B vitamins in depression report associations and biological plausibility rather than robust treatment effects [46, 47]. The omega-3 case is the clearest illustration: EPA and DHA are concentrated in fish and largely absent from plant foods [48]; observational data link higher n-3 intake to lower depression risk [49]; and yet a Cochrane review of 35 randomised trials in adults with major depressive disorder concluded that the evidence does not support n-3 PUFAs as a treatment, finding at most a small-to-modest effect unlikely to be clinically meaningful, with no benefit at all in milder symptomatology [50]. If supplementing the nutrient does not reliably improve depression in people who have depression, the inference that lacking it causes depression in people who do not is not available. Broader reviews of nutrition and behavioural health disorders reach similarly hedged conclusions [51].

There is also a decisive counterfactual buried in the intervention literature. In the NEW Soul trial, both arms received nutritional guidance and both were nutritionally supported, and no mental-health difference emerged [30]. And in every worksite vegan programme reporting mood benefits, participants were counselled and advised to supplement [27, 29]. A nutrient-deficiency mechanism predicts that supplemented vegans should do fine. Broadly, they appear to.

08 The social mechanism, which is usually skipped

If the biology is weak and the association is nonetheless real in Western samples, the remaining candidate is social. Here the evidence is better than most nutrition commentary acknowledges, though it is largely correlational too.

Vegetarianism and especially veganism function as identities, not merely as consumption patterns, and the psychological literature on this is substantial [52]. Vegans and vegetarians are rated as negatively as several conventional targets of prejudice, with vegans rated more negatively than vegetarians [53]. Anticipated stigma is a documented barrier to adopting the diet in the first place — people expect their family and friends to react badly, and often they are right [54]. Media portrayal is systematically derogatory, casting vegans as hostile, ascetic, faddish, or oversensitive [55]. Judgements of vegan advocates specifically turn on whether the observer codes them as morally admirable or moralistically objectionable, and the latter framing is common because ethical veganism carries an implicit reproach [56].

This matters causally because minority stress is a well-established route to distress in other domains, and the ingredients are all present here: a visible practice, enacted several times a day in public, that marks the practitioner as deviant and invites explanation and challenge. Personality data are consistent with the picture without settling it: vegetarians score higher on neuroticism in some samples [57], though a representative Australian study found plant-food consumption associated with greater emotional stability [58]. And the cultural weight of meat in Western societies — its historical entanglement with masculinity, status and national identity — explains why refusing it provokes a reaction that refusing, say, mushrooms does not [59].

The social account also makes a falsifiable prediction that the data appear to bear out: the association between meat avoidance and distress should shrink or reverse where the practice is normative. It reverses in Taiwan [11] and among South Asian Americans [12], and it is absent or reversed among Adventists, for whom vegetarianism is a community norm rather than a deviation [22].

09 Disordered eating: the confound that is also a real risk

A systematic review of 48 studies on the meat-avoidance spectrum found no consensus that meat avoidance is associated with disordered eating overall, but reported that most studies found a significant positive association between both vegetarianism and veganism and orthorexia nervosa — a pathological preoccupation with eating “correctly” [60]. A literature review found the vegetarianism–orthorexia relationship supported in 11 of 14 studies [61], and a recent meta-analysis confirmed an association between vegetarian and vegan diets and orthorexia symptoms [62]. Survey data have reported orthorexia screening positivity around 31% among vegans and vegetarians against roughly 12% among omnivores, with dietary vegans highest [63].

Two things must be held together here, and the debate usually drops one of them.

The first is genuine and clinically important: for some people, vegetarianism is a socially sanctioned vehicle for restriction. Retrospective chart reviews have found that roughly half of patients diagnosed with anorexia nervosa report following a vegetarian diet, and around two thirds of individuals with an eating-disorder history report that their vegetarianism was related to the disorder because it licensed restriction [64]. Studies of females with eating disorders find the two entangled in ways that clinicians need to be able to distinguish [65]. A young person announcing a sudden dietary restriction in the context of weight loss warrants attention, whatever the stated reason.

The second is a measurement problem that cuts the other way. The same systematic review found that eating-disorder instruments perform poorly psychometrically in vegetarians and vegans, with tentative evidence that the factorial validity of widely used scales such as the EDE-Q may be poor in vegans [60]. Orthorexia itself is not a DSM-5-TR diagnosis, and screening tools like the ORTO-15 flag deliberate, principled food selection as pathology by construction. An instrument that asks whether you think carefully about the ethical provenance of your food will mechanically score an ethical vegan as disordered. Some of the elevated prevalence is signal. Some of it is the ruler.

10 Diet quality, not diet category

A recurring finding across recent cross-sectional work is that variation within plant-based diets predicts mental-health outcomes better than the vegan/vegetarian/omnivore category does. Australian studies found depressive symptoms in vegans and vegetarians tracked plant-based diet quality [66, 67]. Iranian data distinguished healthy plant-based patterns (whole grains, fruit, legumes, vegetables, nuts, vegetable oils), which were associated with lower psychological distress, from unhealthy plant-based patterns (refined grains, juices, sugar-sweetened beverages, potatoes, desserts), which were associated with higher anxiety and distress [68]; a multicentre Iranian community study reached compatible conclusions [69].

This is a more useful frame than the categorical one. A diet of chips, white bread and sweetened oat milk is vegan. So is a diet of lentils, greens, nuts and fortified foods. Treating these as one exposure and asking whether “veganism” affects mood is a category error that no amount of statistical adjustment repairs, and it is precisely the error that the categorical literature is built on.

11 Reading the field's incentives

Objectivity requires noticing who funded what, and it requires doing so symmetrically.

The meta-analysis reporting that meat abstention is “clearly associated with poorer mental health” discloses that it was funded in part by an unrestricted research grant from the Beef Checkoff, through the National Cattlemen's Beef Association, and that four of its authors had previously received funding from the same source [9]. The same group has continued to publish in this direction [70]. This does not make the analysis wrong — disclosed industry funding is not disqualifying, and the paper's methodological criticisms of the underlying literature are largely fair. It does mean the framing choices deserve scrutiny.

The countervailing case is equally clear. The GEICO trials were conducted by the Physicians Committee for Responsible Medicine, an organisation whose explicit mission includes promoting plant-based diets [27, 28, 29]. Again, this does not invalidate the work. It does explain why the control arm received no intervention at all, a choice that a neutral investigator designing a diet-versus-diet comparison would be unlikely to make — and which the later matched-support trial did not make, with correspondingly null results [30].

Recent narrative reviews continue to reach opposite summary conclusions from the same evidence base [70, 71], which is itself diagnostic. When a literature is weak enough that prior commitments determine the conclusion, the correct response is not to pick the review that agrees with you.

12 What this means in practice

For an individual considering or maintaining a vegan diet. There is no credible evidence that a well-planned, supplemented plant-based diet causes depression in a well-nourished adult. There is good evidence that unsupplemented vegan diets carry real nutritional risks, of which B12 is the most consequential and the most easily solved [43, 44, 45]. Supplement B12; consider algal EPA/DHA and iodine; check iron and vitamin D status if symptomatic. Attend to diet quality rather than diet label [66, 68]. Expect the social cost to be real and to be highest early, and treat community as a health input rather than a luxury.

For clinicians. Two things are worth doing. Check B12 status and correct deficiency, because it is cheap, safe and occasionally decisive [45]. And take dietary restriction seriously as a possible presentation of an eating disorder, particularly in adolescents and particularly where restriction is recent, accompanied by weight loss, or justified by shifting rationales [60, 65, 64]. Do not treat ethical veganism as a symptom. The evidence does not support advising a distressed patient to resume eating meat.

For advocates. The strongest available position is also the honest one. The cross-sectional association is real and should not be denied; the causal claim built on it is not supported; and the best-designed studies — the British birth cohort and the matched two-year randomised trial — both come out null [19, 30]. Denying the association reads as motivated and is easily rebutted. Explaining it is more persuasive and happens to be true. It also carries an operational implication: if a meaningful part of the association runs through stigma, isolation and moral distress, then community infrastructure is a mental-health intervention, and advocacy that increases the social cost of the identity has a cost of its own.

For everyone. Lifestyle factors including diet, exercise and sleep plausibly influence depression risk [72, 73], and diet quality is worth attending to. But the specific claim that removing animal products from a diet damages mental health does not currently survive contact with the studies capable of testing it.

13 The bottom line

In Western populations, people who avoid meat report somewhat more depressive symptoms than people who do not. That much is reasonably well replicated, though the effect is small, highly heterogeneous, and accompanied in the largest meta-analysis by lower anxiety scores [4].

Almost everything else is contested. The association reverses in cultures where the diet is normative [11, 12, 22]. It weakens or disappears under adjustment [17, 19]. It is stronger in semi-vegetarians and meat-reducers than in settled vegetarians [5, 25]. Where the sequence of events can be reconstructed, the psychiatric problem usually came first [16]. Where the diet has been randomised with matched support, no difference emerged over two years [30]. The one genetic study points both ways at once and rests on relaxed instruments and wide intervals [40]. The most-invoked biological mechanism — omega-3 insufficiency — is undermined by a Cochrane review finding that supplementing omega-3 does not reliably treat depression [50].

The most defensible reading is that the association is largely a selection and social-context effect, with a real but narrow nutritional contribution in people who neither supplement nor plan, and a real overlap with restrictive eating that clinicians should watch for. That is a less satisfying conclusion than either camp offers. It is the one the evidence supports.

What would change this picture: a large prospective cohort with validated, repeatedly measured dietary exposure that separates vegans from vegetarians from meat-reducers; biomarker confirmation of nutritional status rather than diet labels; motivation recorded at baseline; a stigma measure carried alongside the mood measure; and mental-health outcomes as pre-specified primary endpoints rather than secondary subscales. None of that is exotic. Until someone runs it, confident claims in either direction are exceeding their warrant.
