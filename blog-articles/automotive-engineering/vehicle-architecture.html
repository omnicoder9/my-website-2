What Is Fixed, and What Has to Move Around It?

Road-vehicle architecture read as the management of hard points: twenty-seven plates, nine experiments and one ledger

A vehicle architecture is not a list of parts. It is a small set of coordinates and envelopes that are frozen early, plus the rule that everything else must route around them. Types, drive configurations and major systems are three views of the same fact: which points a given vehicle refuses to move, and what it charges everything else for the refusal.

0.1 Identification of the item

1The datum

Ask an engineer what a car's architecture is and you will usually get a list: front-engine, transverse, front-wheel drive, steel unibody, strut front and multilink rear. The list is accurate and it explains almost nothing, because it does not say which of those decisions came first, which of them could still be reversed, and which of them the others were built to accommodate. A vehicle architecture is better described the way a building's architecture is described: not by its materials but by the positions it has committed to. A handful of coordinates get fixed early. Everything after that is routing.

Those coordinates go by an unglamorous name in production engineering. They are hard points: the wheel centres, the seating reference point, the bumper beam height, the base of the A-pillar, the steering column axis, the front and rear crush initiators, the pack or tank envelope. Some are chosen. Most are inherited. A few cannot be chosen by anyone at all, because they follow from the shape of the human body, from an identity in mechanics, or from a regulation that the vehicle engineer had no part in writing. What makes them architectural is not that they are important but that they are early: they are settled before the objects that will have to live around them exist, and reversing one costs more than everything downstream of it put together.

This article takes the standard taxonomy of road vehicles — ten types, seven drive and engine configurations, ten major systems — and reads all twenty-seven entries as answers to a single question. What does this thing fix, and what has to move around it? The three lists turn out not to be three subjects. They are three angles on one structure. A vehicle type is a particular set of refusals: a bus refuses to raise its floor, a motorcycle refuses a second track, a truck refuses to exceed an axle load it did not choose. A drive configuration is a statement about where the densest object sits and which patches are asked to work. A major system is what is left after the refusals have been honoured — the routing problem, made into hardware.

The framing is not original to this article, and its clearest statement comes from an unexpected place. In a study of how much mass can be removed from a vehicle when a lightweighting change is made, Alonso and colleagues observe that the most complex parts, which need the most design detail and cost the most to tool, are designed first and frozen while the rest of the programme continues 1. They then measure what that costs. With every subsystem still available for redesign, one kilogram removed from a vehicle permits a mean of a further 0.95 kg to be removed from the parts that were carrying it. With four of thirteen subsystems locked in, that figure falls to 0.12 kg. Freezing a third of the vehicle removes seven-eighths of its ability to respond.

That is the whole argument of this article, measured. It is worth being precise about why the collapse is so violent, because the mechanism recurs everywhere below. Secondary mass saving is a feedback loop: removing mass allows supporting structures to be resized, which removes more mass, which allows further resizing. If the subsystems that are still free carry a total mass-influence coefficient K, the loop sums to a geometric series and the total saving is amplified by 1/(1 - K). Freezing a subsystem does not subtract a term from the answer. It subtracts from K, and 1/(1 - K) is convex, so the loss outruns its cause. L3 below reproduces the shape from an independent coefficient set and finds something the published summary does not state: reaching 0.12 kg/kg requires the four locked subsystems to carry 78.0% of all mass influence. The freeze in practice does not fall on four arbitrary subsystems. It falls on the four that matter most, because those are exactly the ones with the longest lead times.

Every plate below therefore carries a rung on a freeze ladder. The ladder is ordered by who can move the thing and when, and it runs from F0, not movable by the manufacturer at all, through F1 (a new platform generation), F2 (a body change), F3 (a model year), F4 (per build) to F5, movable after the vehicle has been sold. The rungs are not a metaphor. They correspond to real gates in a vehicle programme 2,3, and the distance between two rungs is the distance between a calibration change and a new press line. The most interesting entries in the ledger are the ones that have moved between rungs within living memory. Brake force distribution used to be a valve, chosen once and fitted for the life of the platform; it is now a control law, and the regulation that governs it prescribes compatibility bands rather than a number 4. Software update, which is the F5 rung, has acquired its own approval requirement 5 and its own management-system requirement alongside it 6. A rung is not a permanent property of a function. It is a property of how the function is implemented, and moving a function down the ladder is one of the few genuinely architectural moves available.

The second thing every plate carries is a currency. Fixing a point is never free, and the bill arrives in one of five forms: mass, package volume, energy, interface count, or risk. These are not interchangeable and they are not reducible to money, which is why treating cost as the universal currency conceals the argument rather than settling it. A decision that costs mass can be paid for with a better material. A decision that costs package volume cannot: volume inside a vehicle envelope is conserved, and the only way to find more of it is to take it from something else. A decision that costs interface count is paid in the number of things that now have to agree with each other, which is the currency that compounds fastest and shows up latest 7,8,9.

Mass deserves a note of its own, because it is the currency that is easiest to measure and the hardest to reason about. It is not merely one cost among the five. It is the only one that feeds back into itself, and the feedback is what makes it architectural rather than merely undesirable. The reference handbooks treat it as a budget to be allocated 10,11; the harmonised life-cycle literature treats it as an input to an energy calculation 12,13; the teardown-based assessments of what could actually be removed from a specific vehicle treat it as a list of opportunities 14. All three are correct and all three understate the problem, because each takes the rest of the vehicle as given. Sensitivity studies that vary mass against a fixed architecture find a modest and well-behaved response 15. The same variation against an architecture still free to respond finds a much larger one. The difference between those two numbers is not a modelling artefact. It is the freeze.

The third thing is the gauge, and it is a package section. Every plate is drawn against the same stylised side elevation, divided into six longitudinal stations — front overhang, front axle, cowl and dash-to-axle, cabin, rear axle, rear overhang — and three vertical bands: below the floor, floor to beltline, and above the beltline. The stations a plate shades are the stations it constrains. This is the drawing that vehicle packaging actually happens on 16,17, and a plate that shades one station is a local decision while a plate that shades all six is a decision about the vehicle. The count of stations is not the ledger's sorting key, though. That job goes to a fourth field: which of the ten major systems must move if this item moves. A blast radius, counted rather than asserted, and the closing section will show that it is inversely related to the freeze rung. What cannot be moved is exactly what moves everything else.

One methodological note. Nine experiments accompany the argument, written in the Python standard library with no randomness anywhere, and every number quoted in the prose, the plates and the figures is produced by them and copied verbatim. Their purpose is not to model vehicles; there are far better tools for that 18,19. It is to force the article's claims into a form where they can be wrong. Three of the nine had to be rebuilt after their first run contradicted the claim they were written to support, and one claim was withdrawn outright. Those rebuilds are disclosed in the lab section rather than quietly repaired, because an article about what gets frozen early should be honest about its own freezes.

1 General construction characteristics

2Ten types, ten refusals

The standard vehicle taxonomies classify by use and by construction: the international vocabulary defines types by body and purpose 20, the approval framework by category and mass 21. Both are useful for paperwork and neither explains why the vehicles look different. A more productive reading is that each type is defined by one thing it will not move, and that everything characteristic about the type is downstream of that refusal.

It is worth being clear about what the official taxonomies are for, because it is not this. The approval categories exist to decide which requirements apply to which vehicle, and they are drawn on mass, seating capacity and body type 20,21. They are administrative boundaries, and like all administrative boundaries they are gamed: the sport utility vehicle exists in the shape it does partly because of where those boundaries were drawn and how efficiency requirements are indexed to vehicle footprint 22, and the resulting change in what the fleet is made of is visible in market statistics 23. That is a real effect and it is not the subject here. What matters for architecture is that even a category invented for regulatory reasons acquires, very quickly, a genuine technical fixed point — a floor height, a tow rating measured to a published procedure 24, a seating position — and from then on it is the fixed point rather than the regulation that shapes the vehicle. Categories start as paperwork and end as geometry 25.

Start with the passenger car, because its fixed point is the one people least expect. It is not the engine and it is not the wheelbase. It is the seating reference point: the position of a seated occupant's hip above the road. That point is not measured on the vehicle. It is measured with a physical device shaped like a human lower body 26, and the driver's eye position is not measured at all but taken from a statistical distribution of where drivers' eyes actually are, given that hip point 27. Every other package dimension in the industry's dimensional vocabulary is referenced to it 28. From the hip point follow the heel point, the seat track, the steering wheel position, the cowl height, the base of the A-pillar, the bonnet line, the mirror positions and the entire forward field of view. A designer can move all of those. What none of them can do is move the occupant, because the occupant is not a component the manufacturer supplies.

The truck's refusal is imposed from outside and is arithmetic. Federal law in the United States sets a single-axle limit, a tandem limit and a bridge formula relating gross weight to axle spacing 29; the European internal market sets its own maxima on dimensions and weights 30. The vehicle engineer's degrees of freedom are the number of axles and the distance between them, and that is very nearly all. Everything else about a heavy vehicle follows: the frame is a ladder because it must be cut and lengthened to different wheelbases 31, the suspension is chosen for load range rather than for wheel control, and the braking system faces a problem that no passenger car has, which is that the same vehicle has two radically different centres of gravity depending on whether it is carrying anything. L5 measures the consequence and it is severe: a fixed proportioning valve tuned to extract the full available deceleration laden extracts 22.6% less when empty, and does so by locking the rear axle first, which is the yaw-unstable direction 32,33.

The sport utility vehicle refuses to lower its floor, and this is the clearest case in the taxonomy of a refusal with a measurable price. Raising the floor raises everything that sits on it, and the centre of gravity is a mass-weighted mean of the whole stack. The regulator's rollover metric is simply track width over twice the centre-of-gravity height, and the published bands separate passenger cars at 1.30 to 1.50 from taller light trucks and vans at 1.00 to 1.30 34. L4 builds seven package stacks from item masses and heights alone, uses no vehicle measurement at all, and reproduces the separation: the saloon lands at 1.371 and the raised-floor sport utility at 1.109. What makes the experiment worth doing is the controlled comparison it also permits. Put the identical upper body on a flat floor with the heaviest single item underneath it, and the same silhouette reaches 1.315 — a shift of +18.6% from a decision about the floor and nothing else.

The bus refuses in two directions at once, and the two refusals fight. It must have a low continuous floor and a wide door aperture, because accessibility regulation specifies the step height and the gangway 35, and it must survive being rolled onto its roof with a defined residual survival space inside 36. The first requirement takes the floor away from the engineer; the second demands a strong ring structure through exactly the sections the doors have removed. The resolution is architectural and it is visible from the street: the driveline goes to the extreme rear, the equipment goes on the roof, and the roof therefore becomes the vehicle's mass problem. L4 finds the roof carrying 9.2% of a city bus's mass and supplying 24.2% of its centre-of-gravity height. Air conditioning, gas cylinders and, increasingly, traction batteries all end up there for the same reason: the floor was given away 37,38,39.

The motorcycle's refusal is the most absolute in the taxonomy: it will not have a second track. Two contact patches on one line means there is no lateral separation, and with no lateral separation there is no couple available to react a roll moment. The consequence is that the whole vehicle must lean until the resultant of gravity and centripetal force passes through the contact line, at an angle of atan(a_y/g) 40,41. Ground clearance stops being a comfort item and becomes the cornering limit: L8 finds a 48-degree hard-part limit capping steady cornering at 1.1106 g, with the five degrees between 43 and 48 worth 0.1781 g. Braking is limited by geometry rather than by rubber, because the rear wheel unloads to nothing: on the sport machine in L8, pitch- over arrives at 1.0769 g and the rear axle is carrying 3.53% of the weight by 1.00 g. In exchange the machine has almost no frontal area, and needs 60% of a saloon's power to hold 30 m/s. Nothing in that trade is a design choice. It all follows from the number of patches.

The racing car is the only entry in the taxonomy whose fixed points were written by somebody else and are rewritten on a schedule. The survival cell and the regulated reference planes are common to every competitor, which converts architecture from a private decision into a shared search problem: everyone is optimising inside the same box, and the box changes. What is instructive is which constraints the class does not have. L4 gives a single-seat stack a static stability factor of 2.8329, a tilt threshold of 70.6 degrees. No tyre approaches it, so rollover is simply not a constraint, and the height freed by its absence is spent on generating normal load aerodynamically instead — which is the one way to raise the contact patch budget without touching the tyre 42,43,44.

The electric vehicle's refusal is the most recent and the most consequential for everything else in this article. It insists on a large rectangular prismatic volume between the axles and below the floor, surrounded by an exclusion zone that crash structure may not intrude into 45 46,47. Cells do not fold and do not tolerate deformation 48,49, so unlike a fuel tank the store cannot be shaped around what is left over. The pack's gravimetric efficiency is a system property, not a cell property, and varies substantially with format and integration approach 50,51. The architectural result is the flat floor, and the flat floor is the reason a body style that used to be a rollover liability is no longer one. It is also the reason the tunnel disappears, the reason the front overhang can shrink, and the reason the interior package suddenly has degrees of freedom it has not had since the 1930s.

There is a limit to that store, and it is not a technology limit. L3 sizes a traction battery for a target range and finds a fixed-point problem: mass carries energy, energy needs mass. Solving it gives a closed form with a pole at R* = 10⁴ e / ERV, where e is pack-level specific energy and ERV is the energy a vehicle consumes per unit mass per unit distance. Published energy-reduction values for road vehicles sit between 0.47 and 1.17 kWh per 100 km per 100 kg 1,52,53. At the middle of that range and a pack at 160 Wh/kg, the pole sits at 2000 km. Doubling range from 600 to 1200 km does not double pack mass; it multiplies it by 3.500. Range is not a specification. It is a packaging decision with a singularity in it, and the position of the singularity moves only with pack-level specific energy — not with motor efficiency, not with aerodynamics, and not with anything that sits above the floor 54,55.

The hybrid refuses to give up either energy path, and pays for it in the one currency that cannot be manufactured. It keeps the fuel tank, the exhaust, the combustion cooling circuit and the front bay, and adds a store, a converter and a second cooling circuit to them 56,57. Nothing about that is unreasonable in isolation. The problem is where the additions go. L7 puts an extra rigid unit into an existing transverse engine bay and watches the rigid fraction go from 61.5% to 75.6% while mean occupant deceleration at the regulatory 56 km/h goes from 41.12 g to 64.93 g. The body did not change. The crush length did, because the crush length was what the new hardware consumed. This is the mechanism behind an observation that puzzles people outside the industry: why a hybrid version of an existing car so often needs new front structure rather than carry-over structure.

The autonomous vehicle's fixed points are the strangest in the taxonomy, because they are not inside the vehicle at all. They are mounting positions on the outside of it, and what fixes them is that a vehicle is opaque to its own sensors. L9 makes this exact. A 360-degree sensor mounted 100 mm above the roofline cannot see the ground closer than 34.97 m ahead of it, and sees none of the near field at all; the relation is a hyperbola in the height above the roof, so the first few hundred millimetres of mast are worth more than any amount of range. Four sensors at the corners invert the result — half the near field, because they sit low and outboard where nothing of the vehicle is in the way — and two more on the flanks take near-field coverage to 91.75%. The positions that work are the four corners and the flanks, which are also the crush initiators, the pedestrian-impact zone 58 and the door apertures. The taxonomy of driving automation levels 59 is silent on all of this, and the safety literature that worries about validation mileage 60,61 and the field data that reports crash rates 62 both take the sensor set as given. It is not given. It is packaged, and the package was decided before the perception stack existed.

Two regulatory episodes illustrate how late the rules arrive relative to the package. The first is the automated lane keeping regulation, the first binding international approval for a system that actually drives, with a speed ceiling and an operational domain written into the approval itself 63. It says a great deal about what such a system must prove and nothing at all about where its sensors are, which is the decision that determines whether it can prove any of it. The second is humbler and more telling. Rear visibility became a mandated camera requirement 64 because the geometry of a modern rear end had already made direct vision impossible — high beltlines, thick pillars and a raised deck, all of them package decisions taken for crash, aerodynamic and styling reasons over decades. The regulation did not change the architecture. It ratified a blind spot the architecture had created, and required an electronic substitute for the view that had been designed out.

The off-road vehicle refuses to be shaped by the styling surface. Approach, departure and breakover angles are terrain geometry, and the wheel travel envelope has to be large enough to keep normal load on every patch over broken ground — which is the actual determinant of off-road tractive performance, rather than peak friction 65,66. The refusal is unusually visible because it collides head-on with another one. Raising the bumper line for an approach angle worsens performance against the frontal geometry the pedestrian protection regulation measures 58, and the two requirements are enforced by different authorities with no mechanism for trading between them. That collision is not resolved by engineering. It is resolved by choosing which market the vehicle is for.

3Seven configurations, one decision counted twice

The seven configurations in the standard list are conventionally taught as two independent choices: where the engine is, and which wheels are driven. They are not independent, and treating them as independent is the commonest error in the popular literature on the subject. What actually determines a vehicle's longitudinal behaviour is the relationship between the two — whether the mass sits over the axle that is being asked to work.

A note on vocabulary, since this is where it bites. Vehicle dynamics has two standard terminologies, one from the North American engineering society and one international 67,68, and they do not agree on every sign convention or axis definition. This is not pedantry. A specification written against one and verified against the other will produce a vehicle that behaves correctly and measures wrong. The convention used here is the one implied by the closed forms below, stated each time it is used. That two mature standards can differ on something this basic is itself an instance of the article's thesis applied to language rather than to hardware: the conventions were fixed early, independently, by bodies with no reason to coordinate, and everything written since has had to route around the discrepancy.

The physics is a one-line identity and it is worth writing down because everything in this section follows from it. Under forward acceleration a_x, load transfers from the front axle to the rear by m a_x h / L, where h is the centre-of-gravity height and L the wheelbase. A front-driven vehicle therefore unloads its own driven axle as it accelerates, and a rear-driven one loads it. Solving each for the acceleration at which the driven axle saturates gives closed forms 69,70: μ(b/L)/(1 + μ h/L) for front drive, μ(a/L)/(1 - μ h/L) for rear drive, and simply μ when every wheel is driven.

L1 holds a reference vehicle completely fixed — same mass, same wheelbase, same centre-of-gravity height, same friction coefficient — and moves only the longitudinal station of a 320 kg powertrain unit. Moving it from ahead of the front axle to behind the rear axle shifts the static front load fraction from 60.5% to 37.6%, 22.9 points, without changing a single component. The front-drive traction ceiling falls 37.9% across that sweep and the rear-drive ceiling rises 58.0%. All-wheel drive holds 0.90 g at every station: it is the only layout in the sweep whose answer does not depend on where the powertrain went, which is precisely what it is bought for.

Two details from that sweep are worth pulling out. The first is where the two two-wheel-drive layouts tie. It is not at 50/50. Because acceleration moves load onto the driven axle in one case and off it in the other, a rear-driven car matches a front-driven one while still carrying 59.17% of its static weight on the front axle, and the offset from even is exactly μ h / 2L. This is the quantitative content of a piece of folklore — that a nose-heavy rear-drive car still out-accelerates a balanced front-drive one — and it is worth having the number rather than the folklore.

The second detail was a mistake the lab caught. The first version of L1 concluded that the architecture matters less on a low-grip surface, on the reasoning that nothing is near the limit anyway. The measurement refuses it. Running the same sweep at μ = 0.30 gives a relative spread across powertrain stations of 37.862097%, identical to the figure at μ = 0.90 to six decimal places, because μ cancels in the ratio. Only the absolute spread scales, by a factor of 2.6901. The architecture's share of the answer is grip-independent. What changes with grip is how much that share is worth. The claim was replaced by the measurement, which is a better claim.

Where the engine goes therefore sets the terms, and the drive layout decides who pays them. The front-engine layout is the default in every mass market and its cost is stated in a currency that only appears at the moment of a crash. The densest single object in the vehicle has been placed in the only volume that also has to absorb a frontal impact. L7 puts numbers on the collision of interests: at the regulatory 56 km/h 71,72, a long bonnet with a 950 mm bay and a 560 mm rigid block gives 31.63 g of mean occupant deceleration, while a 780 mm bay with a 480 mm block gives 41.12 g. What decides the ranking is not the bay length but the rigid fraction: the cab-forward electric case keeps 44% of the long-bonnet bay length and only 33% of its usable crush 73,74,75.

The mid-engine layout buys the best of both — mass inside the wheelbase, short driveline, load on the driven axle — by taking the volume the rear occupants wanted. L1 puts its rear-drive ceiling at 0.6049 g against 0.4786 g for the same unit over the front axle, a 26.4% gain from position alone. It is almost never the right answer for a vehicle that carries four people, and this is not a failure of engineering imagination. Volume inside the wheelbase is conserved, and there is exactly one of it.

The rear-engine layout is the most instructive of the three, because it was once the mass-market default and is now nearly extinct outside one sports-car lineage. It gives the highest rear-drive ceiling in L1's sweep, 0.6876 g, and the widest spread between drive layouts anywhere in the study — the identical mass distribution gives front drive only 0.2860 g. What killed it was not traction but yaw. A large mass behind the rear axle gives a long moment arm and a high yaw inertia, and the resulting behaviour at the limit was for decades the standard illustration in the popular safety literature 76 77,78. It is worth noticing what the electric skateboard has done to this argument: with the largest mass inside the wheelbase and below the floor, the trade that made rear-engine layouts attractive has been dissolved rather than resolved.

Front-wheel drive is the configuration that asks one pair of patches to do two jobs at once, and the friction ellipse is what makes that expensive. A patch has one force budget, μ Fz; any longitudinal force is spent out of the lateral one, and the remainder is sqrt((μ Fz)² - Fx²) 79. The ellipse is flat on top and vertical at the side, so the arithmetic is counter-intuitive: spending half the budget longitudinally costs only 13.4 points of lateral capacity, while the tenth from 0.90 to 1.00 costs 43.6 more. Sharing a patch is nearly free until it is nearly total.

L2 runs a corner exit for four architectures, each pairing a drive layout with the powertrain station that layout actually uses, and includes the load transfer. Against the ideal friction circle the worst front-drive deficit is 0.1691 g, 1.77 times the amidships rear-drive deficit of 0.0956 g, and the front axle is fully spent at 0.45 g with nothing left to steer with. The counterfactual makes the point sharper still. Give front drive the rear-engine mass distribution and it exhausts its front axle at 0.30 g instead of 0.45 g. Drive layout and mass station are not two decisions. They are one decision counted twice, and the reason front-wheel drive works at all in production is that it is always paired with the mass distribution that suits it.

Rear-wheel drive's cost is package, and it is paid in the middle of the cabin. A mechanical driveline has to be straight, and the passenger compartment is in the way, so the tunnel is not a styling artefact but a duct. It is also a structural member, which is why removing it is not a simplification: it changes the torsional load path of the entire body 80,81,82. An electric rear drive unit removes the duct without removing the structural requirement, which is one of the quieter architectural consequences of electrification and one of the more expensive to get wrong.

The last two entries are usually treated as synonyms and are not. Four-wheel drive in the strict sense means a rigid connection between two axles, usually through a selectable transfer case, and it exists to work on surfaces that yield. On a high-friction surface it winds up, because two axles cannot roll at the same rate through a turn 65,83. All-wheel drive means a torque path to every wheel that is present all the time, through a differential or a clutch, and its cost is a parasitic loss paid on every journey rather than a restriction on when it can be used 84,85. Both buy the same thing, which L1 states exactly: an acceleration ceiling of μ at every powertrain station, instead of a station-dependent 0.2860 to 0.6876 g.

Both also occupy an unusual position on the freeze ladder, and it is the clearest illustration in the article of why the ladder matters more than the feature list. Fitting all-wheel drive is F4: it is a build option, chosen per vehicle on the line. Provisioning for it — the transfer case station, the propshaft tunnel, the rear differential mounting, the driveshaft clearances through the floor, the extra cooling — is F1, decided once for the platform generation. The decision that matters was taken years before the customer chose the option, and a platform that was not provisioned cannot be persuaded later at any price 86,87.

The seven-way classification is now being quietly dissolved, and it is worth saying how. An electric drive unit is small enough to sit on an axle, has no intake, no exhaust and no gearbox of consequence, and needs no mechanical connection to anything else. Once that is true, front-engine, mid-engine and rear-engine stop being alternatives: a vehicle can have a unit at each end and nothing in the middle, which is a configuration the classification has no name for. Front, rear and all-wheel drive stop being a decision about a driveline and become a decision about how many units to fit, which is an F4 decision taken on the assembly line rather than an F1 decision taken years earlier 56,85. What does not dissolve is the mass station. L1's result is about where the dense object sits, and a 480 kg pack between the axles and below the floor is a mass station whether or not anything is connected to it. The classification changed. The physics it was a proxy for did not.

4Ten systems, and what they route around

The third list is different in kind from the first two. Types and configurations are choices; systems are consequences. Every one of the ten is in some sense the residue of decisions taken elsewhere, and the interesting question about each is not what it does but what it had to accept before it could do anything.

The powertrain is the largest single rigid envelope in the vehicle, and its architectural significance is entirely a matter of volume and station. L1's result — 22.9 points of static weight distribution and 37.9% of traction ceiling from moving the same unit — is a statement about position, not about the unit. This is also the system that best illustrates the difference between fitting and provisioning. A bay is provisioned once, at F1, with a defined envelope, mounting points, cooling connections and crash behaviour; it is then filled with variants at F4, and the variants can differ by a factor of three in output without the architecture noticing 88,89.

The chassis is the set of load paths between the four contact patches, and it is the correct place to make the article's central observation about conservation. Every force the vehicle exerts on the world arrives at four patches, and every force the world exerts on the vehicle arrives the same way. Everything between those patches and the occupant is a routing decision. The body-in-white is the single heaviest subsystem in L3's breakdown and carries the largest mass-influence coefficient, 21.6% of the total; freezing it alone takes the secondary saving from 0.9500 to 0.6182 kg/kg. This is why the structural decision is the one that has to be made first and the one that can never be revisited, and it is the strongest practical argument for the platform-based approach that dominates the industry 1,80.

There is one component in the chassis system that the vehicle engineer does not design, and it is the one that touches the road. Tyres are bought from a catalogue against published grades: rolling resistance, wet grip and noise are labelled properties in the European market, graded on scales the vehicle manufacturer has no part in setting 90. The consequence for architecture is easy to miss. Every friction-limited number in this article — the traction ceilings of L1, the ellipse of L2, the lock points of L5 — has μ as a leading factor, and μ is a property the architecture selects from a menu rather than designs. The engineer chooses the load on each patch, the camber the patch sees through its travel, and the size of the contact area 79,91. Everything else about the interface arrives as a supplier's compromise between grip, rolling resistance and wear, and moving along that compromise in one direction moves the vehicle along it in the others.

The body is the exterior surface, the apertures cut into it and the structures behind it, and it is being asked to satisfy three regulators at once. Frontal, lateral and roof requirements each specify a deformation limit on the same shell 71,92,93,94, an independent rating scheme adds an offset test that loads the structure outside the main rails 95, and the current European rating scheme scores safe driving, crash avoidance, crash protection and post-crash performance together 96. The body is also the fastest-moving structural item on the freeze ladder, at F2, which is why so much architectural adjustment in practice takes the form of a body change on an unchanged platform.

Suspension is the system whose fixed points are the least visible and the most binding. The linkage hard points determine roll centre height, camber gain and anti-dive or anti-squat geometry simultaneously, which means none of them can be adjusted alone 18,91,97. The wheel also sweeps a volume as it travels, and nothing else may occupy that volume, which is why suspension turrets appear in the middle of what would otherwise be usable luggage space. In L3 the two suspensions together carry 20.3% of the mass influence — more than the engine — which makes them one of the most expensive things in the vehicle to freeze early, and one of the most commonly frozen.

Steering has the distinction of being fixed at both ends by other people. The upper end is set by the seating reference point, which is a property of the human body 26,98; the lower end is set by the suspension hard points, which are a property of the linkage. The column has to reach both and pass through the dash, the crash structure and the front bay on the way, and it is simultaneously a controlled energy-absorbing element in frontal impact 74. Replacing the mechanical connection removes a package problem and creates a functional-safety problem, transferring the argument from geometry into a hazard analysis and an integrity argument 99,100. That is a real trade, but it is a trade between currencies, not an elimination.

Braking is the system that has actually changed rungs, and it deserves the attention. The ideal distribution of braking force between axles depends on the instantaneous centre of gravity, which moves with the payload and moves again with every deceleration. A fixed proportioning valve holds one number, and L5 shows there is no number that is right twice: tuned for the laden state it delivers the full 0.80 g laden and 0.6194 g empty, 22.6% short and by locking the rear axle first; the best fixed compromise gives 0.6839 g in the worse state, in both states. Electronic distribution recovers 0.1161 g, which is 17% more deceleration, and it does so by moving the decision from the platform rung to the software rung. The regulation reflects this by prescribing adhesion utilisation bands rather than a fixed ratio 4,32,101. It is worth being precise about what the architecture did and did not do here. It did not create the friction limit. It created the spread, by putting the payload aft of the front axle on a vehicle whose payload is 60% of its kerb mass.

The same story runs through stability control, which is the case where software did not merely recover lost performance but supplied a function no mechanical architecture could 102. Its effect on real-world crashes was large enough to be mandated 103,104,105,106, and automatic emergency braking has since followed the same path from option to requirement 107,108. Each of these moves a safety function from the hardware rungs to the software rung, and each therefore inherits the software rung's problem, which is that the thing can be changed after it has been sold and must therefore be governed 5,6,109.

The electrical and electronic system is where the article's thesis is easiest to see and hardest to act on, because its physical form is dictated entirely by decisions taken elsewhere. The loads are where the doors, the lamps, the seats and the axles are. L6 places 48 loads at fixed coordinates and compares a central star against a zonal topology, and the first result is the expected one: zonal is 27.4% shorter. The second result is not. At 12 V the zonal harness is 43.2% heavier than the star it replaced, because the topology trades many thin runs for a few thick ones and a 12 V backbone carrying the whole vehicle's current needs a very large cross-section. Only when the backbone moves to 48 V, cutting current by four and conductor area with it, does mass fall — by 19.8%. Zonal architecture and 48 V are not two independent trends. Neither pays without the other 110,111,112.

Sweeping the number of zones makes the dependency structural rather than anecdotal. The minimum-mass zone count is 1 at a 12 V backbone, 5 at 48 V and 10 at 400 V: the optimum is a property of the backbone voltage crossed with where the loads happen to be, and where the loads happen to be is a property of where the doors, the lamps, the axles and the seats were put. None of those were electrical decisions. Meanwhile the network layer has been moving from a broadcast bus designed in the 1980s 113,114 to switched automotive Ethernet at 100 Mb/s, 1 Gb/s and 10 Mb/s over single pairs 115,116,117 with time-sensitive networking for determinism 118, and the software layer from a statically configured platform to one that supports dynamic deployment 119,120,121. Vehicle software has been counted in tens of millions of lines for well over a decade 122, and the network review literature has been describing the topology problem for nearly as long 123,124,125. All of that movement is at F1 and above. The conductor route is still F1, and it is still determined by the body.

The heavy-vehicle side of the same problem has a different history and it is instructive. Commercial vehicles standardised on a single application-layer protocol early, with a parameter-group vocabulary any manufacturer of any component could implement 126, because the industry's business model is a chassis from one company, a body from another and a refrigeration unit from a third. The interface had to be public. Passenger-car architectures, built as integrated products by single manufacturers, never needed that and never did it, which is why the passenger-car network layer is a proprietary configuration on top of a standard physical layer and the commercial-vehicle one is not. Neither approach is wrong. They are the two ends of the same trade — interface count against integration freedom — and the choice was made by the shape of the industry rather than by any engineering argument 7,125.

Thermal management fixes the cooling apertures, and cooling apertures are the most contested real estate on a vehicle. Heat has to be rejected to air that arrives through a hole in the part of the body with the highest pressure and the least available area, and cooling drag is a first-order term in the aerodynamic budget rather than a correction to it 127,128 129. Electrification does not remove the problem; it changes its shape. Fast charging is limited by the rate at which heat can be removed from the cells rather than by the chemistry's willingness to accept current 130,131,132,133, and the charging interface itself has become a standardised fixed point with its own voltage ceiling 134,135,136,137,138.

The interior fixes the occupant volume around the hip point that the type already fixed: seat track travel, knee clearance, headroom, and the reach envelope. What is worth emphasising is that this envelope is a statistical construction fitted to a driver population, not a clearance around a mannequin 16,26,98. It is the one system whose requirement is derived from measurements of people rather than of vehicles, and it is therefore the one that does not improve with technology. L4 finds occupants and seats supplying 20.7% of a saloon's centre-of-gravity height while being 15.3% of its mass, which is a reminder that the payload is high up and always will be.

Safety systems close the list and they sit at F0, because the governing relation is an identity rather than a requirement. Absorbing the kinetic energy of an impact at a bounded deceleration takes a distance: s = v² / 2a, with no free parameters. L7 gives the numbers — 56 km/h needs 617 mm of crush to hold 20 g, 411 mm to hold 30 g, 308 mm to hold 40 g — and they are the reason the front overhang exists. The architecture that answers this was patented in 1952, as a rigid passenger cell surrounded by deliberately deformable ends 139, and the restraint that works with it in 1962 140, validated on 28,000 accident cases 141. Neither has been improved on architecturally in seventy years, which is unusual enough to be worth saying.

There is one more term in the safety equation and it is not under the vehicle's control at all. In a head-on collision the lighter vehicle's velocity change is the closing speed times the other vehicle's share of the combined mass. L7 computes it: a 2600 kg partner gives a 1200 kg car a velocity change 1.368 times larger than a like-for-like crash, and 1.873 times the energy for its structure to absorb over a crush length its own architecture fixed years earlier. Mass is a safety feature whose bill is presented to somebody else, an externality that has been estimated in the economics literature 142 and reflected in the crash-risk literature 143 13. It is also, uncomfortably, encouraged by regulation that indexes efficiency requirements to vehicle footprint 22.

5The lab

Nine experiments, written in the Python standard library, with no randomness of any kind: the same file run twice produces the same bytes. Their purpose is to keep the article's claims falsifiable rather than to model vehicles. Each one uses closed forms or exhaustive enumeration where it can, and states its assumptions where it cannot.

Three of the nine were rebuilt after their first run produced a result that did not support the claim they were written for, and the rebuilds are recorded here rather than quietly absorbed. L1's first version claimed that the architecture matters less at low grip; the measurement showed the relative spread across powertrain stations is exactly independent of the friction coefficient, because μ cancels in the ratio, and the claim was replaced by the measurement. L2's first version had longitudinal load transfer the wrong way round — acceleration unloads the front axle, not the rear — which reversed its conclusion entirely; it was corrected and then rebuilt so that each drive layout is paired with the powertrain station it actually uses, which is the comparison that means something. L9's first version tested occlusion in plan view, which put roof-mounted sensors inside the vehicle's own outline and returned zero coverage for every configuration; it was rebuilt in three dimensions with sensor height as the governing parameter, and only then did the result — that mast height dominates everything else — appear at all.

Two further adjustments are worth flagging because they change how the results should be read. L3's coefficient sum is calibrated to a single published figure, the 0.95 kg/kg secondary saving with all subsystems free 1, so that everything the experiment then says about freezing is a prediction of the model rather than a fit to the answer. L4's first stack put too much mass too high and produced rollover thresholds below every published band; it was rebuilt against the bands the regulator reports 34, and the controlled comparison it exists to make — the same top hat on two floors — was not affected by the recalibration. L6 originally compared a 12 V zonal backbone against a 12 V star, reported a mass increase, and stopped there; adding the 48 V and 400 V cases turned an apparent negative result into the experiment's most useful finding.

What the lab deliberately does not do is worth stating, because the omissions are where its results would break first. Nothing here is transient: every experiment computes a steady state or a closed-form limit, so tyre relaxation, suspension compliance, damper behaviour and every controller in the vehicle are absent 18,79. Lateral load transfer is left out of L1 and L2 because an axle-level model cancels it to first order, which is true and is also a simplification that would matter for any question about balance rather than about ceilings. L4 treats a package stack as point masses at stated heights, which is exactly right for a centre of gravity and says nothing about the roll axis. L7's constant-force crush is a first approximation to a pulse that is never constant 73,75. L9's vehicle is a rectangular box, so it flatters any sensor that would in reality be looking past a mirror. In every case the simplification was chosen so that the quantity being measured survives it. A model that cannot be wrong in an interesting way is not worth running, and a model whose simplifications are hidden cannot be checked at all.

the kinetic energy of an impact at v when s = v^2 / 2a. Nothing in
this identity is negotiable, and it is what fixes the front overhang.

now put the powertrain in the way. A rigid block of length b inside a
bay of geometric length Lb leaves (Lb - b) usable, so

fraction 61.5% -> 75.6% and mean deceleration 41.12 g -> 64.93 g,
57.9% worse on an unchanged body. This is why hybrid
architectures so often need new front structure rather than carry-over
structure: the crush length is what the extra hardware consumed.

the escape is a shorter, stiffer bay, which raises a_mean and hands
the problem to the restraint system: the belt and airbag must then
dissipate more of the occupant's own energy over the same chest
travel. The constraint was not removed. It was passed to another
subsystem with its own fixed points.

L9  SENSOR COVERAGE AGAINST THE VEHICLE'S OWN HARD POINTS

A 4.70 m x 1.85 m x 1.45 m vehicle is an opaque box to its own
sensors. A ground cell counts as seen when the straight line from a
sensor to that cell clears the body box and lies inside that sensor's
field of view. Two exhaustive grids are used: the near field, within
3.0 m of the body, at 0.05 m; and an 80 m x 40 m working area at
0.20 m. Nothing is sampled.

first, one 360-degree sensor on the roof centreline, swept over mast
height. The vehicle's own roofline sets a depression angle, and the
nearest visible ground point follows from it directly:

works is a sensor set mounted in the places the structure most needs
to deform and the body most needs to open.

The result is the one the article has been arguing towards, and it is sharper than expected. The relationship between the two axes is negative and close to monotone: the plates at F0, which the manufacturer cannot move at all, displace a mean of 7.40 of the ten major systems, while those at F4 and F5 — the build option and the over-the-air update — displace a mean of 5.00. What cannot be moved is exactly what moves everything else. That is not a coincidence and it is not a paradox. A decision reaches F0 precisely because so much was built on top of it that no one can afford to revisit it, and the things that can be changed cheaply are the things nothing depends on.

The most-displaced system across all twenty-seven plates is chassis, displaced by 25 of them. That is the correct answer and it is worth sitting with. The body-in-white is not where the interesting engineering happens, it is not what customers evaluate, and it is not what the marketing describes. It is simply the thing that every other decision lands on, which is why it is frozen first, why it carries the largest mass-influence coefficient in L3, and why the platform strategy that dominates the industry exists at all 7,9,86,87.

A dependency structure of this shape has a name outside the automotive literature. It is the argument for design rules: a system in which a small set of decisions is fixed and published, and everything else is developed independently against them 8,144,145. What the ledger shows is that vehicle architecture is not a candidate for that discipline but an instance of it, arrived at empirically over a century and enforced by tooling costs rather than by design theory. The hard points are the design rules. The platform is the published interface. The variants are the modules.

Two cautions about reading the ledger too confidently. The first is that the blast-radius count is an authored judgement rather than a measurement: it records which systems this article says must move, and a different engineer would score several plates differently by one or two. What is robust is the ordering, not the arithmetic, and the ordering survives fairly aggressive reassignment because the gap between the two ends of the ladder is wide. The second is that a low rung is not a criticism. F0 is where the honest constraints live — the human body, an identity in mechanics, a statute — and an architecture with no F0 entries would be one that had not yet met reality. The useful question the ledger raises is narrower: for each plate not at F0, is it there because it must be, or because it was convenient at the time to decide it early?

One entry in the ledger sits alone and deserves its own note. Braking is the only plate at F5, and it got there by changing its own implementation. As a proportioning valve, brake force distribution was an F1 decision with a permanent 17% penalty attached to it, as L5 measures. As a control law it is recomputed every few milliseconds against the load state the vehicle actually has. Nothing about the vehicle's geometry changed. What changed is that the decision stopped having to be made in advance. That is the only genuinely general escape from the argument of this article, and it works exactly as far as the function in question can be moved into software and no further — which is why steering, suspension geometry and crush length remain where they are.

0.2 Conclusion of the record

7What the taxonomy was hiding

The three lists this article started with are the ones every introduction to the subject uses, and there is nothing wrong with them. Ten types, seven configurations, ten systems: it is a serviceable map of what exists. What it conceals is the ordering. Presented as three parallel lists, the taxonomy implies that a vehicle is assembled from selections — pick a type, pick a configuration, pick systems to suit — when the actual dependency runs one way and runs early.

The reading offered here is that all twenty-seven entries are answers to one question, and that the question is about position and timing rather than about function. A type is a refusal: the hip point above the road, the axle load ceiling, the low floor, the single track. A configuration is a statement about where the densest object sits crossed with which patches are asked to work, and L1 and L2 show that these are not two statements. A system is what is left — the routing problem, made into hardware, and constrained by hard points it had no part in choosing.

If there is a single number to take from the article it is Alonso's pair, 0.95 and 0.12 1. Freeze four subsystems of thirteen and seven-eighths of the vehicle's ability to respond disappears — and L3 shows the collapse requires those four to carry 78.0% of the total mass influence, which is to say that the freeze in practice lands precisely on the subsystems that matter most, because those are the ones with the longest lead times. The engineering question is therefore not which architecture is best. It is which decisions genuinely have to be made before the others, and whether any of them could be made later than they currently are.

Most of them could not. The identity s = v²/2a has no free parameters, the seated human body is not a component, the friction ellipse is a circle whatever anyone would prefer, and a vehicle will always be opaque to its own sensors. Those are the F0 entries and there is no engineering response to them, only an accurate accounting of what they cost. Some, though, have moved, and the ones that moved all moved the same way: from a decision embodied in geometry to a decision recomputed at runtime. Brake distribution went first, stability control followed, and torque distribution across axles is going now. Each move converts a permanent compromise into a live calculation, and each pays for it with a system that can be changed after it has been sold, and therefore has to be governed 5,6.

That is the honest shape of the trend, and it is narrower than it is usually described. Software has not made vehicle architecture flexible. It has moved a small number of specific decisions from F1 to F5 while leaving the geometry exactly where it was. The wheel centres, the hip point, the crush length, the pack envelope, the door apertures and the mounting positions where a sensor can actually see the ground are all still fixed before the first line of code is written, and everything the code does has to route around them. That is what a vehicle architecture is, and it is why the discipline has always been less about choosing than about deciding what to decide first.
