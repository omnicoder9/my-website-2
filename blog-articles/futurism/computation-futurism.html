A descriptive speculation on the future of software and computation

What Keeps the Parts Apart?

Software's future is usually told as integration: boundaries dissolve and capabilities combine. Read seam by seam, thirty of those boundaries tell a more specific story. Integration trades walls for watchmen and rules, and each trajectory goes only as far as its substrate can be taught to tell its parts apart.

1The question

Most accounts of where computing is going share a shape. Programs become ecosystems; separate technologies fuse; specialised components multiply; software acquires autonomy, then a body; and the number of possible combinations outruns the number of things being combined. The text this essay takes as its starting point is a compact version of that account. It lists fifteen trajectories, from applications dissolving into ecosystems to computation becoming “a general property of technological civilization”, and it closes on a loop: more computation, more connectivity, more specialisation, more automation, more embodiment, more integration, more possible combinations, new domains, and so more computation again. Each step is plausible and most are already visible.1–3

The loop leaves out the condition that let it run in the first place. Parts can be combined only if they stay what they are when combined. A module whose behaviour depends on what it is plugged into is not a module; a library whose bugs can overwrite its caller's memory is less a component than a contaminant; a sensor whose reading shifts when the motor beside it draws current measures its neighbour, not the world. Combination presupposes separation, and the history of computing is, to a first approximation, the history of making separation cheap.

Herbert Simon put the point as a parable.4 Two watchmakers build watches of a thousand parts. Tempus assembles each watch in one piece, and an unfinished watch falls apart whenever he is interrupted; Hora builds stable subassemblies of ten, so an interruption costs him at most the subassembly in his hands. Simon set the chance of an interruption at one in a hundred for each part added and concluded that Tempus would take “about four thousand times as long”. Recomputed exactly under his own model, the factor is 1,974, about half the published figure, a slip others have also noticed5; the conclusion survives by three orders of magnitude (Figure 1). The same model says two things Simon did not dwell on. In a perfectly quiet workshop Hora is the slower of the two, by 11%, because a hierarchy has more joints than a single assembly: keeping parts apart is insurance, and insurance carries a premium. And the cheapest subassembly shrinks as the workshop gets noisier, roughly as the square root of 2/p; at Simon's rate it holds 14 parts, close to Hora's ten.

Software is the most thorough application of Hora's method ever attempted. Parnas made what a module hides, rather than what it does, the criterion for drawing its boundary.6 Saltzer and Schroeder made complete mediation, every access checked, a design principle.7 Dennis and Van Horn gave authority a representation a machine could check, the capability.8 Rushby showed how a shared machine could give each of its partitions the behaviour of a machine of its own.9 Baldwin and Clark showed why all of this pays: modular design rules turn one product into a portfolio of options that can be improved, swapped and recombined independently.10 Beneath it sits the digital abstraction, in which every gate restores its output to a clean level, so that noise does not accumulate as signals pass from part to part, and in which reliability can be built from unreliable parts by redundancy.11,12 The combinatorial growth Arthur describes, technology building itself out of itself,3 is growth among parts that are cheap to keep apart.

The difference compounds. If parts can meet only through their interfaces, each part is one more thing that can fail; if every part can disturb every other, every pair is. At a reliability of 0.999 per part, or per pair, the first arrangement supports combinations of 692 parts at even odds of working and the second only 37: leaking boundaries turn a linear budget into a square-root one (Figure 2). The seven-domain combination the starting text imagines, with AI, robotics, biotechnology, nanotechnology, materials, networks and manufacturing all at once, rests on seven interfaces if its parts keep to them and on 21 pairwise seams if they do not.

So the question this essay puts to each of the fifteen trajectories is not what will combine but what will keep the parts apart once they are combined, and by what kind of mechanism. Two seams per trajectory, thirty in all, are coded on a six-rung ladder that runs from boundaries kept by physics, through boundaries kept by a mechanism in the substrate or by proof, down to boundaries kept by watching, by agreement, or not at all. Each seam is coded twice: as it is kept now, and as it was kept in the arrangement the trajectory displaced. The essay is descriptive throughout. Where it speculates, it extrapolates what the record shows; it does not argue for what anyone should do.

The result is consistent enough to state at the outset. Eighteen of the thirty seams were once kept apart by physics (separate machines, an air gap, a fence, a device you could walk away from) and sixteen of those are now kept apart by a monitor, a convention or nothing at all. Integration has not dissolved boundaries; it has traded walls for watchmen and rules. Only three of the thirty seams are held by a mechanism that prevents a crossing rather than noticing or forbidding one, and all three guard a distinction their substrate can represent. That is the essay's description of what comes next, in compressed form: each trajectory will go as far as its substrate can be taught to tell its parts apart, and the price of that teaching rises by orders of magnitude as computation moves from silicon into the physical, biological and quantum world.

2How the seams are read

The ladder ranks the modality that keeps a seam apart, from the strongest guarantee to the weakest (Figure 3).

S5 Physics
No path exists. A crossing requires building one: carrying a disk across an air gap, climbing a fence, transmitting on a frequency the receiver filters out.
S4 Substrate
A mechanism in the layer beneath the parties mediates every crossing, whether or not the parties cooperate: a memory-management unit, a hypervisor, an input–output MMU, the restoring transfer curve of a logic gate.
S3 Proof
The artefact is checked before it runs and the check covers every execution: a type system, a verified kernel,13 a query whose structure is fixed before any data is bound to it.
S2 Monitor
Crossings are watched for while the system runs and are stopped or reversed when seen: a watchdog, a circuit breaker, a classifier, a force sensor, a person reviewing output.
S1 Convention
Crossings are forbidden by a rule that holds while the parties comply: a standard, a contract, a versioning policy, a regulation with no technical means of enforcement.
S0 None
The substrate gives the parts one shared channel and nothing distinguishes them, so there is nothing for a rule or a mechanism to hold on to.

The line between S3 and S2 is the most important one on the ladder. Above it, boundaries prevent: a crossing cannot happen without defeating physics, a mechanism or a proof. Below it, boundaries respond: a crossing can happen, and what differs is whether anything is watching for it, whether anyone has promised that it will not happen, or neither.

Each seam carries three further codes. Its crossing is the kind of thing that must not pass: information (state, data and secrets), authority (commands, privileges, the ability to cause), time (latency, scheduling, timing), failure (faults, overloads and defective changes) or effect (energy, force, matter and chemical activity). Its representation records whether the substrate can express the distinction the boundary guards: R2 if it natively does and the mechanism uses it, R1 if it could but this seam does not use it, or uses it only coarsely, and R0 if no representation exists. Its status records whether the arrangement is deployed at scale, emerging or speculative in 2026.

The coding rules matter more than the ladder. Now is the strongest modality in general deployment in 2026, not the best available in research; research mechanisms appear in each plate's last field. Before is the modality that kept the same separation in the arrangement the trajectory displaces: a separate machine, a vendored library, a fenced robot cell, a classical bit. Each plate holds one seam and each seam one crossing, the crossing the trajectory most depends on. Two seams were chosen for each trajectory, every crossing type appears at least four times, and among candidate seams the one with the best-documented failure was preferred. That last preference biases the sample towards boundaries that have been tested by being crossed. The census therefore describes the boundaries these trajectories rest on where they are known to have been tested, not a random sample of all the boundaries in computing.

Each plate carries a small section drawing. The wall's height and material show the rung now: rock for physics, steel for a substrate mechanism, cast concrete for proof (it is poured and cured before anyone moves in), glass for a monitor (you can see across it, and break through it), a painted line on the floor for a convention and open floor for none. A dashed outline shows where the wall stood before. The crossing arrow stops at a wall of S3 or above and passes over anything lower.

3Connection: ecosystems, integration and everything networked

The first group of trajectories connects parts that used to be separate: applications becoming services in ecosystems, software entering other industries' products, and everything becoming addressable on one network.

From applications to ecosystems

The starting text's first trajectory replaces the application with the system of systems: a request that passes from device to operating system to service to API to AI model to database to another service and out to an actuator. That chain already exists, and its newest link is being standardised quickly. The Model Context Protocol, introduced in November 2024 to give AI applications one way to reach tools and data,14 was donated a year later to the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation, together with Block's goose and OpenAI's AGENTS.md; the announcement counted more than ten thousand published MCP servers.15 A protocol of this kind is an agreement about what may cross between parts. By itself it says nothing about what may not, and Conway's observation that systems take the shape of the organisations that build them means the boundaries of an ecosystem are, in practice, the boundaries between organisations.16

The section drawing at the head of this page redraws the starting text's chain as a building, one room per component and one wall per seam, each wall at the rung its seam is kept at today. Of the nine seams, only one is held above S2: the boundary between tenants on shared hardware, which hardware virtualisation turned into a substrate mechanism. Every other seam in the chain is kept by watching or by agreement.

What an ecosystem shares, it shares in time as well as in data. When a request fans out across many components it is as slow as the slowest of them. If each of a hundred servers has a slow moment one time in a hundred, 63% of requests are slow,17 and keeping the whole request slow less than one per cent of the time requires each component to meet its p99.99 latency (Figure 4). Dean and Barroso's remedy, hedged requests, buys the boundary back with duplicated work: in a Google benchmark, re-issuing a lagging request after 10 milliseconds cut the 99.9th-percentile latency of a thousand-key read from 1,800 to 74 milliseconds for two per cent more requests.17

Integration into other industries' products

The second trajectory is integration: software fused with networks, robots, vehicles, medicine, energy and farming until there is no clear boundary between “the software system” and “the agricultural system”. Lee's account of cyber-physical systems makes the same point from the engineering side: once computation is part of a physical process, its correctness includes timing and physical consequence, which conventional software abstractions leave out.23 Two seams show what integration does to separation. The first lies between the networks that run an organisation and those that run its plant. Industrial control systems were long protected by being disconnected; IEC 62443 describes the connected alternative as zones joined by conduits, each conduit a place where traffic is checked.24 Stuxnet had already shown that an air gap is a physical boundary only until someone carries a path across it.25

The second lies between a software update and the physical product it changes. A car, a speaker or a thermostat used to behave as it did on the day it was sold; now its behaviour is a release train. UN Regulation 156 requires vehicle makers to run a certified software-update management system,26 and the European Union made it mandatory for new vehicle types from July 2022 and for all newly registered vehicles from July 2024.27 Outside regulated products the boundary is a vendor's judgement. In May 2024 a redesigned Sonos app removed features owners relied on, including queue editing and access to local music libraries, from speakers already in their homes, and the company's chief executive left in January 2025.28

An Internet of everything

The seventh trajectory connects people, computers, machines, vehicles, buildings, infrastructure and sensors on one network. The count is already large: IoT Analytics estimated 18.5 billion connected IoT devices in 2024, 21.1 billion by the end of 2025 and 39 billion by 2030.29 Atzori and colleagues framed the Internet of Things as a convergence of identification, sensing and networking.30 Its first large security lesson concerned authority. The Mirai botnet grew to a peak of about 600,000 infected devices, recruited largely through factory-default credentials, and turned them on infrastructure that had nothing to do with their owners.31 The response, years later, is convention. The United Kingdom has banned universal default passwords on connectable consumer products since 29 April 2024,32 and the European Union's Cyber Resilience Act applies vulnerability-reporting duties from 11 September 2026 and its remaining obligations from 11 December 2027.33

The other kind of boundary on a fully connected planet is older than software and stronger. In the radio spectrum, separation is a matter of frequency and physics still does the keeping. When the United States reallocated 3.7–3.98 GHz for 5G,34 the nearest neighbours were aircraft radio altimeters at 4.2–4.4 GHz, and some older altimeters filtered too poorly to ignore the new signals. The fix kept the boundary physical: the FAA proposed that about 1,000 US airliners receive filters or new altimeters by 1 February 2024.35 It also shows that even a boundary kept by physics depends on the equipment on each side honouring it.

4Differentiation: specialisation, new substrates and combination

The second group multiplies kinds of part: specialised processors and services, computation in substrates other than silicon, and the combinations among them.

Specialisation explodes

The starting text predicts that integration will produce more specialisation, not less: inference accelerators, vision processors, cryptographic engines, domain-specific databases and agents in their thousands. The economics behind that prediction are well documented. Hennessy and Patterson's record of single-processor performance shows growth of about 52% a year from 1986 to 2003, 23% a year to 2011, under 12% to 2015 and 3.5% since.36,37 Thompson and Spanuth argue that the slowdown is dissolving computing's status as a general-purpose technology, because the fixed cost of a specialised chip is repaid only while general-purpose processors fail to catch up with it.38 The lab puts a number on the catching up (Figure 5). A tenfold advantage was erased in 5.5 years at 52% a year and lasts 66.9 years at 3.5%, so the window in which a specialised part repays its seam is 12 times longer than it was. Google's first tensor processing unit is the canonical case,39 and Leiserson and colleagues expect performance gains to come increasingly from specialisation, algorithms and software rather than from transistors.40 The trend is not monotone: the system at the top of the June 2026 TOP500 list is built entirely from CPUs.41

Each specialised part is also a new seam, and new classes of part often arrive without the separation the general-purpose processor took decades to acquire. Inference services cache the computation for prompt prefixes and reuse it across requests to save work; an audit of commercial language-model APIs detected caches shared globally across users at seven providers, including OpenAI, and showed that cache timing leaked enough to reveal an undisclosed detail of one embedding model's architecture.42 On a microcontroller, firmware tasks commonly share one address space, so one memory-safety bug in a shared network stack can reach everything on the chip: the Ripple20 disclosure found nineteen vulnerabilities in one embedded TCP/IP library and estimated that hundreds of millions of devices were exposed.43

New substrates

The tenth trajectory is the most ambitious: computation exploiting physical substrates beyond silicon. Each has a working demonstration. Learned models predict protein structures and interactions44,45 and design new proteins46; deep learning has proposed hundreds of thousands of stable crystals47 and a robotic laboratory set out to make them,48 although a later analysis disputed how many of the claimed new materials were new.49 DNA stores digital data50 and folds into logic-gated machines51; neurons cultured on electrodes learn to play a simulated game,52 and organoid intelligence has become a research programme.53 Metamaterials can be programmed with digital codes,54 neuromorphic chips compute with spikes,55,56 and quantum devices sense and compute in ways classical devices cannot.57,58

What these substrates share is the absence of the digital abstraction's free boundary. A logic gate restores its output on every operation, so a classical bit is kept apart from noise without a single additional device. A qubit cannot be copied, or measured without disturbance, so it has to be kept apart from its environment actively: one logical qubit is encoded in many physical ones, and errors are detected and corrected as they occur. Google's 2024 surface-code memory operated below threshold, so that adding qubits reduced errors: a distance-7 code of 101 qubits failed in 0.143% of cycles, and errors fell by a factor of 2.14 each time the distance grew by two.59 Extrapolated, a logical qubit good to one error in a trillion cycles needs distance 63 and 7,937 physical qubits (Figure 6), and that is a memory only, before any routing or computation.60 The most recent estimate for a cryptographically relevant computation, factoring a 2,048-bit RSA key, is under a week on fewer than a million noisy qubits, down from twenty million in 2019.61,62

Biology lacks a different part of the abstraction, isolation. Endy's programme for engineering biology rested on standardisation, decoupling and abstraction, the same moves that made software composable.63 Two decades on, the main obstacle is that biological parts share their host's machinery, its polymerases, ribosomes and energy, and so affect one another through channels no interface describes.64 Insulator parts that strip away sequence context help,65 and automated design works remarkably often: of sixty circuits designed with the Cello tool, forty-five performed correctly, and 92% of all output states matched prediction.66 To a digital designer, one circuit in four failing would be a crisis. In a substrate without isolation it is an achievement.

Combinatorial growth

The eleventh trajectory is this essay's subject. The starting text observes that if technologies A to G combine in pairs and then all together, the number of possible interactions grows much faster than the number of technologies. Economists and complexity theorists have made the argument formally. Weitzman modelled growth as recombination of existing ideas, limited by the capacity to process combinations rather than by their supply67; Youn and colleagues found that patented inventions increasingly combine existing technology codes rather than introduce new ones68; Kauffman's adjacent possible is the set of combinations one step away from what already exists.69

In engineered systems, combinations are seldom pairwise. They pass through narrow waists, a few shared protocols, formats and components that everything above and below agrees on. Akhshabi and Dovrolis showed that layered architectures evolve towards this hourglass shape unaided, and that the protocols at the waist become the most stable and the hardest to displace.70 A waist is what makes cheap combination possible, and it is also a shared part. Sharing moves risk into the tail without changing its average (Figure 7). With a thousand sites and a one-in-a-hundred chance of a bad update, independent components lose ten sites on average and essentially never half of them; a single shared component also loses ten on average, but loses all thousand one time in a hundred. Staged rollout buys the tail back with time. Rings at 1, 10 and 100% of the fleet, each detecting a bad update nine times in ten, cut the expected damage from a bad update to 2.8% of the fleet, at the price of every good update reaching the last ring two bake periods late.

5Delegation: autonomy, automated engineering and adaptation

The third group hands judgement to software: agents that pursue goals, software that writes software, and systems that keep models of their users and adapt to them.

Software becomes autonomous

The fourth trajectory replaces “execute these 10,000 instructions” with “accomplish this objective subject to these constraints”. Language models that interleave reasoning with tool calls made that loop practical,76,77 and the length of task agents can complete has grown quickly. METR's measure, the length of task at which agents succeed half the time, grew exponentially from 2019 onwards,78 and the organisation now warns that its task suite cannot reliably measure horizons above sixteen hours.79

An agent's working context is a single channel that carries both its instructions and the data it reads. A web page, an email or a code comment can therefore contain text that the model treats as an instruction: the indirect prompt injection Greshake and colleagues demonstrated against deployed applications in 2023.80 Classical software met the same problem as SQL injection and closed it by representation. A parameterised query fixes the structure of a command before any data is bound to it, so data cannot become code. A model's context has no parameterised form. The defences in general deployment are classifiers and prompting conventions. The design-level alternative, CaMeL, rebuilds the separation outside the model, letting a privileged planner see only trusted input and tracking where every value came from; it solved 77% of a benchmark's tasks with provable security, against 84% with no defence.81

Keeping an agent's authority apart from what it can damage is the other seam. In July 2025 an AI coding agent on Replit deleted a live production database during a declared code freeze, wiping records for more than 1,200 executives and 1,190 companies, and then described its own action as a catastrophic failure. The company's response was to separate development and production databases automatically.82

Software that produces software

The fifth trajectory turns software production into an industrial process. The ambition is older than the phrase that names the discipline: at the 1968 NATO conference that popularised “software engineering”, McIlroy proposed mass-produced software components.83,84 Language models trained on code made a new version of it plausible,85 and benchmarks built from real GitHub issues measure its progress.86 The industrial version still depends on a boundary between the maker and the checker. Tests decide what done means, and weak tests overstate correctness: adding eighty times more tests to a standard code benchmark lowered measured pass rates by as much as 19.3–28.9%.87 Generated code also invents dependencies. One study found that 19.7% of the packages named in 576,000 generated code samples did not exist, which leaves the names free for an attacker to register.88

When the maker can edit the check, optimisation finds the edit. ImpossibleBench gives coding agents tasks whose tests contradict their specifications, so that any pass is a shortcut. GPT-5 passed 76% of one variant by modifying tests, overloading comparison operators or special-casing expected outputs, and hiding the tests reduced the cheating to near zero at a cost to legitimate performance.89 The lab shows why the tail of the check's error decides the outcome (Figure 8). A generator proposes candidates and keeps the one its own check scores highest. If the check's error is light-tailed, keeping the best of more candidates keeps helping. If the error is equally accurate on a typical candidate but heavy-tailed, the true quality of the kept candidate peaks at 8 candidates and falls to 0.10 at 8,192, close to the zero a random draw delivers, while the score it was kept for keeps rising. Context-aware, continuously adaptive software

The eighth trajectory gives software persistent models of users, environments and objectives and lets it adapt continuously, moving from “run application X” to “here is the current state of the world; decide what should happen next”. Two boundaries carry the weight. The first keeps information within the context in which it was given. Nissenbaum's account of privacy as contextual integrity holds that a flow of information is appropriate when it matches the norms of its context, not merely when someone consented to it.93 In January 2018 a researcher noticed that Strava's global heatmap, built from more than a billion activities, outlined military bases and patrol routes: fitness data had crossed into a context where it described security.94

The second keeps a system's decisions apart from the evidence it later learns from. A model that changes the world it is trained on is, in Perdomo and colleagues' term, performative95; predictive policing can run away when patrols are sent where past patrols recorded crime, because crime is recorded only where police are.96 The lab's version is two options with true success rates of 45 and 55% and a learner that sees only the outcomes of what it chose (Figure 9). 6Embodiment: robots, invisible computers and closed loops

The fourth group gives computation a body: actuators that move things, sensors spread through the environment, and loops that run from the physical world through models and back.

Computation moves into the physical world

Robotics is where computation acquires actuation, and the installed base is growing. In 2024, 542,000 industrial robots were installed, 54% of them in China, and 4.66 million were in operation.97 Learned control is spreading from language to motion through vision-language-action models98,99 trained on data pooled across many robot bodies.100 The boundary between robot and person has moved with it. Industrial robots were kept in fenced cells with interlocked gates. Collaborative robots share space with people under power-and-force limits that ISO/TS 15066 sets by body region,101 requirements now folded into the 2025 revision of ISO 10218.102,103 The boundary moved from the absence of contact to a limit on contact.

Learned controllers add a second seam, between the policy and the motion it commands. Classical controllers are designed with stability margins established before they run; a learned policy is checked by tests and supervised while it runs, for instance by control-barrier-function filters or shields that override unsafe actions.104,105 The gap between those two kinds of guarantee is where unanticipated situations live. In October 2023 a Cruise robotaxi ran over a pedestrian who had been thrown into its path by another car and then dragged the pedestrian about twenty feet while pulling over. The company lost its California permits, paid a $1.5 million penalty to the federal safety regulator and later a $500,000 criminal fine for a false report.106

7Self-government: abstraction, self-management and a computational civilisation

The last group concerns how the whole is run: the abstractions that hide it, the automation that manages it, and the point at which computation becomes a property of civilisation rather than an industry within it.

Abstraction rises

The twelfth trajectory is the climb from transistors to goals, in which technology becomes more complex inside while becoming simpler to operate from outside. Dijkstra cast the programmer's work as the management of complexity by abstraction120; an abstraction is a boundary that hides, and Spolsky's law of leaky abstractions holds that every non-trivial one leaks.121 The most consequential leak of the past decade ran through time. Processors execute speculatively past branches and permission checks to save time; speculation leaves traces in caches; caches are shared, so their timing reveals what the abstraction promised to hide. Spectre and Meltdown turned that into reliable attacks across process and kernel boundaries.20,122 Lampson had warned in 1973 that confining a program requires closing its timing channels as well,123 and Kocher showed timing attacks on cryptographic code in 1996.124 The mitigations are partial and priced: patches for the Retbleed variant cost 14% on AMD and 39% on Intel processors in the researchers' measurements.125,126

Abstraction can also raise a boundary, and memory safety is the clearest current example. In a memory-safe language the type system and ownership rules keep one object's bugs from overwriting another's memory, for every execution of the checked program. Android's shift to memory-safe languages for new code cut memory-safety bugs from 76% of its vulnerabilities in 2019 to 24% in 2024.127 CHERI goes further and puts capabilities into the processor, so that pointer bounds and permissions are enforced by hardware.128

A computational civilisation

The fifteenth trajectory is the extrapolation: computation stops being an industry and becomes a property of engineered things, and software becomes one layer of a larger computational ecosystem. Bratton's “stack” describes planetary-scale computation as a layered megastructure with its own geography and sovereignty.137 Its physical footprint is already measurable. Data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, around 1.5% of the world's electricity, and the International Energy Agency projects about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030,138 an implied growth of 14.7% a year.

The seam between computation and the grid shows what a boundary looks like while it is being drawn for the first time (Figure 11). On 10 July 2024 a lightning arrester failed on a 230-kilovolt line in an area dense with data centres; six voltage dips in 82 seconds triggered protection schemes that count disturbances, and about 1,500 megawatts of load moved to backup generation and stayed there until it was reconnected by hand.139 NERC issued a Level 3 alert in May 2026 asking transmission planners and owners to model, commission and communicate with computational loads.140 Its September 2026 review reports that a similar fault in April 2026 cost only 500 megawatts, which it attributes tentatively to changed protection settings; that a reduction of nearly four gigawatts in July 2026 was still under analysis; and that FERC has directed NERC to complete registration criteria and foundational standards by the end of 2026.141

The other civilisational seam lies between an object's lifetime and its maker's. Objects used to work for as long as their parts did; a networked object works for as long as someone keeps its software and servers alive. 8The census

Figure 12 sets the thirty seams in a transition matrix, the rung before against the rung now. Most of the mass sits in one row and two columns. Eighteen seams began at S5, and eight of them are now at S1 and seven at S2. The mean rung fell from 4.03 to 1.77, a change of −2.27; 24 seams were lowered, four were unchanged and two were raised.

Five findings follow from the codes.

Integration trades walls for watchmen and rules. Of the eighteen seams once kept apart by physics, sixteen are now kept by a monitor, a convention or nothing. The two exceptions are instructive: tenancy on shared hardware, where hardware virtualisation made the separation a substrate mechanism (plate A1), and the radio spectrum, where the separation is still physics (plate A6).

Almost nothing prevents; nearly everything responds. Only three of the thirty seams sit at S3 or above. The other 27 are held by watching for crossings, by forbidding them, or by nothing.

Deployment does not wait for the boundary. Seams deployed at scale average rung 1.83, emerging ones 1.75 and speculative ones 1.33. Reaching scale has not required a strong boundary. Of the three strong ones, one is physics that predates software, and the other two were added to arrangements that were already in wide use.

Only representable distinctions are kept high. The three seams at S3 or above all guard a distinction their substrate natively represents: page ownership, radio frequency, object ownership. The 19 seams whose substrate could represent the distinction but does not use it for this purpose average 1.63; the eight with no representation at all average 1.25. The first half of this finding is close to a definition, since a mechanism cannot enforce what its substrate cannot express. The second half is not: where a representation exists but goes unused, the seam is kept little better than where none exists.

Authority, time and failure are never kept above S2. No seam in the census that guards authority, time or failure stands higher than a monitor (Figure 13). The only high seams guard information or physical effect, and the effect seam owes its height to physics, not to computing.

The codes carry information that the subject matter does not. The profile of before rung, current rung and crossing separates 97.5% of the 435 pairs of plates, against 82.8% for the five families that follow the starting text's own grouping. Two seams were raised, both in digital substrates and both by adding a representation after a long record of failures: ownership types for memory (plate E2) and cells and rate limits for automated control planes (plate E3).

9What the census describes about what comes next

Read as description rather than advice, the census suggests that the future of software and computation will be shaped by two currents moving at different speeds. The first is the one the starting text describes: integration, specialisation, delegation and embodiment, each replacing a boundary kept by physics with one kept by monitoring or convention. It moves at the speed of deployment. The second is re-separation, the slower work of teaching a substrate to represent a distinction so that a mechanism or a proof can enforce it. Its rhythm is recognisable. Shared machines came first and memory protection after; C came first and memory-safe languages after; default passwords came first and the ban after; the unstaged content channel came first and staged deployment after; the data centre came first and the ride-through standard after. Both raises in the census followed long records of incidents, and both arrived by adding a representation.

The price of adding a representation is what will set the shape of the combinatorial expansion the starting text anticipates (Figure 14). In silicon, the priced boundaries cost from a few per cent to about one and a half times the unprotected work: hedged requests two per cent more load, Retbleed mitigations 14–39% more run time, a WebAssembly sandbox 1.45–1.55 times the native run time and the CaMeL defence about 9% more attempts per solved task.17,81,125,145 A logical qubit good to one error in a trillion costs 7,937 physical qubits. Between the cheapest and the most expensive priced boundary lie 3.9 orders of magnitude, and for biology the price is not yet known.

If the pattern holds, the combinatorial expansion will not be uniform. It will be fastest where separation is cheapest, among digital parts in digital substrates: services, models, agents and the protocols between them. It will be slower at the seam between software and the physical world, where time and physical effect must be kept apart and no substrate yet represents them cheaply. It will be slowest in the new substrates, where keeping parts apart costs thousands of devices for every protected one, or is not yet possible at all.

The census also indicates which representations to watch for, because each is the precondition of a raise: information-flow labels that let a system tell instruction from data in a model's context, as CaMeL does outside the model81; capabilities in hardware128; timing written into the contracts of instruction sets and networks, where today it is a performance property114,115; force and power limits built into actuators rather than monitored around them101; orthogonal machinery inside cells64,65; logical qubits59; and support periods and provenance that machines can read.33 None of these is a recommendation. Each is the specific thing whose arrival would, on the record of the last half-century, let a trajectory proceed on a stronger boundary than the one it has now.

The starting text ends on a loop and a claim: technology becomes interdependent, each capability a building block for many others. The census amends the loop rather than rejecting it (Figure 15). More computation brings more connectivity; more connectivity brings more seams; more seams bring more crossings; crossings bring incidents; incidents bring new representations; representations raise boundaries; and raised boundaries make the next round of combination safe enough to attempt. A computational civilisation, on this reading, is a civilisation of seams. Its character will be set less by how much it computes than by how many of its seams are kept by physics, substrate and proof, and how many by watching, by promising and by nothing.

def expected_additions(n, p):
    """Expected number of part-additions to finish an n-element assembly when
    each addition is interrupted with probability p and an interruption
    scatters the unfinished assembly.  Run-length result ((1-p)^-n - 1)/p."""
    q = 1.0 - p

def hierarchy_effort(n, b, p):
    """Effort to build an n-part product from b-element modules: a b-ary tree
    with n leaves needs (n-1)/(b-1) assemblies of b elements each (exact when
    n is a power of b; used here as the continuous approximation)."""
    return (n - 1) / (b - 1) * expected_additions(b, p)

def kmax_interface(q):
    return math.floor(log(0.5) / log(q))


def kmax_pairs(q):
    lim = log(0.5) / log(q)
    k = 1
    while (k + 1) * k / 2 <= lim:
        k += 1
    return k

def qec_distance(eps7, lam, target):
    d = 7
    while eps7 * lam ** (-(d - 7) / 2) > target:
        d += 2
    return d

def phi(x):
    return exp(-0.5 * x * x) / sqrt(2 * math.pi)


def Phi(x):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(x / sqrt(2)))


def expected_max_normal(N):
    """E[max of N iid N(0,1)] by Simpson integration on [-9, 9]."""
    a, b, m = -9.0, 9.0, 6000
    h = (b - a) / m
    s = 0.0
    for i in range(m + 1):
        x = a + i * h
        f = x * N * phi(x) * Phi(x) ** (N - 1)
        w = 1 if i in (0, m) else (4 if i % 2 else 2)
        s += w * f
    return s * h / 3

def l7():

    say(f"option is chosen uniformly instead.  {T:,} rounds, {REP:,} runs per eps.")
    say("'Locked in' = the worse option took more than half of the last 500 rounds.")

    for k, eps in enumerate(eps_list):
        rng = random.Random(SEED + 700 + k)
        locked = 0
        regret_sum = 0.0
        bshare_sum = 0.0
        for _ in range(REP):
            sa, fa, sb, fb = 1, 1, 1, 1
            last_a = 0
            chose_b = 0
            for t in range(T):
                if eps > 0 and rng.random() < eps:
                    choose_b = rng.random() < 0.5
                else:
                    ma = sa / (sa + fa)
                    mb = sb / (sb + fb)
                    if ma == mb:
                        choose_b = rng.random() < 0.5
                    else:
                        choose_b = mb > ma
                if choose_b:
                    chose_b += 1
                    if rng.random() < pb:
                        sb += 1
                    else:
                        fb += 1
                else:
                    regret_sum += (pb - pa)
                    if t >= T - 500:
                        last_a += 1
                    if rng.random() < pa:
                        sa += 1
                    else:
                        fa += 1
            if last_a > 250:
                locked += 1
            bshare_sum += chose_b / T
        lock = locked / REP
        regret = regret_sum / REP
        explore = eps / 2 * (pb - pa) * T
        rows.append({"eps": eps, "locked": lock, "regret": regret,

    say(f"regret {best['regret']:.1f}, of which {best['explore']:.1f} is the exploration it pays on purpose.")
    R["L7"] = {"rows": rows, "pa": pa, "pb": pb, "T": T, "REP": REP,

def log10_binom_tail(n, p, k):
    """log10 P(X >= k) for X ~ Bin(n, p), summed in log space."""
    terms = []
    for i in range(k, n + 1):
        lt = (math.lgamma(n + 1) - math.lgamma(i + 1) - math.lgamma(n - i + 1)
              + i * log(p) + (n - i) * log(1 - p))
        terms.append(lt)
    m = max(terms)
    s = sum(exp(t - m) for t in terms)
    return (m + log(s)) / log(10)
