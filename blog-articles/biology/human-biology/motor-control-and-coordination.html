Motor Control and Coordination

How the nervous system turns intention into force: from motor units and spinal reflexes to cortex, basal ganglia and cerebellum, and what fails in movement disorders.

Technical review article · Citations in Vancouver style · Last updated 27 October 2026

Abstract

Every voluntary movement is produced by graded contraction of skeletal muscle, which is commanded exclusively by spinal and brainstem motor neurons, the "final common path" of the motor system [1,2]. Above that final path sits a distributed architecture: spinal interneuronal circuits that generate reflexes and rhythms [3,4], brainstem and cortical descending pathways [5,6], and two large subcortical loops, through the basal ganglia and the cerebellum, that shape which actions are selected and how accurately they unfold [7,8]. This review traces the history of the field, then works upward from the motor unit to the cortex, describes the computational principles proposed to explain coordination and motor learning, and closes with the clinical disorders that reveal how each level contributes.

1The problem of motor control

Moving the body is computationally hard. The human arm has more joints, and far more muscles, than are needed to place the hand at a point in space, so any given goal can be reached in an infinite number of ways. Nikolai Bernstein framed this as the degrees-of-freedom problem: the nervous system must master redundant degrees of freedom and turn them into a controllable system [9]. Two further facts make the task harder. Motor commands are noisy, and the noise grows with the size of the command [10]. Sensory feedback is delayed by tens of milliseconds, so a controller that relied only on feedback would be slow and unstable; the shortest stretch-reflex responses in human arm muscles appear only about 20 to 50 ms after a perturbation, and responses that depend on cortical processing take longer [11].

The solution the nervous system has evolved is hierarchical but not strictly top-down. Spinal circuits handle fast, local corrections and rhythmic patterns; brainstem pathways stabilise posture; motor cortex specifies skilled, fractionated movements; and the basal ganglia and cerebellum, which have no direct output to motor neurons, modulate those descending signals through loops with the thalamus and cortex [2,12]. 2A short history

The modern study of movement began with the discovery that muscle is electrically excitable. Luigi Galvani reported in 1791 that electrical stimulation of nerve made frog muscle contract [13]. In 1822 François Magendie showed that the ventral spinal roots carry motor and the dorsal roots sensory fibres, separating the efferent and afferent limbs of the reflex [14]. Two years later Pierre Flourens found that removing the cerebellum in birds left movement possible but badly coordinated [15], the first clear evidence that coordination is a separable function.

The idea that the cerebral cortex itself controls movement dates to 1870, when Gustav Fritsch and Eduard Hitzig evoked contralateral limb movements by electrically stimulating the frontal cortex of dogs [16]. David Ferrier extended the mapping to monkeys [17], and Korbinian Brodmann's cytoarchitectonic map identified the agranular areas 4 and 6 that we now call primary motor and premotor cortex [18]. Wilder Penfield and Edwin Boldrey later used stimulation during neurosurgery to chart the somatotopic map in humans, the origin of the motor homunculus [19].

Charles Sherrington supplied the conceptual framework for the spinal cord. He described reciprocal innervation of antagonist muscles [20], characterised flexion and crossed-extension reflexes [21], and in The Integrative Action of the Nervous System introduced the synapse as a functional concept and the motor neuron as the "final common path" [1]. With Edward Liddell he defined the stretch reflex [22] and the motor unit [23]. Thomas Graham Brown, Sherrington's contemporary, showed that the spinal cord can generate stepping without sensory input [24]. Edgar Adrian and Detlev Bronk recorded single motor axons and showed that muscle force is graded partly by the firing frequency of motor neurons [25].

The second half of the twentieth century brought single-neuron recording in behaving animals. Edward Evarts related pyramidal tract neuron discharge to the force of wrist movements [26], and Apostolos Georgopoulos showed that direction of reaching is encoded by broadly tuned populations of motor cortical neurons [27,28]. Clinical neurology contributed in parallel: Parkinson's essay of 1817 [29], Huntington's description of hereditary chorea in 1872 [30], Gordon Holmes's analysis of cerebellar war wounds [31], and the discovery of striatal dopamine loss in Parkinson's disease [32] each tied a specific movement disorder to a specific structure.

3Muscle and the motor unit

Contraction

Skeletal muscle generates force when myosin cross-bridges pull actin filaments past them, shortening each sarcomere without either filament changing length. This sliding-filament mechanism was proposed simultaneously in 1954 by Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke and by Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson [33,34]. Because force depends on the overlap of thick and thin filaments, isometric tension varies with sarcomere length and peaks over a narrow plateau of optimal overlap [42]. The length–tension and force–velocity properties of muscle mean that identical neural commands produce different forces in different postures, a complication the nervous system has to take into account.

Neuromuscular transmission

Each muscle fibre is driven by a single motor neuron through the neuromuscular junction, where the transmitter is acetylcholine [43]. Fatt and Katz observed spontaneous miniature end-plate potentials and recognised that transmitter is released in discrete packets, or quanta [44]. Under normal conditions the junction has a large safety factor, so every motor neuron action potential produces a muscle action potential; diseases that erode this margin, such as myasthenia gravis, produce fatigable weakness [45].

The motor unit and recruitment

A motor neuron and all the muscle fibres it innervates form a motor unit, the smallest unit of force the nervous system can command [23,46]. The number of fibres per unit varies widely: muscles used for fine control have small units, whereas large limb muscles have units containing many hundreds of fibres or more [2,47]. Motor units differ in contractile speed and fatigue resistance. In the cat gastrocnemius, Burke and colleagues classified units as slow (S), fast fatigue-resistant (FR) and fast fatigable (FF) [48], and these classes correspond broadly to fibre types defined by myosin heavy chain isoforms [49].

Force is graded by two mechanisms. The first is recruitment. Elwood Henneman showed that motor neurons are recruited in order of increasing size, because small motor neurons are more excitable; this size principle means that small, slow, fatigue-resistant units are active in weak contractions and large, fast, fatigable units are added only when more force is needed [35,46]. The second is rate coding: once recruited, a motor neuron increases its discharge rate as force rises, which summates successive twitches into a fused contraction [25,50]. The relative contribution of recruitment and rate coding differs between muscles [50]. The size principle is an elegant example of a control problem solved by biophysics rather than by explicit computation: the brain can send a single graded excitatory drive to a motor neuron pool, and an orderly recruitment sequence follows automatically.

4Proprioception and spinal reflexes

Muscle receptors

Muscles carry their own sensors. Muscle spindles lie in parallel with ordinary (extrafusal) fibres and signal muscle length and its rate of change through group Ia and group II afferents; Golgi tendon organs lie in series with the muscle and signal active force through group Ib afferents [51,52]. Spindles have their own motor supply, the small gamma (fusimotor) motor neurons, identified by Kuffler, Hunt and Quilliam [53]. Gamma drive keeps the spindle taut as the muscle shortens. Microneurographic recordings in humans showed that during voluntary contraction fusimotor and skeletomotor neurons are activated together, the so-called alpha–gamma coactivation [54]. These receptors, together with joint and skin receptors, give rise to the sense of limb position and movement [52].

The stretch reflex and reciprocal inhibition

Stretching a muscle excites its spindles, and Ia afferents make monosynaptic excitatory connections onto motor neurons of the same muscle, producing a reflex contraction that resists the stretch [2,22]. The tendon jerk tested in clinical examination is the phasic form of this reflex. Ia afferents also excite inhibitory interneurons that suppress motor neurons of the antagonist muscle, so that the antagonist relaxes as the agonist contracts [2,20] (Figure 2). Motor neurons in turn excite Renshaw cells through axon collaterals, and Renshaw cells feed back inhibition onto motor neurons [55]; Eccles and colleagues showed that this synapse is cholinergic [56].

Polysynaptic reflexes

A painful stimulus to the foot evokes the flexion-withdrawal reflex, in which flexors of the stimulated limb contract, and in standing animals it is accompanied by a crossed-extension reflex that stiffens the opposite limb to bear weight [21]. These responses are polysynaptic, involve interneurons spanning several segments, and are strongly modulated by descending pathways and by the phase of ongoing movement [2]. Reflexes are therefore not fixed reactions but building blocks that the spinal cord assembles flexibly.

Feedback beyond the spinal cord

Stretching a muscle during a task produces a sequence of responses: a short-latency component mediated by spinal circuits and a long-latency component, roughly 50 to 100 ms after the perturbation, that involves transcortical pathways and is adjusted to the behavioural goal [11]. The importance of proprioception is clearest when it is lost. A patient with a severe large-fibre sensory neuropathy could still generate rapid movements of the correct direction but performed poorly on fine manual tasks without vision [57], and deafferented patients fail to compensate for the interaction torques that one moving joint exerts on another, producing distorted multijoint movements [58].

5Central pattern generators and locomotion

Rhythmic movements such as walking, breathing, chewing and swimming are produced by central pattern generators (CPGs), networks that can produce patterned motor output without patterned sensory input or descending command [4,59]. Graham Brown observed alternating flexor and extensor activity in the hindlimbs of spinal, deafferented cats [24] and proposed that each limb is driven by two mutually inhibiting "half-centres", one for flexors and one for extensors [60]. Grillner and Zangger later confirmed that a locomotor pattern persists in the spinal cat after deafferentation [61].

Molecular genetics in mice has since dissected the mammalian locomotor CPG into identifiable classes of spinal interneurons. Distinct populations coordinate left–right alternation, flexor–extensor alternation and rhythm generation, and the speed of locomotion is linked to the recruitment of different interneuron classes [3]. Locomotion is initiated and its speed set by supraspinal centres. Electrical stimulation of the mesencephalic locomotor region in decerebrate cats produced walking, trotting and galloping as stimulus strength increased [62], and current work maps the brainstem circuits that connect these command regions to the spinal cord [63].

Whether humans have a spinal CPG was long debated because human locomotion depends more heavily on descending control. Epidural stimulation of the lumbar cord in people with clinically complete spinal cord injury evoked rhythmic, step-like leg muscle activity, which is evidence that the human cord retains locomotor circuitry [64]. This circuitry is the target of neuroprosthetic therapies discussed in Section 11.

6Motor cortex and descending pathways

Maps and their limits

Primary motor cortex (M1, Brodmann area 4) lies in the precentral gyrus, with premotor cortex and the supplementary motor area (SMA) in area 6 anterior to it [2,18]. Stimulation mapping in humans revealed an orderly but overlapping representation of the body, with disproportionate territory for the hand and face [19]. The homunculus is a simplification. Long trains of stimulation in monkey precentral cortex evoke coordinated, multijoint movements that bring the hand to particular locations in space, suggesting that the cortex maps actions as well as muscles [65]. Precision functional imaging in humans has shown that the foot, hand and mouth regions of M1 are separated by "inter-effector" regions that belong to a distinct somato-cognitive action network connected to brain systems for action planning and arousal [41].

What motor cortical neurons encode

Evarts found that the discharge of pyramidal tract neurons in monkeys relates to the force required for a movement more than to the displacement achieved [26]. Georgopoulos and colleagues showed that individual M1 neurons are broadly tuned to the direction of reaching and that a vector sum of a population's activity predicts the direction of the movement [27,28]. Debate about whether M1 represents muscles, forces or kinematics has partly given way to a dynamical-systems view, in which preparatory activity sets the initial state of a population whose evolving dynamics generate the motor command; during reaching, population activity shows strong rotational structure [66].

Premotor and supplementary areas

Activity in the human brain begins well before a self-initiated movement. Kornhuber and Deecke recorded a slowly rising scalp potential, the Bereitschaftspotential or readiness potential, preceding voluntary finger movements [67]. Blood-flow studies showed that the SMA is engaged when people plan or mentally rehearse sequences of finger movements, even without executing them [68]. Premotor areas are not merely upstream of M1: each projects directly to the spinal cord as well [69]. In ventral premotor cortex of the macaque, "mirror neurons" discharge both when the monkey performs a goal-directed hand action and when it observes another individual performing a similar action [70].

Descending pathways

Descending motor pathways fall into two broad groups [5,6]. Medial brainstem pathways (reticulospinal, vestibulospinal and tectospinal) terminate on interneurons controlling axial and proximal muscles and support posture, balance and orienting. Lateral pathways, the corticospinal and rubrospinal tracts, control distal limb muscles and skilled movement. The human corticospinal tract contains roughly a million fibres in each medullary pyramid [71], and most of them cross at the pyramidal decussation, although the proportion varies between individuals [72].

In primates some corticospinal neurons synapse directly onto motor neurons. These cortico-motoneuronal cells produce post-spike facilitation of hand and forearm muscles [73] and are concentrated in a caudal subdivision of M1 buried in the central sulcus [74]. Their functional importance is shown by lesions: after bilateral section of the pyramids, monkeys recovered the ability to walk, climb and reach but permanently lost the capacity for independent finger movements [75]. The direct cortico-motoneuronal system is a recent evolutionary specialisation that is most developed in species with dexterous hands, including humans [5].

7Basal ganglia

The basal ganglia comprise the striatum (caudate and putamen), the external and internal segments of the globus pallidus (GPe, GPi), the subthalamic nucleus (STN) and the substantia nigra, with its dopaminergic pars compacta (SNc) and GABAergic pars reticulata (SNr). They receive input from almost the whole cerebral cortex and send output, via the thalamus, back to frontal cortex. Alexander, DeLong and Strick proposed that these connections form parallel, functionally segregated loops, including a "motor" loop centred on the putamen and supplementary motor area, alongside oculomotor, prefrontal and limbic loops [7].

Within the motor loop, the influential "rate model" distinguishes two routes from striatum to output [38,76]. In the direct pathway, striatal neurons expressing D1 dopamine receptors inhibit GPi/SNr, releasing the thalamus from tonic inhibition and facilitating movement. In the indirect pathway, striatal neurons expressing D2 receptors inhibit GPe, which disinhibits the STN, which in turn excites GPi/SNr and suppresses movement [77]. Dopamine excites the direct pathway through D1 receptors and inhibits the indirect pathway through D2 receptors, so loss of dopamine tilts the balance towards suppression [38,77]. Optogenetic experiments in mice provided direct support: activating indirect-pathway neurons produced parkinsonian freezing and slowness, and activating direct-pathway neurons restored locomotion in a dopamine-depleted mouse model [78]. A third, hyperdirect pathway from cortex to STN can rapidly suppress movement before the other two take effect [79].

What the basal ganglia compute is still debated. One influential view is that they select among competing motor programs, facilitating the desired action while inhibiting competitors [80]. Dopamine neurons add a learning signal: in monkeys their phasic firing behaves like a reward prediction error, increasing for unexpected rewards and pausing when an expected reward is omitted [81]. Through this teaching signal, cortico-striatal circuits contribute to reinforcement learning and to the gradual conversion of goal-directed actions into habits [82]. The rate model explains the opposite signs of hypokinetic and hyperkinetic disorders well, but it does not account for every clinical observation, such as the benefit of lesioning or stimulating an already abnormal circuit, which is one reason the field has moved towards models that emphasise abnormal firing patterns as well as firing rates [76,83].

8Cerebellum and coordination

The cerebellum contains about 69 billion of the brain's roughly 86 billion neurons, most of them granule cells [84]. It does not initiate movement, but its damage produces characteristic deficits. From soldiers with cerebellar gunshot wounds, Gordon Holmes described hypotonia, weakness, errors in the range and direction of movement, intention tremor, and the breakdown of smooth multijoint movements into their components [31]. These signs, often summarised as ataxia, dysmetria and dysdiadochokinesia, indicate that the cerebellum is needed for movements that are accurately scaled, timed and coordinated across joints [85].

Circuit

The cerebellar cortex has a strikingly regular, repeated circuit [86] (Figure 4). Mossy fibres from the pons, spinal cord and vestibular nuclei excite granule cells, whose axons ascend to the molecular layer and bifurcate into parallel fibres that each contact many Purkinje cells. Each Purkinje cell also receives a single climbing fibre from the inferior olive, which makes powerful excitatory contact along the dendrite. Purkinje cells, the sole output of the cerebellar cortex, are GABAergic and inhibit neurons of the deep cerebellar and vestibular nuclei, which in turn project to the thalamus, red nucleus and brainstem [2,86].

Learning and internal models

The regularity of this circuit inspired theories of cerebellar learning. David Marr and James Albus independently proposed that climbing fibre input acts as a teaching signal that changes the strength of the parallel fibre synapses active at the same time [36,37]. Ito and Kano then demonstrated long-term depression of parallel fibre to Purkinje cell transmission after conjunctive stimulation of parallel and climbing fibres [87]. In monkeys adapting to a changed load, complex spikes (the Purkinje cell response to climbing fibre input) increased during adaptation, followed by changes in simple-spike responses [88].

A widely accepted computational interpretation is that the cerebellum learns internal models of the body and environment: forward models that predict the sensory consequences of motor commands, and inverse models that compute commands needed for a desired movement [8]. Prediction lets the motor system act on where the limb will be rather than where delayed feedback says it was. Patients with lesions of the olivocerebellar system fail to adapt their throwing when wearing prism goggles that displace the visual field [89], consistent with a role in error-driven recalibration. The cerebellum has also been implicated in the precise timing of movements and perceptual intervals [90], and lesions of the posterior lobe can produce a cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome with impairments in executive function, spatial cognition, language and affect [91].

9Principles of coordination

Synergies and redundancy

Bernstein's redundancy problem [9] has prompted several proposals. One is that the nervous system reduces dimensionality by activating muscles in fixed groupings, or muscle synergies, that can be combined to build diverse movements; synergy-like modules have been identified in spinal circuits of frogs and in the muscle activity of several species [92]. A complementary approach examines how variability is structured. The uncontrolled manifold analysis showed that, in tasks such as standing up from a chair, joint configurations vary much more in directions that do not affect the task variable than in directions that do [93]. Variability is therefore not eliminated but channelled.

Coordination dynamics

When people move both index fingers rhythmically in an anti-phase pattern and gradually increase frequency, they switch involuntarily to an in-phase pattern beyond a critical frequency [94]. The Haken–Kelso–Bunz model describes this transition as a change in the stability of a coupled nonlinear system, much like a phase transition in physics [95]. Coordination in this view emerges from the dynamics of coupled components, not from a central program specifying each joint.

Equilibrium points and planned trajectories

The equilibrium-point hypothesis proposes that the brain specifies a threshold or equilibrium position for the muscles, and the limb's mechanical and reflex properties carry it there [96]. Experiments in deafferented monkeys, whose arms still reached the intended position after unexpected perturbations, were taken as support for a related view of posture and trajectory control [97]. Point-to-point arm movements tend to follow roughly straight hand paths with smooth, bell-shaped velocity profiles, which Flash and Hogan reproduced by assuming that the brain minimises the rate of change of acceleration ("jerk") [98]. Harris and Wolpert offered a more biological account: if motor noise grows with the size of the command, smooth movements are those that minimise endpoint variability [10].

Speed and accuracy

Robert Woodworth showed at the end of the nineteenth century that rapid movements are less accurate and that aimed movements contain an initial impulse followed by visually guided corrections [99]. Paul Fitts quantified the trade-off. For aimed movements of amplitude D to a target of width W, movement time MT increases linearly with an index of difficulty [100]:

MT = a + b · log2(2D / W)

where a and b are empirical constants. Fitts' law is one of the most robust quantitative regularities in human movement and is still used in the design of human–computer interfaces.

Optimal feedback control

Optimal feedback control theory unifies many of these ideas [39,101]. It treats movement as the outcome of a feedback controller that is continuously optimised for the task goal, with costs for error and effort. A key prediction is the minimal intervention principle: the controller corrects deviations only when they interfere with the goal, which explains why variability accumulates in task-irrelevant directions [39] and why long-latency stretch responses are tuned to task demands [11]. The theory requires state estimation, and so it connects naturally with cerebellar forward models [8].

Efference copy and posture

To distinguish self-generated sensory input from external events, the nervous system uses a copy of its own motor commands, an idea formalised as the reafference principle [102]. One everyday consequence is that people cannot tickle themselves effectively: self-produced tactile stimuli are perceived as less intense than identical external stimuli, because their consequences are predicted [103]. Prediction also governs posture. Before a person raises an arm while standing, muscles of the legs and trunk are activated in advance to counteract the coming perturbation; these anticipatory postural adjustments were described in the 1960s [104,105]. Responses to unexpected loss of balance are organised as a small repertoire of strategies, such as ankle and hip strategies, selected according to the support conditions [106].

10Motor learning

Fitts and Posner described skill acquisition as progressing from a cognitive stage, in which the learner works out what to do, through an associative stage, to an autonomous stage in which performance is fast, consistent and requires little attention [107]. Modern work distinguishes several processes, including error-based adaptation, reinforcement learning, use-dependent learning and the acquisition of new skills, each with partly distinct neural substrates [108].

Adaptation

Adaptation restores performance after a perturbation by recalibrating an existing behaviour. Shadmehr and Mussa-Ivaldi had people reach while holding a robot that applied velocity-dependent forces. Subjects gradually regained straight movements, and when the forces were removed they showed after-effects in the mirror-image direction, indicating that the brain had learned an internal model of the new dynamics [109]. Brashers-Krug, Shadmehr and Bizzi reported that such learning became resistant to interference from a second, opposing field after a few hours [110], but a later multi-laboratory study failed to reproduce this consolidation effect [111], a reminder that even well-known findings need replication.

Skill and memory systems

Motor skill memory can be separated from conscious memory. The amnesic patient H.M., unable to form new declarative memories after bilateral medial temporal lobe resection, nevertheless improved on motor tasks such as rotary pursuit across sessions [112]. In the serial reaction time task, people become faster when stimuli follow a repeating sequence, often without being able to report it [113]. Practising a finger sequence over several weeks expands its representation in M1 as measured by fMRI [114]. Imaging studies indicate that cortico-striatal and cortico-cerebellar systems contribute differently, with the cerebellum particularly involved in adaptation and the striatum in the long-term retention of sequences [115,116].

Learning after injury

Recovery after brain injury relies on the same plasticity. In squirrel monkeys with small infarcts in the hand area of M1, rehabilitative training prevented the further loss of hand territory in adjacent undamaged cortex that occurred without training, and in some cases the hand representation expanded [117]. In stroke survivors, the clearest evidence of benefit comes from therapies that use high-intensity, repetitive, task-specific practice [118].

11Disorders of motor control

Clinical neurology classifies motor disorders largely by the level of the system affected. The most fundamental distinction is between lesions of upper motor neurons, in the cortex and descending tracts, and of lower motor neurons in the brainstem and spinal cord [2,12].

Upper motor neuron syndromes

Spasticity is defined as a velocity-dependent increase in tonic stretch reflexes with exaggerated tendon jerks, resulting from hyperexcitability of the stretch reflex as one component of the upper motor neuron syndrome [119]. Stroke is the commonest cause of acquired upper motor neuron weakness in adults, and loss of functional movement is a frequent consequence [118]. Cerebral palsy describes a group of permanent disorders of movement and posture, attributed to non-progressive disturbances of the developing fetal or infant brain [120].

Motor neuron, junction and spinal disorders

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive degeneration of both upper and lower motor neurons, producing a combination of spasticity, weakness, atrophy and fasciculations; most cases are sporadic, and an expanded hexanucleotide repeat in C9orf72 is the most common known genetic cause [121]. Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune disease in which antibodies, most often against the acetylcholine receptor, impair neuromuscular transmission and cause fatigable weakness [45]. After spinal cord injury, the circuits below the lesion survive but are disconnected from the brain. Targeted epidural electrical stimulation, timed to the phases of gait and combined with rehabilitation, restored voluntary control of walking in people with chronic incomplete injuries [122], and a wireless "digital bridge" that links decoded cortical signals to spinal stimulation allowed a person with chronic tetraplegia to stand and walk more naturally [123].

Basal ganglia disorders

James Parkinson's 1817 essay described the "shaking palsy" [29]. Parkinson's disease is now defined clinically by parkinsonism, meaning bradykinesia together with rest tremor, rigidity or both [124]. Its pathological hallmarks are loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta and intracellular aggregates of α-synuclein (Lewy bodies), and it has prominent non-motor features [83]. An estimated 6.1 million people had Parkinson's disease worldwide in 2016 [125]. The discovery of striatal dopamine depletion [32] led directly to dopamine replacement, first with intravenous levodopa [126] and then with oral treatment at high doses [127]. Deep brain stimulation followed: high-frequency stimulation of the thalamus suppressed tremor [128], stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus improved the cardinal signs of Parkinson's disease [129], and a randomised trial showed better quality of life with stimulation plus medication than with medication alone in selected patients with motor complications [130].

Hyperkinetic disorders show the opposite imbalance. Huntington's disease, described by George Huntington in 1872 [30], is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by an expanded CAG trinucleotide repeat in the HTT gene, identified in 1993 [131]; it produces chorea together with cognitive and psychiatric decline, and early loss of indirect-pathway striatal neurons is thought to contribute to the chorea [38,132]. Dystonia is characterised by sustained or intermittent muscle contractions that cause abnormal, often repetitive movements, postures or both [133].

Cerebellar disorders

Cerebellar damage, whether from stroke, tumour, toxins or degeneration, produces the ataxia, dysmetria and intention tremor that Holmes described [31]. The spinocerebellar ataxias are a genetically diverse group of autosomal dominant neurodegenerative diseases, many caused by repeat expansions, with progressive ataxia as the core feature [134].

Restoring control with brain–computer interfaces

Because motor cortical activity persists after the pathways below it are damaged, it can be decoded to drive external devices. A person with tetraplegia used a microelectrode array in M1 to control a computer cursor and a simple robotic device [40], and a later participant produced text at about 90 characters per minute by attempting to handwrite letters, decoded from motor cortex [135].

12Outlook

Motor control has moved from mapping where movements are represented to asking how distributed circuits compute. Several themes run through current work. Population dynamics rather than single-neuron tuning increasingly frames the study of motor cortex [66]. Optimal feedback control provides a normative account of coordination that links behaviour, reflexes and cortical activity [39,101]. Genetic and circuit tools are resolving spinal and brainstem networks into identified cell types [3,63], and new imaging is revising long-held maps of the motor cortex itself [41]. On the clinical side, neuromodulation and brain–computer interfaces are turning this knowledge into therapies for people who have lost movement [122,123,135]. Open questions remain about how the basal ganglia and cerebellum interact, how skills are stored over a lifetime, and how best to harness plasticity after injury [108].
