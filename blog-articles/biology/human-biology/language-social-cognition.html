Language and Social Cognition

How the human brain turns sound, sign and print into meaning, and how it models the minds of other people: from Broca's lesion studies to dual-stream networks, the mentalizing system, and the disorders that pull these abilities apart.

Citation style: Vancouver (ICMJE)146 referencesPrepared 27 October 2026

Summary

Language is a combinatorial system that maps sounds, signs or written symbols onto meanings, and every typically developing child acquires it without formal teaching [1,2]. Around 7,000 languages are spoken or signed today [3]. Social cognition is the set of processes by which people perceive, interpret and predict the behaviour of others, including recognizing faces and emotions, sharing others' feelings, and attributing beliefs and intentions to them ("theory of mind") [4,5]. The two are closely linked: language is learned through shared attention and is used mainly to change what other people know, and comprehension depends on inferring what a speaker means [6–8]. In the brain, however, they rest on separable networks. A left-lateralized frontotemporal language network is distinct from a "mentalizing" network centred on the temporoparietal junction and medial prefrontal cortex [9–11], and brain injury or developmental differences can impair one while leaving the other relatively intact [12–14].

01History

The modern study of language and the brain began with clinical-anatomical correlation. In 1861 Paul Broca described Louis Victor Leborgne, a patient who for years could produce little except the syllable "tan" but who seemed to understand speech; at autopsy, Broca found a lesion centred on the posterior left inferior frontal gyrus [15]. High-resolution MRI of the preserved brain in 2007 showed that the damage extended well beyond the surface region now called Broca's area, into deep white matter and insula [16]. After gathering further cases, Broca concluded in 1865 that articulate language depends predominantly on the left hemisphere [17].

In 1874 Carl Wernicke described patients with fluent but meaningless speech and poor comprehension after damage to the posterior superior temporal gyrus. He proposed a connectionist model in which an auditory word-image centre in the temporal lobe is linked to a motor speech centre in the frontal lobe, and he predicted that cutting the connection would produce a third syndrome, later named conduction aphasia [18]. Ludwig Lichtheim extended the scheme in 1885 with a "concept centre", which allowed a classification of aphasias that is still taught today [19]. In 1892 Joseph Dejerine linked "pure word blindness" (alexia without agraphia) to a left occipital lesion that disconnected visual input from language areas [20]. Korbinian Brodmann's cytoarchitectonic map of 1909 supplied the area numbers, such as 44 and 45 for Broca's region, that are still used to label cortex [21].

The study of social behaviour had its own founding case. In 1848 an iron tamping rod passed through the frontal lobes of the railway foreman Phineas Gage. He survived, but his physician John Harlow reported in 1868 that he had become impulsive, profane and unreliable, so that his friends said he was "no longer Gage" [22]. A 1994 reconstruction from his skull placed the damage in ventromedial prefrontal cortex of both hemispheres [23].

In the mid-20th century, neurosurgery made direct testing possible. Wilder Penfield and Lamar Roberts used electrical stimulation of the exposed cortex in awake patients to map sites where stimulation arrested or distorted speech [24], and Juhn Wada and Theodore Rasmussen introduced intracarotid sodium amytal injection to determine which hemisphere controls speech before surgery [25]. Studies of "split-brain" patients, whose corpus callosum had been cut to treat epilepsy, showed that objects presented only to the right hemisphere could not be named, though they could be recognized [26]. Norman Geschwind revived and expanded Wernicke's connectionism in 1965 [27], and in 1968 he and Walter Levitsky showed that the planum temporale, part of Wernicke's area, is larger on the left in most human brains [28].

Social cognition became a distinct field after David Premack and Guy Woodruff asked in 1978 whether chimpanzees have a "theory of mind" [29]. Heinz Wimmer and Josef Perner developed the false-belief test for children [30], and Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie and Uta Frith used it to propose that autistic children have a specific difficulty in representing other people's beliefs [14]. Functional neuroimaging then made it possible to study both language and social cognition in the healthy brain [31–33].

02Language networks

The classical model and its limits

The Wernicke–Lichtheim–Geschwind model placed speech production in Broca's area (the pars opercularis and pars triangularis of the left inferior frontal gyrus, Brodmann areas 44 and 45), comprehension in Wernicke's area (the posterior superior temporal gyrus), and joined the two with the arcuate fasciculus [18,19,27]. The model remains a useful clinical shorthand, but several findings have outgrown it. A lesion confined to Broca's area usually causes only transient speech difficulty; lasting Broca's aphasia requires larger damage that includes underlying white matter, insula and surrounding cortex [42]. Areas 44 and 45 differ in size and borders between individuals [43], and stimulation mapping shows that critical language sites are small, often a square centimetre or less, and vary in position from person to person [44]. Lesion–symptom mapping in stroke patients found that sentence comprehension depends most on the left middle temporal gyrus and several other regions rather than on Wernicke's area as traditionally defined [13]. Some authors now argue that the terms "Broca's area" and "Wernicke's area" are too loosely defined to be useful in research [45,46].

The dual-stream model

The current consensus framework, developed by analogy with the "what" and "where/how" streams of the visual and auditory systems, proposes that speech is processed along two routes that diverge from auditory cortex in the superior temporal gyrus [39,47]:

  • A dorsal stream, strongly left-lateralized, maps sound onto articulation. It runs from posterior superior temporal cortex through a region at the end of the Sylvian fissure (area Spt, at the parietal–temporal boundary) to premotor cortex and the posterior inferior frontal gyrus. It supports repetition, learning new words, and verbal working memory [39].
  • A ventral stream, more bilateral, maps sound onto meaning. It runs from the superior temporal gyrus and sulcus to the middle and inferior temporal gyri and the anterior temporal lobe, and supports recognizing words and understanding them [39,47].

Combined functional and diffusion MRI in healthy volunteers supports this division: a task requiring repetition of pseudowords engaged a dorsal pathway via the arcuate and superior longitudinal fasciculi, while comprehension of sentences engaged a ventral pathway through the extreme capsule [48]. Friederici distinguishes two dorsal tracts, one to premotor cortex for sensorimotor mapping and one to area 44 for processing complex syntax [49]. Lesion–symptom mapping in 159 people with chronic stroke aphasia produced patterns broadly consistent with the dual-stream account [50].

White-matter pathways

Diffusion tractography shows that the perisylvian language areas are linked by a direct long segment of the arcuate fasciculus and by an indirect route with anterior and posterior segments that relay through the inferior parietal lobule, a region sometimes called Geschwind's territory [51]. Comparative imaging indicates that the human arcuate fasciculus is much more extensive than its counterpart in chimpanzees and macaques, particularly in its projections into the middle and inferior temporal cortex [52]. Ventral connections include the extreme capsule fibre system (inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus) and the uncinate fasciculus, which joins the anterior temporal lobe to orbital and inferior frontal cortex [48,49].

A specialized language network

When functional MRI regions are defined in each individual rather than averaged across a group, the frontal and temporal regions that respond to sentences more than to non-word strings respond little to arithmetic, general working memory, cognitive control or music [9]. People with severe aphasia can still do arithmetic, follow logical arguments and reason about other people's thoughts, which suggests that the language network is a specialized system for communication rather than the medium of thought itself [8,12]. Twenty years of PET and fMRI studies point in the same direction: the core regions for heard speech, spoken output and reading overlap in the left superior and middle temporal cortex and inferior frontal cortex, with additional task-specific regions [53].

Semantic memory

Meaning is represented more widely than the core language network. A meta-analysis of 120 neuroimaging studies identified a left-dominant semantic system including the angular gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, fusiform and parahippocampal gyri, dorsomedial and inferior prefrontal cortex, and posterior cingulate [54]. Evidence from semantic dementia, in which atrophy of the anterior temporal lobes erodes knowledge across all categories and modalities, suggests that the anterior temporal lobes act as a "hub" that binds features stored in modality-specific "spoke" regions [55]. Voxel-wise modelling of fMRI recorded while people listened to narrative stories showed that semantic categories are mapped in a systematic, largely bilateral pattern across much of the cortex [56].

03Lateralization

The left hemisphere is dominant for language in most people, but the proportion depends on handedness. In Wada testing of patients without early brain injury, about 96% of right-handers had left-hemisphere speech; among left- and mixed-handers about 70% were left-dominant, 15% right-dominant and 15% bilateral [57]. Studies in healthy volunteers give similar results: with functional transcranial Doppler ultrasound, right-hemisphere dominance rose from 4% in strong right-handers to 15% in ambidextrous people and 27% in strong left-handers [58]. An fMRI study of 297 people balanced for handedness found that strongly atypical (right-dominant) lateralization occurred only in left-handers, in about 7% of them [59]. Early damage to the left hemisphere can shift speech to the right [57].

Structural asymmetry accompanies functional asymmetry: in 65 of 100 adult brains examined post mortem the left planum temporale was larger than the right, and in 11 it was smaller [28]. Lateralization is evident early in life; three-month-old infants listening to speech show greater activation in left than right temporal cortex [60]. Lateralization reflects the type of processing rather than the auditory or vocal modality. Deaf signers who have left-hemisphere strokes develop sign-language aphasias that resemble spoken-language aphasias, whereas right-hemisphere damage impairs their visuospatial abilities but generally spares their signing [61]. The right hemisphere nonetheless contributes to language, particularly to prosody (the melody and stress pattern of speech) and to its emotional tone. Ross described "aprosodias" after right-hemisphere lesions, organized in a way that mirrors the left-hemisphere aphasias [62].

04Speech perception and comprehension

Sound reaches primary auditory cortex on Heschl's gyrus and is then analysed in the surrounding superior temporal gyrus and sulcus [39,63]. Recordings from electrodes placed on the cortical surface in neurosurgical patients show that sites in the superior temporal gyrus respond selectively to groups of speech sounds that share phonetic features, such as manner of articulation (plosive, fricative, nasal), rather than to individual phonemes [64]. Neural oscillations in auditory cortex appear to track the temporal structure of speech: activity in the theta range (about 4–8 Hz) follows the syllable rate, and faster gamma activity has been linked to finer phonemic detail [65].

Event-related potentials recorded from the scalp distinguish stages of sentence comprehension. A word that does not fit the meaning of a sentence ("He spread the warm bread with socks") evokes a negative deflection that peaks about 400 ms after the word, the N400 [66]. The N400 is now understood as a general index of how easily a word's meaning is retrieved given its context [67]. Syntactic violations instead evoke a later positive wave, the P600 [68]. Imaging and lesion studies relate syntactic processing to the left inferior frontal gyrus (particularly area 44) and posterior temporal cortex, and lexical-semantic processing to the middle temporal gyrus and anterior temporal lobe [13,49].

Comprehension in conversation goes beyond decoding sentences. When a speaker's brain activity was recorded while telling a story and listeners later heard it, the listeners' activity was coupled to the speaker's with a short delay, and the strength of this coupling predicted how well they understood the story [69].

05Speech production

Psycholinguistic models divide word production into conceptual preparation, selection of a word from the mental lexicon (lemma retrieval), retrieval of its sound form, syllabification and phonetic encoding, and finally articulation [70]. A meta-analysis of imaging studies assigned approximate brain regions and time windows to these stages: in picture naming, articulation typically begins roughly 600 ms after the picture appears, with lexical selection linked to the left middle temporal gyrus and phonological encoding to posterior superior temporal and inferior frontal regions [71].

Articulation itself is controlled by the ventral sensorimotor cortex. High-density cortical recordings show that the lips, jaw, tongue and larynx are represented somatotopically along this cortex, and that its activity patterns are organized by how the vocal tract moves [72]. The DIVA ("Directions Into Velocities of Articulators") model describes speech motor control as a combination of feedforward commands, learned for each syllable, and feedback correction from auditory and somatosensory cortex [73]. Planning of articulation is disrupted in apraxia of speech. One study found that all 25 patients with this disorder had lesions involving the left precentral gyrus of the insula [74], but a later study that combined structural and perfusion imaging in acute stroke related apraxia of speech more closely to dysfunction of Broca's area [75].

Because the speech motor cortex often remains intact in people paralysed by brainstem stroke or amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), it can be used to drive speech prostheses. With microelectrode arrays in ventral premotor cortex and area 44, a participant with ALS reached 62 words per minute, with a 23.8% word error rate on a 125,000-word vocabulary [40]. A later system decoded attempted speech with about 97.5% accuracy over months of self-paced conversation [41].

06Reading

Writing was invented only about 5,000 years ago, too recently for evolution to have produced dedicated brain circuitry. The "neuronal recycling" hypothesis proposes that learning to read repurposes part of the ventral visual cortex that already recognizes objects and shapes [76]. In skilled readers a region in the left lateral occipitotemporal sulcus, called the visual word form area (VWFA), responds to written words regardless of case, font or position in the visual field [77,78]. Damage to this region or to its input causes pure alexia, the syndrome Dejerine described [20,78]. Comparing adults who learned to read in childhood, adults who learned as adults, and adults who never learned showed that literacy increases VWFA responses to written words, slightly reduces its responses to faces, and enhances responses to spoken language in the planum temporale [79].

Cognitive models of reading aloud propose two routes (Figure 2). A lexical route recognizes familiar written words as wholes and retrieves their meanings and pronunciations; it is needed for irregular words such as "yacht". A sublexical route converts letters to sounds by rule; it is needed for new words and pseudowords such as "blint" [80]. Acquired "surface dyslexia" (regularization of irregular words) and "phonological dyslexia" (inability to read pseudowords) are interpreted as selective damage to one route or the other [80].

07Language development

Infants begin to learn language before they say anything. In their first months they can discriminate speech-sound contrasts from any language, but between about 6 and 12 months their discrimination of non-native contrasts declines while discrimination of native contrasts is maintained or improves. English-learning infants, for example, lose the ability to distinguish certain Hindi and Salish consonants by 10–12 months [81,82]. Eight-month-old infants can segment words from a continuous stream of syllables after two minutes of exposure, using only the statistical regularities of which syllables follow which [83]. Canonical babbling (well-formed syllables such as "baba") normally begins between 6 and 10 months; it is markedly delayed in deaf infants, showing the role of auditory feedback [84].

The timing of later milestones varies widely between children. Large normative studies with parent-report inventories found that first words typically appear near the first birthday, followed by a period of rapid vocabulary growth and the first word combinations in the second year, with large individual differences at every age [85]. The language network matures over childhood and adolescence: ventral temporal–frontal connections are present early, while the dorsal pathway from temporal cortex to area 44, associated with complex syntax, matures later [86].

Sensitive periods

Eric Lenneberg proposed in 1967 that there is a critical period for language acquisition ending around puberty [88]. Among Chinese and Korean immigrants to the United States, grammatical proficiency in English declined steadily with age of arrival after about 7 years [89]. An online study of about two-thirds of a million English speakers estimated that the ability to learn grammar remains high until about 17 years of age and then declines, but that learners need to start by about 10 years of age to reach native-like grammar [90]. Language can also be created anew by children: deaf children brought together in Nicaraguan schools from the late 1970s developed Nicaraguan Sign Language, and successive cohorts of younger learners introduced more systematic, combinatorial structure [91].

Genetics

Mutation of the transcription factor gene FOXP2 was identified in the "KE family", in which about half the members across three generations have a severe speech and language disorder involving difficulty sequencing orofacial movements [38]. The human FOXP2 protein differs from the chimpanzee version by two amino acids, and the human version may have been subject to natural selection [92]. Because FOXP2 encodes a transcription factor that regulates other genes, it is better regarded as one component of the pathways that build speech-related circuits than as a "language gene" [38,92].

08Social cognition

The "social brain hypothesis" proposes that primates' large brains evolved mainly to meet the demands of living in large, complex social groups. It is supported by the finding that relative neocortex size across primate species predicts typical social group size [93]. In humans, social cognition is usually divided into several components that rely on partly separate brain systems: perceiving social signals such as faces, voices and body movement; responding to and sharing others' emotions; and representing their mental states [4,5]. Theory of mind and the mentalizing network

Theory of mind, or mentalizing, is the ability to attribute mental states such as beliefs, desires and intentions to oneself and others, and to use them to explain and predict behaviour [29,94]. Its classic test is the false-belief task. In the "Sally–Anne" version, a child watches Sally put a marble in a basket and leave; Anne moves it to a box; the child is asked where Sally will look for it. Passing requires the child to represent Sally's belief as different from reality [14,30]. A meta-analysis of 178 studies found that most children pass between 3 and 5 years of age, with a similar developmental pattern across countries [87]. Studies measuring where infants look suggested sensitivity to false beliefs as early as 15 months [95], but a systematic replication attempt failed to reproduce several of these implicit findings, so their interpretation is debated [96].

Neuroimaging consistently identifies a mentalizing network comprising the temporoparietal junction (TPJ), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus, and the anterior temporal lobes [11,94]. The right TPJ responds selectively when people think about another person's thoughts, compared with other information about people or false representations such as outdated photographs [33]. Disrupting the right TPJ with transcranial magnetic stimulation made people rely less on an agent's beliefs, and more on outcomes, when judging whether an action was morally permissible [97]. Lesions of the left TPJ can impair false-belief reasoning even when other demands of the task are controlled [98]. The medial prefrontal cortex is engaged when people reflect on their own and others' mental states and on social reputation [99]. The mentalizing network overlaps substantially with the brain's "default network", which is active during rest and self-generated thought [100].

Face and social perception

Faces are processed by a distributed network. Haxby and colleagues proposed a core system, consisting of the inferior occipital gyrus, the lateral fusiform gyrus (which handles invariant aspects such as identity) and the superior temporal sulcus (which handles changeable aspects such as gaze and expression), and an extended system that includes the amygdala, insula and regions for knowledge about people [101]. The fusiform face area responds more strongly to faces than to other objects [32], and scalp recordings show a face-sensitive negative wave peaking about 170 ms after a face appears (the N170) [102]. In macaques, single-unit recording within fMRI-defined "face patches" found that 97% of visually responsive cells were face-selective [103]. The posterior superior temporal sulcus responds to biological motion, eye gaze and mouth movements [104], and along its length it shows a rough posterior-to-anterior ordering of responses to theory of mind, biological motion, faces, voices and language [105].

The amygdala is important for recognizing fear in faces. A patient with bilateral amygdala damage (SM) could not recognize fear in facial expressions [106]. Her deficit arose in part because she did not look at the eyes; when instructed to look at the eyes, her recognition of fear temporarily became normal [107].

Mirror neurons: a debated mechanism

In 1992 researchers recording from area F5 of the macaque ventral premotor cortex found neurons that fired both when the monkey performed a goal-directed hand action, such as grasping, and when it watched the experimenter perform a similar action [37,110]. These "mirror neurons", together with similar cells in the inferior parietal lobule, were proposed to support understanding of others' actions by simulating them in one's own motor system [108]. Because F5 is considered homologous to part of Broca's area, it was also proposed that the mirror system was a precursor for the evolution of language [111]. Direct evidence in humans comes from recordings in neurosurgical patients, which found cells with mirror properties in the supplementary motor area and medial temporal lobe [112].

These broader claims are disputed. Critics point out that action understanding can survive damage to motor regions, that mirror responses could result from learned associations rather than cause understanding, and that simulation cannot explain how observers understand actions they cannot perform themselves [113]. The "broken mirror" theory of autism has likewise received little consistent support: a systematic review found that most studies of mirror-system function in autistic people showed no clear impairment [114].

Empathy

Empathy is usually divided into an affective component (sharing another person's emotional state), a cognitive component (understanding that state, which overlaps with mentalizing) and regulatory processes that keep self and other distinct [115]. In a study of couples, a participant's anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex were activated both when she received a painful stimulus and when she saw a signal that her partner was receiving one, but somatosensory regions were activated only by her own pain [116]. A meta-analysis confirmed that empathy for pain consistently engages the anterior insula and anterior midcingulate cortex [109]. Empathic distress, a self-focused aversive response to others' suffering, is distinguished from compassion, a warm, other-focused response that can be trained and engages partly different networks [117].

09Where language meets mentalizing

Language and social cognition depend on each other during development. Infants learn words largely in episodes of joint attention, in which child and adult attend to the same object and each knows the other is attending to it [6]. A meta-analysis found a moderate-to-strong relation between children's language ability and their false-belief understanding, with language ability predicting later false-belief performance more strongly than the reverse [118]. Deaf children raised by hearing parents, who often have limited access to conversation in early childhood, performed poorly on false-belief tests compared with hearing children, at a level similar to that of autistic children [119].

Everyday comprehension relies on mentalizing. Philosopher H. P. Grice argued that listeners routinely infer meanings that are not stated, such as reading "Some of the students passed" as implying "not all", on the assumption that the speaker is being cooperative and informative [7]. Yet in adults the two abilities rest on separable systems. Individually defined language and theory-of-mind networks in fMRI are functionally distinct, although their activity is correlated at rest and during story comprehension, which suggests they interact [10]. A man with severe agrammatic aphasia, unable to understand or produce grammatical sentences, could still pass non-verbal false-belief tests [12], which fits the view that language is mainly a tool for communication rather than the substrate of thought [8].

10Disorders

Aphasia after stroke

Aphasia is an acquired disorder of language caused by brain damage, most commonly stroke. In a population-based study, about 30% of patients with a first ischaemic stroke had aphasia [120]. Classical syndromes are distinguished by fluency, comprehension, repetition and naming (Table 2), although many patients do not fit a single category and lesion locations are only approximately related to syndromes [13,50,121].

Recovery involves reorganization of language networks. Serial fMRI after stroke has shown an early phase of reduced activity in the remaining left-hemisphere language areas, a subacute phase of increased activity including right-hemisphere homologues, and a later return toward left-hemisphere activity that accompanied further improvement [122]. A Cochrane review of randomized trials found that speech and language therapy improves functional communication, reading, writing and expressive language compared with no therapy, and that higher-intensity and longer therapy may be more beneficial [123]. In a multicentre trial in chronic aphasia, three weeks of intensive therapy (at least 10 hours per week) improved verbal communication compared with deferred treatment [124].

Primary progressive aphasia

Primary progressive aphasia is a neurodegenerative syndrome in which language is the main area of decline in the early stages. Consensus criteria recognize three variants: a non-fluent/agrammatic variant with effortful speech and agrammatism (left posterior frontal and insular atrophy); a semantic variant with impaired naming and single-word comprehension (anterior temporal atrophy); and a logopenic variant with word-finding pauses and impaired sentence repetition (left posterior temporal and inferior parietal atrophy) [125].

Developmental language disorder

Developmental language disorder (DLD) is the term agreed by an international consensus panel for persistent language difficulties that have no known biomedical cause and that affect daily life or educational progress [126]. In a population study of children starting school in England, its estimated prevalence was 7.6% [127].

Developmental dyslexia

Developmental dyslexia is a specific learning disability characterized by difficulty with accurate or fluent word recognition and by poor spelling and decoding, typically resulting from a deficit in the phonological component of language [128]. Because reading ability is continuously distributed, dyslexia represents the lower tail of that distribution, and its estimated prevalence depends on the threshold used [129]. It is highly heritable and arises from multiple genetic and environmental risk factors, and phonological deficits are the most consistent cognitive finding [130]. A meta-analysis of neuroimaging studies found reduced activity in dyslexic readers in left temporoparietal and occipitotemporal regions, including the area of the VWFA [131].

Autism

Autism spectrum disorder is defined in DSM-5-TR by persistent differences in social communication and interaction together with restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities, present from early development [132]. Kanner and Asperger published the first clinical descriptions in 1943 and 1944 [34,35]. Historical research has since documented Asperger's collaboration with Nazi-era "race hygiene" policies in Vienna [133]. The US surveillance network estimated that 1 in 36 children aged 8 years had been identified with autism in 2020 [134]. A meta-analysis put the male-to-female ratio at about 3:1, lower than older estimates, which suggests that autistic girls are under-diagnosed [135]. Twin studies indicate that autism is highly heritable, with meta-analytic estimates of 64–91% [136]. Its presentation, causes and support needs are heterogeneous [137].

The theory-of-mind account began with the finding that 16 of 20 autistic children failed the Sally–Anne test, whereas most children with Down syndrome and typically developing children passed [14]. Many autistic adults pass explicit false-belief tests, but in one study adults diagnosed with Asperger syndrome did not show the anticipatory eye movements toward the location a deceived agent would search that neurotypical adults made spontaneously [138]. In eye-tracking studies of film scenes, autistic viewers looked less at people's eyes and more at their mouths and at objects [139]. These findings are now often interpreted in relational terms. The "double empathy problem" proposes that communication difficulties between autistic and non-autistic people are mutual, arising from differences in how each group communicates rather than from a deficit in one party [140]. Consistent with this, when information was passed along chains of people, chains of autistic people transferred it as accurately as chains of non-autistic people, while mixed chains transferred it less accurately [141].

Acquired disorders of social cognition

Damage to the ventromedial and orbital prefrontal cortex, as in Phineas Gage, can change personality and social conduct while leaving intelligence and language largely intact [22,23]. Two people who sustained such damage before 16 months of age grew up with severely impaired social and moral behaviour and defective social and moral reasoning [142]. Patients with orbitofrontal lesions had difficulty recognizing faux pas, a social error that requires understanding what a speaker did not know [143]. After severe traumatic brain injury, impaired recognition of emotion, poor theory of mind and difficulty understanding sarcasm and indirect remarks are common and contribute to social isolation [144].

Behavioural-variant frontotemporal dementia is diagnosed partly on social features, including early disinhibition, apathy and early loss of sympathy or empathy [145]. Prosopagnosia, the inability to recognize familiar faces, can follow damage to occipitotemporal cortex [36,101], and it also occurs as a lifelong developmental condition: in a survey of German students, about 2.5% met criteria for hereditary prosopagnosia [146]. Damage to the right hemisphere can impair the comprehension and production of emotional prosody [62], and bilateral amygdala damage can impair recognition of fear [106].
