Organization of the Human Brain

A technical review of how the brain is built and divided: the cerebral cortex, the cerebellum, the brainstem and the limbic structures, with the history of how each was understood and the organizing principles they share.

1Overview and developmental plan

The adult human brain weighs about 1.5 kg and contains roughly 86 billion neurons, together with a similar number of non-neuronal cells[1]. These neurons are distributed very unevenly. The cerebral cortex makes up about 82% of brain mass but holds only about 19% of all neurons (around 16 billion), whereas the much smaller cerebellum holds about 69 billion, roughly 80% of the total[1,2].

The plan of the brain follows from its embryology. Early in development the rostral neural tube expands into three primary vesicles, the prosencephalon (forebrain), mesencephalon (midbrain) and rhombencephalon (hindbrain). The forebrain then divides into the telencephalon and diencephalon, and the hindbrain into the metencephalon and myelencephalon, giving five secondary vesicles whose derivatives form the adult regions discussed here[4–6]. The fluid-filled cavity of the tube persists as the ventricular system.

This article covers four divisions in turn. The cerebral cortex is the folded outer mantle of the telencephalon. The cerebellum sits behind the brainstem under the occipital lobes. The brainstem (midbrain, pons and medulla) connects the forebrain to the spinal cord. The limbic structures are a group of medial forebrain regions, among them the hippocampal formation, amygdala and cingulate gyrus, that have historically been grouped together for their roles in emotion and memory. Spinal cord, peripheral nerves and autonomic ganglia are outside the scope.

2History of the field

Ideas about brain organization moved from the ventricles to the tissue, then from the tissue as a whole to localized parts, and finally to distributed networks. Classical and medieval writers following Galen placed mental faculties in the fluid-filled ventricles rather than in brain tissue[8]. Andreas Vesalius's illustrated anatomy of 1543 and Thomas Willis's Cerebri anatome of 1664 redirected attention to the substance of the brain itself, and Willis assigned functions to the cerebrum, cerebellum and other parts[8–10]. Franz Joseph Gall's phrenology at the start of the nineteenth century claimed that faculties lived in particular cortical "organs"; its method was wrong, but the idea of cortical localization survived it[8].

  1. 1824

    Pierre Flourens shows by lesion experiments in birds that the cerebellum coordinates movement and argues against strict cortical localization[11].

  2. 1861

    Paul Broca links loss of articulate speech to a lesion in the left inferior frontal gyrus in his patient Leborgne[12].

  3. 1868

    John Harlow reports the change in personality of Phineas Gage after an iron bar passed through his frontal lobe[13].

  4. 1870

    Gustav Fritsch and Eduard Hitzig evoke limb movements by electrically stimulating the dog's frontal cortex, the first experimental evidence for a motor cortex[14].

  5. 1874

  6. 1878

    Broca names the "grand lobe limbique", the ring of cortex bordering the corpus callosum and brainstem[16].

  8. 1937

3Organizing principles

A handful of concepts recur across all four divisions and make the details easier to hold together.

Gray matter, white matter and nuclei

Neuronal cell bodies and their local connections form gray matter. Gray matter is arranged either in layered sheets (the cortices of the cerebrum and cerebellum) or in clusters called nuclei (for example the thalamic, cranial nerve and deep cerebellar nuclei). Myelinated axons running between regions form white matter, organized into commissures that cross the midline, association fibers within a hemisphere, and projection fibers to and from lower centers[4,7].

Localization and distributed networks

Lesion and stimulation work from Broca to Penfield showed that particular functions depend on particular regions[12,14,19]. Later anatomical and imaging work showed that those regions act as nodes in larger networks. Felleman and Van Essen catalogued more than 30 visual and visual-association areas in the macaque linked by over 300 pathways, arranged in a hierarchy[30]. Resting-state functional MRI divides the human cortex into a small number of large-scale networks, seven at the coarsest level described by Yeo and colleagues, including the default network that is most active when a person is not engaged in an external task[31,32].

Topographic maps

Sensory and motor regions keep an orderly map of the body surface, retina or cochlea. The best-known example is the somatotopic map along the pre- and postcentral gyri, drawn by Penfield as the "homunculus", in which face and hand take up a disproportionately large area[19]. Retinotopic and tonotopic maps follow the same rule in visual and auditory cortex[4].

Layers and columns

Cortical tissue is organized both horizontally into layers that differ in cell type and connections, and vertically into columns of neurons with shared response properties that run through the layers[24,25,33]. Douglas and Martin proposed that a recurring "canonical" local circuit underlies this arrangement across cortical areas[34].

Crossed control and lateralization

Each cerebral hemisphere mainly receives sensation from and controls movement of the opposite side of the body, because most long sensory and motor tracts cross the midline in the brainstem or spinal cord[4,5]. The cerebellum is the exception: through a double crossing it influences the same side of the body[5]. The two hemispheres are also functionally asymmetric. Language is left-dominant in the large majority of people. In a study using functional transcranial Doppler sonography, right-hemisphere dominance occurred in about 4% of strong right-handers and 27% of strong left-handers[35]. Split-brain patients showed that each hemisphere can process information the other does not have access to[26].

4Cerebral cortex

The cerebral cortex is a folded sheet of gray matter covering the two cerebral hemispheres. Its thickness varies between about 1 and 4.5 mm and averages roughly 2.5 mm[3]. Folding into gyri (ridges) and sulci (grooves) lets a large surface area fit inside the skull; about two-thirds of the surface lies buried within sulci[4,7].

Lobes and landmarks

By convention each hemisphere is divided into frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital lobes, plus the insula, which lies hidden deep to the lateral sulcus. Some authors add a limbic lobe on the medial surface[6,7]. The central sulcus separates the frontal lobe from the parietal lobe, and the lateral (Sylvian) sulcus separates the temporal lobe from the frontal and parietal lobes above it (Figure 1). On the medial surface the parieto-occipital sulcus and the calcarine sulcus mark the occipital lobe, and the calcarine sulcus holds primary visual cortex[6].

Cytoarchitecture: six layers and their exceptions

About 90% of the human cortex is neocortex (isocortex), which has six layers at some point in development. The remainder is allocortex, with fewer layers: the three-layered hippocampal formation (archicortex) and the olfactory cortex (paleocortex), joined to the neocortex by transitional mesocortex such as the cingulate and parahippocampal gyri[4,7].

Two broad classes of neuron populate the cortex. Excitatory, glutamatergic pyramidal and spiny stellate cells form the majority. Inhibitory GABAergic interneurons, which make up roughly 20 to 30% of cortical neurons, come in many morphological and physiological types and shape the timing and gain of cortical activity[36]. The layers differ in their connections (Figure 2). Layer IV is the main recipient of specific thalamic input. Layers II and III send axons to other cortical areas. Layer V provides the main output to the striatum, brainstem and spinal cord. Layer VI projects back to the thalamus[4,34].

Cortex develops from the inside out. Neurons born in the ventricular zone migrate along radial glial fibers, with later-born neurons passing earlier ones to settle in more superficial layers. Rakic's radial unit hypothesis proposed that this migration builds the cortex as an array of ontogenetic columns, and that the number of such units determines cortical surface area[37].

Areal parcellation

Because layer thickness and cell density vary across the cortical sheet, the cortex can be divided into cytoarchitectonic areas. Brodmann distinguished 43 such areas in the human, numbered up to 52 across species, and his numbering is still the common reference[17,38]. Modern parcellations combine architecture, connectivity, function and topography. Glasser and colleagues used multimodal MRI from the Human Connectome Project to delineate 180 areas per hemisphere, 97 of them newly described[29].

Primary, association and multimodal cortex

Primary sensory and motor areas occupy a relatively small part of the human cortex. Most of the sheet is association cortex, which combines information across modalities and supports planning, language, attention and memory[4,5]. Visual information leaving the occipital lobe divides into two streams. A ventral stream into the inferior temporal lobe supports object recognition, and a dorsal stream into the posterior parietal lobe supports spatial vision and visually guided action[39,40]. The frontal lobes, particularly the prefrontal cortex, are needed for planning and for control of social behavior, as the case of Phineas Gage first suggested[13].

5Cerebellum

The cerebellum lies in the posterior cranial fossa, behind the pons and medulla and beneath the tentorium cerebelli. It is attached to the brainstem by three pairs of peduncles[6]. Though it occupies about a tenth of brain volume, it contains about 80% of the brain's neurons, most of them tiny granule cells[1,4]. Its cortex is folded into thin, parallel folia, so densely that an MRI reconstruction found its unfolded surface to be about 78% that of the neocortex[41].

Gross anatomy

Two deep fissures divide the cerebellum into three lobes. The primary fissure separates the anterior lobe from the posterior lobe, and the posterolateral fissure separates the posterior lobe from the small flocculonodular lobe. Across these, the cerebellum is divided from medial to lateral into the midline vermis, the paravermal (intermediate) zone and the large lateral hemispheres[6,7]. Buried in the white matter are the deep cerebellar nuclei: from medial to lateral, the fastigial, globose and emboliform (together the interposed nucleus) and dentate nuclei[4].

Cortical circuit

The cerebellar cortex has the same three layers everywhere: an outer molecular layer, a single row of Purkinje cell bodies, and a deep granular layer[4,27]. Two kinds of input fiber reach it (Figure 3). Mossy fibers from the pons, spinal cord and vestibular nuclei excite granule cells, whose axons ascend and split into parallel fibers that run along the folia through the molecular layer. Each Purkinje cell's flat dendritic tree receives synapses from on the order of 100,000 or more parallel fibers. Climbing fibers come only from the inferior olive, and in the adult each Purkinje cell receives a single climbing fiber that wraps its dendrites and makes a very powerful excitatory connection[4,27]. Purkinje cells are GABAergic and inhibitory, and they are the only output of the cerebellar cortex; they project to the deep nuclei, which send the cerebellum's output to the rest of the brain[27].

Theories of cerebellar function

Marr and Albus independently proposed that the climbing fiber acts as a teaching signal that changes the strength of parallel fiber synapses onto Purkinje cells, so that the cerebellum learns to associate contexts with appropriate motor outputs[42,43]. Ito and colleagues later found long-term depression of parallel fiber to Purkinje cell synapses when parallel and climbing fiber inputs coincide, a plausible cellular basis for that learning[44]. The cerebellum is now widely modeled as a learning device that builds internal models, predicting the sensory consequences of motor commands and correcting errors, as in adaptation of the vestibulo-ocular reflex[45,46].

Clinical signs and nonmotor roles

Gordon Holmes's studies of soldiers with gunshot wounds of the cerebellum defined the classic deficits on the same side as the lesion: hypotonia, ataxia, dysmetria, intention tremor and dysdiadochokinesia[47]. Over the past three decades the cerebellum has also been tied to cognition and emotion. The lateral dentate nucleus projects through the thalamus to prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex as well as motor cortex, and it contains separate motor and nonmotor output domains[48,49]. Schmahmann and Sherman described a cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome of executive, visuospatial, language and personality changes after posterior lobe lesions[50]. Resting-state mapping shows that most of the human cerebellum is coupled to cerebral association networks, with motor representations concentrated in the anterior lobe and lobule VIII[51].

6Brainstem

The brainstem comprises the midbrain, pons and medulla oblongata. It is continuous rostrally with the diencephalon and caudally with the spinal cord at the foramen magnum[6]. Though small, it carries every long tract between the forebrain and spinal cord, contains the nuclei of cranial nerves III to XII, and holds the networks that control consciousness, breathing and circulation[4,5].

Divisions and cranial nerve nuclei

The brainstem has a common plan at every level. A ventral (basal) part carries descending motor tracts, a central tegmentum holds cranial nerve nuclei, ascending sensory tracts and the reticular formation, and in the midbrain a dorsal tectum forms the superior and inferior colliculi[4,7]. Cranial nerve nuclei lie in columns, with motor nuclei medial and sensory nuclei lateral, a layout inherited from the sulcus limitans of the embryonic neural tube[5,7].

Long tracts

Descending corticospinal fibers travel in the cerebral peduncles of the midbrain and the basilar pons, then form the medullary pyramids, where most of them cross at the pyramidal decussation[4,6]. The dorsal column pathway for fine touch and proprioception relays in the gracile and cuneate nuclei of the medulla and crosses as the medial lemniscus[5]. Pain and temperature fibers in the spinothalamic tract have already crossed in the spinal cord and ascend through the lateral brainstem[5]. A small brainstem lesion can therefore produce a "crossed" syndrome, with cranial nerve signs on the same side and limb signs on the opposite side[4].

Reticular formation and arousal

The reticular formation is a loosely organized core of neurons running through the tegmentum of all three levels. Moruzzi and Magoun found that stimulating it in anesthetized cats switched the EEG from a sleep-like to an awake pattern. This established the idea of an ascending reticular activating system[21]. In humans, coma is typically caused by bilateral cortical damage or by lesions of the upper pons and midbrain tegmentum, which carry these arousal pathways[52,53]. Testing brainstem reflexes, such as pupillary, oculocephalic and corneal responses, is therefore central to the clinical examination of a comatose patient[53].

Neuromodulatory nuclei

Small brainstem nuclei send widely branching axons to most of the brain and spinal cord. The locus coeruleus in the pons is the main source of forebrain noradrenaline and is thought to adjust arousal and the gain of cortical processing[54]. The raphe nuclei along the midline provide serotonin[4]. The substantia nigra pars compacta and the ventral tegmental area of the midbrain provide dopamine. Their phasic firing signals the difference between expected and received reward, a reward prediction error[55].

Autonomic and respiratory control

The nucleus of the solitary tract in the medulla receives visceral sensory input carried by the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves, including signals from baroreceptors and chemoreceptors, and it organizes reflex control of blood pressure, heart rate and breathing[4,5]. The basic rhythm of breathing arises in the ventrolateral medulla. Smith and colleagues identified the pre-Bötzinger complex there as a site that can generate inspiratory rhythm on its own[56].

7Limbic structures

Origin and limits of the concept

The limbic concept began as anatomy. Broca described a "great limbic lobe", from the Latin limbus for border, made up of the cingulate and parahippocampal gyri that encircle the corpus callosum and the upper brainstem[16]. In 1937 Papez proposed that a closed loop through the hippocampus, fornix, mammillary bodies, anterior thalamus and cingulate cortex was the anatomical basis of emotion[20]. MacLean added the amygdala, septum and hypothalamus, called the collection the "visceral brain", and in 1952 introduced the term "limbic system"[22,57].

The limbic system is still a useful teaching term, but its boundaries were never precise, and many authors now argue that it is not one functional system. LeDoux noted that the concept lacks an agreed anatomical definition and does not explain specific emotions[60]. Rolls argued that emotion and memory depend on separate systems, the first centered on the amygdala and orbitofrontal cortex, the second on the hippocampus[59]. Catani and colleagues proposed a revised model of partly overlapping networks for memory, emotion and behavior, defined by tractography of their white matter connections[58].

Hippocampal formation and memory

Patient H.M. had the medial temporal lobes removed on both sides in 1953 to treat epilepsy. He was left with a severe and lasting inability to form new long-term declarative memories, while his intelligence, perception and short-term memory were spared[23]. Later work in humans and animals defined a medial temporal lobe memory system, in which the hippocampus and the adjacent entorhinal, perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices are needed to form declarative memories but not to store them permanently[61]. The hippocampus also represents space. O'Keefe and Dostrovsky found hippocampal "place cells" that fire when a rat is in a particular location[28], and the Mosers' group later found "grid cells" in the medial entorhinal cortex, whose firing fields form a regular triangular lattice across the environment[62].

Open question

Whether the adult human hippocampus keeps making new neurons is still disputed. Eriksson and colleagues reported newly generated neurons in the dentate gyrus of adult patients[63]. Sorrells and colleagues found that neurogenesis fell to undetectable levels by adulthood[64], while Moreno-Jiménez and colleagues, using different tissue handling, found thousands of immature neurons into old age that declined sharply in Alzheimer's disease[65].

Amygdala and emotion

Klüver and Bucy found that bilateral removal of the temporal lobes in monkeys produced tameness, loss of fear, compulsive oral exploration of objects and altered sexual behavior[66]. Later studies traced the emotional parts of that syndrome largely to the amygdala. In animals the amygdala is essential for Pavlovian fear conditioning, receiving sensory input directly from the thalamus and indirectly from cortex, and acting through outputs to the hypothalamus and brainstem[60]. In humans, bilateral amygdala damage impairs recognition of fear in facial expressions[67]. In one study, a patient with selective amygdala damage failed to acquire a conditioned autonomic response but remembered the facts of the conditioning, while a patient with hippocampal damage showed the reverse pattern[68].

Hypothalamus, septum and reward

The hypothalamus is often grouped with limbic structures because of its dense connections with the amygdala and hippocampus. It links the forebrain to the endocrine system through the pituitary and to autonomic centers in the brainstem. Its suprachiasmatic nucleus is the master circadian clock, and hypothalamic circuits switch the brain between sleep and wakefulness[69]. Olds and Milner found that rats would work to deliver electrical stimulation to the septal area and other sites[70], which led to the study of brain reward circuits centered on midbrain dopamine neurons and their targets, such as the nucleus accumbens[55].

8How the parts work together

The four divisions are connected by loops rather than arranged in a simple chain. Motor cortex projects through the pontine nuclei to the contralateral cerebellum, which returns signals through the dentate nucleus and thalamus to motor and prefrontal cortex[48,49]. Brainstem arousal systems and neuromodulatory nuclei set the state of the forebrain, the pattern of sleep and waking, and the gain of cortical processing[21,54,69]. Medial temporal and cingulate structures exchange information with association cortex, binding experiences into memories and attaching emotional value to them[58,61]. Large-scale networks identified with functional MRI include matching zones in the cerebellum, which suggests that cortex and cerebellum form repeated parallel loops across motor and cognitive domains[31,51].

The thalamus and basal ganglia, which are not covered in detail here, sit at the center of many of these loops[4,7]. Current neuroscience therefore treats each anatomical division as a set of nodes in a connected system. This view keeps the localization established in the nineteenth century while placing it inside the distributed organization revealed by modern connectivity mapping[29–31].
