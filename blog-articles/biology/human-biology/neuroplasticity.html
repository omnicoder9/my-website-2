Neuroplasticity and Brain Development

How the human brain builds itself from a sheet of embryonic cells, refines its wiring through experience, and keeps changing in response to learning, injury and age.

Citation style: Vancouver (ICMJE)188 referencesPrepared 27 October 2026

Summary

Neuroplasticity is the capacity of the nervous system to change its structure and function in response to activity, experience and injury [1,2]. Brain development and plasticity are two ends of one continuum: genetically directed processes generate neurons, guide their migration and lay down a coarse wiring plan before birth, and activity and experience then select, strengthen and eliminate connections [3,4]. The adult human brain contains about 86 billion neurons [5], yet the number of synapses, the extent of myelination and the volume of grey and white matter continue to change from infancy through adolescence and into old age [6–8]. Plasticity is greatest during developmental critical periods, is constrained by molecular "brakes" in adulthood, and remains sufficient to support lifelong learning and partial recovery after brain injury [9,10]. Disturbances of these processes underlie neurodevelopmental disorders such as fragile X syndrome, autism and neural tube defects [11–13].

01Key concepts

The term plasticity refers to persistent change, as opposed to the moment-to-moment variation in neural activity that encodes ongoing perception and action [1]. It operates at several levels: the efficacy of individual synapses, the shape and number of dendritic spines and axonal boutons, the birth of new neurons and myelinating cells, and the large-scale organization of cortical maps [1,14–16]. Developmental neuroscientists distinguish experience-expectant plasticity, in which the brain relies on input that virtually every member of the species encounters (such as patterned light) during a predictable window, from experience-dependent plasticity, which stores information unique to the individual and continues throughout life [17].

A critical period is a window in which particular experience is required for normal development and after which the effect of deprivation is irreversible; a sensitive period is a broader window in which experience has unusually strong effects but in which later change remains possible [9,18]. Plasticity is not inherently beneficial. The same mechanisms that support learning and recovery can entrench maladaptive states such as chronic pain, tinnitus, focal dystonia and addiction [19–22].

02History

William James used the word plasticity for the nervous system in 1890, defining it as "the possession of a structure weak enough to yield to an influence, but strong enough not to yield all at once", and argued that habits reflect new pathways worn into nervous tissue [37]. Around the same time the Italian psychiatrists Eugenio Tanzi and Ernesto Lugaro proposed that learning might change the junctions between nerve cells, and Lugaro is credited with some of the earliest uses of "plasticity" in that sense [38]. In his 1894 Croonian Lecture, Santiago Ramón y Cajal suggested that mental exercise could strengthen connections by promoting the growth of new terminal branches [39]. Cajal shared the 1906 Nobel Prize with Camillo Golgi for work on the structure of the nervous system [40], yet he also wrote that in adult nerve centres "the nerve paths are something fixed, ended, immutable", a view of the mature brain's limited regenerative capacity that shaped neurology for most of the following century [41].

Jerzy Konorski in 1948 and Donald Hebb in 1949 independently formalised the idea that coincident activity strengthens connections [42,43]. Hebb's postulate held that when a cell A "repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing" cell B, "some growth process or metabolic change" increases A's efficiency in firing B [43]. The idea was later condensed into the phrase "cells that fire together wire together" [44].

  1. 1963Torsten Wiesel and David Hubel show that closing one eye of a kitten leaves most visual cortical neurons responsive only to the open eye, whereas the same deprivation in an adult cat has little effect [45]. They later define the period of susceptibility in kittens [46]; they shared the 1981 Nobel Prize with Roger Sperry [47].
  2. 1963Sperry's chemoaffinity hypothesis proposes that molecular labels guide axons to their targets [48].
  3. 1964Marian Diamond, David Krech and Mark Rosenzweig report a thicker cerebral cortex in rats reared in enriched environments [49].
  4. 1965Joseph Altman and Gopal Das present autoradiographic evidence of new neurons in the hippocampus of postnatal rats [50].
  5. 1969Paul Bach-y-Rita shows that blind people can perceive objects through a tactile image projected onto the skin of the back, an early demonstration of sensory substitution [51].
  6. 1970Eric Kandel's group traces habituation of the Aplysia gill-withdrawal reflex to changes at identified synapses [52], work later extended to the molecular basis of long-term memory [53] and recognised with the 2000 Nobel Prize [54].
  7. 1973Timothy Bliss and Terje Lømo describe long-term potentiation in the rabbit hippocampus [23].
  8. 1983–1991Michael Merzenich, Jon Kaas and colleagues show that somatosensory maps in adult monkeys reorganise after nerve injury, over distances of 10–14 mm after long-term deafferentation [35,55].
  9. 1998Peter Eriksson, Fred Gage and colleagues report new neurons in the adult human hippocampus, labelled with bromodeoxyuridine given to patients with cancer [56].

03Embryonic and fetal development

Neural induction and neurulation

The nervous system arises from the ectoderm. During the third week after conception, signals from the organiser region of the embryo, acting in part by antagonising bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signalling, induce the overlying ectoderm to form the neural plate [3,57]. In neurulation the edges of the plate rise as neural folds, which meet and fuse in the dorsal midline to form the neural tube; in humans closure begins at about day 22 and the posterior neuropore closes by about day 28 [13,58]. The rostral tube expands into the forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain vesicles, the caudal tube becomes the spinal cord, and cells at the margins of the folds form the neural crest, which gives rise to most of the peripheral nervous system [3,59].

Failure of closure causes neural tube defects: anencephaly when the cranial tube remains open and spina bifida when the caudal tube does [13]. The Medical Research Council Vitamin Study showed in 1991 that folic acid supplementation before conception gave a 72% protective effect against recurrence in women who had had a previously affected pregnancy, the basis for current periconceptional folate recommendations [58,60].

Patterning and proliferation

Opposing gradients of secreted signals pattern the neural tube. Sonic hedgehog from the notochord and floor plate specifies ventral cell types, while BMP and Wnt signals from the roof plate specify dorsal fates, each acting through combinatorial codes of transcription factors [64]. In the forebrain, graded expression of transcription factors such as Emx2 and Pax6, together with signalling centres, establishes a coarse map of cortical areas that thalamic input later refines [65,66].

Neurons are generated by progenitors lining the ventricles. Radial glial cells, once thought to be only scaffolds, are themselves neuronal progenitors that divide asymmetrically to produce neurons [63]. In humans an expanded outer subventricular zone contains a large population of outer radial glia, an adaptation thought to underlie the great expansion of the primate neocortex [67,68]. Most neocortical neurons are produced during the first half of gestation [69,70].

Migration

Pasko Rakic showed that young cortical neurons migrate along radial glial fibres from the ventricular zone to the cortical plate [61]. Neurons born later bypass those born earlier, so the six-layered neocortex is assembled "inside-out", as shown first with tritiated-thymidine birth-dating in mice [62]. Reelin, a protein secreted by Cajal–Retzius cells in the marginal zone and missing in the reeler mouse, is needed for this orderly lamination [71]. Inhibitory interneurons follow a different route, migrating tangentially from the ganglionic eminences of the ventral forebrain into the cortex [72]. In the human fetus a transient subplate zone acts as a waiting compartment where thalamocortical axons pause before entering the cortical plate [73]. Defects of migration cause malformations such as lissencephaly, in which the cortex is smooth; mutation of LIS1 causes the classic form [74].

Axon guidance and target selection

Growing axons are led by a motile growth cone that samples attractive and repulsive cues, acting at short range (on cell surfaces) or long range (by diffusion) [75]. Netrins attract commissural axons toward the ventral midline of the spinal cord [76], and Slit proteins acting on Robo receptors repel them so that they do not recross [77]. Graded expression of ephrins and Eph receptors maps the retina topographically onto the tectum, a molecular confirmation of Sperry's chemoaffinity hypothesis [48,78].

Neurotrophic support, cell death and activity-dependent refinement

Neurons are produced in excess. In many neuronal populations about half of the cells generated die during development, and survival depends on limited supplies of target-derived neurotrophic factors [79]. The first of these, nerve growth factor, was discovered by Rita Levi-Montalcini and Stanley Cohen, who shared the 1986 Nobel Prize [80,81]. Synapses are also initially made in excess and pruned by competition: at the neuromuscular junction each muscle fibre is innervated by several motor axons at birth but by only one shortly afterward [82].

Spontaneous activity shapes connections before the senses are used. Waves of correlated bursts sweep across the developing retina before eye opening [83], and blocking retinal activity with tetrodotoxin prevents the segregation of the two eyes' inputs into ocular dominance columns in the cat visual cortex [84]. Early wiring therefore follows Hebbian rules in which correlated inputs are stabilised and uncorrelated ones are lost [4].

04Childhood and adolescence

Synaptogenesis and synaptic pruning

In the human frontal cortex, synaptic density rises during infancy, peaks at age one to two years at about 50% above the adult mean, and declines between about 2 and 16 years [6]. The timing differs by region: peak synaptic density is reached by about three months after birth in auditory cortex but only after about 15 months in prefrontal cortex, where elimination continues into mid-adolescence [85]. Dendritic spine density on prefrontal pyramidal neurons can be two to three times the adult value in childhood and continues to decline into the third decade of life [86].

Synapse elimination is an active process. Proteins of the classical complement cascade tag synapses for removal in the developing visual system [87], and microglia engulf less active presynaptic inputs in a complement-dependent manner [88]. Brain energy use tracks this period of construction: cerebral glucose metabolism measured with positron emission tomography exceeds adult values in childhood and falls to adult levels in adolescence [89], and at its peak in childhood the brain's glucose use is equivalent to about 66% of the body's resting metabolism [90].

Grey and white matter across the lifespan

Longitudinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) shows that cortical grey matter volume follows an inverted-U trajectory, peaking in frontal and parietal lobes around ages 11–12 and in temporal lobes around age 16, while white matter volume increases more steadily [91]. Cortical maturation proceeds roughly from back to front: primary sensorimotor areas mature first, and higher-order association areas such as dorsolateral prefrontal cortex mature last [92,93]. Normative "brain charts" built from more than 100,000 MRI scans place the peak of total grey matter volume at about 6 years and of white matter volume at about 29 years [7].

Myelination

Myelination begins before birth and proceeds in a stereotyped order, generally from caudal to rostral and from sensory and motor pathways to association pathways [94,95]. Diffusion MRI shows that white matter microstructure continues to mature into early adulthood, with frontal–temporal association tracts maturing last [96]. Human neocortical myelination continues beyond late adolescence, whereas in chimpanzees it is largely complete by sexual maturity [97]. Myelination is itself plastic: neuronal activity promotes the generation of oligodendrocytes and thickens myelin [31], and mice that cannot make new oligodendrocytes fail to learn a complex motor skill [15,30].

The adolescent brain

Adolescence brings continued pruning of prefrontal synapses, strengthening of long-range connections and maturation of inhibitory circuits [86,98]. One influential account holds that subcortical systems sensitive to reward and emotion mature earlier than the prefrontal systems that regulate them, which may contribute to the increase in risk-taking seen in adolescence [99,100]. Some researchers regard adolescence as a sensitive period for higher-order cognition in its own right [98].

05Critical and sensitive periods

Ocular dominance as the model system

Hubel and Wiesel found that susceptibility to the effects of monocular eye closure in kittens begins near the start of the fourth week after birth, when the animals begin to use their eyes, is greatest over the following few weeks, and has largely disappeared by about three months [46]. Their experiments established that normal experience during a limited window is needed to maintain balanced input from the two eyes [45,46].

The timing of critical periods is set largely by the maturation of inhibition. Mice lacking the GABA-synthesising enzyme GAD65 do not show ocular dominance plasticity until inhibition is enhanced with benzodiazepines, which can trigger the critical period at any age [9,101]. Closure of the period coincides with the appearance of molecular brakes. Degrading the perineuronal nets that enwrap inhibitory interneurons with chondroitinase ABC restores ocular dominance plasticity in adult rats [102], and plasticity also persists in adult mice lacking the Nogo receptor [103] or Lynx1, a protein that dampens nicotinic acetylcholine receptor signalling [104].

Evidence from human development

Children born with dense cataracts in both eyes show permanent deficits in some visual functions even after early surgery, and different visual capacities have different sensitive periods [105]. Amblyopia, reduced vision in an eye that sent a degraded image to the brain during childhood, responds best to treatment such as patching when it is started early [106]. In congenitally deaf children, auditory cortical responses mature normally when a cochlear implant is fitted before about 3.5 years of age, but often remain abnormal when implantation occurs after about 7 years [107,108].

In language, infants at six to eight months discriminate speech sounds from languages they have never heard, but by 10–12 months this ability declines for non-native contrasts while native-language perception sharpens [109,110]. Adults who arrived in the United States before about age seven reached native-like English grammar, while performance declined progressively with later age of arrival [111]. Psychosocial development shows similar timing effects. In the Bucharest Early Intervention Project, a randomised trial of foster care versus continued institutional care, children placed in foster care before about two years of age showed the greatest cognitive gains [112], and Romanian children adopted into UK families before six months of age showed almost complete developmental catch-up by age four [113].

06Mechanisms of plasticity

Long-term potentiation and the NMDA receptor

Long-term potentiation (LTP) is a persistent increase in synaptic strength produced by high-frequency or paired activity, first described by Bliss and Lømo in 1973 [23]. At most excitatory synapses in the hippocampus its induction requires the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor [114,115]. At resting potentials the NMDA receptor channel is blocked by magnesium ions; the block is relieved only when the postsynaptic membrane is depolarised while glutamate is bound [116,117]. The receptor therefore acts as a molecular coincidence detector that implements Hebb's rule. Blocking NMDA receptors with the antagonist AP5 prevents both hippocampal LTP and spatial learning in rats [118].

Calcium entering through NMDA receptors activates calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), which is necessary for LTP [119]. A principal result is the insertion of additional AMPA-type glutamate receptors into the postsynaptic membrane [120]. Some synapses contain NMDA but no AMPA receptors and are functionally "silent" at rest; LTP can convert them into active synapses [121,122]. Prolonged low-frequency stimulation, which produces a smaller calcium rise, induces long-term depression (LTD) instead [24,115].

Consolidation, timing rules and stability

Early LTP lasts one to a few hours and depends on modification of existing proteins; late LTP requires new gene transcription and protein synthesis, a distinction that parallels short- and long-term memory [53]. The synaptic tagging hypothesis explains how newly made proteins are captured only by recently active synapses [123]. Neurotrophins, especially brain-derived neurotrophic factor, regulate both developmental circuit formation and adult synaptic plasticity [124].

The relative timing of spikes matters. When a presynaptic spike precedes a postsynaptic spike by a few tens of milliseconds or less the synapse is potentiated, and when the order is reversed it is depressed, a rule called spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) [25,26].

Purely Hebbian learning is unstable, because strengthened synapses make further strengthening more likely. Neurons counteract this with homeostatic plasticity: blocking activity in cortical cultures scales up the strength of all excitatory synapses, and raising activity scales it down, preserving their relative weights [27]. The Bienenstock–Cooper–Munro theory proposed that the threshold between potentiation and depression slides according to the neuron's recent activity [125], an example of metaplasticity, the plasticity of synaptic plasticity [126].

Structural plasticity

Stimulating a single dendritic spine with glutamate released by light rapidly and lastingly enlarges it, coupling structural to functional LTP [127]. Repeated two-photon imaging in living mice shows that most spines in adult cortex are stable while a minority form and disappear, and that experience shifts this balance [14]. Motor learning rapidly forms new spines in motor cortex; a small fraction persist for months and correlate with retained skill [28,29].

Adult neurogenesis

In rodents, new neurons are added throughout life to the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus, and their number increases with environmental enrichment and voluntary running [32,128]. In humans, measurement of carbon-14 from atmospheric nuclear-bomb tests in genomic DNA indicated that roughly 700 new neurons are added to each hippocampus per day in adults, with only a modest decline with age [129]. The same method found negligible postnatal neurogenesis in the human olfactory bulb [130] but evidence of new interneurons in the adult striatum [131].

Whether adult human hippocampal neurogenesis is substantial remains debated. One study found that markers of new neurons fall sharply in childhood to undetectable levels in adults [33], whereas others, using different tissue handling, reported immature neurons into the ninth decade and a sharp reduction in Alzheimer's disease [34,132]. Differences in post-mortem interval, fixation and marker specificity are major sources of disagreement [133]. Single-cell transcriptomic studies have also disagreed [134], although a 2025 study combining single-nucleus RNA sequencing with machine learning identified proliferating neural progenitors in the dentate gyrus of adult humans [135].

Cortical map plasticity in adults

Cortical representations expand with use. Owl monkeys trained to touch a rotating disk with their fingertips developed enlarged representations of the stimulated skin in somatosensory cortex [136], and frequency discrimination training enlarged the representation of the trained frequencies in auditory cortex [137]. In humans, string players have an enlarged cortical representation of the fingers of the left hand, especially those who began playing young [138]. Practice of a five-finger piano exercise over five days enlarged the cortical motor map of the involved muscles, as measured by transcranial magnetic stimulation [139].

Structural MRI shows corresponding changes in grey matter. London taxi drivers had larger posterior hippocampi than controls, and the volume correlated with time spent as a taxi driver [140]. Three months of juggling practice transiently increased grey matter in motion-sensitive visual cortex, which receded after practice stopped [141]. In children, 15 months of instrumental music training produced structural brain changes in motor and auditory regions that correlated with behavioural improvement [142].

Deprived cortex can be recruited by other senses. Primary visual cortex is activated when people blind from an early age read Braille [36], and disrupting occipital cortex with transcranial magnetic stimulation impairs their Braille reading, showing that this recruitment is functional [143].

07Plasticity after injury and in rehabilitation

Stroke

After an ischaemic stroke, the tissue surrounding the infarct enters a period of heightened plasticity, with increased growth-promoting gene expression, dendritic spine turnover and axonal sprouting [10,144]. In squirrel monkeys, a small infarct in motor cortex led to further loss of hand representation in adjacent undamaged cortex unless the animals received rehabilitative training, which preserved and partly expanded it [145]. Most spontaneous motor recovery in humans occurs within roughly the first three months [146], and many patients regain about 70% of their potential improvement in arm function in this period, a pattern known as proportional recovery [147]. This early window is thought to combine with training to drive recovery [148].

Clinical trials have tested how to exploit it. Constraint-induced movement therapy, in which the less affected arm is restrained to force use of the weaker one, improved arm function in the EXCITE trial in patients 3 to 9 months after stroke [149]. More rehabilitation is not always better: in the AVERT trial, very early, high-dose mobilisation begun within 24 hours of stroke reduced the odds of a favourable outcome at three months [150]. In the CPASS trial, 20 extra hours of upper-limb therapy produced the largest gains when given 60–90 days after stroke, smaller gains when started within 30 days, and no significant benefit when started at six months or later [151]. Pairing rehabilitation with vagus nerve stimulation, which releases neuromodulators thought to promote plasticity, improved arm function more than rehabilitation with sham stimulation in people with chronic stroke [152].

Spinal cord injury and regeneration

Axons in the adult central nervous system regenerate poorly, in part because of growth-inhibitory molecules in myelin, such as Nogo-A [153], and chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans in the glial scar [154]. Digesting these proteoglycans with chondroitinase ABC promoted axon regrowth and functional recovery after spinal cord injury in rats [155]. In people with chronic incomplete spinal cord injury, targeted epidural electrical stimulation of the lumbar spinal cord combined with intensive rehabilitation restored voluntary control of walking, and some participants regained voluntary leg movement even with the stimulation switched off [156].

Age at injury

Margaret Kennard reported in the 1930s that motor cortex lesions in infant monkeys caused less lasting impairment than the same lesions in adults, an observation later called the Kennard principle [157]. The principle is not a general rule. Early brain injury can be especially damaging when it disrupts processes that are still developing, and outcome depends on the age, size and site of the lesion and on the child's environment [158]. Nevertheless, children who undergo removal or disconnection of an entire cerebral hemisphere for intractable epilepsy often retain or develop substantial language and motor function; in one series of 71 children, outcome depended mainly on the underlying cause [159].

Phantom limbs and maladaptive plasticity

After arm amputation, the cortical territory of the hand can be invaded by neighbouring face representations, and touching the face may evoke sensations referred to the missing hand [160]. The amount of this reorganisation correlated closely with the intensity of phantom limb pain [161]. Similar maladaptive reorganisation is implicated in tinnitus after hearing loss [20], in focal hand dystonia in musicians, whose finger representations become fused [21], in central sensitisation in chronic pain [19] and in the persistence of drug-seeking in addiction [22].

08The ageing brain

A review of 56 longitudinal MRI studies found that brain volume declines by about 0.2% per year after age 35, accelerating to about 0.5% per year at age 60 and more thereafter [8]. The decline is not uniform: prefrontal cortex and hippocampus shrink faster than primary sensory cortex [162]. Neuron loss in normal ageing is modest. Stereological counts estimate that about 10% of neocortical neurons are lost over the adult lifespan [163], and much of the cognitive change seen in normal ageing is better explained by changes in synapses, dendritic spines and neuromodulation than by neuron death [164,165].

Synaptic plasticity persists but changes. In aged rats, LTP decays faster and this faster decay is associated with poorer spatial memory [166,167]. Older adults often recruit both hemispheres for tasks that young adults perform with predominantly one, which may reflect compensation [168]. The concept of cognitive reserve proposes that education, occupation and cognitively stimulating activity allow some people to tolerate more brain pathology before showing symptoms [169].

Plasticity can be engaged in late life. In a randomised trial in older adults, one year of aerobic exercise increased anterior hippocampal volume by 2%, effectively reversing age-related loss by one to two years, whereas stretching controls showed decline [170]. The FINGER trial found that a two-year multidomain intervention of diet, exercise, cognitive training and vascular risk management improved or maintained cognitive function in at-risk older adults [171]. The 2024 Lancet Commission estimated that nearly half of dementia cases worldwide could in theory be prevented or delayed by addressing 14 modifiable risk factors across the lifespan [172].

09Neurodevelopmental disorders

Neurodevelopmental disorders are a group of conditions with onset in the developmental period that produce deficits in personal, social, academic or occupational functioning; they include intellectual disability, communication disorders, autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), specific learning disorders and motor disorders [173]. They can be grouped by the developmental process that fails.

Fragile X and Rett syndromes

Fragile X syndrome, the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability, results from expansion of a CGG repeat in the FMR1 gene, which silences it [176]. In Fmr1 knockout mice, LTD that depends on group I metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR) is exaggerated [177], leading to the mGluR theory of fragile X [11]. Drugs that block mGluR5 corrected many abnormalities in animal models but failed in clinical trials, a lesson in the difficulty of translating synaptic mechanisms into treatments [181]. Rett syndrome, which affects mainly girls, is caused by mutations in the X-linked gene MECP2 [178]. Restoring Mecp2 expression in adult mice that had developed symptoms reversed many neurological defects, showing that some neurodevelopmental deficits are not permanently fixed [182].

Autism spectrum disorder and ADHD

Autism spectrum disorder is characterised by difficulties in social communication and by restricted, repetitive behaviours and interests [12]. In 2022 US surveillance found autism in about 1 in 31 eight-year-old children [183]. Heritability is about 80% [184], and exome sequencing has implicated more than 100 genes, many of which regulate gene expression or synaptic function [185]. Post-mortem temporal cortex from children and adolescents with autism has higher dendritic spine density than that of controls, consistent with reduced developmental pruning [179]. In ADHD, cortical maturation follows the normal sequence but is delayed: the median age at which cortical thickness peaked was about three years later in children with ADHD than in typically developing children [186].

Schizophrenia as a disorder of development

Schizophrenia usually begins in late adolescence or early adulthood. Irwin Feinberg proposed in 1982 that it might result from a fault in programmed synaptic elimination during adolescence [187], and Daniel Weinberger argued that an early brain lesion might stay silent until the circuits it affects mature [188]. Genetic support came in 2016, when variants of the complement component 4 gene that increase its expression were associated with increased risk, and complement 4 was shown to promote synapse elimination in mice [180].

10Synthesis and open questions

The human brain is built by a sequence of overlapping processes: induction, proliferation, migration, axon guidance, synapse formation, pruning and myelination [3,70]. Genes set out the plan and spontaneous activity refines it, then experience tunes it during sensitive periods whose opening and closing are controlled by identifiable cellular mechanisms [4,9]. In adulthood plasticity is reduced but not lost, and it supports learning, partial recovery after injury and adaptation to ageing [10,14,167].

Several major questions remain open. The extent and function of adult human neurogenesis is unresolved [133,135]. Whether critical-period brakes can be safely released in people, for example to treat amblyopia or aid stroke recovery, is not yet known [2,9]. It is also unclear how to time and dose rehabilitation for the best effect [150,151], and how findings on synaptic mechanisms in animal models can be turned into treatments for neurodevelopmental disorders [181].
