The Human Nervous System

Structure, electrical signalling, integration and disease of the body's fastest control system, from the neuron doctrine to the global burden of neurological disorders.

Citation style: Vancouver (ICMJE)64 referencesPrepared 27 October 2026

Summary

The nervous system detects changes inside and outside the body, integrates that information, and produces coordinated responses through muscles and glands [1,2]. Its working unit, the neuron, signals with brief, regenerative changes in membrane voltage called action potentials and communicates with other cells chiefly at chemical synapses [3,4]. The adult human brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons and a similar number of non-neuronal cells [5]. Disorders of the nervous system affected an estimated 3.40 billion people in 2021, 43.1% of the world's population, and were the leading cause of disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) among all disease groups [6].

01History

The earliest known written description of the brain is in the Edwin Smith Surgical Papyrus, an Egyptian text dating from about the 17th century BCE, which describes the meninges, the brain's surface convolutions and cerebrospinal fluid in case reports of head injury [7]. In the 2nd century CE, Galen showed experimentally that cutting the recurrent laryngeal nerve of a pig silenced its squealing, tying a specific function to a specific nerve [8]. Andreas Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica (1543) set a new standard for anatomical illustration of the brain and nerves [9], and Thomas Willis's Cerebri anatome (1664) gave a systematic account of the brain's structure and blood supply and introduced the term "neurologie" [10].

Experimental neurophysiology began when Luigi Galvani reported in 1791 that electrical stimulation made frog muscle contract, which led to the idea that nerves carry an intrinsic "animal electricity" [11]. In 1861 Paul Broca linked loss of articulate speech to a lesion of the left inferior frontal lobe, an early demonstration of cortical localization of function [12]. Late in the 19th century the reticular theory of Camillo Golgi, which held that the nervous system was a continuous network, was challenged by Santiago Ramón y Cajal, whose silver-stained preparations showed that the nervous system is made of discrete cells. This "neuron doctrine" became the foundation of modern neuroscience, and the two men shared the 1906 Nobel Prize [13]. Charles Sherrington introduced the term "synapse" for the junction between neurons and analysed reflexes as integrated actions of the spinal cord [14].

02Organization

Anatomically, the nervous system has two divisions. The central nervous system (CNS) consists of the brain and spinal cord; the peripheral nervous system (PNS) consists of the cranial and spinal nerves, their ganglia and the sensory receptors and motor endings they serve [1,27]. Functionally, the PNS carries afferent (sensory) signals toward the CNS and efferent (motor) signals away from it. Efferent output is divided into the somatic motor system, which controls skeletal muscle, and the autonomic (visceral motor) system, which controls smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands [2]. The autonomic system has sympathetic, parasympathetic and enteric divisions; the enteric nervous system in the wall of the gut contains an estimated 200–600 million neurons and can run many gut reflexes on its own [28].

03Neurons and glial cells

Neurons

Isotropic-fractionator counts put the adult human brain at about 86 billion neurons and 85 billion non-neuronal cells, so the commonly quoted figure of ten glial cells for every neuron is not supported by direct counts. About 69 billion of those neurons (roughly 80%) lie in the cerebellum, and about 16 billion in the cerebral cortex [5]. The brain makes up about 2% of body mass but accounts for about 20% of the body's resting energy consumption [29].

A typical neuron has four functional regions [1]. Dendrites receive synaptic input. The cell body (soma) holds the nucleus and most of the protein-synthesis machinery. The axon carries output signals, often over long distances. Presynaptic terminals release transmitter onto target cells. Action potentials normally start at the axon initial segment, a specialized stretch of membrane next to the soma with a high density of voltage-gated sodium channels, where synaptic inputs are converted into a firing decision [30]. By shape, neurons are classed as multipolar (most motor neurons and interneurons), bipolar (for example, retinal bipolar cells) or pseudounipolar (primary sensory neurons in dorsal root ganglia) [2].

Glial cells

Glia were long described as passive support, but they are now known to take part in synapse formation, transmitter clearance, blood flow regulation and immune defence [31]. The main types are:

  • Astrocytes regulate extracellular potassium and glutamate, supply metabolic support to neurons, and their end-feet surround brain capillaries, where they help induce and maintain the blood–brain barrier [31,32].
  • Oligodendrocytes (CNS) and Schwann cells (PNS) form myelin. One oligodendrocyte can myelinate segments of many axons, whereas a myelinating Schwann cell wraps a single segment of one axon. Beyond insulation, myelinating glia also give axons metabolic support [33].
  • Microglia are the resident macrophages of the CNS. They derive from yolk-sac progenitors that colonize the brain during embryonic development, and they survey the tissue, prune synapses and respond to injury [34].
  • Ependymal cells line the ventricles and central canal; specialized ependymal cells of the choroid plexus secrete cerebrospinal fluid [35]. Satellite glial cells surround neuron cell bodies in peripheral ganglia [2].

04Brain and spinal cord

Protection and environment

The CNS is wrapped in three meninges (dura mater, arachnoid mater and pia mater) and floats in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). The adult CSF volume is about 150 mL, and about 500 mL is produced each day, mostly by the choroid plexuses, so the fluid is renewed several times daily [35]. The blood–brain barrier, formed by endothelial tight junctions together with pericytes and astrocyte end-feet, restricts the passage of most blood-borne solutes into brain tissue [32]. Waste clearance from brain tissue is aided by CSF exchange along paravascular spaces and by lymphatic vessels in the meninges [25,26].

Major divisions of the brain

  • Cerebrum. Two hemispheres covered by the cerebral cortex, a folded sheet of grey matter. Each hemisphere has frontal, parietal, occipital and temporal lobes. The precentral gyrus holds the primary motor cortex and the postcentral gyrus the primary somatosensory cortex, both arranged as maps of the body surface [1,16]. In most people, language depends mainly on the left hemisphere, including Broca's area in the inferior frontal gyrus and Wernicke's area in the posterior superior temporal gyrus [2,12].
  • Deep structures. The basal ganglia (caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, with the subthalamic nucleus and substantia nigra) regulate the initiation and scaling of movement. The thalamus relays and gates almost all sensory information on its way to the cortex. The hypothalamus controls autonomic and endocrine function and drives such as hunger, thirst and temperature regulation [1]. The hippocampus in the medial temporal lobe is required for forming new declarative memories [19].
  • Cerebellum. Coordinates the timing and accuracy of movement and contributes to motor learning. Despite its small size, it contains about four-fifths of the brain's neurons [1,5].
  • Brainstem. The midbrain, pons and medulla carry ascending and descending tracts, contain the nuclei of cranial nerves III–XII, and house centres that control breathing, heart rate, blood pressure and consciousness [2,36].

Spinal cord

The spinal cord runs from the foramen magnum to the level of the first or second lumbar vertebra in adults, where it tapers as the conus medullaris. Below that, the lumbar and sacral nerve roots continue in the vertebral canal as the cauda equina [27]. In cross-section, central grey matter shaped like a butterfly is surrounded by white-matter tracts. The dorsal horn receives sensory input; the ventral horn contains the cell bodies of α and γ motor neurons; and, from the T1 to L2 segments, the lateral horn (intermediolateral column) holds sympathetic preganglionic neurons [2,37]. The white matter is organized into dorsal, lateral and ventral funiculi carrying ascending sensory tracts and descending motor tracts (Figure 4).

05Peripheral nerves

The PNS includes 12 pairs of cranial nerves and 31 pairs of spinal nerves: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral and 1 coccygeal [27]. Each spinal nerve forms where a dorsal (sensory) root, whose neuron cell bodies sit in the dorsal root ganglion, joins a ventral (motor) root. The strip of skin supplied by the sensory fibres of one spinal nerve is a dermatome. In the cervical, brachial, lumbar and sacral regions, the ventral rami of spinal nerves interweave to form plexuses that supply the limbs [2,27].

A peripheral nerve is a bundle of axons organized by three connective-tissue layers: the endoneurium around individual fibres, the perineurium around fascicles, and the epineurium around the whole nerve [27]. Conduction velocity rises with axon diameter and with myelination, a relationship first established by Gasser and Erlanger's recordings of compound action potentials [38]. Peripheral axons can regenerate after injury. The distal segment degenerates (Wallerian degeneration), Schwann cells form guidance channels, and axons regrow at roughly 1 mm per day, so functional recovery after a proximal nerve injury can take months [1]. Regeneration in the adult CNS is far more limited, which is one reason spinal cord injury is usually permanent [39].

06Action potentials and synapses

The resting membrane potential

Neurons keep their inside about 60–70 mV negative relative to the outside. This resting potential arises because ions are unequally distributed across the membrane and the membrane at rest is much more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺ [1,2]. The gradients are maintained by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, which uses ATP to move three Na⁺ ions out and two K⁺ ions in per cycle [20,36]. In mammalian neurons, intracellular K⁺ is about 140 mM against about 5 mM outside, and extracellular Na⁺ about 145 mM against 5–15 mM inside [2].

The equilibrium potential for a single ion is given by the Nernst equation, and the resting potential, which depends on several ions at once, by the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation [1]:

Eion = (RT / zF) · ln([ion]out / [ion]in)
Vm = (RT / F) · ln( (PK[K⁺]o + PNa[Na⁺]o + PCl[Cl⁻]i) / (PK[K⁺]i + PNa[Na⁺]i + PCl[Cl⁻]o) )

At body temperature, with the concentrations above, EK is close to −90 mV and ENa close to +60 mV. The resting potential lies near EK because K⁺ permeability dominates at rest [2].

The action potential

When depolarization reaches threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ rushes in, and the depolarization becomes self-reinforcing. The membrane potential briefly overshoots 0 mV toward ENa. Na⁺ channels then inactivate and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open, repolarizing the membrane and briefly hyperpolarizing it (the after-hyperpolarization). Hodgkin and Huxley described this sequence quantitatively with voltage-clamp recordings from the squid giant axon [3]. Patch-clamp recordings later showed that these macroscopic currents are the sum of many individual channels opening and closing [23].

The action potential is all-or-none: above threshold its amplitude does not depend on stimulus strength, and stimulus intensity is encoded instead by firing frequency. During the absolute refractory period, when most Na⁺ channels are inactivated, no second spike can be triggered; during the following relative refractory period a stronger stimulus is needed. Refractoriness keeps propagation one-way and limits the maximum firing rate [1,36].

Propagation and myelin

In unmyelinated axons the action potential spreads continuously, and velocity scales roughly with the square root of diameter. Myelin raises membrane resistance and lowers capacitance, so current spreads passively under the sheath and the spike is regenerated only at the nodes of Ranvier, where Na⁺ channels are concentrated. This saltatory conduction lets thin myelinated fibres conduct as fast as much thicker unmyelinated ones while using less space and energy [1,17,33].

Synaptic transmission

Neurons communicate at two kinds of synapse. Electrical synapses are gap junctions, channels built from connexin proteins that let current pass directly and almost instantly between cells. Chemical synapses, the large majority, use a neurotransmitter released into a cleft about 20–40 nm wide [1,2]. At a chemical synapse (Figure 6), the arriving action potential opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels in the presynaptic terminal. Ca²⁺ binds synaptotagmin, which triggers SNARE-protein-driven fusion of docked vesicles with the membrane within a fraction of a millisecond [4]. Transmitter is released in quanta that correspond to the contents of single vesicles [18]. It then binds postsynaptic receptors: ionotropic receptors are ligand-gated channels that give fast responses, and metabotropic receptors act through G proteins and second messengers, producing slower, longer-lasting effects [1].

An excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) moves the membrane toward threshold, and an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP) moves it away or clamps it. A single neuron may receive thousands of synaptic inputs, which it sums in space (from many synapses at once) and in time (from repeated activation of one synapse). Whether the axon initial segment reaches threshold decides whether the neuron fires [1,30]. Transmitter action ends through reuptake into neurons or glia, enzymatic breakdown (acetylcholinesterase, for instance, hydrolyses acetylcholine), or diffusion [2].

07Sensory and motor pathways

Sensory receptors and coding

Sensory receptors convert a physical stimulus (pressure, stretch, temperature, chemicals, light) into a receptor potential, which sets the firing rate of the afferent fibre. The type of receptor determines the modality; the location of the receptor's receptive field determines where the stimulus is felt; and intensity is coded by firing rate and by the number of receptors recruited. Rapidly adapting receptors signal change, while slowly adapting ones signal sustained stimuli [1,2].

Ascending somatosensory pathways

Somatic sensation reaches the cortex mainly by two pathways, each a chain of three neurons [1,2]:

  • Dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway (fine touch, vibration, conscious proprioception). Large first-order fibres ascend on the same side in the dorsal columns to the gracile and cuneate nuclei of the medulla. Second-order fibres cross the midline as internal arcuate fibres and ascend in the medial lemniscus to the ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus of the thalamus. Third-order neurons project to the primary somatosensory cortex.
  • Anterolateral (spinothalamic) system (pain, temperature, crude touch). First-order Aδ and C fibres synapse in the dorsal horn. Second-order axons cross within one or two segments in the anterior white commissure and ascend in the contralateral anterolateral column to the thalamus.

Because the two pathways cross at different levels, a lesion of one half of the spinal cord (Brown-Séquard syndrome) causes loss of fine touch and proprioception on the same side below the lesion, but loss of pain and temperature on the opposite side [2].

Descending motor pathways

Voluntary movement is driven mainly by the corticospinal tract. Upper motor neurons in the primary motor cortex and neighbouring areas send axons through the internal capsule and the brainstem. At the pyramidal decussation in the lower medulla about 90% of these fibres cross to form the lateral corticospinal tract, and the rest descend uncrossed in the anterior corticospinal tract [2]. They end on lower motor neurons in the ventral horn, directly or through interneurons. A lower motor neuron and all the muscle fibres it innervates form a motor unit, the final common path for movement [1,14]. Damage to upper motor neurons typically produces weakness with spasticity, hyperreflexia and an extensor plantar (Babinski) response. Damage to lower motor neurons produces weakness with wasting, fasciculations and reduced reflexes [1].

The basal ganglia and cerebellum do not project directly to the spinal cord. They shape movement through loops that return to the motor cortex by way of the thalamus: the basal ganglia help select and scale movements, and the cerebellum compares intended with actual movement to correct errors [1,2].

08Autonomic nervous system

The autonomic nervous system controls involuntary functions such as heart rate, blood pressure, airway calibre, digestion, bladder function, sweating and pupil size. It is regulated by the hypothalamus and brainstem centres, which receive visceral sensory input [36,37]. Unlike the somatic system, its efferent pathway has two neurons in series: a preganglionic neuron in the CNS and a postganglionic neuron in a peripheral ganglion [37].

  • Sympathetic division (thoracolumbar outflow, T1–L2). Preganglionic neurons in the lateral horn project to the paravertebral sympathetic chain or to prevertebral ganglia. Postganglionic fibres are long and mostly release noradrenaline onto α- and β-adrenergic receptors. Sympathetic postganglionic fibres to most sweat glands are an exception, releasing acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors. The adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion whose chromaffin cells release mainly adrenaline into the blood [36,37].
  • Parasympathetic division (craniosacral outflow). Preganglionic fibres leave the brainstem in cranial nerves III, VII, IX and X and the spinal cord at S2–S4, and synapse in ganglia close to or within the target organ. Postganglionic fibres are short and release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors. The vagus nerve carries about three-quarters of all parasympathetic fibres [36,37].
  • Enteric division. The myenteric and submucosal plexuses in the gut wall control motility and secretion, and they are modulated by, but not wholly dependent on, sympathetic and parasympathetic input [28].

09Reflexes and neural plasticity

Spinal reflexes

A reflex is a rapid, stereotyped response to a stimulus, mediated by a reflex arc made up of a receptor, an afferent neuron, one or more central synapses, an efferent neuron and an effector [1,14]. The stretch (myotatic) reflex, characterized by Liddell and Sherrington, is monosynaptic: it has a single central synapse [2,40]. Tapping the patellar tendon stretches the quadriceps and its muscle spindles. Group Ia afferents excite α motor neurons of the same muscle directly, and the muscle contracts. Through an inhibitory interneuron, the same afferents also suppress motor neurons to the antagonist hamstrings, which is called reciprocal inhibition (Figure 9) [1].

Polysynaptic reflexes include the flexor withdrawal reflex, in which a painful stimulus causes flexion of the limb, and the accompanying crossed extensor reflex, which extends the opposite limb to support body weight [2,14]. Golgi tendon organs, through Ib afferents and inhibitory interneurons, signal muscle tension and regulate force [2]. Testing reflexes is a central part of the neurological examination because their pattern helps localize a lesion to the upper or lower motor neuron [1].

Neural plasticity

Plasticity is the nervous system's capacity to change its structure and function with experience, learning or injury. In 1949 Donald Hebb proposed that when one cell repeatedly helps to fire another, the connection between them is strengthened [41]. Bliss and Lømo provided physiological evidence for this kind of change when they showed that brief high-frequency stimulation produces a long-lasting increase in synaptic strength in the hippocampus, known as long-term potentiation (LTP) [22].

At many excitatory synapses, LTP depends on the NMDA-type glutamate receptor. That receptor conducts Ca²⁺ only when glutamate is bound and the postsynaptic membrane is depolarized enough to expel the Mg²⁺ ion that blocks its pore, so it acts as a detector of coincident pre- and postsynaptic activity. The resulting Ca²⁺ signal triggers kinase cascades and the insertion of additional AMPA receptors into the synapse. Weaker, prolonged activity can instead produce long-term depression (LTD) [42]. These mechanisms are thought to underlie aspects of learning and memory, including the hippocampus-dependent declarative memory lost in patient H.M. [19,42].

Plasticity also operates at larger scales. Cortical maps reorganize after changes in use or after injury, which underlies much of the recovery seen after stroke and other brain injuries [1]. Whether substantial numbers of new neurons are generated in the adult human hippocampus is disputed. Neurogenesis was reported in adult human tissue in 1998 [43]; a 2018 study found it fell to undetectable levels in adults [44]; and a 2019 study using different tissue processing found thousands of immature neurons in healthy older adults [45].

10Diseases and disorders

In the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, conditions affecting the nervous system accounted for 443 million DALYs, more than any other disease group. Global DALY counts rose 18.2% between 1990 and 2021, driven mainly by population growth and ageing, even though age-standardized rates fell. The ten largest contributors to DALYs were stroke, neonatal encephalopathy, migraine, Alzheimer's disease and other dementias, diabetic neuropathy, meningitis, epilepsy, neurological complications of preterm birth, autism spectrum disorder and nervous system cancers [6]. The World Health Organization's intersectoral global action plan for 2022–2031 sets targets for improving prevention, diagnosis and care of these conditions [46].

Cerebrovascular disease

In 2019 there were an estimated 12.2 million new strokes, 101 million people living with stroke and 6.55 million deaths from stroke worldwide, making stroke the second-leading cause of death. Most of the burden was attributable to modifiable risk factors, with high systolic blood pressure the leading one [47]. Ischaemic stroke results from arterial occlusion, while intracerebral and subarachnoid haemorrhage result from vessel rupture; the deficit reflects the vascular territory involved [1,47].

Neurodegenerative diseases

Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause of dementia. It is defined pathologically by extracellular plaques of amyloid-β and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau, and clinically by progressive memory loss followed by wider cognitive decline [48]. Alois Alzheimer first described these changes in 1907 [60]. The 2024 Lancet Commission estimated that about 45% of dementia cases are potentially attributable to 14 modifiable risk factors, including less education, hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, hypertension, smoking, physical inactivity, air pollution and untreated vision loss [61].

Parkinson's disease is the fastest-growing neurological disorder worldwide [49]. Its motor signs (bradykinesia, rigidity and rest tremor) follow loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta. Non-motor features such as constipation, loss of smell and REM sleep behaviour disorder can appear years earlier [49]. Its hallmark lesions, Lewy bodies, are made largely of aggregated α-synuclein [50].

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis causes progressive degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons. About 5–10% of cases are familial [55]. Mutations in SOD1 were the first genetic cause identified [62]. Huntington's disease is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by an expanded CAG repeat in the huntingtin gene. It produces chorea, psychiatric symptoms and dementia, with prominent loss of striatal neurons [24,56].

Demyelinating and immune-mediated disorders

Multiple sclerosis is a chronic immune-mediated disease in which inflammatory lesions demyelinate CNS axons and eventually cause axonal loss. It typically begins in young adulthood, most often with a relapsing–remitting course [51]. Diagnosis rests on showing lesions disseminated in space and time, using clinical findings, MRI and cerebrospinal fluid oligoclonal bands, as set out in the 2017 McDonald criteria [63]. In the PNS, Guillain–Barré syndrome causes rapidly progressive, usually symmetric weakness with reduced or absent reflexes, often following an infection. It is treated with intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange [57]. Myasthenia gravis produces fatigable weakness of ocular, bulbar and limb muscles because autoantibodies, most often against the acetylcholine receptor, impair neuromuscular transmission [58].

Paroxysmal and pain disorders

Epilepsy is defined by the International League Against Epilepsy as at least two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, one unprovoked seizure with a high probability of recurrence, or diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome [52]. Seizures reflect abnormal, excessive, synchronous neuronal activity, which may have genetic, structural, metabolic, immune or infectious causes [53]. The WHO estimates that around 50 million people have epilepsy and that up to 70% could live seizure-free with appropriate treatment [64]. Migraine is a recurrent primary headache disorder involving activation and sensitization of the trigeminovascular system. Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) has a key role, which led to the development of CGRP-targeted therapies [54].

Peripheral and traumatic disorders

Diabetic neuropathy develops in up to half of people with diabetes. It typically begins as a length-dependent, distal symmetric sensory loss in the feet, and it is a major cause of foot ulceration and amputation [59]. Traumatic spinal cord injury combines an immediate mechanical injury with a secondary cascade of ischaemia, excitotoxicity and inflammation. Deficits depend on the level and completeness of the lesion, and the adult CNS's limited regenerative capacity remains the central obstacle to recovery [39].
