1Introduction

At any moment the sensory systems deliver far more information than the brain can fully process, and far more possible actions are available than can be carried out. Attention is the set of processes that prioritise some information over the rest. Executive functions are the top-down processes that let a person hold goals in mind, resist distraction and habitual responses, and switch strategy when circumstances change [1,2]. The two ideas overlap heavily. Selecting information according to a goal is itself an act of control, and executive control in turn depends on attention to keep the relevant goal and information active [2,3].

Both depend on distributed networks centred on the prefrontal and parietal cortices, tuned by ascending neuromodulatory systems that carry noradrenaline, dopamine and acetylcholine [4,5]. They develop slowly through childhood and adolescence, decline selectively in later life, and are disrupted in many neurological and psychiatric conditions [1,6]. This article traces how these ideas arose and summarises current understanding at the cognitive, anatomical, cellular and clinical levels.

2Historical foundations

Introspection and the first definitions

William James gave the best-known definition in The Principles of Psychology (1890): attention is "the taking possession by the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of what seem several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought", with "focalization, concentration, of consciousness" as its essence [7]. James distinguished attention that is passive and reflexive from attention that is active and voluntary, which anticipates the modern split between stimulus-driven and goal-driven attention [7,8]. Hermann von Helmholtz had already shown that attention can be moved to a region of the visual field without moving the eyes, the phenomenon now called covert orienting [9].

The frontal lobes and the case of Phineas Gage

In September 1848 a railway foreman, Phineas Gage, survived a tamping iron being driven up through his left cheek and out of the top of his skull. His physician, John Harlow, reported in 1868 that while Gage's intellect and memory seemed preserved, he had become "fitful, irreverent", impatient of restraint and unable to keep to plans, so that his friends said he was "no longer Gage" [10]. The case became the founding example of the frontal lobes' role in self-regulation, though many later retellings exaggerated it, and there is evidence that Gage regained a good deal of social function in the years before his death [11]. Reconstructions from his preserved skull place the damage in the prefrontal cortex, with the extent of right-hemisphere and ventromedial involvement still debated [12,13].

Experimental work followed. David Ferrier reported that monkeys with frontal lesions became apathetic and inattentive although their movement and sensation were intact [14]. Carlyle Jacobsen showed in the 1930s that frontal lesions in primates impaired delayed-response tasks, in which an animal must remember for a few seconds where food was hidden, an early demonstration of what would later be called working memory [15]. Alexander Luria, drawing on patients with wartime brain injuries, described the frontal lobes as the brain's system for programming, regulating and verifying behaviour [16].

Information processing: filters and bottlenecks

After the Second World War, attention returned to experimental psychology through problems in communications and air traffic control. Colin Cherry's dichotic listening experiments of 1953, framed around the "cocktail party problem", showed that listeners who repeat (shadow) a message in one ear can report almost nothing about the content of the other, although they notice gross physical changes such as a switch from a male to a female voice [17]. Donald Broadbent's Perception and Communication (1958) explained such results with a filter model, in which incoming messages are selected on physical features before their meaning is analysed by a limited-capacity channel [18].

The strict early filter was soon challenged. Neville Moray found that about a third of listeners noticed their own name in the unattended ear [19], and Anne Treisman showed that listeners sometimes followed a meaningful passage when it moved to the unattended ear [20]. Treisman proposed that unattended messages are attenuated rather than blocked, while J. Anthony and Diana Deutsch argued for late selection, in which all inputs are analysed for meaning and selection occurs only at the stage of response or memory [21]. Figure 1 compares the three accounts. Daniel Kahneman's Attention and Effort (1973) recast attention as a limited pool of mental capacity allocated flexibly between tasks [22], and Treisman's feature integration theory (1980) proposed that attention is the "glue" that binds separately coded features such as colour and shape into perceived objects [23]. Nilli Lavie later reconciled early and late selection with the idea of perceptual load: when a task is perceptually demanding, distractors are excluded early; when it is easy, spare capacity spills over to process them [24].

Cognitive neuroscience: networks and the central executive

Two frameworks from the 1970s and 1980s still organise the field. Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch replaced the idea of a single short-term store with a multicomponent working memory controlled by a "central executive" [25]. Donald Norman and Tim Shallice proposed a Supervisory Attentional System that intervenes when routine action schemas are insufficient, as in novel situations, planning, error correction and overcoming strong habits [26]. Meanwhile Michael Posner's spatial cueing paradigm made covert orienting measurable [27], and Posner and Steven Petersen's 1990 review proposed that attention comprises separate anatomical networks for alerting, orienting and executive control [28]. Earlier still, Giuseppe Moruzzi and Horace Magoun had shown that stimulation of the brainstem reticular formation produces cortical arousal, laying the foundation for the study of alertness [29].

3Varieties of attention

"Attention" is a family of processes rather than one faculty. The distinctions below recur throughout the literature.

Goal-driven and stimulus-driven control

Attention can be directed endogenously, by current goals and expectations (top-down), or captured exogenously by salient events such as a sudden flash or loud sound (bottom-up) [8,27]. Exogenous capture is fast and transient. In cueing studies, a peripheral flash speeds responses to targets at that location for roughly the first few hundred milliseconds, after which responses there become slower than elsewhere, an effect called inhibition of return that may discourage re-inspection of locations already searched [35].

Overt and covert orienting

Orienting can be overt, with movements of the eyes and head, or covert, a shift of processing priority without any movement [9,27]. The two are tightly linked: according to the premotor theory, covert shifts reflect the preparation of eye movements that are not carried out [36], and subthreshold microstimulation of the frontal eye field in monkeys enhances visual responses at the location to which an eye movement would have been made [37].

Selective attention

Selective or focused attention is the prioritisation of one source, location, feature or object over competitors. Classic paradigms include dichotic listening [17], visual search, in which finding a target defined by a conjunction of features takes longer as more items are added while a target defined by a single feature "pops out" [23], and spatial cueing [27]. Selection has costs as well as benefits. In inattentional blindness, people engaged in a demanding task fail to notice an unexpected, clearly visible event. In the best-known demonstration, roughly half of observers counting basketball passes did not see a person in a gorilla suit walk through the scene [38]. Selection is also limited in time: in the attentional blink, detection of a second target in a rapid stream is impaired for a few hundred milliseconds after a first target is identified [39].

Divided attention and multitasking

When two tasks must be performed together, performance usually suffers. In the psychological refractory period paradigm, the response to a second stimulus is delayed when it follows the first closely, which is evidence for a central bottleneck at the stage of response selection that can process only one decision at a time [40]. Interference is greater when tasks compete for the same perceptual modality, processing code or response channel, as described by multiple-resource theory [41]. These limits have practical consequences: in simulated driving, conversation on a mobile phone, whether handheld or hands-free, increased missed traffic signals and slowed braking, whereas listening to the radio did not [42].

Sustained attention and vigilance

Sustained attention is the ability to maintain responsiveness over time, especially to infrequent signals. Norman Mackworth, studying radar operators, found that detection of rare signals declined within the first half hour of watch, the vigilance decrement [31]. Current models treat sustained attention as fluctuating between "in the zone" and lapse-prone states, linked to the balance between task-positive networks and the default mode network, and to arousal systems [43]. Tasks such as the Sustained Attention to Response Task, which requires withholding a response to a rare target in a monotonous stream, capture everyday attentional failures in both healthy people and patients with brain injury [44].

Alertness

Alertness refers to the general readiness to respond. Intrinsic (tonic) alertness changes slowly with time of day and fatigue, whereas phasic alertness is a brief increase in readiness triggered by a warning signal [4,45]. Imaging and lesion studies link both to a predominantly right-lateralised fronto-parietal-thalamic network and the brainstem noradrenergic system [45].

4Attention networks of the brain

Posner and Petersen's three networks

Posner and Petersen proposed that attention is carried out by anatomically distinct networks that are separate from the sensory and motor systems they modulate [28]. Their 2012 update retained three networks: alerting, orienting and executive control [4]. The Attention Network Test (ANT) combines warning cues, spatial cues and flanker conflict in one reaction-time task to estimate the efficiency of each network. The three scores are largely uncorrelated, which supports their functional independence [46].

Posner's cueing task (Figure 2) underlies the orienting measure. A cue indicates where a target is likely to appear; responses are faster when the cue is valid and slower when it is invalid, relative to a neutral cue. The difference measures the benefit of orienting and the cost of having to disengage and reorient [27].

Dorsal and ventral attention systems

Maurizio Corbetta and Gordon Shulman divided orienting into two interacting systems (Figure 3) [8]. A bilateral dorsal attention network, centred on the intraparietal sulcus, superior parietal lobule and frontal eye fields, generates and maintains goal-directed selection of locations and features and sends biasing signals to visual cortex. A right-lateralised ventral attention network, centred on the temporoparietal junction and ventral frontal cortex, including the inferior frontal gyrus, responds to behaviourally relevant stimuli that appear unexpectedly, acting as a "circuit breaker" that interrupts the current focus and reorients it [8]. Diffusion imaging shows that the superior longitudinal fasciculus, the white matter tract connecting frontal and parietal attention regions, is larger in the right hemisphere, and the degree of asymmetry predicts individual biases in spatial attention [47]. This right-hemisphere specialisation helps to explain why spatial neglect is more frequent and severe after right-hemisphere damage (section 11).

5How selection works in cortex

Attention changes how sensory neurons respond. In a landmark single-unit study, Jeffrey Moran and Robert Desimone placed two stimuli inside the receptive field of a neuron in visual area V4 and found that the neuron's response was governed largely by whichever stimulus the monkey attended, as if the receptive field had shrunk around it [49]. In humans, event-related potentials showed that early sensory responses, such as the auditory N1 at about 100 ms, are larger for attended than unattended inputs, which demonstrates that selection can act early in processing [50].

These findings are explained by the biased competition model. Stimuli in the visual field compete for representation, and top-down signals from frontal and parietal cortex bias the competition in favour of behaviourally relevant items [51]. Neuroimaging shows that activity in visual cortex rises in anticipation of a stimulus at an attended location, even before anything appears, reflecting such a top-down bias [52]. The normalization model formalises this, describing attention as a multiplicative gain on stimulus drive followed by divisive normalisation by the activity of the surrounding neural population. This single computation accounts for a range of seemingly different attentional effects on firing rates [53].

Attention also changes the timing of neural activity. Attended stimuli increase gamma-band synchronisation among V4 neurons, which may make their output more effective at driving downstream targets [54]. The pulvinar nucleus of the thalamus regulates synchrony between cortical visual areas according to attentional demands [55]. The sources of top-down bias are causal: microstimulation of the frontal eye field enhances V4 responses [37]; in monkeys searching for a target, prefrontal neurons signal the target location first during goal-directed search and parietal neurons first during pop-out [56]; and in humans, disrupting the inferior frontal junction with transcranial magnetic stimulation reduces attentional modulation of visual cortex and impairs working memory for the attended feature [57].

6Core executive functions

Executive functions (also called executive control or cognitive control) are needed when acting on automatic pilot or instinct would be inadequate or wrong [1]. Adele Diamond's widely used framework identifies three core executive functions, inhibitory control, working memory and cognitive flexibility, from which higher-order functions such as reasoning, problem solving and planning are built [1].

Evidence for this structure comes from latent-variable studies. Akira Miyake and colleagues gave adults batteries of tasks and found that shifting, updating of working memory and inhibition were moderately correlated but separable factors, a pattern described as "unity and diversity" [34]. Later work found that a common executive factor explains the shared variance, that inhibition is almost entirely accounted for by it, and that updating and shifting each have additional, specific components. The factors are highly heritable [58]. A practical consequence is the "task impurity" problem: any single executive task also draws on non-executive processes, so scores from individual tests must be interpreted with caution [34].

Working memory

Working memory is the ability to hold information in mind and manipulate it over seconds [1,25]. In Baddeley's model (Figure 4) a central executive allocates attention and coordinates two storage subsystems: a phonological loop for speech-based information and a visuospatial sketchpad. An episodic buffer, added in 2000, integrates information from these systems and long-term memory into multimodal episodes [59]. George Miller's famous estimate of short-term span was "seven, plus or minus two" items [60]. When rehearsal and chunking are controlled, capacity is closer to three to five chunks [61], and visual working memory holds about four objects, each with several features bound together [62]. Individual differences in working memory capacity predict fluid intelligence and are thought to reflect the ability to maintain goal-relevant information in the face of interference, an ability that depends on the prefrontal cortex [3].

The n-back task, in which participants indicate whether each item matches the one presented n steps earlier, is a standard measure of working memory updating [63]. Meta-analyses of neuroimaging studies show that working memory tasks reliably engage dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, premotor cortex, dorsal cingulate and posterior parietal cortex [64,65].

Inhibitory control

Inhibitory control is the ability to suppress dominant responses, ignore distractors and resist temptation [1]. Diamond distinguishes response inhibition (self-control) from interference control at the level of attention and thought [1]. In the Stroop task, naming the ink colour of a colour word is slower when the word names a different colour, because reading is the more automatic process [30,66]. In the flanker task, responses to a central target are slowed by flanking items associated with the opposite response [67]. The stop-signal task measures the ability to cancel a response that has already been initiated. Logan and Cowan's race model treats performance as a race between a "go" process and a "stop" process, allowing the latency of the unobservable stop process, the stop-signal reaction time, to be estimated [68]. Lesion and imaging studies implicate the right inferior frontal cortex, working with the pre-supplementary motor area and the subthalamic nucleus, in stopping actions [69], although some argue that this region supports the detection of behaviourally relevant signals more generally rather than inhibition specifically [8,69].

Cognitive flexibility

Cognitive flexibility, or set shifting, is the ability to change perspective, rules or strategy [1]. The Wisconsin Card Sorting Test requires participants to discover a sorting rule (colour, shape or number) from feedback and to adapt when the rule changes without warning [70]. Brenda Milner showed that patients with dorsolateral frontal excisions made many perseverative errors, continuing to sort by the old rule despite being told they were wrong [32]. Task-switching experiments show a "switch cost": responses are slower and less accurate on trials where the task changes, even when the change is predictable and there is time to prepare, which suggests that reconfiguring a task set requires an active control process that cannot be completed entirely in advance [71,72].

Higher-order executive functions

Planning, reasoning and problem solving draw on the core functions in combination [1]. In the Tower of London, participants must move coloured balls to match a target arrangement in a minimum number of moves, which requires planning a sequence of sub-goals. Patients with left anterior frontal lesions were impaired in this task [73]. Within Norman and Shallice's framework, such tasks rely on the Supervisory Attentional System because no ready-made routine is available [26].

7Prefrontal cortex and control networks

Persistent activity and the cellular basis of working memory

In 1971 Joaquín Fuster and Garrett Alexander recorded prefrontal neurons in monkeys that fired persistently during the delay between a cue and a response [33]. Shintaro Funahashi, Charles Bruce and Patricia Goldman-Rakic later showed that dorsolateral prefrontal neurons have "memory fields": each fires during the delay when a remembered location falls in its preferred part of space [74]. Goldman-Rakic proposed that recurrent excitation within networks of pyramidal cells in layer III sustains this activity, a key cellular model of working memory [75].

The guided activation theory of prefrontal function

Earl Miller and Jonathan Cohen proposed that the prefrontal cortex actively maintains patterns of activity representing goals and the means to achieve them. These patterns send bias signals to other brain structures, guiding the flow of activity along the pathways needed to perform a task [2]. On this view, the prefrontal cortex does not perform tasks itself but configures the rest of the brain to do so, and attention, inhibition and working memory are different aspects of the same biasing mechanism [2].

Control within the frontal lobe appears to be organised along a rostro-caudal axis. More anterior regions represent increasingly abstract rules and longer time frames, while posterior regions are closer to concrete stimulus-response mappings [76,77].

Large-scale control networks

Executive control depends on several interacting networks.

  • The multiple-demand or fronto-parietal control network. John Duncan showed that a common set of regions, including the inferior frontal sulcus, anterior insula and frontal operculum, dorsal anterior cingulate and pre-supplementary motor area, and intraparietal sulcus, is recruited by many different demanding tasks. He proposed that this system assembles the sequence of cognitive steps a task requires and that it is closely linked to fluid intelligence [78].
  • Dual networks for top-down control. Resting-state connectivity analyses separate a fronto-parietal network, thought to initiate and adjust control on a trial-by-trial basis, from a cingulo-opercular network, thought to maintain task sets stably over a whole task [48]. Petersen and Posner suggested that these two networks together form the executive control network of their model [4].
  • The salience network. Intrinsic connectivity analyses identified a salience network, anchored in the dorsal anterior cingulate and frontoinsular cortex, which is separable from a dorsolateral prefrontal and parietal executive control network [79].
  • The default mode network. Medial prefrontal, posterior cingulate and lateral parietal regions decrease their activity during externally focused tasks. Marcus Raichle and colleagues described this as a default mode of brain function [80]. Activity in this network is associated with lapses of sustained attention [43].

The anterior cingulate cortex plays a special role. The conflict-monitoring theory proposes that it detects conflict between competing responses and signals the need for more control to the lateral prefrontal cortex [81]. Its dorsal division is engaged mainly by cognitive tasks and its rostral-ventral division by emotional ones [82].

Fronto-striatal loops

The frontal lobes do not work alone. Garrett Alexander, Mahlon DeLong and Peter Strick described parallel, segregated circuits running from regions of the frontal cortex through the basal ganglia and thalamus back to the cortex, including a dorsolateral prefrontal circuit [83]. Computational models propose that the basal ganglia act as a gate, determining when new information is allowed to update prefrontal working memory and when current contents are protected from distraction [84]. This architecture explains why executive dysfunction can result from subcortical as well as cortical damage [85].

8Neuromodulators

Attention and executive control depend on ascending neuromodulatory systems that originate in small brainstem and basal forebrain nuclei and project widely to the cortex [5]. Their effects are often non-linear: both too little and too much modulation impairs prefrontal function (Figure 5) [86,87]. This echoes the Yerkes-Dodson relation between arousal and performance, which was first described in mice learning a discrimination task [88].

Noradrenaline and the locus coeruleus

The locus coeruleus in the pons is the main source of cortical noradrenaline. Gary Aston-Jones and Jonathan Cohen's adaptive gain theory proposes that the locus coeruleus operates in two modes. In the phasic mode, bursts of activity linked to task-relevant events support focused, exploitative engagement with the current task. In the tonic mode, high baseline activity with weak phasic responses promotes distractibility and exploration of alternatives. Moderate tonic activity with strong phasic responses gives optimal task performance [89]. In the prefrontal cortex, moderate levels of noradrenaline act on α2A-adrenoceptors on dendritic spines, closing HCN channels and strengthening connections between pyramidal neurons that share similar memory fields. This is a mechanism of action of guanfacine, which is used in the treatment of ADHD [90,91]. In contrast, the high catecholamine levels released during uncontrollable stress engage α1 and β1 receptors and dopamine D1 receptors in excess, rapidly weakening prefrontal network activity and shifting control towards more habitual, subcortical circuits [86].

Dopamine

Dopamine reaching the prefrontal cortex from the ventral tegmental area is essential for working memory. In 1979, Brozoski and colleagues showed that depleting dopamine in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of monkeys impaired spatial delayed-response performance almost as severely as removing the cortex itself, and that dopaminergic drugs reversed the deficit [92]. Graham Williams and Goldman-Rakic found that the dose of a D1 receptor antagonist determined its effect on memory fields: low doses enhanced delay activity, and higher doses suppressed it [93]. In humans, dopaminergic drugs have an inverted-U effect: they improve working memory and cognitive control in people with low baseline function and can impair those with high baseline function [87]. A common functional polymorphism (Val158Met) in the COMT gene, which encodes an enzyme that breaks down dopamine mainly in the prefrontal cortex, illustrates this. Carriers of the high-activity Val allele, who are thought to have lower prefrontal dopamine, make more perseverative errors on the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test and show less efficient prefrontal activation during working memory tasks [94].

In the striatum, dopamine supports the gating of information into working memory and the flexible updating of rules [84,87]. This is one reason why the executive deficits of Parkinson's disease, in which striatal dopamine is depleted, partly respond to dopaminergic medication [95].

Acetylcholine

Cholinergic neurons in the basal forebrain project throughout the cortex. Acetylcholine enhances the detection of attended signals and their processing relative to distractors. Its release in the prefrontal cortex increases when attentional demands increase, and lesions of the cholinergic projection impair the detection of signals in sustained attention tasks [96]. In Posner and Petersen's model, acetylcholine is the principal modulator of the orienting network [4].

9Development, ageing and state

Childhood and adolescence

Executive functions begin to emerge in infancy. In Piaget's A-not-B task, infants who have repeatedly found a toy hidden at location A keep searching there after watching it be hidden at B. Adele Diamond and Patricia Goldman-Rakic showed that infants improve on this task between about 7½ and 12 months and that monkeys with dorsolateral prefrontal lesions fail it in the same way, linking the change to prefrontal maturation [97]. Using a child-friendly version of the ANT, Rueda and colleagues found that conflict resolution improved considerably between ages 4 and 7 and changed little after that, while alerting continued to develop into later childhood and beyond [98]. Inhibition, working memory and shifting follow different developmental courses, and working memory and shifting continue to improve into adolescence [99].

Longitudinal MRI shows that cortical grey matter matures from back to front: primary sensory and motor regions mature first and higher-order association areas, including dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, last, into early adulthood [100]. One influential model attributes adolescent risk taking to an imbalance between relatively early maturation of subcortical reward systems and later maturation of prefrontal control [101].

Executive function in childhood predicts later outcomes. In the Dunedin cohort of about 1000 children, lower self-control in childhood predicted poorer physical health, more substance dependence, lower income and more criminal convictions at age 32, even after accounting for intelligence and social class [102]. Earlier studies linked preschoolers' ability to delay gratification (the "marshmallow test") to later competence [103]. A larger conceptual replication, however, found weaker associations that were largely explained by family background and early cognitive ability [104].

Ageing

Executive functions and processing speed decline more with age than vocabulary and general knowledge. Timothy Salthouse argued that a general slowing of processing speed explains much of the age-related variance in cognition [105], while Lynn Hasher and Rose Zacks proposed that older adults are less able to inhibit irrelevant information, which then clutters working memory [106]. Imaging studies frequently show that older adults recruit prefrontal regions more bilaterally than young adults during the same tasks. This may reflect compensation or a loss of neural specificity [6,107].

State factors: sleep and stress

Attention is sensitive to physiological state. A meta-analysis of short-term sleep deprivation found that simple attention and vigilance were the most affected cognitive domains, with smaller effects on complex executive tasks [108]. The Psychomotor Vigilance Test, a simple reaction time task that counts lapses, is the standard measure of these effects [109]. Acute uncontrollable stress impairs prefrontal function through the catecholamine mechanisms described above [86].

Two limitations are important. First, performance tests and rating scales of executive function correlate only weakly (a median correlation of about 0.19 in one review), so they seem to capture different aspects of executive function: efficiency in a structured setting versus goal pursuit in everyday life [116]. Second, standard tests can underestimate real-world impairment. Some patients with frontal lesions perform normally on conventional tests and yet are severely disorganised in daily life, a pattern shown in Eslinger and Damasio's patient EVR and in Shallice and Burgess's Multiple Errands Test, which is carried out in a real shopping street [117,118]. These observations have encouraged the development of tests with greater ecological validity [119]. Brief screening tools such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment include executive items, such as an alternating trail task, alongside other domains [120].

11Disorders

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder defined by a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interferes with functioning. DSM-5-TR requires at least six symptoms in one or both domains (five for those aged 17 and over) for at least six months, with several symptoms present before age 12 and in two or more settings [121]. George Still described children with defects of "moral control" in 1902 [122], and in 1937 Charles Bradley reported that the stimulant Benzedrine dramatically improved the behaviour and schoolwork of some children [123].

An international consensus statement estimated the prevalence at about 5.9% in children and adolescents and 2.5% in adults [124]. Twin studies estimate heritability at about 74%, and the genetic risk is polygenic [125]. A meta-analysis found that groups with ADHD show weaknesses in response inhibition, vigilance, working memory and planning, with moderate effect sizes. Yet many individuals with ADHD do not have executive deficits, so executive dysfunction is neither necessary nor sufficient for the diagnosis [126]. This contrasts with earlier theories that placed deficient behavioural inhibition at the core of the disorder [127].

Neuroimaging shows a delay in cortical maturation: in one large study, the median age at which cortical thickness peaked was about three years later in children with ADHD (10.5 years) than in typically developing children (7.5 years), with the greatest delay in prefrontal regions [128]. A mega-analysis of more than 3,000 participants found small reductions in the volumes of several subcortical structures, including the caudate, putamen, nucleus accumbens and amygdala, most evident in children [129].

Stimulant medication is the most effective treatment. Methylphenidate blocks the dopamine transporter, and therapeutic oral doses occupy more than half of striatal transporters [130]. Methylphenidate and amphetamines increase catecholamine levels in the prefrontal cortex, which can explain their benefits for attention and working memory in terms of the inverted-U mechanisms described above [91]. A network meta-analysis supported methylphenidate as the first-choice medication for children and adolescents and amphetamines for adults, taking into account efficacy and tolerability [131]. Non-stimulants include atomoxetine, a noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor, and the α2A agonist guanfacine [91,131].

Frontal lobe and dysexecutive syndromes

Executive functions are not localised exclusively in the frontal lobes, and frontal damage produces several different syndromes [132]. Jeffrey Cummings described three behavioural syndromes associated with the frontal-subcortical circuits: a dorsolateral syndrome with executive deficits such as poor planning, set shifting and organisation; an orbitofrontal syndrome with disinhibition, impulsivity and socially inappropriate behaviour; and a medial frontal/anterior cingulate syndrome with apathy and reduced spontaneous activity [85]. Damage to the circuits' subcortical components can produce similar syndromes [85]. Patients with orbitofrontal or ventromedial damage, as in patient EVR, may have normal intelligence test scores but make disastrous decisions in daily life [117], and they choose disadvantageous decks on the Iowa Gambling Task, showing insensitivity to future consequences [113]. Some patients with frontal damage show utilization behaviour, picking up and using objects placed in front of them without being asked, which reflects an imbalance between environmental triggers and internal goals [133].

Executive dysfunction is common after traumatic brain injury, where the frontal lobes and their connections are particularly vulnerable to contusion and diffuse axonal injury [134]. It is a defining feature of behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia, whose diagnostic criteria include early disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsive behaviour, hyperorality and an executive profile with relative sparing of memory and visuospatial function [135]. Executive deficits related to fronto-striatal dysfunction are also common in Parkinson's disease [95], and impairments in working memory and goal maintenance are a core feature of schizophrenia that is strongly related to functional outcome [136].

Spatial neglect

Unilateral spatial neglect is a failure to attend, respond or orient to stimuli on the side opposite a brain lesion that cannot be explained by sensory or motor deficits. Patients may eat food from only one side of the plate, shave half their face or omit one side of a drawing. In a large acute stroke cohort, neglect was found in 43% of patients with right-hemisphere stroke and 20% of patients with left-hemisphere stroke about a week after onset, and it was more severe after right-hemisphere lesions [137]. Neglect usually follows damage to the right inferior parietal lobule and temporoparietal junction, but also to frontal regions, subcortical structures and the white matter connecting them [138,139].

Several theories explain the right-hemisphere predominance. Heilman and Mesulam proposed that the right hemisphere attends to both sides of space, while the left attends mainly to the right, so left-hemisphere damage is compensated and right-hemisphere damage is not [138,140]. Kinsbourne proposed that each hemisphere directs attention to the opposite side and that the two inhibit each other, so damage to one releases the other [141]. Neglect can affect imagined as well as perceived space: when Bisiach and Luzzatti asked patients to imagine Milan's Piazza del Duomo from one end and then the other, they omitted details on the left side from each viewpoint [142]. Corbetta and colleagues found that ventral lesions disrupt the structurally intact dorsal attention network, producing an imbalance in activity between the two hemispheres, and that recovery of neglect parallels the restoration of this balance [139]. Bilateral parietal-occipital damage can produce Bálint's syndrome, in which the patient sees only one object at a time (simultanagnosia), has difficulty reaching under visual guidance (optic ataxia) and cannot voluntarily direct gaze [143].

12Can these abilities be trained?

Because executive functions predict outcomes, there has been great interest in improving them. In children, a review found benefits from several types of intervention, including computer training, specific school curricula, aerobic exercise and martial arts. The gains were largest in the children with the weakest executive functions, and they were specific to the skills practised; transfer to untrained skills was limited [144]. A meta-analysis of working memory training found short-term improvements in the trained tasks and similar tasks but no convincing evidence of transfer to other abilities such as verbal ability, arithmetic or word decoding [145]. A comprehensive review of commercial "brain training" programmes similarly found extensive evidence of improvements on trained tasks, less evidence of transfer to closely related tasks and little evidence of improvement in everyday cognitive performance [146].

13Summary

  • Attention is a set of processes, including alerting, orienting and executive control, as well as selective, divided and sustained attention, that are supported by partially separate brain networks [4,8].
  • Selection works by biasing competition among neural representations, increasing the gain and synchrony of neurons that encode attended information under top-down control from frontal and parietal cortex [37,51,53].
  • Executive functions consist of related but separable components, namely working memory, inhibition and flexibility, which share a common factor [1,34,58].
  • The prefrontal cortex maintains goal representations that guide processing elsewhere; it works within fronto-parietal, cingulo-opercular and fronto-striatal circuits [2,48,83].
  • Noradrenaline, dopamine and acetylcholine tune these systems, often with an inverted-U dose-response relation [87,89,96].
  • These abilities mature late, decline selectively with age and are impaired in ADHD, frontal lobe injury and dementia, neglect and other disorders [6,85,100,124,139].
