Emotion, Motivation and Reward

How the brain assigns value to the world, prepares the body to act on it, and learns from the gap between what it expected and what it got.

Technical reviewReferences in Vancouver stylePublished October 2026

Abstract

Emotion, motivation and reward are overlapping functions of a set of evolutionarily conserved circuits that evaluate stimuli, coordinate autonomic, endocrine and behavioural responses, and update future behaviour. This review traces the field from nineteenth-century accounts of emotional expression and bodily feedback, through the limbic system concept and the discovery of brain stimulation reward, to current models built on the amygdala, insula, hypothalamus, mesolimbic dopamine system and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. It sets out the competing theories of emotion, the distinction between wanting and liking, the reward prediction error account of dopamine, and the physiology of stress, then shows how disturbances in these systems contribute to depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder and addiction.

1Definitions and scope

There is no single agreed definition of emotion, and the disagreement is substantive rather than semantic [1,2]. A working distinction separates emotional states, which are coordinated changes in physiology, behaviour and cognition triggered by an event that matters to the organism, from feelings, the conscious experience of those states [1,3]. Affect is usually used for the core dimensions of pleasantness (valence) and activation (arousal) that colour experience moment to moment [4], while mood refers to longer-lasting affective states that are not tied to a particular object.

Motivation describes the processes that energise and direct behaviour towards or away from goals. It is shaped both by internal physiological need, as in hunger and thirst, and by learned or innate incentive value of external stimuli [5,6]. Reward, in neuroscience, is not one thing: it has separable components of hedonic impact ("liking"), incentive motivation ("wanting") and learning about predictive cues [6,7]. These components map onto partly distinct neural substrates, a point that matters for interpreting dopamine research and for understanding addiction and depression.

The three topics share circuitry. The same structures that assign value to stimuli, chiefly the amygdala, ventral striatum, orbitofrontal and medial prefrontal cortex, insula and hypothalamus, also generate emotional responses and drive goal-directed action [1,8].

2A short history

Charles Darwin argued in 1872 that emotional expressions are inherited, are continuous between humans and other animals, and originated as actions that were once useful to the organism [9]. His framing made emotion a biological subject open to comparative study.

In 1884 William James proposed that bodily changes follow directly on the perception of an exciting fact, and that the feeling of those changes as they occur is the emotion [10]. Carl Lange independently developed a similar vasomotor theory in 1885 [11]. Walter Cannon's 1927 critique of the James–Lange theory argued that the viscera are relatively insensitive and slow to respond, that similar visceral changes accompany very different emotions, that artificial induction of visceral changes does not produce emotion, and that separating the viscera from the central nervous system does not abolish emotional behaviour [12]. Cannon and Philip Bard placed the integrating centre for emotional expression in the diencephalon: Bard showed that cats whose cortex had been removed still produced coordinated "sham rage", provided the caudal hypothalamus was intact [13]. Cannon also coined the term homeostasis for the coordinated physiological processes that keep the internal environment stable [14], a concept that later underpinned drive theories of motivation.

Walter Hess showed that electrical stimulation of the cat hypothalamus could evoke integrated defensive and autonomic responses, work recognised with the 1949 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine [15]. In 1937 James Papez proposed a circuit linking the hippocampus, mammillary bodies, anterior thalamus and cingulate cortex as the anatomical basis of emotional experience [16]. Paul MacLean extended this into the "visceral brain", later called the limbic system, adding the amygdala and other structures [17]. The limbic system concept remains useful as shorthand, but it has been criticised because its boundaries were never defined by clear anatomical or functional criteria and because several of its core structures, notably the hippocampus, are more important for memory than for emotion [18].

Lesion studies pointed towards the temporal lobe. Heinrich Klüver and Paul Bucy found that bilateral temporal lobectomy in monkeys produced tameness, loss of fear, excessive oral exploration and altered sexual behaviour [19], and Lawrence Weiskrantz attributed the emotional components of this syndrome to the amygdala [20]. Earlier, John Harlow's account of Phineas Gage, whose personality changed after an iron bar passed through his frontal lobes in 1848, had linked the prefrontal cortex to the regulation of social and emotional behaviour [21].

The modern study of reward began in 1954, when James Olds and Peter Milner found that rats would repeatedly press a lever to deliver electrical stimulation to the septal area and other sites [22]. Robert Heath later reported that human patients would also self-stimulate septal electrodes [23]. Arvid Carlsson's demonstration that L-DOPA reversed the akinesia caused by reserpine helped establish dopamine as a neurotransmitter in its own right [24], and histofluorescence mapping by Dahlström and Fuxe and by Ungerstedt identified the midbrain dopamine cell groups and their ascending projections [25,26]. By the 1980s, dopamine was widely viewed as a mediator of reward [27]. In 1997 Schultz, Dayan and Montague showed that the phasic firing of dopamine neurons resembles a reward prediction error, the teaching signal of temporal-difference learning algorithms [28].

Stress research followed a parallel path. Hans Selye described in 1936 a stereotyped syndrome produced by diverse noxious agents [29], and the corticotropin-releasing factor that initiates the pituitary–adrenal response was isolated and sequenced by Vale and colleagues in 1981 [30].

3Theories of emotion

Theories of emotion differ on three questions: what comes first (bodily response, cognitive evaluation, or central brain activity), whether emotions are discrete natural kinds, and whether a feeling is the same thing as the brain state that produces behaviour.

Peripheral and central theories

The James–Lange theory is a peripheral feedback theory: feelings are read out from the body [10,11]. The Cannon–Bard theory is a central theory in which the thalamus and hypothalamus simultaneously drive bodily expression and cortical experience [12,13]. Neither survives intact, but each anticipated a modern position. Bodily feedback returns in the somatic marker hypothesis, which holds that body-state signals, represented in ventromedial prefrontal cortex, bias decision-making [3]. In the Iowa Gambling Task, healthy participants began to choose advantageously, and to generate anticipatory skin conductance responses to risky decks, before they could state the rule, whereas patients with ventromedial prefrontal damage did not [34].

Cognitive and appraisal theories

Schachter and Singer argued that emotion requires both physiological arousal and a cognitive label. In their experiment, participants injected with epinephrine who were not told about its effects were more influenced by a confederate's euphoric or angry behaviour than informed participants [31]. Appraisal theories, introduced by Magda Arnold and developed by Richard Lazarus, hold that emotions arise from evaluations of what an event means for one's wellbeing, such as whether it is relevant, congruent with goals and controllable [35,36].

Basic, dimensional and constructionist theories

Basic emotion theory proposes a small set of evolved emotions, each with a characteristic expression and physiology [37]. Ekman and Friesen reported that members of a preliterate group in New Guinea matched facial expressions to emotional stories much as Western participants did [38]. Later work using less constrained tasks found that recognition of facial expressions varies more across cultures than this result implied [39], and a major review concluded that facial movements do not reliably and specifically signal particular emotional states [40].

Dimensional models describe affect in terms of continuous axes. Russell's circumplex places affective states in a two-dimensional space defined by valence and arousal [4]. Constructionist theories go further, proposing that discrete emotions are not hard-wired circuits but categories the brain constructs from interoceptive signals, prior experience and context [2]. Consistent with this, a meta-analysis of neuroimaging studies found little evidence that discrete emotion categories map consistently onto specific brain regions [41].

Other researchers retain specific subcortical systems. Panksepp described primary-process affective systems, including SEEKING, FEAR, RAGE, LUST, CARE, PANIC and PLAY, on the basis of brain stimulation and lesion work in animals [42]. LeDoux proposed that the field separate conserved survival circuits, which detect and respond to challenges such as threat or energy need, from the conscious feelings that humans report, which may depend on additional cortical processes [1].

4The amygdala and limbic–prefrontal circuits

Structure and fear conditioning

The amygdala is a group of nuclei in the anterior medial temporal lobe. The lateral nucleus receives sensory input from the thalamus and cortex, the basal nucleus receives contextual information from the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, and the central nucleus is the main output to brainstem and hypothalamic effector regions [18,44]. Much of this anatomy was worked out through Pavlovian fear conditioning, in which a neutral conditioned stimulus is paired with an aversive unconditioned stimulus. Association of the two depends on plasticity in the lateral amygdala, and the central nucleus then drives the components of the conditioned fear response: freezing via the periaqueductal grey, blood pressure and heart rate changes via the lateral hypothalamus and brainstem, hormone release via the paraventricular nucleus, and potentiated startle via the reticular formation [18,45]. Sensory information can reach the lateral amygdala directly from the thalamus as well as through sensory cortex, a fast subcortical route that may allow crude rapid responses before full cortical analysis [18].

Evidence from humans

Patient S.M., who has bilateral amygdala damage due to Urbach–Wiethe disease, was impaired at recognising fear in facial expressions [33] and, in later testing, showed a near absence of experienced fear across exposure to snakes, spiders, a haunted house and frightening films [47]. Functional imaging shows that the human amygdala responds to fearful faces even when they are masked so that participants do not report seeing them [43]. The amygdala also strengthens the consolidation of emotionally arousing memories, an effect that depends on noradrenergic activity in the basolateral amygdala and is modulated by stress hormones [48]. Human and rodent findings broadly converge on the amygdala's role in fear learning, attention to emotionally significant stimuli and emotional memory [49].

More than fear

The amygdala is not a fear centre. Distinct populations of basolateral amygdala neurons encode positive and negative valence, and their projections to different targets can support approach or avoidance [44]. Optogenetic activation of basolateral amygdala terminals in the central nucleus reduced anxiety-related behaviour in mice, while inhibiting the same projection increased it [50]. Davis and colleagues distinguished brief, cue-specific fear, which depends on the central amygdala, from sustained anxiety to unpredictable threat, which depends on the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis in the extended amygdala [45].

Prefrontal and cingulate contributions

The orbitofrontal cortex represents the value of rewards and links that value to hedonic experience [51]; single orbitofrontal neurons in monkeys encode the economic value of offered and chosen goods [52]. The anterior cingulate cortex has been divided into a dorsal region more involved in cognitive control and a rostral–ventral region more involved in emotion [53], and a later synthesis proposed that dorsal anterior cingulate and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex support the appraisal and expression of negative emotion, while ventral regions regulate it [54]. In rats, infralimbic prefrontal neurons signal the memory of fear extinction, and stimulating them reduces conditioned freezing [46]; in humans, ventromedial prefrontal activity tracks extinction recall [55]. Deliberate regulation strategies such as cognitive reappraisal engage lateral and medial prefrontal cortex and change amygdala responses [56,57]. As threat comes closer, human brain activity shifts from ventromedial prefrontal cortex towards the periaqueductal grey, suggesting a progression from flexible to reflexive defence [58].

5Interoception and the insula

Interoception is the sensing of the body's internal physiological state, including signals of heart rate, respiration, temperature, pain, visceral distension and metabolic need [59]. Craig described a pathway in which small-diameter afferents relay to lamina I of the spinal and trigeminal dorsal horn, then via the thalamus to the dorsal posterior insula, which forms a primary interoceptive cortex. This representation is re-represented in the mid insula and then in the anterior insula, especially on the right, where it may support subjective awareness of feelings [59,60].

Critchley and colleagues found that activity in the right anterior insula predicted how accurately participants could detect their own heartbeat, and that grey matter volume in the same region correlated both with heartbeat detection accuracy and with self-reported negative emotional experience [61]. Interoception is now treated as having several dimensions: objective accuracy, self-reported sensibility, and metacognitive awareness of one's own accuracy, which can dissociate from one another [62].

Predictive accounts treat interoception as inference. The brain generates predictions about bodily states and updates them using ascending signals; emotional feelings, on this view, reflect the content of interoceptive predictions [2,63]. These ideas restate the James–Lange emphasis on the body in computational terms. Altered interoception has been reported across anxiety, depression, eating disorders and addiction, which has made it a target for transdiagnostic research [64].

6Motivation and homeostatic drives

Psychological frameworks

Thorndike's law of effect stated that responses followed by satisfying outcomes are strengthened [65], and Skinner formalised the study of how reinforcement shapes operant behaviour [66]. Hull's drive-reduction theory proposed that biological needs create drives, and that behaviours which reduce a drive are reinforced [5]. Drive reduction cannot explain why animals work for stimuli that satisfy no physiological need, including electrical brain stimulation [22], which helped move the field towards incentive theories in which learned cues acquire motivational value of their own [6]. The Yerkes–Dodson finding that learning was fastest at an intermediate strength of stimulation, with the optimum depending on task difficulty, is often cited as an early demonstration of a non-linear relation between arousal and performance [67]. In humans, self-determination theory distinguishes intrinsic motivation, in which an activity is rewarding in itself, from extrinsic motivation, and links intrinsic motivation to needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness [68].

The hypothalamus and feeding

Lesions of the ventromedial hypothalamus in rats produced overeating and obesity [69], while lesions of the lateral hypothalamus produced aphagia [70]. These findings supported a dual-centre model of satiety and hunger centres. That model has been replaced by molecularly defined circuits in which specific hypothalamic cell types act as interfaces between internal state signals and goal-directed behaviour [71,72].

The adipocyte hormone leptin was identified by positional cloning of the mouse obese gene [32], and children with congenital leptin deficiency develop severe early-onset obesity [73]. Ghrelin, an acylated peptide secreted by the stomach [74], acts as a circulating hunger signal. In the arcuate nucleus, leptin inhibits orexigenic neurons co-expressing agouti-related peptide (AgRP) and neuropeptide Y and activates anorexigenic pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons [75]. Optogenetic or chemogenetic activation of AgRP neurons in sated mice evokes intense feeding within minutes, without any prior training [76]. Thirst is organised in a similar way: in the subfornical organ, one population of excitatory neurons drives drinking and a population of inhibitory neurons suppresses it [77].

From motivation to action

Mogenson and colleagues proposed that the nucleus accumbens is an interface where limbic signals about motivational state gain access to motor systems that produce behaviour [78]. Social motivation uses some of the same machinery. In monogamous prairie voles, the formation of partner preferences depends on oxytocin, vasopressin and dopamine signalling in ventral forebrain regions including the nucleus accumbens [79].

7The dopamine reward system

Anatomy

Midbrain dopamine neurons are found mainly in the ventral tegmental area (VTA, cell group A10) and the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc, A9) [25]. The mesolimbic projection runs from the VTA to the nucleus accumbens (ventral striatum), amygdala and other limbic targets; the mesocortical projection runs from the VTA to the prefrontal cortex; and the nigrostriatal projection runs from the SNc to the dorsal striatum [26,80]. The VTA is heterogeneous. It contains GABAergic and glutamatergic neurons as well as dopamine neurons, and dopamine neurons differ in their projections and their responses [80].

Wanting is not liking

Addictive drugs share an ability to raise dopamine. Using microdialysis in freely moving rats, Di Chiara and Imperato found that drugs abused by humans, including amphetamine, cocaine, morphine, ethanol and nicotine, increased extracellular dopamine more in the nucleus accumbens than in the dorsal caudate [82]. This and earlier pharmacological work supported the idea that dopamine mediates pleasure [27]. That idea has been substantially revised. Rats with near-complete dopamine depletion still show normal facial "liking" reactions to sweet tastes, even though they no longer seek out food, which led Berridge and Robinson to propose that dopamine mediates incentive salience, the "wanting" that makes reward cues attractive, rather than hedonic impact [6]. Hedonic "liking" instead depends on small hedonic hotspots; for example, stimulating mu-opioid receptors in a restricted region of the medial shell of the nucleus accumbens increases liking reactions to sucrose [7,83]. Mesolimbic dopamine is also needed for effortful behaviour: dopamine depletion in the accumbens shifts animals away from high-effort, high-value options towards low-effort alternatives [84].

In humans, functional MRI shows that nucleus accumbens activity increases with the size of an anticipated monetary reward [85], and medial orbitofrontal cortex activity correlates with the subjective pleasantness of rewards [51].

8Reward prediction error

Learning theory had already established that associations form when outcomes are surprising. The Rescorla–Wagner model holds that the change in associative strength on each trial is proportional to the difference between the outcome obtained and the outcome predicted [86]. Temporal-difference (TD) reinforcement learning extends this to predictions that unfold in time [87]. Its teaching signal is the prediction error:

δt = rt + γ·V(st+1) − V(st)

where r is reward received, V the predicted value of a state, and γ a discount factor. Schultz and colleagues recorded midbrain dopamine neurons in monkeys and found that they fire a burst to an unexpected reward; that after learning, the burst transfers to the earliest cue that predicts reward and no longer occurs to the fully predicted reward itself; and that when a predicted reward is withheld, firing dips below baseline at the moment the reward should have arrived [28]. This is the pattern expected of δ.

Dopamine responses scale with reward probability and magnitude: phasic responses to a cue increase with the probability of reward, and responses to the reward itself decrease as it becomes more probable [88]. Recordings from identified cell types in mouse VTA showed that dopamine neurons compute prediction errors in a way consistent with subtraction of expected from received reward, while neighbouring GABA neurons signal expected reward, a plausible source for the subtraction [89]. The lateral habenula provides a complementary negative signal; its neurons are excited by reward omission and inhibit dopamine neurons [81].

Causal experiments support the theory. Optogenetically driven phasic firing of VTA dopamine neurons was sufficient to produce a conditioned place preference in mice [90]. In rats, stimulating dopamine neurons at the time of an expected reward, mimicking a positive prediction error, allowed learning about a cue that would otherwise have been blocked [91].

The prediction error account has been refined rather than replaced. Some dopamine neurons respond to aversive or novel events and to alerting stimuli as well as to reward [92], and the phasic dopamine response appears to have an early component that reflects salience and stimulus detection, followed by a later component that encodes value and prediction error [93].

9Stress and the HPA axis

Selye described a general adaptation syndrome, a common pattern of responses to many different damaging agents [29]. Today, the stress response is described as two linked systems. The sympathetic–adrenomedullary system acts within seconds, releasing noradrenaline from sympathetic nerves and adrenaline from the adrenal medulla. The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis acts over minutes: neurons in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus release corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and arginine vasopressin into the hypophyseal portal circulation; these stimulate the anterior pituitary to secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH); and ACTH stimulates the adrenal cortex to secrete glucocorticoids, mainly cortisol in humans [30,94].

Glucocorticoids act through two receptor types: the high-affinity mineralocorticoid receptor, which is largely occupied at basal hormone levels, and the lower-affinity glucocorticoid receptor, which becomes occupied as levels rise during stress and the circadian peak [95]. Their effects can be permissive, suppressive, stimulatory or preparative, and many of them serve to limit and terminate the stress response rather than amplify it [97]. Negative feedback at the pituitary, PVN and hippocampus brings the axis back to baseline [94,95]. Limbic regions shape the response: the amygdala tends to activate the HPA axis, whereas the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex tend to inhibit it, largely through relays in the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis and peri-PVN GABAergic neurons [94,96].

McEwen introduced the concept of allostatic load to describe the cumulative cost to the body when stress mediators are activated too often, fail to shut off, or respond inadequately, a mechanism linking chronic stress to cardiovascular, metabolic, immune and neural damage [98].

10Disorders of emotion and motivation

Psychiatric diagnosis is based on clinical criteria such as those of DSM-5-TR [99], but the circuits described above cut across diagnostic categories. The US National Institute of Mental Health's Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework organises research around functional domains including negative valence systems (threat, loss) and positive valence systems (reward responsiveness, reward learning and valuation) [100]. The World Health Organization estimated that in 2015 about 322 million people (4.4% of the world population) had depression and about 264 million (3.6%) had an anxiety disorder [101].

Depression

Major depressive disorder is characterised by persistent low mood and/or anhedonia, together with changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration and self-worth [99,102]. Anhedonia in depression often reflects reduced motivation and reward anticipation, that is, impaired wanting, more than reduced consummatory pleasure, consistent with the dissociation of dopamine-dependent incentive processes from hedonic liking [7,103]. A meta-analysis of functional imaging found that, compared with healthy controls, people with depression show greater responses to negative stimuli in the amygdala, insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, and lower responses in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and dorsal striatum [104].

HPA axis hyperactivity, including raised cortisol and failure to suppress cortisol after dexamethasone, is found in a substantial subset of patients, and impaired glucocorticoid receptor feedback has been proposed as a causal mechanism [105,106]. The classical monoamine hypothesis has weakened: a 2022 umbrella review found no consistent evidence that depression is caused by low serotonin activity [107]. A single sub-anaesthetic dose of the NMDA receptor antagonist ketamine improved depressive symptoms within hours to days in an early controlled trial [108], which has focused attention on glutamatergic signalling and synaptic plasticity [109]. Deep brain stimulation of white matter adjacent to the subgenual cingulate cortex produced sustained remission in four of six patients with treatment-resistant depression in an early study, implicating this region in the circuitry of mood [110].

Anxiety disorders

Anxiety disorders, including generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder and specific phobias, involve excessive fear or anxiety and avoidance that are out of proportion to actual threat [99,111]. A meta-analysis of imaging studies found that PTSD, social anxiety disorder and specific phobia share hyperactivity of the amygdala and insula in response to negative emotional stimuli [112]. The distinction between phasic fear mediated by the central amygdala and sustained anxiety mediated by the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis offers one circuit-level account of why anticipatory, generalised anxiety differs from acute fear [45].

Post-traumatic stress disorder

PTSD follows exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, and involves intrusive memories, avoidance, negative alterations in mood and cognition, and hyperarousal [99,113]. Lifetime prevalence was 7.8% in the US National Comorbidity Survey [114] and 3.9% across the cross-national World Mental Health Surveys [115]. Neurocircuitry models describe an amygdala that is hyper-responsive to threat, together with a ventromedial prefrontal cortex and hippocampus that fail to provide adequate top-down regulation and contextual control, impairing fear extinction [116,117]. In a study of identical twins discordant for combat exposure, the unexposed co-twins of veterans with PTSD also had smaller hippocampi, suggesting that smaller hippocampal volume can be a pre-existing vulnerability factor rather than only a consequence of trauma [118]. Unlike depression, PTSD is often associated with low or normal cortisol and enhanced glucocorticoid negative feedback [119].

Addiction

Addiction is a chronic, relapsing disorder of compulsive drug seeking and use despite harm [120]. Koob and Volkow describe a three-stage cycle: binge and intoxication, involving reward and incentive salience in the basal ganglia; withdrawal and negative affect, involving the extended amygdala and stress systems; and preoccupation and anticipation, involving prefrontal cortex and loss of executive control [121]. The incentive-sensitisation theory proposes that repeated drug use sensitises the dopamine-dependent wanting system, so that drug cues trigger excessive craving even as liking for the drug stays the same or declines [122]. Over time, control of drug seeking is thought to shift from ventral to dorsal striatum, from goal-directed action towards habit and compulsion [123]. In people with cocaine dependence, positron emission tomography showed blunted striatal dopamine release in response to methylphenidate along with reduced self-reported euphoria [124]. The strength of evidence for a central role of dopamine differs between drug classes; it is strongest for stimulants and weaker for opioids and cannabis [125].

11Synthesis and open questions

Three conclusions stand out from this literature. First, emotion is not housed in a single limbic system but distributed across circuits that detect significant events, regulate the body and guide action, with conscious feeling likely depending on further cortical and interoceptive processing [1,41,60]. Second, reward is a family of processes: dopamine is closely tied to prediction error, learning and incentive motivation, while hedonic pleasure depends on separate opioid and other hotspots [7,93]. Third, motivational and stress systems are tightly coupled to bodily state through the hypothalamus, the insula and the HPA axis, which helps explain why physical health, stress and mood interact so strongly [72,98].

Important questions remain open. It is unclear how far circuits defined by optogenetics in rodents correspond to human emotional experience [1], how discrete emotion categories relate to the overlapping activity seen in human imaging [2,41], and how circuit-level dysfunction maps onto clinical diagnoses whose boundaries were not drawn with biology in mind [100]. Answering them will need methods that link single cell types, large-scale networks, physiology and subjective report in the same individuals.
