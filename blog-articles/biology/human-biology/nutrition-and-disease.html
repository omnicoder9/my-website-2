Nutrition and Disease

How what people eat shapes obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and malnutrition, from the first controlled trial against scurvy to the global burden of dietary risks.

Citation style: Vancouver (ICMJE)190 referencesPrepared 27 October 2026

Summary

Nutrition supplies the energy and the essential chemical building blocks the body cannot make for itself, and both too little and too much of either can cause disease [1,2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) uses "malnutrition" for all of these states: undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, and overweight with its diet-related noncommunicable diseases [3]. Poor diet is one of the largest preventable causes of death: an analysis for the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study attributed 11 million deaths and 255 million disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) in 2017 to dietary risks, led by high sodium, low whole grains and low fruit intake [4]. In 2024, 2.6 billion adults were overweight and 940 million were living with obesity [5], 589 million adults aged 20–79 had diabetes [6], cardiovascular diseases caused an estimated 19.8 million deaths in 2022 [7], and 150.2 million children under five were stunted [8].

01History

Deficiency diseases and the discovery of vitamins

In 1747, aboard HMS Salisbury, the naval surgeon James Lind divided twelve sailors with scurvy into six pairs and gave each pair a different remedy. The two men given oranges and lemons recovered fastest, and Lind published the result in his Treatise of the Scurvy in 1753 [9,10]. The Royal Navy did not issue lemon juice routinely until 1795, and confusion between lemons and less potent West Indian limes allowed scurvy to return on polar expeditions in the 19th century [10]. A usable animal model came only in 1907, when Axel Holst and Theodor Frølich produced scurvy in guinea pigs, which, like humans, cannot synthesise the antiscorbutic factor [11]. In 1932 Joseph Svirbely and Albert Szent-Györgyi identified that factor as hexuronic acid, later named ascorbic acid [12].

Beriberi gave a second line of evidence. In the 1880s the Japanese naval surgeon Kanehiro Takaki largely eliminated beriberi from the navy by replacing part of the polished-rice ration with barley, meat and milk [13]. In 1897 Christiaan Eijkman, working in the Dutch East Indies, described a beriberi-like polyneuritis in chickens fed polished rice that was prevented by rice bran [14]. In 1912 Frederick Gowland Hopkins showed that young rats fed purified diets failed to grow unless given small amounts of milk, which he attributed to "accessory food factors" [15], and Casimir Funk proposed the term "vitamine" for the anti-deficiency substances in the same year [16]. Eijkman and Hopkins shared the 1929 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine [17].

Earlier, the chemistry of nutrition had grown out of Antoine Lavoisier's demonstration that respiration is a slow combustion, and by the end of the 19th century Wilbur Atwater had measured the energy values of foods in human calorimeters [18]. The new vitamin concept then explained several endemic diseases. Elmer McCollum and Marguerite Davis reported a fat-soluble growth factor ("fat-soluble A") in 1913 [19], and McCollum's group separated an antirachitic factor, vitamin D, in 1922 [20]. Joseph Goldberger showed in 1915 that pellagra in orphanages could be prevented by changing the diet, contrary to the prevailing view that it was an infection [21], and Conrad Elvehjem identified nicotinic acid (niacin) as the curative factor in 1937 [22]. Work on goitre led from iodine treatment in the 1820s to the iodisation of salt in Switzerland and the United States in the early 1920s [23].

Protein-energy malnutrition and starvation

In 1933 Cicely Williams, working in the Gold Coast (now Ghana), described an oedematous disease of weaned young children on maize diets, and in 1935 she introduced the local Ga name "kwashiorkor" [25,26]. Between 1944 and 1945 Ancel Keys and colleagues at the University of Minnesota put 36 volunteer conscientious objectors through 24 weeks of semi-starvation followed by rehabilitation. Their two-volume report described the physical and psychological effects of losing about a quarter of body weight and remains a reference on human starvation [27].

The turn to chronic disease

After the Second World War, attention moved from deficiency to the chronic diseases of affluent populations [28,29]. Insulin had been extracted and used to treat diabetes in Toronto in 1922 [30], and in 1936 Harold Himsworth distinguished "insulin-sensitive" from "insulin-insensitive" diabetes, anticipating the modern separation of type 1 and type 2 diabetes [31]. Nikolai Anitschkow had shown in 1913 that feeding cholesterol to rabbits produced arterial lesions resembling human atherosclerosis [32].

The Framingham Heart Study began in 1948 in Massachusetts with 5,209 adults followed prospectively [33,34], and in 1961 its investigators introduced the phrase "factors of risk" for characteristics, including raised serum cholesterol and blood pressure, that predicted coronary heart disease [35]. Ancel Keys derived equations predicting the change in serum cholesterol from changes in dietary saturated and polyunsaturated fat [36], and his Seven Countries Study, begun in 1958, related population diets to coronary mortality across cohorts of middle-aged men [37,38]. In 1972 John Yudkin argued that sugar, not fat, was the main dietary cause of coronary disease [39]. Internal documents later showed that a sugar industry trade group funded a 1967 review that played down sugar and emphasised fat [40]. The 1977 Dietary Goals for the United States recommended cutting fat, saturated fat, cholesterol, sugar and salt, and set the template for later dietary guidelines [29,41].

02Key concepts

Nutrients and energy

The macronutrients (carbohydrate, fat and protein) supply energy, conventionally about 17 kJ (4 kcal) per gram for carbohydrate and protein and 37 kJ (9 kcal) per gram for fat; alcohol supplies about 29 kJ (7 kcal) per gram [1]. Essential nutrients are those the body cannot make in adequate amounts: nine indispensable amino acids, two essential fatty acids (linoleic and α-linolenic acid), thirteen vitamins and a range of minerals and trace elements [2].

Dietary reference values describe population requirements. In the North American system, the Estimated Average Requirement (EAR) meets the needs of half of healthy people in a group, the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) meets the needs of 97–98%, an Adequate Intake (AI) is used when evidence is insufficient to set an EAR, and the Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) marks the highest intake unlikely to cause harm [48]. The same framework sets acceptable macronutrient distribution ranges for adults of 45–65% of energy from carbohydrate, 20–35% from fat and 10–35% from protein [48].

Energy balance

Body energy stores change by the difference between energy intake and energy expenditure. Total energy expenditure has three main components: resting (basal) metabolic rate, which in sedentary adults is the largest; the thermic effect of food, roughly 10% of intake; and physical activity, which is the most variable [49]. Fat-free mass is the main determinant of resting expenditure [49]. Measurements with doubly labelled water in more than 6,000 people showed that expenditure adjusted for body size is stable from about 20 to 60 years of age and declines after 60 [50]. Hadza hunter-gatherers, despite high physical activity, have total daily expenditure similar to that of adults in industrialised countries once body size is accounted for, which suggests that differences in intake drive much of the modern rise in obesity [51].

Food quality

Foods with the same energy content can affect metabolism differently. The glycaemic index ranks carbohydrate foods by the rise in blood glucose they produce relative to a reference food [52]. The NOVA system classifies foods by the extent and purpose of industrial processing, and defines "ultra-processed foods" as formulations made largely from substances extracted from foods, with additives such as flavours, colours and emulsifiers [53]. In a randomised crossover trial in which 20 adults lived on a metabolic ward, an ultra-processed diet led to about 500 kcal (2.1 MJ) per day greater ad libitum intake than an unprocessed diet matched for presented calories, sugar, fat, fibre and macronutrients, and to weight gain of about 0.9 kg in two weeks [54].

How diet–disease evidence is built

Much of what is known about diet and chronic disease comes from prospective cohort studies using food-frequency questionnaires, which are subject to measurement error and confounding by other behaviours [55,56]. Randomised trials with hard clinical endpoints are expensive and rare, and critics argue that many reported associations between single foods and mortality are implausibly large [57]. Austin Bradford Hill's criteria (strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment and analogy) remain the usual framework for judging whether an association is causal [58]. Isolated nutrients do not always reproduce the benefits of foods: in the Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta-Carotene trial of 29,133 male smokers, β-carotene supplements increased lung cancer incidence by 18% [59]. Mendelian randomisation, which uses genetic variants as instruments for an exposure, has supported a causal role of higher body mass index (BMI) in type 2 diabetes and coronary heart disease [60].

Nutrition transition and the double burden

As incomes rise and populations urbanise, diets shift towards refined carbohydrates, added sugars, edible oils and animal-source foods, while physical activity falls. This "nutrition transition" has moved the burden of overweight rapidly into low- and middle-income countries [61]. Many countries, communities, households and even individuals now carry both undernutrition and overweight, a pattern called the double burden of malnutrition [62]. The Lancet Commission on obesity described obesity, undernutrition and climate change as a "global syndemic" with shared drivers in food systems [63].

Nutrition early in life also has lasting effects. Young men exposed to the Dutch famine of 1944–45 in the first half of gestation had higher rates of obesity at 19 years [64], and later follow-up of the Dutch famine birth cohort linked prenatal famine exposure to impaired glucose tolerance and coronary heart disease [65]. The gut microbiota is another modifier: microbiota from obese mice harvested more energy from food and transferred greater fat gain to germ-free recipients [66], and the glycaemic response to identical meals varies widely between people, partly in relation to their gut microbiome [67].

03Obesity

Definition and measurement

WHO defines overweight and obesity as abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that presents a risk to health [5]. In adults they are classified by BMI, weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres, a ratio described by Adolphe Quetelet in the 19th century and named "body mass index" by Keys and colleagues in 1972 [68]. Because Asian populations have a higher proportion of body fat and higher cardiometabolic risk at a given BMI, a WHO expert consultation identified lower public-health action points for these populations [69]. Children are classified against growth references using BMI-for-age [5].

BMI does not distinguish fat from lean mass or show where fat is stored. In the 52-country INTERHEART study, the waist-to-hip ratio was more strongly associated with myocardial infarction than BMI [71]. In 2025 a Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology commission proposed that excess adiposity be confirmed by direct body-fat measurement or by at least one anthropometric criterion such as waist circumference in addition to BMI, and that "clinical obesity", a chronic illness with objective organ dysfunction or limitations of daily activities caused by excess adiposity, be separated from "preclinical obesity", in which organ function is preserved [72].

Prevalence

In 2024, 45% of adults aged 18 years and over (2.6 billion) were overweight, and 940 million (16%) were living with obesity. About 35 million children under five and 400 million children and adolescents aged 5–19 years were overweight, 170 million of the latter with obesity [5]. A pooled analysis of 3,663 population-based studies estimated that more than one billion people were living with obesity in 2022, and that adult obesity prevalence had more than doubled in women and nearly tripled in men since 1990 [73].

Physiological regulation of body weight

Food intake and energy expenditure are regulated by the brain in response to signals from fat tissue and the gut [74]. Adipose tissue is an endocrine organ that secretes leptin, adiponectin and inflammatory cytokines [75]. Leptin circulates in proportion to fat mass and acts on the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus, where it stimulates anorexigenic pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons and inhibits orexigenic neurons that co-express agouti-related peptide (AgRP) and neuropeptide Y [74]. POMC neurons release α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone, which activates melanocortin-4 receptors (MC4R) in the paraventricular nucleus to reduce intake, while AgRP antagonises those receptors [74]. Insulin acts in the hypothalamus in a similar way to leptin [74]. Ghrelin, secreted by the stomach, rises before meals and falls after eating, consistent with a role in meal initiation [76].

Causes

Body weight is highly heritable. In Swedish twins reared apart, the heritability of BMI was about 0.70 in men and 0.66 in women [77]. Common variants each have small effects: carriers of two copies of the risk allele at the FTO locus weigh about 3 kg more and have 1.67-fold higher odds of obesity than non-carriers [78], and a genome-wide study of 339,224 people identified 97 BMI-associated loci that together accounted for about 2.7% of BMI variation, with genes acting in the central nervous system prominent among them [79]. Rare monogenic forms show the importance of the leptin–melanocortin pathway. Mutations in MC4R were found in 5.8% of a cohort with severe early-onset obesity [80], and children with congenital leptin deficiency develop extreme hyperphagia and obesity [47] that is reversed by recombinant leptin [81].

The rapid rise in obesity since the 1980s, too fast to reflect genetic change, has been attributed mainly to changes in the global food system, with increased supply of cheap, palatable, energy-dense and heavily marketed foods acting on susceptible people [82]. Diets high in ultra-processed foods increase ad libitum energy intake under controlled conditions [54], and sugar-sweetened beverages are associated with weight gain [83].

The body defends against weight loss. In the classic studies of Leibel and colleagues, maintaining a body weight 10% below the usual weight reduced total energy expenditure by more than predicted from the change in body composition [84]. Six years after a televised weight-loss competition, participants' resting metabolic rate was still about 500 kcal per day lower than expected for their body size [85]. One year after diet-induced weight loss, circulating leptin, peptide YY and cholecystokinin remained lower, ghrelin higher, and reported hunger greater than at baseline [86].

Consequences

In an individual-participant meta-analysis of 239 prospective studies among never-smokers without pre-existing chronic disease, all-cause mortality was lowest at a BMI of 20–25 and rose steadily above that range [87]. Higher-than-optimal BMI caused an estimated 3.7 million deaths in 2023, mainly from cardiovascular disease, diabetes and cancers [5]. The International Agency for Research on Cancer has concluded that excess body fat causes cancer at 13 sites, including the oesophagus (adenocarcinoma), colorectum, liver, pancreas, kidney, post-menopausal breast and endometrium [88]. Excess fat in the liver, now called metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [89], and in muscle impairs insulin action [90]. Obese adipose tissue shows increased expression of tumour necrosis factor-α [91] and infiltration by macrophages [92], which link obesity to chronic low-grade inflammation and insulin resistance. Mendelian randomisation analyses support causal effects of BMI and waist-to-hip ratio on type 2 diabetes and coronary heart disease [60].

Treatment

Diet composition matters less for weight loss than sustained energy deficit. In the DIETFITS trial of 609 adults, a healthy low-fat and a healthy low-carbohydrate diet produced similar 12-month weight loss (5.3 and 6.0 kg), and neither genotype pattern nor baseline insulin secretion predicted which diet worked better [93]. In Look AHEAD, intensive lifestyle intervention in 5,145 adults with type 2 diabetes produced 8.6% weight loss at one year versus 0.7% with diabetes support and education, but the trial was stopped for futility after a median of 9.6 years without a reduction in cardiovascular events [94]. Pharmacotherapy based on gut hormones has changed management. Weekly semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, reduced body weight by 14.9% versus 2.4% with placebo over 68 weeks [95], and tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide and GLP-1 receptor agonist, by up to 20.9% at 72 weeks [96]. In the SELECT trial of 17,604 adults with cardiovascular disease and overweight or obesity but without diabetes, semaglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events by 20% [97]. In the Swedish Obese Subjects study, bariatric surgery was associated with lower long-term mortality than usual care (adjusted hazard ratio 0.71) [98]. Reviews of management recommend combining these approaches by severity and complications [99,100].

04Type 2 diabetes

Definition and diagnosis

Type 2 diabetes is characterised by a progressive loss of adequate β-cell insulin secretion, frequently on a background of insulin resistance, and accounts for 90–95% of all diabetes [101]. Diagnosis rests on plasma glucose or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), which WHO endorsed as a diagnostic test in 2011 [101,102].

Burden

The International Diabetes Federation estimates that 589 million adults aged 20–79, about one in nine, were living with diabetes in 2024, that this number will reach 853 million by 2050, that about 252 million people are undiagnosed, and that diabetes causes over 3.4 million deaths a year and more than US$1 trillion in health spending [6]. A pooled analysis using a broader age range estimated 828 million adults with diabetes of all types in 2022, with the largest increases in low- and middle-income countries, where most people with diabetes were not treated [103]. People of East Asian and South Asian ancestry develop type 2 diabetes at lower BMI than people of European ancestry [104].

Pathophysiology

Type 2 diabetes results when β-cells can no longer secrete enough insulin to compensate for insulin resistance [105]. Gerald Reaven described insulin resistance as a central feature that clusters with hypertension, dyslipidaemia and glucose intolerance [106], a cluster now harmonised as the metabolic syndrome [107]. The hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp, developed by DeFronzo and colleagues, became the reference method for measuring insulin sensitivity [108]. In 1963 Philip Randle proposed that increased fatty acid oxidation inhibits muscle glucose use [109]; more recent work implicates accumulation of lipid intermediates such as diacylglycerol in liver and muscle, which activate protein kinase C isoforms and impair insulin signalling [90]. DeFronzo has emphasised that, beyond muscle, liver and β-cells, adipocytes, gut incretins, pancreatic α-cells, kidney and brain all contribute to hyperglycaemia [110].

Insulin secretion is inadequate from early in the disease. Autopsy studies found that β-cell volume was 63% lower in obese people with type 2 diabetes than in weight-matched people without diabetes, with increased β-cell apoptosis [111]. In the UK Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS), β-cell function was already about half of normal at diagnosis and continued to decline whatever the treatment [112]. Roy Taylor's "twin cycle" hypothesis proposes that excess fat in the liver drives hepatic insulin resistance and that fat accumulating in the pancreas impairs β-cell function [113]. In support, eight weeks of a very-low-energy diet normalised fasting glucose and first-phase insulin secretion together with falls in liver and pancreas fat [114].

Genetic and early-life factors

Variants in TCF7L2, first reported in 2006, have the largest common-variant effect on type 2 diabetes risk [115]. By 2018, fine-mapping in nearly 900,000 people had identified 243 risk loci, many acting through β-cell function [116]. Neel's "thrifty genotype" and Hales and Barker's "thrifty phenotype" hypotheses both try to explain why diabetes is common when food is plentiful, the first through selection by past famines and the second through fetal undernutrition [42,45].

Diet and prevention

Adiposity is the strongest modifiable risk factor [60,105]. Each daily serving of sugar-sweetened beverages was associated with an 18% higher incidence of type 2 diabetes, or 13% after adjustment for adiposity [117]. In three US cohorts with an updated meta-analysis, each 100 g per day of unprocessed red meat was associated with a 19% higher risk, and each 50 g per day of processed meat with a 51% higher risk [118]. Higher intakes of dietary fibre and whole grains were associated with 15–30% lower incidence of type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, stroke and all-cause mortality [119].

Three randomised trials showed that lifestyle change prevents or delays type 2 diabetes in people with impaired glucose tolerance. The Da Qing study in China reduced incidence with diet, exercise or both [120]. The Finnish Diabetes Prevention Study [121] and the US Diabetes Prevention Program [122] both found that lifestyle intervention reduced incidence by 58%; in the US trial metformin reduced it by 31% [122].

Remission

Type 2 diabetes was long considered irreversible. In the DiRECT trial in UK primary care, a programme of total diet replacement (about 825–853 kcal per day for 3–5 months) followed by food reintroduction and weight-maintenance support achieved remission at 12 months in 46% of participants versus 4% of controls; remission occurred in 86% of those who lost 15 kg or more [123].

Complications and treatment

Chronic hyperglycaemia damages blood vessels through overproduction of mitochondrial superoxide, which activates the polyol, hexosamine, protein kinase C and advanced glycation end-product pathways [124]. The results are microvascular disease (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and accelerated atherosclerosis. Across 102 prospective studies, diabetes roughly doubled the risk of coronary heart disease and ischaemic stroke [125]. In the UKPDS, intensive glucose control reduced microvascular complications [126], and in its observational analysis each 1 percentage-point reduction in HbA1c was associated with 37% fewer microvascular complications and 14% fewer myocardial infarctions [127]. Newer glucose-lowering drugs also protect the heart and kidney: in EMPA-REG OUTCOME, the sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor empagliflozin reduced cardiovascular death by 38% [128], and in LEADER, the GLP-1 receptor agonist liraglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events [129]. Current management combines lifestyle, weight management and pharmacotherapy chosen by cardiovascular and renal risk [130].

05Cardiovascular disease

Burden

Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of death worldwide. An estimated 19.8 million people died from them in 2022, about 32% of all deaths; 85% of these deaths were due to heart attack and stroke, and more than three-quarters occurred in low- and middle-income countries [7]. In the GBD analysis of cardiovascular burden for 1990–2022, high systolic blood pressure was the largest single contributor, and dietary risks, high low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and high BMI were among the other leading modifiable risks [131].

Atherosclerosis

Most cardiovascular deaths result from atherosclerosis, a chronic inflammatory disease of large and medium arteries [132,133]. Lipoproteins containing apolipoprotein B, chiefly LDL, enter and are retained in the arterial intima, where they are modified and taken up by macrophages that become lipid-laden foam cells. Smooth muscle cells form a fibrous cap over the growing lipid core, and rupture or erosion of that cap triggers the thrombosis that causes most heart attacks and many strokes [133]. Genetic, epidemiological and trial evidence shows that LDL causes atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, with risk proportional to both the magnitude and the duration of exposure [134]. Mutations in the LDL receptor cause familial hypercholesterolaemia and premature coronary disease [43]. Cholesterol lowering with statins reduced total mortality by 30% in the Scandinavian Simvastatin Survival Study of 4,444 patients with coronary heart disease [135], and across 26 trials each 1.0 mmol/L reduction in LDL cholesterol lowered the risk of major vascular events by about 22% [136].

Blood pressure and salt

In a meta-analysis of individual data from one million adults, mortality from ischaemic heart disease and stroke doubled with each 20 mmHg higher usual systolic blood pressure (or 10 mmHg diastolic) from 115/75 mmHg upward, in people aged 40–69 [137]. The number of adults aged 30–79 with hypertension reached 1.28 billion in 2019, about half of whom were undiagnosed [138]. The Intersalt study of more than 10,000 adults in 52 centres related 24-hour urinary sodium to blood pressure and its rise with age; in four remote populations with very low salt intake, blood pressure was low and barely rose with age [139].

Feeding trials established that diet lowers blood pressure. The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, rich in fruits, vegetables and low-fat dairy and reduced in saturated fat, lowered blood pressure by 11.4/5.5 mmHg in participants with hypertension [140], and reducing sodium lowered blood pressure further on both the DASH and a typical American diet [141]. A Cochrane review found that a modest reduction in salt intake of about 4.4 g per day for at least four weeks lowered blood pressure by about 4.2/2.1 mmHg [142]. In the Salt Substitute and Stroke Study, 20,995 people in rural China with previous stroke, or aged 60 or over with hypertension, were randomised by village to a salt substitute (75% sodium chloride, 25% potassium chloride) or regular salt; the substitute reduced stroke by 14%, major cardiovascular events by 13% and death by 12% [143]. WHO recommends that adults consume less than 2 g of sodium (5 g of salt) per day [144], but global mean intake is about 4.3 g of sodium (10.8 g of salt) [145].

Dietary fats

Keys and colleagues showed in metabolic-ward studies that saturated fatty acids raise serum cholesterol and polyunsaturated fatty acids lower it [36], and a WHO-commissioned regression analysis of 84 trials confirmed that replacing saturated with unsaturated fat, especially polyunsaturated fat, lowers LDL cholesterol [146]. Industrially produced trans fatty acids both raise LDL and lower high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol [147]; in prospective studies, 2% of energy from trans fat was associated with a 23% higher incidence of coronary heart disease [148]. An American Heart Association advisory concluded that replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated vegetable oil reduced cardiovascular disease by about 30% in the core randomised trials [149]. A 2020 Cochrane review found that reducing saturated fat for at least two years reduced combined cardiovascular events by 17%, with little or no effect on all-cause or cardiovascular mortality [150]. What replaces saturated fat matters: in the Women's Health Initiative trial of 48,835 postmenopausal women, a low-fat dietary pattern did not significantly reduce coronary heart disease or stroke [151].

Dietary patterns

Whole dietary patterns have been tested more successfully than single nutrients. In the Lyon Diet Heart Study of patients after a first myocardial infarction, a Mediterranean-type diet rich in α-linolenic acid reduced recurrent coronary events by 50–70% [152]. In PREDIMED, 7,447 Spanish adults at high cardiovascular risk were assigned to a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts, or to a control diet with advice to reduce fat; both Mediterranean arms had about 30% fewer major cardiovascular events. The trial was retracted and republished in 2018 after irregularities in randomisation were found, with similar results in reanalyses [153]. In a dose–response meta-analysis, the risk of cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality fell with fruit and vegetable intake up to about 800 g per day [154].

Population evidence

Comparisons of populations and migrants show that cardiovascular risk is largely environmental. Among men of Japanese ancestry, the incidence of coronary heart disease rose stepwise from Japan to Hawaii to California [156]. In the INTERHEART case–control study in 52 countries, nine potentially modifiable factors, including abnormal lipids, hypertension, diabetes, abdominal obesity and low daily consumption of fruits and vegetables, accounted for about 90% of the population-attributable risk of myocardial infarction in men and 94% in women [157]. In eastern Finland, where the North Karelia Project began community-wide prevention in 1972, coronary heart disease mortality among working-aged adults fell by 82% in men and 84% in women by 2012, and falls in serum cholesterol, blood pressure and smoking explained much of the decline [158]. The gut microbiota may also link diet to atherosclerosis: microbial metabolism of dietary phosphatidylcholine generates trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), which promoted atherosclerosis in mice and was associated with cardiovascular disease in humans [159].

06Malnutrition

Definitions

WHO defines malnutrition as deficiencies, excesses or imbalances in a person's intake of energy and/or nutrients, and groups it into undernutrition (wasting, stunting and underweight), micronutrient-related malnutrition, and overweight, obesity and diet-related noncommunicable diseases [3]. Child anthropometric indicators are expressed as z-scores against the WHO Child Growth Standards, which were derived from healthy, breastfed children in six countries [160].

Burden and causes

Undernutrition is linked to about 45% of deaths among children under five [3]. In 2011 fetal growth restriction, stunting, wasting, deficiencies of vitamin A and zinc, and suboptimal breastfeeding together caused about 3.1 million child deaths [162]. Progress has been uneven: stunting has fallen in most regions, but more slowly in sub-Saharan Africa, and wasting has changed little [163]. In 2024 about 8.2% of the world's population, roughly 673 million people, were undernourished, down from 8.5% in 2023 [164].

The UNICEF conceptual framework, published in 1990, separates the immediate causes of child undernutrition (inadequate dietary intake and disease, which reinforce each other) from underlying household and community causes and from basic social, economic and political causes [162,165].

Physiology of starvation

In starvation, liver glycogen sustains blood glucose for about a day, after which glucose is made by gluconeogenesis, largely from amino acids released from muscle protein. As fasting continues, the liver produces ketone bodies from fatty acids, and the brain's use of ketones spares body protein and prolongs survival [166]. The Minnesota experiment showed that semi-starvation in healthy men reduced resting metabolic rate and caused weakness, oedema, depression and preoccupation with food [27].

Severe acute malnutrition

Severe childhood malnutrition presents as non-oedematous wasting (marasmus) or as oedematous malnutrition (kwashiorkor), with oedema, skin and hair changes, and fatty liver [167]. Why some children develop kwashiorkor rather than marasmus on similar diets is not fully understood. In Malawian twin pairs discordant for kwashiorkor, faecal microbiota from the affected twin, transplanted into germ-free mice fed a Malawian diet, caused marked weight loss together with metabolic disturbances [168].

Before the 2000s, most children with severe acute malnutrition were treated in hospital. The development of ready-to-use therapeutic foods, energy-dense pastes that do not need water or cooking, allowed children without complications to be treated at home through community-based management [169]. WHO guidance now covers both community and inpatient care, and the 2023 guideline extended it to the prevention and management of wasting and nutritional oedema in infants and children under five [161,170]. Re-feeding severely malnourished patients too quickly can cause refeeding syndrome, with hypophosphataemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, fluid retention and thiamine deficiency [171].

Long-term consequences

Undernutrition in early childhood is associated with shorter adult height, less schooling, lower economic productivity and lower birthweight of the next generation [172]. In Guatemala, men who as young children had received a protein-energy supplement in a randomised community trial earned 46% higher hourly wages as adults [173]. Fetal undernutrition is also linked to later obesity, diabetes and cardiovascular disease [44,45,65].

Micronutrient deficiencies

A pooled analysis of population surveys estimated that 56% of preschool-aged children (372 million) and 69% of non-pregnant women of reproductive age (1.2 billion) worldwide were deficient in at least one of three key micronutrients [174]. Anaemia affected 1.92 billion people in 2021, 24.3% of the global population, and dietary iron deficiency was its leading cause [175]. Vitamin A supplementation reduced childhood mortality by 34% in a community trial in Aceh, Indonesia [176], and by 12% in a Cochrane meta-analysis of later trials [177]. Iodine deficiency is the world's most common preventable cause of brain damage and is controlled mainly by salt iodisation [178]. Mandatory folic acid fortification of cereal products in the United States was followed by a 35% fall in neural tube defects, about 1,300 affected births prevented each year [179].

Disease-related malnutrition and the double burden

In high-income settings malnutrition often accompanies illness, ageing and hospital admission. The Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria diagnose it in adults by combining at least one phenotypic criterion (unintentional weight loss, low BMI or reduced muscle mass) with at least one aetiological criterion (reduced food intake or assimilation, or inflammation and disease burden) [180]. Undernutrition and overweight increasingly coexist in the same populations, households and individuals, for example a stunted child with an overweight mother, or an adult with obesity and iron deficiency [62].

07Dietary risk factors

The global burden of dietary risks

The GBD 2017 diet study estimated the deaths attributable to fifteen dietary risks in 195 countries. In 2017, 11 million deaths and 255 million DALYs were attributable to dietary risks. Cardiovascular disease accounted for most diet-related deaths (about 10 million), followed by cancers and type 2 diabetes. High sodium intake (3 million deaths), low intake of whole grains (3 million) and low intake of fruits (2 million) were the leading risks [4]. In the GBD 2021 comparative risk assessment of 88 risk factors, the diet-related metabolic risks of high systolic blood pressure, high fasting plasma glucose and high BMI were among the leading causes of DALYs, and the burden attributable to high BMI and high fasting glucose increased substantially between 2000 and 2021 [181].

Specific risks

Sodium. High salt intake raises blood pressure, and reducing it lowers blood pressure and cardiovascular events, as described in section 05 [142,143].

Low whole grains, fibre, fruit and vegetables. Higher fibre and whole-grain intakes are associated with lower mortality and lower incidence of coronary heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer, with the greatest benefit at fibre intakes of about 25–29 g per day or more [119]. Fruit and vegetable intake shows a dose–response relation with lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [154].

Red and processed meat. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classified processed meat as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) and red meat as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A), estimating an 18% higher risk of colorectal cancer per 50 g of processed meat eaten daily [182]. Processed meat is also associated with type 2 diabetes [118].

Free sugars and sugar-sweetened beverages. WHO strongly recommends limiting free sugars to less than 10% of energy intake, and conditionally recommends less than 5% [183]. Meta-analyses of trials show that changing sugar intake changes body weight in adults, an effect explained by the accompanying change in energy intake [184]. Sugar-sweetened beverages are associated with weight gain, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease [83,117].

Ultra-processed foods. An umbrella review of 45 pooled analyses found convincing evidence that higher ultra-processed food intake is associated with higher risks of cardiovascular disease-related death and type 2 diabetes, among other adverse outcomes [185]. Trial evidence for a causal effect is limited, though controlled feeding shows that these foods increase energy intake [54].

Trans fats and fat quality. Industrial trans fats are the most harmful dietary fats per gram of energy [148], and replacing saturated with polyunsaturated fat lowers cardiovascular risk [149,150].

Alcohol. The GBD 2016 alcohol study found that the level of consumption that minimised overall health loss was zero [186]. WHO estimates that alcohol caused 2.6 million deaths in 2019 [187].

Healthy diets and policy

WHO advises adults to eat at least 400 g of fruit and vegetables a day, to keep free sugars below 10% of energy, total fat below 30%, saturated fat below 10% and trans fat below 1% of energy, and to eat less than 5 g of salt a day [188]. Reviews of the evidence rank fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes, fish, vegetable oils, whole grains and yogurt among foods to increase, and processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, refined starches and sodium among those to reduce [155]. The EAT–Lancet Commission proposed a "planetary health diet" rich in plant foods with low amounts of animal foods, designed to be both healthy and environmentally sustainable [189].

Individual advice alone has limited effect when food environments promote overconsumption [63,82]. Population measures that have shown effects include taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages, where a 10% tax was associated with about a 10% fall in purchases and intake [190]; reformulation and salt substitution to lower sodium [143,145]; elimination of industrial trans fats [148]; and fortification of staple foods with iodine and folic acid [178,179]. Community-wide risk-factor programmes such as North Karelia show that population diets and cardiovascular mortality can change together over decades [158].
