Human Aging

How cells, organs and whole people change with time, why risk of disease and death rises so steeply in later life, and what is known about extending healthy years.

Citation style: Vancouver (ICMJE)154 referencesPrepared 27 October 2026

Summary

Aging, or biological senescence, is the progressive, time-dependent loss of physiological integrity that impairs function and increases vulnerability to death [1]. In adult humans the risk of dying rises roughly exponentially with age, a regularity first described by Benjamin Gompertz in 1825 [2,3]. Chronological age is the largest single risk factor for most chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, cancer and neurodegeneration, which is the central premise of geroscience [4,5]. Twelve interconnected "hallmarks" of aging, including genomic instability, telomere attrition and cellular senescence, are now used to organize its molecular causes [1]. The world's population is aging fast: by 2030 one person in six will be aged 60 or over, and the number aged 80 or over is expected to triple between 2020 and 2050 to 426 million [6].

01History

Quantitative study of aging began with actuaries rather than biologists. In 1825 Benjamin Gompertz, analysing life tables, proposed that the "force of mortality" in adults increases in geometric progression with age [2]. In 1860 William Makeham added an age-independent term for deaths from causes unrelated to aging, giving the Gompertz–Makeham law that still underlies demographic models [3,7].

Evolutionary explanations followed. August Weismann argued in the 1880s that aging arises because somatic cells, unlike the germ line, have a limited capacity for renewal [8]. Peter Medawar proposed in 1952 that the force of natural selection weakens with age, because few individuals in the wild survive long enough for late-acting harmful mutations to matter, so such mutations accumulate [9]. George Williams extended this in 1957 with "antagonistic pleiotropy": genes that improve early-life fitness can be favoured even if they cause harm later [10]. Thomas Kirkwood's "disposable soma" theory (1977) framed aging as the outcome of an evolved trade-off between investment in reproduction and in somatic maintenance and repair [11,12].

Mechanistic ideas developed in parallel. Clive McCay showed in 1935 that rats fed a restricted diet lived longer than freely fed controls [13]. Denham Harman's free radical theory (1956) proposed that aging results from cumulative damage by reactive oxygen species [14], although later experiments that manipulated antioxidant defences in mice often failed to change lifespan [15]. The Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging, begun by Nathan Shock in 1958, showed that physiological decline varies widely between individuals and that cross-sectional data can mislead [16].

  1. 1961Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead show that normal human fibroblasts stop dividing after a finite number of population doublings, overturning the belief that cultured cells are immortal [17]. Hayflick later put the limit for fetal cells at about 50 ± 10 doublings, now called the Hayflick limit [18,19].
  2. 1972–73James Watson identifies the "end-replication problem" of linear DNA [20], and Alexey Olovnikov proposes that the resulting loss of chromosome ends could count cell divisions [21].
  3. 1980James Fries proposes the "compression of morbidity": illness could be squeezed into a shorter period before a relatively fixed end of life [22].
  4. 1983–93The first long-lived mutants of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans are isolated [23]. Mutation of age-1 extends lifespan [24], and Cynthia Kenyon's group shows that daf-2 mutants live about twice as long as wild type, establishing that aging is genetically regulated by insulin/IGF-1 signalling [25,26].
  5. 1985Carol Greider and Elizabeth Blackburn discover telomerase [27]. Blackburn, Greider and Jack Szostak share the 2009 Nobel Prize for work on telomeres and telomerase [28].
  6. 1990Telomeres are shown to shorten as human fibroblasts age in culture [29]. In 1998, adding the telomerase catalytic subunit (hTERT) to normal cells extends their replicative life span [30].
  7. 1997Jeanne Calment dies in France aged 122 years and 164 days, the longest fully documented human life [31].
  8. 2001Linda Fried and colleagues define a frailty phenotype [32], and Arnold Mitnitski and Kenneth Rockwood introduce the deficit-accumulation frailty index [33].
  9. 2011Genetic removal of p16Ink4a-expressing senescent cells delays age-related disorders in mice [34].
  10. 2013López-Otín and colleagues publish "The hallmarks of aging" [35], and Steve Horvath describes a multi-tissue DNA methylation "epigenetic clock" [36]. The hallmarks are expanded from nine to twelve in 2023 [1].

02Key concepts

Lifespan, life expectancy and healthspan

Life expectancy is a population statistic: the average number of years a person would live if current age-specific death rates persisted. Maximum lifespan is the longest life observed for a species, about 122 years in humans [31]. Healthspan is the portion of life spent in good health; the WHO measures it as health-adjusted life expectancy (HALE) [37]. Across 183 WHO member states the average gap between life expectancy and healthspan was estimated at 9.6 years in 2019, and was largest in the United States at 12.4 years [38].

The Gompertz law of mortality

Gompertz's law states that the hazard of death μ at age x rises exponentially, and Makeham's addition gives a constant background term A [2,7]:

μ(x) = A + B·eG·x    mortality-rate doubling time = ln 2 / G

In humans the adult mortality rate doubles roughly every 8 years [3,39]. The exponential rise does not continue indefinitely: in high-quality Italian data, the annual hazard levels off after about age 105 at roughly 50% per year, a "mortality plateau" [40].

Why do we age? Evolutionary theory

Modern evolutionary theory treats aging as a by-product of the declining force of natural selection with age rather than an adaptive programme [9–11]. The mutation-accumulation, antagonistic-pleiotropy and disposable-soma models are not mutually exclusive, and all predict that species facing low extrinsic mortality should evolve slower aging [12].

The hallmarks of aging

The 2023 update of the hallmarks framework lists twelve features that appear during normal aging, accelerate aging when experimentally aggravated, and slow aging when experimentally reduced [1]. They are grouped as primary hallmarks (sources of damage), antagonistic hallmarks (responses that are protective in moderation but harmful when chronic), and integrative hallmarks (the resulting failures of tissue homeostasis) [1,35].

Geroscience

Geroscience is the hypothesis that because the same aging mechanisms drive many chronic diseases, targeting those mechanisms could delay several diseases at once, rather than treating each separately [4]. It underlies trial proposals such as Targeting Aging with Metformin (TAME), which would use a composite of age-related diseases as its outcome [41].

Biological age and biomarkers of aging

People of the same chronological age differ in physiological condition, and much effort has gone into estimating "biological age" [42]. The most studied tools are DNA methylation clocks. First-generation clocks (Horvath, Hannum) were trained to predict chronological age [36,43]. Second-generation clocks such as PhenoAge and GrimAge were trained on clinical markers or mortality, and predict health outcomes better [44,45]. DunedinPACE estimates the rate of aging from longitudinal decline across organ systems [46].

Physiological reserve

Many organs show little change at rest with age but a markedly reduced capacity to respond to stress, such as exercise, infection or surgery [42,47]. This narrowing of reserve, rather than baseline failure, explains why older people are more likely to decompensate under challenge and is the physiological basis of frailty [48].

03Cellular senescence

Cellular senescence is a state of stable cell-cycle arrest in which the cell stays alive and metabolically active, resists apoptosis, and changes its gene expression and secretion [49,50]. It was first recognized as the replicative limit of cultured fibroblasts [17], but it can be triggered by many stresses, not only repeated division [49].

Triggers

  • Replicative (telomere-dependent) senescence. DNA polymerases cannot fully copy chromosome ends, so telomeres shorten with each division in cells without telomerase [20,21,29]. Critically short telomeres are recognized as DNA damage and activate a persistent DNA damage response (DDR) [51]. Expressing telomerase prevents this form of senescence [30].
  • Oncogene-induced senescence. Activated oncogenes such as RAS cause premature senescence with accumulation of p53 and p16INK4a, a barrier against cancer [52].
  • Stress-induced senescence. DNA-damaging agents including radiation and chemotherapy, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction can also induce senescence [49,53].

Molecular mechanisms

Two tumour-suppressor pathways enforce the arrest. The DDR activates p53, which induces the cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitor p21CIP1. Independently, p16INK4a inhibits CDK4 and CDK6. Both routes keep the retinoblastoma protein (Rb) in its active, hypophosphorylated form, which represses E2F-driven genes needed for S phase [49,50]. In some senescent cells, Rb directs the formation of senescence-associated heterochromatin foci that silence proliferation genes [54].

No single marker identifies senescence, so a combination is recommended: cell-cycle arrest plus markers such as p16INK4a or p21, senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity detectable at pH 6.0, loss of nuclear lamin B1 and secretory features [49,55].

The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)

Senescent cells secrete a mix of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines (such as interleukin-6 and interleukin-8), growth factors and matrix-degrading proteases, termed the senescence-associated secretory phenotype [56]. Cytoplasmic chromatin fragments in senescent cells activate the cGAS–STING DNA-sensing pathway, which helps drive the SASP [57]. Through the SASP, a small number of senescent cells can alter their tissue environment, reinforce senescence in neighbouring cells, and contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation in old age ("inflammaging") [53,56,58].

Beneficial roles

Senescence is not simply a defect. It is a potent tumour-suppressive mechanism that halts proliferation of damaged or oncogene-activated cells [52,59]. Programmed senescence occurs in the mammalian embryo and contributes to tissue patterning [60,61]. In adult tissues, transient senescent cells promote wound closure through secretion of PDGF-AA [62], and senescence of activated hepatic stellate cells limits liver fibrosis [63]. These beneficial effects generally depend on the senescent cells being cleared by the immune system afterwards [53].

Senescent cells in aging tissues

Markers of senescence increase with age in many tissues. Expression of p16INK4a rises with age in most rodent tissues [64] and in human peripheral blood T cells, where it also increases with smoking and physical inactivity [65]. In baboon skin, fibroblasts carrying telomere-associated DNA damage foci rose to more than 15% of cells in very old animals [66]. Antagonistic pleiotropy offers an explanation: a tumour-suppressive response useful in youth may harm tissue in old age as senescent cells accumulate [59].

Evidence that senescent cells drive aging

Causal evidence comes from mouse models in which p16Ink4a-positive cells can be selectively killed. In progeroid BubR1-deficient mice, clearing these cells delayed age-related disease of fat, skeletal muscle and the eye [34]. In normally aging mice, their removal delayed tumour formation, reduced age-related deterioration of kidney, heart and fat, and extended median lifespan by about a quarter [67]. Transplanting small numbers of senescent cells into young mice was enough to cause persistent physical dysfunction [68].

Senotherapeutics

Senolytics are drugs that selectively kill senescent cells by disabling their pro-survival networks. The first, identified in 2015, were the tyrosine-kinase inhibitor dasatinib and the flavonoid quercetin [69]. Given intermittently to old mice, this combination improved physical function and increased median post-treatment lifespan by 36% [68]. Early human studies have been small and short: an open-label pilot in 14 people with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis reported improved physical function [70], and a trial in diabetic kidney disease showed reduced senescent-cell burden in adipose tissue and skin [71]. Larger randomized trials are needed before senolytics can be considered clinically effective [72]. Senomorphics, by contrast, suppress the SASP without killing the cell [53].

04Changes in organ function

Organ aging is heterogeneous: different systems decline at different rates, and individuals of the same age vary widely [16,42]. Longitudinal data are important because cross-sectional comparisons confound age with birth cohort and selective survival [16,73]. Table 1 summarizes characteristic changes.

Cardiovascular system

The large arteries stiffen with age because of intimal–medial thickening, elastin fragmentation, collagen deposition and endothelial dysfunction, which raises pulse-wave velocity [74]. In the Framingham Heart Study, systolic pressure rose steadily from age 30 to 84, whereas diastolic pressure rose until about age 50 to 60 and then fell, widening the pulse pressure [75]. In the heart, left ventricular wall thickness increases, early diastolic filling slows and filling becomes more dependent on atrial contraction, while resting ejection fraction is preserved [47]. Responsiveness to β-adrenergic stimulation declines [47]. Maximal heart rate falls predictably with age and is better estimated as 208 − 0.7 × age than by the older "220 − age" formula [76]. In the Baltimore Longitudinal Study, peak aerobic capacity declined 3–6% per decade in the 20s and 30s but more than 20% per decade from the 70s onward [77].

Respiratory system

Lung function peaks in early adulthood and then declines [79,94]. Loss of lung elastic recoil, stiffening of the chest wall and weakening of respiratory muscles reduce forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and vital capacity, while residual volume increases [78]. The Global Lung Function Initiative reference equations, which cover ages 3 to 95, incorporate this age dependence so that older adults are not mislabelled as having disease [94].

Kidneys

In the Baltimore Longitudinal Study, creatinine clearance declined on average by about 0.75 mL/min per year, but about one-third of participants showed no decline over the follow-up period [73]. Structural changes include nephrosclerosis (glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis and arteriosclerosis) and loss of nephrons [80]. Reduced renal reserve matters for drug dosing and for vulnerability to acute kidney injury [80].

Skeletal muscle and sarcopenia

Irwin Rosenberg coined the term sarcopenia for age-related loss of muscle [95]. A quantitative review estimated that at age 75 muscle mass is lost at about 0.6–1% per year, while strength is lost at 2.5–4% per year [81]. In the Health ABC Study, strength declined about three times faster than mass, and maintaining or gaining mass did not prevent the loss of strength, implying that muscle quality also deteriorates [82]. Whole-muscle studies of the vastus lateralis attributed most age-related atrophy to a reduced number of fibres [96]. The revised European consensus (EWGSOP2) therefore makes low muscle strength the primary criterion for sarcopenia, confirmed by low muscle quantity or quality [97].

Bone

After peak bone mass is reached in young adulthood, bone resorption exceeds formation. Women undergo an accelerated phase of loss after menopause driven by oestrogen deficiency, and both sexes undergo a slower, age-related phase [83,84]. The WHO defines osteoporosis as a bone mineral density T-score of −2.5 or less [98]. In a Swedish population, the remaining lifetime risk of an osteoporotic fracture at age 50 was about 46% for women and 22% for men [99].

Brain and cognition

Lifespan brain charts built from over 100,000 MRI scans show that grey matter volume peaks in childhood (around age 6) and white matter volume around age 29, with decline thereafter that accelerates in later life [100]. Atrophy is most pronounced in frontal and medial temporal regions [85]. Neuron loss is modest: stereological counts estimated that the neocortex loses about 10% of its neurons between ages 20 and 90 [101], so synaptic and white matter changes are thought to matter more. Processing speed declines across adulthood and may account for much of the age-related variance in other cognitive abilities [86].

Sensory systems

The crystalline lens grows and stiffens throughout life, progressively reducing accommodation until near vision requires correction (presbyopia) in middle age [87]. In US national survey data, 63% of adults aged 70 or over had hearing loss [88].

Immune system

Immunosenescence describes the remodelling of the immune system with age [89]. The thymus involutes from early life onward, reducing output of new naïve T cells [90], and the T-cell repertoire becomes less diverse [89]. Older adults produce weaker antibody responses to influenza vaccine than younger adults [102]. At the same time, a chronic, low-grade, sterile inflammatory state, called inflammaging, develops and predicts disability and mortality [58,103].

Endocrine and metabolic systems

Menopause, the permanent end of menstruation caused by depletion of ovarian follicles, occurs at a median age of about 51 in high-income countries [91]. In men, total and free testosterone decline gradually; in the Baltimore Longitudinal Study, the proportion with hypogonadal total testosterone was about 20% over age 60, 30% over 70 and 50% over 80 [92]. Serum DHEA-S peaks in early adulthood and then falls steadily in both sexes [93]. Glucose tolerance worsens with age through increased insulin resistance and impaired β-cell function, contributing to a high prevalence of type 2 diabetes in older adults [104].

05Frailty and age-related disease

Frailty

Frailty is a clinical state of increased vulnerability to stressors, resulting from cumulative decline across multiple physiological systems; minor events such as an infection or a new drug can trigger disproportionate deterioration [48,105]. An international consensus defined physical frailty as "a medical syndrome with multiple causes and contributors that is characterized by diminished strength, endurance, and reduced physiologic function that increases an individual's vulnerability for developing increased dependency and/or death" [106]. In the Cardiovascular Health Study of 5,317 adults aged 65 or over, 7% met the phenotype criteria; frailty predicted incident falls, worsening mobility and disability, hospitalization and death [32]. Fried and colleagues proposed a self-reinforcing "cycle of frailty" linking sarcopenia, reduced energy expenditure and undernutrition (Figure 4) [32]. A systematic review of community studies found a weighted average prevalence of 10.7%, with wide variation depending on the definition used [108]. Rockwood's Clinical Frailty Scale grades frailty from very fit to severely frail by clinical judgement and is widely used in hospitals [107]. Simple performance measures carry strong prognostic information: in pooled data from nine cohorts, each 0.1 m/s faster usual gait speed was associated with lower mortality (hazard ratio 0.88) [109].

Geriatric syndromes

Falls, delirium, incontinence, pressure ulcers and functional decline are called geriatric syndromes because each has several interacting causes rather than a single disease origin, and they share risk factors such as older age, cognitive impairment, functional impairment and impaired mobility [110]. Falls are a major cause of injury death worldwide, accounting for an estimated 684,000 deaths each year, with adults over 60 suffering the largest number of fatal falls [111]. For older adults admitted to hospital, comprehensive geriatric assessment increases the likelihood of being alive and living at home at follow-up [112].

Age-related disease and multimorbidity

Age is the dominant risk factor for the leading non-communicable diseases [4,5]. Multiple conditions commonly coexist: in a Scottish primary-care population of 1.75 million, multimorbidity (two or more chronic conditions) was present in 65% of people aged 65–84 and 82% of those aged 85 or over [113].

  • Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death globally, with an estimated 18.6 million deaths in 2019 [114]. Arterial aging forms a substrate on which hypertension, atherosclerosis and heart failure develop [47,74].
  • Cancer incidence rises steeply with age, reflecting accumulated somatic mutations, epigenetic change, declining immune surveillance and a pro-tumorigenic tissue environment shaped in part by senescent cells [59,115].
  • Dementia affected about 57 million people worldwide in 2021, and Alzheimer's disease accounts for 60–70% of cases [116]. Alzheimer's disease is defined by amyloid-β plaques and tau tangles and its risk increases sharply with age [117]. The 2024 Lancet Commission estimated that about 45% of dementia cases are attributable to 14 potentially modifiable risk factors [118].
  • Musculoskeletal disease: osteoarthritis is among the most common causes of chronic pain and disability in older adults [119], and osteoporotic fractures cause major morbidity [84].
  • Type 2 diabetes becomes more prevalent with age as insulin resistance increases and β-cell function declines [104].

Mechanistic links between aging and disease

Several hallmarks connect directly to specific diseases. Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), in which blood stem cells carrying somatic mutations in genes such as DNMT3A, TET2 and ASXL1 expand, is rare before age 40 but found in about 10% of people over 70; it is associated with higher risks of hematologic cancer and all-cause mortality [120] and with nearly double the risk of coronary heart disease [121]. Chronic inflammation links aging to cardiovascular disease, frailty and disability [103]. Segmental progeroid syndromes show that defects in single pathways can accelerate many aging features: Hutchinson–Gilford progeria is caused by de novo mutations in the lamin A gene LMNA [122], and Werner syndrome by mutations in the RecQ helicase gene WRN [123].

06Longevity and healthy aging

Trends in life expectancy

Record female life expectancy (the highest national value in a given year) rose almost linearly by about three months per year from 1840 to 2000, showing no sign of a fixed ceiling over that period [124]. Globally, life expectancy fell by 1.8 years between 2019 and 2021 because of the COVID-19 pandemic, to 71.4 years, and healthy life expectancy fell to 61.9 years [37]. Population aging is now universal: the United Nations projects that by the late 2070s people aged 65 or over will outnumber children under 18 [125], and the number of people aged 60 or over is expected to reach 2.1 billion by 2050 [6].

Is there a limit to human lifespan?

This remains disputed. In 1990 Olshansky and colleagues argued that average life expectancy was unlikely to exceed 85 years without fundamental advances in slowing aging [126]. Analysis of international data in 2016 suggested that gains in the maximum reported age at death had plateaued since the 1990s, implying a natural limit around 115 years [127], whereas the Italian mortality plateau suggests no fixed maximum [40]. A 2024 analysis of the longest-lived populations found that gains in life expectancy have slowed since 1990 and argued that radical life extension in this century is implausible without interventions that slow biological aging [128].

Genetics of longevity

The heritability of human lifespan is modest. A study of 2,872 Danish twin pairs estimated it at about 0.25 [129], and an analysis of large genealogical pedigrees concluded that true heritability is probably below 10%, because earlier estimates were inflated by assortative mating [130]. Genetic influence appears stronger at extreme ages. Variants of FOXO3A, a transcription factor downstream of insulin/IGF-1 signalling, were associated with longevity in Japanese-American men [131], consistent with the conserved pathways discovered in worms [26]. A meta-analysis of genome-wide studies confirmed APOE as the strongest longevity locus and identified additional loci [132].

Centenarians

Centenarians reach exceptional age by different routes. In the New England Centenarian Study, 38% were "survivors" diagnosed with an age-associated disease before 80, 43% were "delayers" whose first such disease came at 80 or later, and 19% were "escapers" with none of the major diseases at 100 [133]. Among supercentenarians (aged 110 or over), health span tends to approximate life span, an example of compression of morbidity at the limit of life [134].

Healthy aging

Rowe and Kahn distinguished "usual" from "successful" aging [135], later defining the latter as low risk of disease and disability, high mental and physical function, and active engagement with life [136]. The WHO's 2015 World Report reframed the goal as healthy ageing: "the process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables wellbeing in older age" [137]. Functional ability depends on the person's intrinsic capacity (the composite of physical and mental capacities, grouped into locomotion, vitality, cognition, psychological, and sensory domains) and on their environment [137,138]. The UN Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030) and WHO's Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) guidelines aim to monitor and preserve intrinsic capacity in primary care [139,140].

Lifestyle factors with the strongest evidence

  • Physical activity. Physical inactivity was estimated to cause 9% of premature deaths worldwide in 2008 [141]. WHO recommends 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week for adults, and for older adults multicomponent activity emphasizing balance and strength on three or more days per week [142]. In the LIFE trial of 1,635 sedentary adults aged 70–89, a structured activity programme reduced major mobility disability compared with health education (hazard ratio 0.82) [143].
  • Combined lifestyle. In two large US cohorts, adults who at age 50 had five low-risk factors (never smoking, healthy body weight, regular physical activity, moderate alcohol intake and a high-quality diet) had a life expectancy 14.0 years longer for women and 12.2 years longer for men than those with none [144].
  • Social connection. A meta-analysis of 148 studies found that people with stronger social relationships had a 50% greater likelihood of survival over the follow-up periods studied [145].

07Open questions

Key unresolved questions include which hallmarks are causal drivers in humans rather than correlates, how they interact [1]; whether biological-age clocks respond to interventions in ways that predict real changes in health [46,150]; and whether gains in life expectancy will be accompanied by compression or expansion of morbidity, given the widening healthspan–lifespan gap [22,38]. Senolytic and other geroscience-guided trials will test whether targeting aging mechanisms can delay several diseases together [4,72].
