Neurological and Psychiatric Disorders

How disease of the brain shows up as disturbed movement, sensation, cognition, mood and behaviour: the history of the two specialties, how their disorders are classified, what is known of their mechanisms, and how they are diagnosed and treated.

Citation style: Vancouver (ICMJE)154 referencesPrepared 27 October 2026

Summary

Disorders of the nervous system affected an estimated 3.40 billion people in 2021, 43.1% of the world's population, and were the leading cause of disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) of any disease group [1]. Mental disorders affected about 970 million people in 2019, roughly one person in eight [2,3]. Neurology and psychiatry grew from a shared root in 19th-century medicine and divided mainly by method: neurology by locating lesions, psychiatry by describing syndromes of mind and behaviour [4,5,6]. Genetics, imaging and fluid biomarkers now show that the dividing line is porous. Psychiatric disorders share much of their inherited risk with one another, many neurological diseases carry high rates of depression, anxiety and psychosis, and some conditions, such as autoimmune encephalitis and functional neurological disorder, sit squarely on the border [7,8,9]. This review covers the history and classification of these disorders, their global burden, the biology, diagnosis and treatment of the major neurological and psychiatric conditions, and the themes that run across both.

01History

Modern clinical neurology took shape in Paris at the Salpêtrière hospital under Jean-Martin Charcot, who in 1882 became the first holder of a chair devoted to diseases of the nervous system. Charcot used the anatomo-clinical method, correlating signs observed in life with lesions found at autopsy, to define multiple sclerosis and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis as distinct diseases, and he gave Parkinson's name to the "shaking palsy" [5]. James Parkinson had described that syndrome of tremor, flexed posture and festinating gait in his 1817 Essay on the Shaking Palsy [10], and George Huntington described the hereditary chorea that bears his name in 1872, noting its transmission from parent to child [11]. Charcot also studied hysteria, and Sigmund Freud's period of study with him in 1885–86 influenced Freud's later psychological theories [5].

Psychiatry developed in the asylums. In the 1790s Philippe Pinel and others promoted "moral treatment", and in the mid-19th century Wilhelm Griesinger argued that mental illnesses are diseases of the brain [6]. Emil Kraepelin then built a nosology on the course of illness rather than on its symptoms at one moment. In the sixth edition of his textbook (1899) he separated dementia praecox, which he saw as a deteriorating illness, from manic-depressive insanity, which ran an episodic course [12]. Eugen Bleuler renamed dementia praecox "schizophrenia" in 1911, emphasising a splitting of psychic functions rather than an inevitable decline [13]. In Kraepelin's Munich clinic, Alois Alzheimer described in 1906–07 a woman with progressive memory loss and the plaques and neurofibrillary tangles now used to define Alzheimer's disease [14].

Several discoveries showed how arbitrary the line between the two fields could be. General paresis of the insane, then one of the commonest reasons for admission to an asylum, was shown in 1913 to be caused by Treponema pallidum in the brain [15]. Hans Berger, a psychiatrist, recorded the first human electroencephalogram in 1929 [16]. Most of the first effective psychiatric drugs were found by clinical observation in a single decade.

02Concepts and classification

Where neurology ends and psychiatry begins

The division between neurology and psychiatry is largely historical. Neurology came to deal with disorders that could be localised to a lesion in the nervous system, while psychiatry dealt with disturbances of thought, mood and behaviour that had no visible lesion [4]. Both specialties study the same organ, and the boundary has moved whenever a cause has been found. Neurosyphilis left psychiatry once its cause was known [15]. Many patients with anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis first present with psychosis, agitation and catatonia and are admitted to psychiatric services before antibodies are detected [8]. Functional neurological disorder, in which weakness, seizures or movement disorders arise from altered brain function without structural damage, is now diagnosed by positive clinical signs and treated by neurologists, psychiatrists and therapists together [9].

Classification systems

Two systems dominate. The World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision (ICD-11), adopted in 2019 and in use for reporting since January 2022, is the global standard for health statistics [35]. The American Psychiatric Association's Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, now in its fifth edition text revision (DSM-5-TR, 2022), gives criteria for mental disorders and is widely used in research and in North American practice [36]. Since DSM-III in 1980, psychiatric categories have been defined by explicit lists of symptoms, duration and impairment, which made diagnoses more reliable between clinicians but did not tie them to causes [22,26]. ICD-11 made two changes relevant to this review: it moved cerebrovascular disease into the nervous-system chapter, and it gave sleep–wake disorders a chapter of their own [35].

Categories, dimensions and circuits

Categorical diagnoses carve continuous variation into present-or-absent classes, and most psychiatric disorders share symptoms, risk genes and treatments [37,38]. Two research frameworks try to address this. The US National Institute of Mental Health's Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) organise research around functional domains, such as negative valence or cognitive control, studied across levels from genes to behaviour [39]. The Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) uses the statistical structure of symptoms to define dimensions, from narrow symptom components up to broad spectra such as internalising and a general factor of psychopathology [38]. Neither has replaced DSM or ICD in clinical work.

03Global burden

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study measures health loss in disability-adjusted life-years, the sum of years of life lost to early death and years lived with disability. By that measure, disorders of the nervous system as a group caused 443 million DALYs in 2021, more than any other disease group. The largest contributors were stroke, neonatal encephalopathy, migraine and dementia [1]. Mental disorders caused 125.3 million DALYs in 2019, 4.9% of the global total, with depressive and anxiety disorders the largest contributors [2]. These figures understate mortality: in a meta-analysis of 203 studies, people with mental disorders died a median of about 10 years earlier than the general population, and an estimated 14.3% of deaths worldwide, about 8 million each year, were attributable to mental disorders [40]. More than 700 000 people die by suicide each year [41].

Much of this burden goes untreated. In World Mental Health surveys across 21 countries, only 16.5% of people with major depressive disorder in the past year received minimally adequate treatment, and the proportion was much lower in low- and lower-middle-income countries [42]. Up to 70% of people with epilepsy could live seizure-free with appropriate treatment, but about three-quarters of those in low-income countries do not receive it [43].

04Cerebrovascular disease

A stroke is an acute focal neurological deficit caused by vascular injury to the brain: arterial occlusion (ischaemic stroke) or rupture of a vessel into the brain substance (intracerebral haemorrhage) or the subarachnoid space (subarachnoid haemorrhage) [49]. In 2021 there were 11.9 million new strokes and 7.3 million deaths from stroke, which made it the third leading cause of death worldwide [45]. About 84% of stroke DALYs were attributable to 23 modifiable risk factors, led by high systolic blood pressure [45]. In the INTERSTROKE case–control study across 32 countries, ten modifiable risk factors, including hypertension, inactivity, diet, smoking, alcohol and atrial fibrillation, together accounted for about 90% of the population-attributable risk of stroke [50].

Mechanism

When an artery is occluded, the territory it supplies divides into an ischaemic core, where blood flow is too low to maintain ion gradients and cells die within minutes, and a surrounding penumbra, where neurons are electrically silent but still viable and can be saved if flow is restored [49]. Without treatment, the penumbra is progressively recruited into the core. In a typical large-vessel ischaemic stroke, an estimated 1.9 million neurons and 14 billion synapses are lost every minute [51].

Diagnosis and treatment

Brain imaging, usually CT, is needed before treatment to exclude haemorrhage, and CT or MR angiography identifies large-vessel occlusions; perfusion imaging can estimate the size of core and penumbra and select patients for treatment later after onset [49]. Intravenous alteplase given within 3 hours of onset made patients at least 30% more likely to have minimal or no disability at three months, at the cost of a higher rate of symptomatic intracerebral haemorrhage (6.4% versus 0.6%) [30]. Mechanical thrombectomy for large-vessel occlusion is more effective still. In a pooled analysis of five trials, 46.0% of patients treated with thrombectomy were functionally independent at 90 days, compared with 26.5% of controls, and fewer than three patients needed treatment for one to have reduced disability [52]. Long-term prevention targets blood pressure, lipids, antithrombotic therapy, anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation and carotid revascularisation when indicated [49]. Depression follows about one stroke in three [53].

05Epilepsy

A seizure is a transient occurrence of signs or symptoms due to abnormal, excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain. The International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) defines epilepsy as at least two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, one unprovoked seizure with a probability of further seizures of at least 60% over the next 10 years, or the diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome [54]. Seizures are classified by onset as focal, generalised or unknown, and focal seizures by whether awareness is retained [55]. About 50 million people have epilepsy, nearly 80% of them in low- and middle-income countries [43].

Seizures arise when the balance between excitation and inhibition in neuronal networks fails. Causes include structural lesions such as hippocampal sclerosis, cortical malformations, tumours and scars from stroke or trauma; genetic variants, often in ion channels, as in SCN1A-related Dravet syndrome; infections; autoimmune encephalitis; and metabolic disorders [56]. Diagnosis rests on the clinical history, supported by electroencephalography (EEG) to identify epileptiform discharges and MRI to identify structural causes [56]. Depression, anxiety and cognitive problems are common companions of epilepsy [56].

Most people respond to the first or second well-chosen antiseizure medication. Of 470 previously untreated patients in one cohort, 47% became seizure-free on their first drug, but only 14% more did so on a second or third drug and 3% on a combination of two (Figure 2) [57]. On this basis the ILAE defines drug-resistant epilepsy as failure of two tolerated, appropriately chosen and used drug schedules [58]. Such patients should be assessed for surgery. In a randomised trial in temporal-lobe epilepsy, 58% of patients were free of seizures that impaired awareness one year after surgery, compared with 8% of those who continued medical treatment [59].

06Dementia

Dementia is an acquired decline in memory and other cognitive abilities severe enough to interfere with independence in everyday life [36,46]. About 57 million people were living with dementia in 2021, with nearly 10 million new cases each year. Alzheimer's disease accounts for an estimated 60–70% of cases [46]. The 2024 Lancet Commission estimated that about 45% of dementia cases could in principle be prevented or delayed by addressing 14 modifiable risk factors across life, among them low education, hearing loss, hypertension, smoking, physical inactivity, high LDL cholesterol, untreated vision loss and social isolation [60].

Alzheimer's disease

Alzheimer's disease is defined pathologically by extracellular plaques of amyloid-β (Aβ) and intraneuronal neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau, with loss of synapses and neurons [14,61]. Tangles spread in a stereotyped order, from the transentorhinal cortex through the limbic system to the neocortex, and their extent correlates with cognitive decline better than plaque load does [62]. The amyloid cascade hypothesis proposes that accumulation of Aβ is the initiating event that drives tau pathology, neuronal loss and dementia [63]. It is supported by the fact that mutations causing familial early-onset disease alter Aβ production [61]. The ε4 allele of apolipoprotein E (APOE) is the strongest common genetic risk factor for late-onset disease, with risk rising and age of onset falling with each copy [64]. Variants in TREM2, a receptor on microglia, also raise risk substantially and point to the brain's innate immune response as part of the disease process [65,66].

Because pathology begins long before symptoms (Figure 3) [67], diagnosis increasingly relies on biomarkers: amyloid and tau PET, cerebrospinal fluid Aβ42 and phosphorylated tau, and, more recently, blood tests [61]. Plasma tau phosphorylated at threonine 217 (p-tau217) distinguished Alzheimer's disease from other neurodegenerative disorders with accuracy similar to that of CSF and PET measures [68]. A 2024 Alzheimer's Association workgroup proposed defining Alzheimer's disease biologically, by abnormal core biomarkers, rather than by its clinical syndrome [69].

Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine improve symptoms modestly but do not alter the course of the disease [61]. Monoclonal antibodies that clear Aβ are the first treatments to slow decline. In an 18-month trial in early Alzheimer's disease, lecanemab reduced the worsening of the Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes by 0.45 points relative to placebo, about 27% less decline. Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities with oedema (ARIA-E) occurred in 12.6% of treated participants versus 1.7% on placebo [33]. Donanemab produced a similar degree of slowing, and ARIA-E occurred in 24.0% of treated participants [34]. The clinical significance of benefits of this size remains debated.

Other dementias

Dementia with Lewy bodies is characterised by fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder and parkinsonism. It shares α-synuclein pathology with Parkinson's disease [70]. Frontotemporal dementia presents with changes in behaviour or language, usually before age 65. Most cases have aggregates of either tau or the RNA-binding protein TDP-43 [71]. An expanded GGGGCC hexanucleotide repeat in C9orf72 causes both frontotemporal dementia and ALS, which places the two disorders on one spectrum [72]. Vascular cognitive impairment, from stroke and small-vessel disease, often coexists with Alzheimer pathology in older people [60,61].

07Parkinson's disease and movement disorders

The number of people with Parkinson's disease more than doubled between 1990 and 2016, to 6.1 million, driven partly by population ageing [47]. The disease is diagnosed clinically by bradykinesia combined with rest tremor, rigidity or both [73]. Non-motor features, including loss of smell, constipation, REM sleep behaviour disorder and depression, may precede the motor signs by years [73,74].

Mechanism

The motor syndrome results from the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, which project to the striatum [20,74]. Surviving neurons contain Lewy bodies, inclusions whose main component is aggregated α-synuclein [75]. Mutations in the α-synuclein gene SNCA cause familial disease [76], and variants in LRRK2 and GBA1 are among the most common genetic risk factors [74]. Braak and colleagues proposed that Lewy pathology begins in the lower brainstem and olfactory bulb and ascends to the midbrain and then the cortex, which matches the early appearance of non-motor symptoms [77].

Classical models of basal ganglia circuitry explain how dopamine loss slows movement. Dopamine excites striatal neurons of the "direct" pathway through D1 receptors and inhibits those of the "indirect" pathway through D2 receptors. When dopamine is lost, the balance shifts towards the indirect pathway, the output nuclei (internal globus pallidus and substantia nigra pars reticulata) become overactive, and they excessively inhibit the thalamus and its drive to motor cortex (Figure 4) [78,79].

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, supported where needed by dopamine transporter imaging [73,74]. An α-synuclein seed amplification assay in cerebrospinal fluid, which detects misfolded α-synuclein by its ability to template further aggregation, identified Parkinson's disease with a sensitivity of 87.7% and a specificity of 96.3% in a large cohort [80]. Levodopa, introduced in effective doses in the late 1960s [21], remains the most effective symptomatic treatment, though with years of use most patients develop motor fluctuations and dyskinesias [74]. Deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus improved quality of life and motor function more than best medical treatment alone in patients with advanced disease and motor complications [81]. No treatment yet slows neurodegeneration [74].

Huntington's disease is caused by an expanded CAG repeat in HTT that encodes a polyglutamine tract in the huntingtin protein, and it is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait [11,27]. It combines chorea with cognitive decline and psychiatric symptoms, and is a clear example of a single gene producing a disorder that is both neurological and psychiatric.

08Multiple sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic immune-mediated disease of the central nervous system in which focal inflammation causes demyelination, axonal injury and gliosis [82]. An estimated 2.8 million people were living with MS in 2020, about 1 in 3000 people worldwide, and women are affected about twice as often as men [48]. Most patients present with a relapsing–remitting course, with episodes such as optic neuritis, myelitis or brainstem syndromes. Many later enter a secondary progressive phase, and a minority have progressive disease from onset [82].

Genetic studies have identified more than 200 risk loci, the strongest in the HLA class II region, and implicate peripheral immune cells and microglia [83]. Among environmental factors, Epstein–Barr virus infection has the strongest evidence. In a cohort of more than 10 million US military personnel followed over 20 years, infection increased the risk of MS 32-fold, and serum neurofilament light chain, a marker of axonal damage, rose only after infection [84].

Diagnosis uses the McDonald criteria, which require evidence of lesions disseminated in space and in time, from clinical episodes or MRI. Oligoclonal bands in cerebrospinal fluid can substitute for dissemination in time [85]. Disease-modifying treatment began with interferon beta-1b, which reduced relapse rates in a 1993 trial [29]. Ocrelizumab, a monoclonal antibody that depletes CD20-positive B cells, lowered relapse rates by about half relative to interferon beta-1a in relapsing MS [86], and it became the first therapy to show a modest effect on disability progression in primary progressive MS [87]. The success of B-cell depletion showed that B cells, and not only T cells, drive the disease [82].

09Motor neuron disease

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), the most common motor neuron disease, is a progressive degeneration of upper motor neurons in the motor cortex and lower motor neurons in the brainstem and spinal cord. It causes weakness, wasting, spasticity and eventually respiratory failure, usually within 3–5 years of onset [88,89]. About 10% of cases are familial [88]. The first gene identified was SOD1, which encodes superoxide dismutase [28]. The most common genetic cause in people of European ancestry is the C9orf72 repeat expansion [72,88]. In most cases, familial or sporadic, affected neurons contain cytoplasmic aggregates of TDP-43 [71,89]. Up to half of patients develop cognitive or behavioural impairment, and some meet criteria for frontotemporal dementia [89].

Riluzole, which reduces glutamatergic transmission, modestly prolongs survival [90]. Tofersen, an antisense oligonucleotide that reduces production of SOD1 protein, lowered CSF SOD1 and plasma neurofilament light chain in SOD1-ALS. It did not significantly change the primary clinical outcome at 28 weeks, but longer follow-up suggested benefit with earlier treatment [91]. Multidisciplinary care, non-invasive ventilation and nutritional support remain central to management [89].

10Migraine and traumatic brain injury

Migraine

Migraine affected about 1.04 billion people in 2016 and was the second leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide [44]. It is diagnosed by the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) criteria: recurrent attacks lasting 4–72 hours with at least two of unilateral location, pulsating quality, moderate or severe intensity and aggravation by physical activity, plus nausea or sensitivity to light and sound [92]. About a third of people with migraine have aura, typically visual, and migraine is two to three times more common in women than in men [93].

Aura is thought to correspond to cortical spreading depolarisation, a slowly propagating wave of neuronal and glial depolarisation first described by Aristides Leão in 1944 [93,94]. Headache arises from activation of the trigeminovascular system, the trigeminal sensory fibres that innervate meningeal and cerebral vessels [93]. Levels of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) rise in jugular venous blood during attacks [95]. This finding led to treatments that block CGRP or its receptor. In a phase 3 trial, the anti-CGRP receptor antibody erenumab reduced monthly migraine days by 3.2–3.7 days, compared with 1.8 days on placebo [96]. Triptans, serotonin 5-HT1B/1D agonists, remain standard acute treatment [93].

Traumatic brain injury

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of death and disability in young adults, and falls in older people are a growing cause [97]. Severity is graded with the Glasgow Coma Scale, which scores eye opening, verbal response and motor response from 3 to 15 [23]: scores of 13–15 indicate mild injury, 9–12 moderate and 8 or less severe [97]. Primary injury from the mechanical impact, including contusions, haematomas and diffuse axonal injury, is followed by secondary injury from hypoxia, hypotension, raised intracranial pressure, excitotoxicity and inflammation, which acute care aims to prevent [97]. In the CRASH-3 trial, tranexamic acid given within 3 hours of injury reduced head-injury deaths in patients with mild to moderate TBI, but not in those with severe injury [98]. Repeated head impacts, as in contact sports, are associated with chronic traumatic encephalopathy, a progressive tauopathy identified at autopsy [99]. Depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder and cognitive problems are common after TBI of any severity [97].

11Mood disorders

Major depressive disorder

Depressive disorders affected about 280 million people in 2019 [3]. DSM-5-TR requires at least five of nine symptoms for at least two weeks, one of which must be depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure. The others include changes in sleep, appetite or weight, fatigue, psychomotor change, feelings of worthlessness or guilt, poor concentration and thoughts of death or suicide [36]. Depression is about twice as common in women as in men, often begins in adolescence or early adulthood, and tends to recur [100,101].

Twin studies put the heritability of major depression at about 37% [102], and a genome-wide meta-analysis of more than 800 000 people identified 102 independent risk variants, enriched in genes expressed in the prefrontal cortex [103]. Stressful life events, childhood adversity and chronic illness are major environmental risks [101]. The monoamine hypothesis grew out of the observation that the first antidepressants increased synaptic serotonin and noradrenaline [19,100]. An umbrella review in 2022 found no consistent evidence that depression is caused by low serotonin activity [104], and current models emphasise dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, altered neuroplasticity, and inflammation [101]. On average, people with depression have higher blood concentrations of interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor-α than controls [105].

Treatment combines psychological therapies, such as cognitive behavioural therapy, with medication [100]. A network meta-analysis of 522 trials found that all 21 antidepressants studied were more effective than placebo for acute treatment of adults, with modest effect sizes [106]. In the STAR*D trial, about two-thirds of outpatients eventually reached remission over up to four sequential treatment steps, but remission became less likely with each step [107]. Electroconvulsive therapy is the most effective acute treatment for severe depression [108]. A single subanaesthetic dose of the NMDA receptor antagonist ketamine improved depressive symptoms within hours [109], which pointed to glutamatergic mechanisms and led to new rapid-acting treatments [101].

Bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder is defined by episodes of mania (bipolar I) or hypomania (bipolar II), usually alternating with episodes of depression [36,110]. Mania is a distinct period of abnormally elevated or irritable mood and increased energy lasting at least a week, with symptoms such as reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts and risky behaviour [36]. In World Mental Health surveys, lifetime prevalence was 0.6% for bipolar I, 0.4% for bipolar II and 1.4% for subthreshold bipolar disorder [111]. Twin studies estimate heritability at about 85%, among the highest of any psychiatric disorder [112]. Lithium remains a first-line maintenance treatment [110], and it reduces the risk of suicide in people with mood disorders [113]. Valproate, lamotrigine and several antipsychotics are also used, depending on the phase of illness [110].

12Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia has a lifetime morbid risk of about 0.7% [114] and typically begins in late adolescence or early adulthood [115]. Its symptoms fall into three groups. Positive symptoms are delusions, hallucinations and disorganised thought. Negative symptoms are reduced motivation, emotional expression and speech. Cognitive symptoms are deficits in memory, attention and executive function [115]. People with schizophrenia die on average about 14.5 years earlier than the general population, largely from cardiovascular and other physical diseases [116].

Mechanism

The heritability of schizophrenia is about 79% [117]. The largest genome-wide study identified 287 associated loci, concentrated in genes expressed in neurons and involved in synaptic function [32]. The strongest common-variant association, in the major histocompatibility complex, arises partly from variation in complement component 4 (C4). Higher C4A expression increases risk, which suggests that excessive complement-mediated pruning of synapses during adolescence contributes to the disorder [118].

The dopamine hypothesis began with the finding that the clinical potency of antipsychotic drugs tracks their affinity for dopamine D2 receptors [24,25]. Imaging studies show increased presynaptic dopamine synthesis and release in the striatum of people with psychosis, which Howes and Kapur proposed as the final common pathway to psychosis [119]. NMDA receptor antagonists such as ketamine produce positive, negative and cognitive symptoms in healthy volunteers, which implicates glutamatergic hypofunction as well [120]. The psychosis of anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis supports the same idea [8].

Treatment

Antipsychotic drugs are the mainstay of treatment. In a network meta-analysis of 32 oral antipsychotics, differences in efficacy between drugs were small, while differences in side effects, such as weight gain, sedation and movement disorders, were large [121]. Clozapine is the only drug with established superiority in treatment-resistant schizophrenia. In a pivotal trial, 30% of treatment-resistant patients responded to clozapine compared with 4% given chlorpromazine [122]. Xanomeline–trospium, which stimulates muscarinic acetylcholine receptors rather than blocking D2 receptors, reduced total symptom scores by 9.6 points more than placebo in a phase 3 trial, the first new mechanism shown to work in schizophrenia in decades [123]. Psychosocial interventions, family work and supported employment improve outcomes alongside medication [115].

13Anxiety, obsessive–compulsive and trauma-related disorders

Anxiety disorders

Anxiety disorders are the most common mental disorders, affecting about 301 million people in 2019 [3]. They include generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, agoraphobia and specific phobias, and most begin in childhood, adolescence or early adulthood [124]. Their heritability is moderate, about 30–50% [128]. Fear and anxiety depend on circuits centred on the amygdala, which detects threat and drives defensive responses through its projections to the hypothalamus and brainstem, under regulation by the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus [128,129]. Cognitive behavioural therapy, including exposure, and SSRIs or SNRIs are first-line treatments [124].

Obsessive–compulsive disorder

Obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) is characterised by intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images or urges (obsessions) and repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed to reduce the distress they cause (compulsions) [36]. Its lifetime prevalence in the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication was 2.3% [130]. Imaging implicates cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuits linking orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate cortex with the striatum [125]. Exposure and response prevention and SSRIs, often at higher doses than used for depression, are effective. Deep brain stimulation is used for severe treatment-refractory cases [125].

Post-traumatic stress disorder

About 70% of people in World Mental Health surveys reported exposure to at least one traumatic event in their lifetime [131], but only a minority develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD); its lifetime prevalence across the surveys was 3.9% [132]. PTSD involves intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, negative changes in mood and cognition, and hyperarousal [36]. DSM-5 moved it from the anxiety disorders to a new group of trauma- and stressor-related disorders [36], and ICD-11 added complex PTSD [35]. A central mechanism is impaired extinction of conditioned fear, involving the amygdala, hippocampus and ventromedial prefrontal cortex [133,134]. Trauma-focused psychotherapies are first-line treatments [134].

14Substance use disorders

Substance use disorders are defined by impaired control over use, continued use despite harm, and, for many drugs, tolerance and withdrawal [36]. Alcohol dependence was the most prevalent of them in 2016, affecting about 100 million people, and alcohol and drug use together were a major cause of premature death and disability [135].

In 1954 James Olds and Peter Milner found that rats would work to receive electrical stimulation of certain brain regions, the first evidence of a brain reward system [136]. Drugs of abuse share the ability to increase dopamine in the nucleus accumbens, the main target of the mesolimbic pathway [137]. Addiction has been described as a three-stage cycle. Binge and intoxication engage the basal ganglia. Withdrawal and negative affect engage the extended amygdala and brain stress systems. Preoccupation and anticipation (craving) engage the prefrontal cortex, whose weakened control over behaviour is thought to sustain compulsive use [138,139]. Treatment includes psychosocial interventions and medication. For opioid use disorder, methadone and buprenorphine retain patients in treatment and reduce illicit opioid use [127], and all-cause mortality is substantially lower during opioid agonist treatment than outside it, with the highest risk in the first weeks after treatment stops [140].

15Neurodevelopmental disorders

Neurodevelopmental disorders begin in the developmental period and impair personal, social, academic or occupational functioning [36]. Leo Kanner described 11 children with "autistic disturbances of affective contact" in 1943 [141]. DSM-5 merged autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder and related diagnoses into a single autism spectrum disorder defined by persistent deficits in social communication and restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities [36,142]. The median global prevalence in studies published since 2012 is about 1 in 100 children [143].

Autism is highly heritable, about 83% in a Swedish population study [144]. Its genetic architecture combines many common variants of small effect, the first of which reached genome-wide significance in 2019 [145], with rare and de novo variants of large effect [142]. Large studies show no association between vaccination and autism [146]. Early behavioural and developmental interventions improve communication and adaptive skills [142].

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is defined by developmentally inappropriate inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity [36]. Its worldwide prevalence in children and adolescents is about 5.3% [147], its heritability about 74% [148], and it often persists into adulthood [149]. In a network meta-analysis, methylphenidate in children and amphetamines in adults were the preferred first-choice medications on efficacy and tolerability [126].

16Cross-cutting themes

Genetic architecture

Many neurological diseases have Mendelian forms caused by single genes of large effect, such as HTT in Huntington's disease, SNCA in familial Parkinson's disease and SOD1 in familial ALS [27,28,76]. Psychiatric disorders are mostly polygenic, with risk spread across thousands of common variants of small effect plus rarer copy-number and de novo variants [150]. The Brainstorm Consortium compared genome-wide data for 25 brain disorders and found that psychiatric disorders share much of their common-variant risk with each other, while neurological disorders are genetically more distinct from one another and from psychiatric disorders [7]. A cross-disorder analysis of eight psychiatric disorders identified 109 loci that affect more than one disorder and grouped the disorders into three genetically related clusters [37]. These findings help explain why current diagnostic categories overlap so much [38].

Protein misfolding and neuroinflammation

Many neurodegenerative diseases are proteinopathies: Aβ and tau in Alzheimer's disease, α-synuclein in Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies, TDP-43 in ALS and frontotemporal dementia, and huntingtin in Huntington's disease [27,61,71,75]. Stanley Prusiner showed that prion diseases are transmitted by a misfolded protein that templates the misfolding of its normal counterpart [151]. A similar seeding mechanism is thought to help other aggregated proteins spread through the brain, and it is the basis of α-synuclein seed amplification assays [80,151]. Inflammation is a second shared process. It drives MS [82], microglial responses influence risk in Alzheimer's disease [65,66], complement variation affects risk of schizophrenia [118], and inflammatory markers are raised in depression [105]. Autoimmune encephalitis shows that antibodies against a single neuronal receptor can produce a syndrome that resembles primary psychiatric illness [8].

Biomarkers

Neurology has a growing set of fluid and imaging biomarkers (Table 4). Neurofilament light chain, a structural protein of axons, is released into CSF and blood when axons are damaged and rises in MS, ALS, frontotemporal dementia and TBI. It is a sensitive but non-specific measure of neuroaxonal injury [152]. Psychiatry has many candidate biomarkers, from imaging measures to polygenic scores and electrophysiology, but none is yet validated for routine diagnosis [153]. Reasons include the heterogeneity of DSM categories, small effect sizes, and the lack of a pathological gold standard against which to test them [154].

Comorbidity across the boundary

Neurological disease often brings psychiatric illness with it. About a third of stroke survivors develop depression [53]. Depression and anxiety are common in epilepsy and Parkinson's disease, and may precede the motor signs of Parkinson's [56,74]. Visual hallucinations are a core feature of dementia with Lewy bodies [70], and psychiatric symptoms are frequent after TBI [97]. These overlaps argue for training and services that cross the two specialties [4].

17Outlook

Treatment is beginning to target mechanisms rather than symptoms. Examples include thrombectomy for stroke [52], anti-amyloid antibodies for Alzheimer's disease [33], B-cell depletion for MS [86], an antisense oligonucleotide for SOD1-ALS [91], CGRP blockade for migraine [96] and muscarinic agonism for schizophrenia [123]. Blood biomarkers may allow diagnosis before symptoms and selection of patients for these treatments [68,152], and genetic findings are reshaping how psychiatric disorders are grouped [7,37]. The greatest gains in population health, however, are likely to come from prevention and wider access to treatments that already exist: control of blood pressure and other vascular risk factors for stroke and dementia [45,60], and closing the treatment gap for depression and epilepsy in low- and middle-income countries [42,43].
