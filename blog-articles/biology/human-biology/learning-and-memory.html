The Biology of Learning and Memory

How the human brain acquires, stores and retrieves information: from Ebbinghaus's nonsense syllables and the case of H.M. to NMDA receptors, CREB, sleep replay and the diseases that take memory away.

§1Definitions and scope

Learning is the process by which experience changes the nervous system so that later behaviour is altered; memory is the retention of that change over time and its later expression, whether as conscious recollection or as altered performance.1,2 The two are separable only in emphasis: learning refers to acquisition, memory to persistence and retrieval. Modern neuroscience treats both as properties of neural circuits whose synaptic connections are modified by activity, a view anticipated by Ramón y Cajal's proposal that mental exercise strengthens connections between neurons3 and formalised by Hebb.4

Memory is not a single faculty. Lesion, imaging and behavioural evidence show that distinct brain systems support different kinds of memory, operate on different time scales, and fail in different diseases.2,5 This article follows memory from its historical study, through its psychological structure, to its cellular and molecular basis, and ends with its disorders and practical implications.

§2A short history

The experimental study of memory began with Hermann Ebbinghaus, who in 1885 used himself as the sole subject, memorising lists of nonsense syllables and measuring the time saved when relearning them after delays from 20 minutes to 31 days.6 His "savings" method produced the first forgetting curve, a steep early loss that flattens over time; a 2015 replication reproduced its shape closely.7 A few years earlier, Théodule Ribot had observed that brain injury tends to spare old memories more than recent ones, a gradient now known as Ribot's law.8 William James distinguished a fleeting "primary memory" from a durable "secondary memory",9 and in 1900 Müller and Pilzecker introduced the term consolidation after finding that new learning interferes with recently acquired associations.10,11

The search for the physical trace was framed by Richard Semon, who coined the word engram,12 and by Karl Lashley, whose cortical lesion experiments in rats failed to find a single storage site and led him to conclude, in 1950, that memory is widely distributed.13 The decisive turn came in 1957, when Scoville and Milner described the patient H.M. (Henry Molaison), who developed profound anterograde amnesia after bilateral medial temporal lobe resection for intractable epilepsy, while his intelligence, perception and short-term memory remained intact.14,15

H.M. was studied for more than five decades. Magnetic resonance imaging later showed that his lesion removed the anterior hippocampus, amygdala and most of the entorhinal cortex bilaterally,15 and his brain was sectioned and digitally reconstructed after his death in 2008.26

§3Forms of learning

Nonassociative learning

The simplest learning involves a single stimulus. In habituation the response to a repeated harmless stimulus declines; in sensitization a strong or noxious stimulus enhances responses to other stimuli. Both were analysed cellularly in the gill-withdrawal reflex of the sea slug Aplysia californica, where habituation reflects reduced transmitter release from sensory neurons.1,27

Associative learning

In classical (Pavlovian) conditioning a neutral conditioned stimulus comes to elicit a response after being paired with an unconditioned stimulus.17 In instrumental (operant) conditioning the probability of a behaviour changes according to its consequences, as described by Thorndike's law of effect and later by Skinner.16,28 The Rescorla–Wagner model formalised conditioning as learning driven by prediction error, the difference between expected and received outcomes,29 and midbrain dopamine neurons were later shown to signal such reward-prediction errors.30

Critical periods

Some learning is constrained to windows of heightened plasticity during development. Hubel and Wiesel showed that closing one eye of a kitten during a sensitive period permanently shifts visual cortical responses toward the open eye, while the same deprivation in adulthood has little effect;31 the maturation of cortical inhibitory circuits is a key regulator of when such periods open and close.32

§4Memory systems

Sensory, short-term and working memory

Atkinson and Shiffrin's multistore model divided memory into sensory registers, a limited short-term store and a durable long-term store.33 Sperling's partial-report experiments revealed a high-capacity visual sensory memory that decays within about a second.34 Miller estimated short-term capacity at "seven, plus or minus two" items,35 whereas later work that controls for rehearsal and chunking places the limit nearer four chunks.36

Baddeley and Hitch reframed short-term memory as working memory: a central executive controlling a phonological loop and a visuospatial sketchpad,37 later joined by an episodic buffer that binds information across modalities.38 Neurons in the primate prefrontal cortex sustain firing during the delay of memory tasks, a cellular correlate of holding information "on line".39,40

Declarative and nondeclarative memory

The finding that H.M. could acquire a motor skill without remembering the training sessions19 was the first clear sign that long-term memory has separable components. Amnesic patients also learn to read mirror-reversed words,41 show normal priming from fragmented words,42,43 and learn probabilistic classification tasks that depend on the striatum, which patients with Parkinson's disease fail.44 These dissociations led to the distinction between declarative memory (facts and events, consciously accessible, dependent on the medial temporal lobe) and nondeclarative memory (skills, habits, priming, conditioning and nonassociative learning, expressed through performance).2

Tulving further divided declarative memory into episodic memory for personally experienced events located in time and place, and semantic memory for general knowledge,45 and linked episodic memory to "autonoetic" awareness, the experience of mentally travelling back in time.46 Priming is now considered a distinct perceptual memory system.47

§5Encoding, consolidation and retrieval

Encoding

How well an event is remembered depends on how it is processed at the time. Craik and Lockhart showed that "deep" semantic processing yields better retention than shallow processing of surface features.48 Functional MRI studies published in 1998 demonstrated the subsequent memory effect: greater activity in the parahippocampal and prefrontal cortex during encoding predicts which items will later be remembered.49,50 Emotional arousal enhances encoding and consolidation through noradrenergic activation of the basolateral amygdala, which modulates storage in other regions.51

Consolidation

Newly formed memories are initially labile and become stable over time. Two processes are distinguished.52 Synaptic (cellular) consolidation takes minutes to hours and requires new gene expression and protein synthesis: protein synthesis inhibitors given around training block long-term but not short-term memory.53 Systems consolidation takes weeks to years and reorganises the memory so that it becomes less dependent on the hippocampus and more dependent on the neocortex.54 Evidence includes temporally graded retrograde amnesia in patients, consistent with Ribot's law,8,54 and in rats, where hippocampal lesions abolish recently acquired contextual fear but spare remotely acquired fear.55

The standard model holds that the hippocampus is eventually unnecessary for retrieval of all declarative memories,54 whereas multiple trace theory argues that vivid episodic detail always requires the hippocampus and that only semantic, gist-like versions become independent of it.56 Computational work suggests a rationale for slow cortical learning: a fast-learning hippocampus and a slow-learning neocortex together avoid "catastrophic interference" between new and old knowledge.57

Retrieval and reconstruction

Recall succeeds when cues present at retrieval match the way information was encoded, the encoding specificity principle.58 Divers who learned word lists underwater recalled them better underwater than on land, and vice versa.59 Retrieval is also reconstructive. Bartlett showed that people reshape stories to fit their own schemas,18 and Loftus and Palmer found that witnesses asked how fast cars "smashed" into each other gave higher speed estimates, and later more often falsely reported broken glass, than those asked how fast they "hit".60 Schacter grouped such errors, along with transience and absent-mindedness, as the "seven sins of memory".61

Reconsolidation

Retrieval can return a consolidated memory to a labile state. Nader and colleagues showed that infusing the protein synthesis inhibitor anisomycin into the rat amygdala immediately after a fear memory was reactivated erased the memory, whereas the same infusion without reactivation did not.62 Reconsolidation has since been explored as a way to weaken traumatic memories.63

Forgetting

Forgetting follows the negatively accelerated curve first measured by Ebbinghaus (Figure 2).6,7 Its causes include passive decay, interference from other learning and retrieval failure; Wixted argued that interference with not-yet-consolidated traces accounts for much of the shape of the forgetting curve.64 Forgetting can also be active: people instructed to suppress the retrieval of a learned word pair later recalled it less well.65

§6The hippocampus and medial temporal lobe

The medial temporal lobe memory system comprises the hippocampus proper (fields CA1–CA3), the dentate gyrus and subiculum, and the adjacent entorhinal, perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices.66 Damage confined to this system produces amnesia. A decisive case was patient R.B., whose severe anterograde amnesia after an ischaemic episode was traced at autopsy to a bilateral lesion restricted to the CA1 field.67

Circuitry

Information from association cortices converges on the entorhinal cortex, which projects to the hippocampus through a largely unidirectional loop (Figure 3).68 Theoretical work by Marr proposed that the hippocampus stores memories as patterns that can be completed from partial cues,69 and recordings in rodents support a division of labour in which the dentate gyrus separates similar inputs into distinct representations while recurrently connected CA3 completes patterns.70 The hippocampus is thought to bind the separate elements of an experience, represented in different cortical regions, into a single retrievable episode.71

Space and the cognitive map

Tolman proposed that rats navigate using an internal "cognitive map".72 O'Keefe and Dostrovsky then found hippocampal neurons that fire only when a rat occupies a particular location, now called place cells,20 and grid cells in the medial entorhinal cortex fire at the vertices of a regular triangular lattice spanning the environment.24 Together with head-direction and border cells, these form a spatial coordinate system;73 O'Keefe and the Mosers shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for these discoveries.74 In humans, licensed London taxi drivers have larger posterior hippocampi than controls, and the volume correlates with time spent driving a taxi.75

§7Other memory circuits

Striatum. Habit learning, in which stimulus–response associations are acquired gradually across many trials, depends on the basal ganglia; patients with Parkinson's disease are impaired on probabilistic classification learning despite intact declarative memory for the testing episode.44

Cerebellum. Classical conditioning of the eyeblink reflex requires the cerebellum: lesions of the ipsilateral deep cerebellar nuclei abolish the learned response in rabbits without affecting the reflex itself.76,77 Long-term depression at parallel fibre–Purkinje cell synapses has been proposed as a cellular mechanism of cerebellar motor learning.78

Amygdala. The lateral and central nuclei of the amygdala are central to Pavlovian fear conditioning,79 and the basolateral amygdala strengthens the storage of emotionally arousing declarative memories in other brain regions.11,51

Prefrontal cortex. Persistent delay-period activity in prefrontal neurons supports working memory,39,40 and prefrontal activity at encoding predicts later memory.50

Neocortex. Long-term declarative memories are ultimately thought to be stored in distributed neocortical networks, the regions that originally processed the information, with the hippocampus serving as an index during consolidation.54,57

§8Synaptic plasticity

Hebb proposed in 1949 the principle that bears his name:

When an axon of cell A is near enough to excite a cell B and repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing it, some growth process or metabolic change takes place in one or both cells such that A's efficiency, as one of the cells firing B, is increased.
D. O. Hebb, The Organization of Behavior, 1949, p. 624

Long-term potentiation

Bliss and Lømo provided the first physiological evidence for such a mechanism. Brief high-frequency stimulation of the perforant path in anaesthetised rabbits produced an increase in synaptic responses in the dentate gyrus that lasted for hours.21 LTP has properties expected of a memory mechanism: it is input-specific (only active synapses are potentiated), cooperative (a threshold number of inputs must be co-active) and associative (a weak input is potentiated if paired with a strong one).80

At the CA3–CA1 Schaffer collateral synapse, the best-studied form of LTP depends on the NMDA-type glutamate receptor.80,81 At resting membrane potential this receptor's channel is blocked by extracellular Mg2+; the block is relieved by depolarisation.82 The NMDA receptor therefore opens only when presynaptic glutamate release coincides with postsynaptic depolarisation, a molecular implementation of Hebb's rule. The resulting Ca2+ influx activates Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), which is required for LTP;83,84 the main expression mechanism is the insertion of additional AMPA receptors into the postsynaptic membrane,85,86 including at "silent" synapses that previously contained only NMDA receptors.87,88

Linking LTP to learning

Several lines of evidence connect LTP to memory. Blocking NMDA receptors in the rat hippocampus with AP5 prevented both LTP and learning of the hidden platform position in the Morris water maze.22 Mice lacking αCaMKII had deficient hippocampal LTP,84 and mice overexpressing the NR2B NMDA receptor subunit showed enhanced LTP and enhanced learning.89 Inhibitory avoidance training in rats produced LTP-like increases in hippocampal synaptic strength and AMPA receptor delivery,90 and optogenetically inducing LTD erased a conditioned fear memory in rats, which subsequent LTP induction restored.91

Long-term depression and timing rules

Synapses can also weaken. Prolonged low-frequency stimulation (1 Hz) of Schaffer collaterals produces NMDA receptor-dependent long-term depression (LTD),92 giving circuits a way to reverse potentiation and refine stored patterns. In spike-timing-dependent plasticity, a presynaptic spike that precedes a postsynaptic spike by a few tens of milliseconds produces potentiation, whereas the reverse order produces depression.93,94 Slower homeostatic mechanisms scale all of a neuron's synapses up or down to keep firing rates in a stable range.95

§9Molecular mechanisms and engrams

Short-term and long-term memory differ in mechanism

Work in Aplysia showed that short-term sensitization lasting minutes requires only covalent modification of existing proteins. Serotonin released by modulatory interneurons raises cyclic AMP in sensory neurons, activates protein kinase A (PKA) and enhances transmitter release. Long-term sensitization lasting days requires repeated training, translocation of PKA and MAP kinase to the nucleus, activation of the transcription factor CREB-1, relief of repression by CREB-2, and synthesis of new proteins.1 Long-term memory in Aplysia is accompanied by growth of new synaptic connections, whereas long-term habituation is accompanied by their loss.96

The same molecular logic is conserved across species. A dominant-negative CREB transgene blocked long-term but not short-term memory in Drosophila,97 and mice with a targeted mutation of CREB had deficient long-term memory with intact short-term memory.98 In humans, a common variant of the brain-derived neurotrophic factor gene (BDNF Val66Met) that reduces activity-dependent BDNF secretion is associated with poorer episodic memory and altered hippocampal activation.99

Synaptic tagging and structural change

Because new proteins are made in the cell body or dendrites, a question arises as to how they reach only the potentiated synapses. Frey and Morris found that weak stimulation sets a local "tag" at a synapse that can capture plasticity-related proteins produced by strong stimulation of other inputs on the same neuron, converting short-lasting into long-lasting LTP.100 At the level of single synapses, glutamate uncaging that induces LTP enlarges the stimulated dendritic spine, and spine enlargement correlates with increased AMPA receptor currents.101

Engrams

Semon's engram, a physical change encoding a specific memory,12 can now be studied directly. Liu and colleagues labelled dentate gyrus neurons active during fear conditioning with channelrhodopsin and showed that light reactivation of these cells alone evoked freezing in a different context.25 Subsequent work indicates that memories are stored in sparse ensembles of neurons allocated according to their excitability at the time of learning, distributed across several connected brain regions.102

§10Sleep and memory

In 1924 Jenkins and Dallenbach found that their two participants remembered nonsense syllables better after intervals of sleep than after equal intervals of waking.103 Later research attributes this benefit not only to reduced interference but to active consolidation during sleep.104

During slow-wave sleep, hippocampal place-cell ensembles that were co-active during waking exploration fire together again,105 and this replay is coordinated with replay in the neocortex.106 Replay is concentrated in sharp wave–ripples, brief high-frequency oscillations of the hippocampus.107 Selectively suppressing ripples during post-training sleep impaired spatial learning in rats, indicating that they are necessary for consolidation.108

Human experiments support a causal role. Boosting slow oscillations with transcranial stimulation during early night sleep improved retention of word pairs,109 and re-presenting an odour that accompanied learning during slow-wave sleep, but not during REM sleep, improved memory for object locations and activated the hippocampus.110 Procedural memory also benefits: finger-tapping speed improved after a night of sleep but not after an equal waking interval.111 The "active systems consolidation" account holds that repeated reactivation during slow-wave sleep transfers memories to neocortical networks,104 while the synaptic homeostasis hypothesis proposes that sleep downscales synaptic strengths that rise during waking, improving signal-to-noise ratio.112

§11Disorders of memory

Amnesic syndromes

Amnesia is a selective impairment of declarative memory with preserved intelligence and attention. Anterograde amnesia is the inability to form new memories; retrograde amnesia is the loss of memories acquired before the injury and is typically temporally graded.5,54 Causes include bilateral medial temporal lesions (surgical, as in H.M., or after hypoxia, herpes simplex encephalitis or stroke)14,67 and diencephalic damage.

Korsakoff syndrome results from thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, most often in chronic alcohol misuse, and often follows Wernicke's encephalopathy. Pathology involves the mammillary bodies and medial thalamus; patients show severe anterograde amnesia and frequently confabulate.113,114

Transient global amnesia is a sudden, self-limited episode of anterograde amnesia lasting up to 24 hours, usually in middle-aged and older adults; small diffusion-weighted MRI lesions in hippocampal CA1 are often detected in the days that follow.115

Alzheimer's disease

Alois Alzheimer described the disease in 1907 in a woman with progressive memory loss whose cortex contained plaques and neurofibrillary tangles.116 Tangle pathology typically begins in the transentorhinal and entorhinal cortex and spreads to the hippocampus and then the neocortex, matching the early prominence of episodic memory loss.117 Loss of cholinergic neurons was an early neurochemical finding118 and underlies the use of cholinesterase inhibitors. The amyloid cascade hypothesis proposes that accumulation of amyloid-β peptide initiates the disease process,119 and the ε4 allele of the apolipoprotein E gene is the strongest common genetic risk factor for late-onset disease.120 Anti-amyloid antibodies have modestly slowed decline in early disease in phase 3 trials: lecanemab reduced worsening on the Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes by 0.45 points over 18 months,121 and donanemab showed similar slowing,122 although both carry a risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (oedema and microhaemorrhage).121,122

Other conditions

In semantic dementia, a variant of frontotemporal lobar degeneration, progressive atrophy of the anterior temporal lobes erodes semantic knowledge while day-to-day episodic memory is relatively preserved, the reverse of the early pattern in Alzheimer's disease.123 In post-traumatic stress disorder, emotional memories are excessively strong and intrusive, consistent with noradrenergic and amygdala enhancement of storage.51 A small study found that the β-blocker propranolol given after reactivating a traumatic memory reduced physiological responses to later trauma imagery, an attempt to exploit reconsolidation clinically.63

§12Applied findings and open questions

What memory research implies for studying

Retrieval practice. Taking a test on studied material improves long-term retention more than restudying it for the same time, even without feedback.124,125

Spacing. Distributing practice across separate sessions yields better long-term retention than massing it, and the optimal gap grows with the desired retention interval.126

Depth and sleep. Processing material for meaning improves later memory,48 and sleep after learning supports its consolidation.104

Open questions

Adult neurogenesis. New neurons are generated in the rodent dentate gyrus throughout life, and a 1998 study detected dividing cells in the adult human hippocampus.127 Whether this continues at meaningful levels in adult humans is disputed: one study found that it drops to undetectable levels in adulthood,128 while another, using different tissue processing, reported large numbers of immature neurons in healthy adults up to old age that decline in Alzheimer's disease.129

Systems consolidation. Whether detailed episodic memories ever become independent of the hippocampus remains debated between the standard and multiple trace models.52,54,56

The engram. Engram studies show that specific cell ensembles are sufficient and necessary for recall in rodents,25,102 but how such ensembles encode the content of a human memory, and how they change with consolidation and reconsolidation, are still open questions.
