Sleep and Circadian Rhythms

How the brain alternates between waking, non-REM and REM sleep; how a molecular feedback loop in nearly every cell keeps time; and what happens when either system fails.

Humans spend roughly a third of their lives asleep, yet sleep is not a single state. It is a regulated cycle between two physiologically distinct conditions, non-rapid eye movement (NREM) and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, timed by an internal clock that runs close to, but not exactly at, 24 hours 26,27. Two largely separable control systems govern this behaviour. A homeostatic process tracks how long an individual has been awake and builds pressure for sleep; a circadian process, driven by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus, sets the preferred time of day for sleep and wake 13,28. This article traces how those systems were discovered, describes their anatomy, physiology and molecular basis, and summarises the main disorders that result when they are disrupted.

§ 1Historical foundations

The idea of an endogenous biological clock predates modern neuroscience. In 1729 the French astronomer Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan reported that the leaves of a mimosa plant kept opening and closing on a daily schedule even when the plant was kept in constant darkness, which showed that the rhythm did not simply follow sunlight 1.

The study of human sleep as a measurable brain state began with the electroencephalogram (EEG). Hans Berger published the first human EEG recordings in 1929 and described the alpha rhythm of relaxed wakefulness 2. In 1937 Loomis, Harvey and Hobart used all-night EEG to show that sleep passes through distinct, recurrent electrical patterns, which they classified into stages 4. Around the same time, Constantin von Economo, studying patients with encephalitis lethargica, observed that lesions of the posterior hypothalamus produced profound somnolence while lesions of the anterior hypothalamus produced insomnia, the first anatomical evidence for dedicated wake-promoting and sleep-promoting regions 3. In 1949 Moruzzi and Magoun showed that stimulating the brainstem reticular formation converted the synchronised EEG of a sleeping cat into the desynchronised pattern of waking, establishing the concept of an ascending arousal system 5.

Nathaniel Kleitman at the University of Chicago did much to define sleep as a field of study. In 1938 he and his student Bruce Richardson lived for about a month in Mammoth Cave, Kentucky, attempting to adopt a 28-hour day in the absence of sunlight; their body temperature rhythms resisted the imposed schedule, an early indication of a robust internal timekeeper 29. In 1953 Kleitman and his graduate student Eugene Aserinsky reported periods of rapid, conjugate eye movements that recurred during sleep and were associated with vivid dream reports on waking 6. William Dement and Kleitman then showed in 1957 that these REM periods recur cyclically through the night, alternating with NREM stages, in a cycle of roughly 90 minutes 7. In France, Michel Jouvet demonstrated that this "paradoxical sleep" is accompanied by loss of muscle tone and is generated by the pons 8,30.

Circadian biology advanced in parallel. Jürgen Aschoff studied volunteers living in an underground bunker without time cues and found that their sleep–wake and temperature rhythms continued with a free-running period slightly longer than 24 hours 31. Later forced-desynchrony experiments, which control for the resetting effect of self-selected light, placed the average intrinsic period of the human pacemaker at about 24.2 hours 27. In 1972 two groups independently showed that lesions of the SCN abolish circadian rhythms in rats 11,12, and in 1990 transplantation experiments proved that the SCN sets the period of the whole animal 14.

The genetic era began with Konopka and Benzer's 1971 isolation of period mutants in Drosophila with short, long or absent rhythms 10. Work in the laboratories of Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young cloned period, showed that its protein product suppresses its own transcription, and identified the partner gene timeless 15,32. These three investigators received the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discoveries of molecular mechanisms controlling the circadian rhythm" 25.

§ 2Measuring and staging sleep

Sleep is measured clinically by polysomnography, which combines EEG, electro-oculography (EOG) and chin electromyography (EMG), usually with airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, electrocardiography and leg EMG 24. The record is divided into 30-second epochs, and each epoch is assigned a single stage. For four decades scoring followed the 1968 manual of Rechtschaffen and Kales, which divided NREM sleep into stages 1 to 4 9. In 2007 the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) merged stages 3 and 4 into a single stage, N3, giving the five-stage scheme used today: W, N1, N2, N3 and R 24.

The EEG rhythms of NREM sleep arise from thalamocortical circuits. As the brain leaves wakefulness and neuromodulatory input from the brainstem falls, thalamic neurons shift from tonic firing to rhythmic burst firing. GABAergic neurons of the thalamic reticular nucleus pace sleep spindles, and the cortex generates a slow oscillation below 1 Hz that groups spindles and delta waves together 33,34. REM sleep, in contrast, shows a cortex that is electrically similar to waking, with active, dream-laden mentation, combined with near-complete paralysis of skeletal muscle except for the eyes and the diaphragm 6,35.

§ 3Sleep architecture across the lifespan

A healthy young adult enters sleep through N1, descends through N2 into N3, and reaches the first REM period after about 80 to 100 minutes. NREM and REM then alternate in cycles of about 90 minutes, giving four to six cycles a night. N3 is concentrated in the first third of the night, while REM periods lengthen in the second half, so the composition of each cycle changes across the night (Figure 1) 7,26.

This pattern changes markedly with age. Newborns sleep about 16 hours a day, and roughly half of that sleep is active (REM-like) sleep, a proportion that falls to adult values of 20 to 25% during childhood 36. A meta-analysis of 65 polysomnographic studies across ages 5 to 102 years found that total sleep time, sleep efficiency, the percentage of slow-wave sleep and the percentage of REM sleep all decline with age, while wakefulness after sleep onset increases; most of these changes are already present by about age 60 37. Ageing also reduces the amplitude of slow waves and the density of sleep spindles, changes associated with atrophy of the prefrontal cortex and with weaker overnight memory retention 38.

Adolescence brings a distinct shift in timing. Pubertal development delays the circadian phase and slows the build-up of homeostatic sleep pressure, which pushes adolescents toward later bedtimes even though their sleep need remains high 39. In a population survey of over 25,000 people, the timing of sleep on free days grew steadily later through the teenage years, peaked around age 20 and then moved earlier again, which the authors proposed as a biological marker of the end of adolescence 40.

Consensus panels of the AASM and the Sleep Research Society recommend that adults sleep at least 7 hours per night on a regular basis 41. Recommended amounts for children are higher: 12 to 16 hours (including naps) for infants aged 4 to 12 months, 9 to 12 hours for children aged 6 to 12 years, and 8 to 10 hours for teenagers aged 13 to 18 years 42.

§ 4The two-process model

In 1982 Alexander Borbély proposed that the timing and depth of sleep result from the interaction of two processes 13. Process S is a homeostatic sleep pressure that rises during wakefulness and falls during sleep. Process C is a circadian rhythm, independent of prior sleep, that modulates the thresholds at which sleep begins and ends. The model has proved durable and has been extended to explain napping, sleep deprivation, shift work and the effects of the circadian system on sleep structure 28.

The homeostat

The best physiological marker of Process S is EEG slow-wave activity (spectral power at roughly 0.75–4.5 Hz) during NREM sleep. Slow-wave activity is highest at the start of the night, declines over successive cycles, and increases after sleep deprivation in a way that a simple exponential model of S can predict quantitatively 43. At the molecular level, extracellular adenosine accumulates in the basal forebrain during prolonged wakefulness and falls during recovery sleep, and adenosine signalling promotes sleep 44,45. Caffeine, the world's most widely used stimulant, produces arousal by blocking adenosine A2A receptors; mice lacking these receptors do not respond to caffeine's wake-promoting effect 46.

The circadian process

Forced-desynchrony protocols, in which subjects live on a day much longer or shorter than 24 hours so that sleep falls at all circadian phases, allow the two processes to be separated. Such studies show that the circadian drive for sleep is strongest near the end of the biological night, shortly after the minimum of core body temperature, and that circadian wake drive is strongest in the late evening, just before habitual bedtime. This opposition explains why people can stay awake through a long day and then sleep consolidated through the night, even as homeostatic pressure dissipates. REM sleep and sleep spindles are strongly modulated by circadian phase, whereas slow-wave activity depends mainly on prior wake duration 47.

Under chronic sleep restriction the two processes reveal hidden costs. When healthy adults were limited to 6 hours in bed per night for 14 days, their attention and working memory deteriorated progressively to levels comparable with one to two nights of total sleep deprivation, while their subjective sleepiness levelled off, so they underestimated their own impairment 48.

§ 5Circuits that switch sleep and wake

The ascending arousal system

Wakefulness is maintained by several parallel groups of neurons in the brainstem, hypothalamus and basal forebrain that project widely to the thalamus and cortex 49,50. One branch, made up largely of cholinergic neurons in the pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei, activates thalamic relay neurons. A second branch projects directly to the hypothalamus, basal forebrain and cortex and includes noradrenergic neurons of the locus coeruleus, serotonergic neurons of the dorsal and median raphe, dopaminergic neurons of the ventral periaqueductal grey, and histaminergic neurons of the tuberomammillary nucleus, joined by cholinergic and GABAergic neurons of the basal forebrain 49. Glutamatergic neurons of the parabrachial nucleus and the orexin (hypocretin) neurons of the lateral hypothalamus are also important for sustaining arousal 50.

The sleep switch

The sleep-promoting counterpart sits in the region von Economo identified. Neurons of the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO) become active during sleep 52, contain the inhibitory transmitters GABA and galanin, and project to the main arousal groups 49. Because the arousal groups in turn inhibit the VLPO, the two sides form a mutually inhibitory circuit that Saper and colleagues likened to an electrical flip-flop switch. Such a switch favours rapid, complete transitions and avoids intermediate states 49,51. Orexin neurons excite the arousal side without directly inhibiting the VLPO, and so act as a stabiliser that holds the switch in the waking position. Loss of orexin destabilises the switch, which is the basis of narcolepsy (see §10) 51,53. Optogenetic and chemogenetic experiments in mice have since identified additional sleep-promoting and wake-promoting populations in the hypothalamus, brainstem and basal forebrain 54.

REM sleep circuitry

The reciprocal-interaction model of McCarley and Hobson proposed in 1975 that REM sleep emerges when cholinergic "REM-on" neurons are released from inhibition by monoaminergic "REM-off" neurons, which fall silent during REM 55. Later work identified a second, GABAergic flip-flop between REM-on neurons of the sublaterodorsal nucleus and REM-off neurons of the ventrolateral periaqueductal grey and lateral pontine tegmentum 56. Glutamatergic sublaterodorsal neurons drive the muscle atonia of REM sleep through inhibitory relays in the medulla and spinal cord; in rats, REM paralysis requires both GABAB and GABAA/glycine receptor-mediated inhibition of motoneurons 35,57.

§ 6The suprachiasmatic nucleus

The SCN is a paired nucleus of the anterior hypothalamus that lies just above the optic chiasm and contains roughly 20,000 neurons in rodents 58. Lesions abolish rhythms in locomotor activity, drinking and adrenal corticosterone secretion 11,12. Proof that the SCN is the master clock, rather than a relay, came from the tau mutant hamster, whose free-running period is about 20 hours. When SCN tissue was transplanted into hamsters made arrhythmic by SCN lesions, rhythms were restored with the period of the donor rather than the host 14. The tau mutation was later found to lie in the gene for casein kinase 1ε, an enzyme that phosphorylates PER proteins 59.

Timekeeping in the SCN is cell autonomous. Individual SCN neurons dispersed on a multielectrode array keep firing with circadian rhythms, but each with its own period and phase 60. In the intact nucleus, neuropeptide signalling, principally by vasoactive intestinal polypeptide, synchronises these cellular oscillators into a coherent, robust ensemble that resists perturbation 58.

The SCN is not the only clock in the body. In 1998 Balsalobre, Damiola and Schibler showed that a pulse of serum induces circadian gene expression in cultured fibroblasts, which demonstrated that cells outside the brain carry functional oscillators 61. These peripheral clocks are normally entrained by the SCN through neural, hormonal and body-temperature signals, but they can be uncoupled from it: restricting food to the normal rest phase shifts the phase of the liver clock while leaving the SCN unchanged 62,63. An atlas of 12 mouse organs found that 43% of protein-coding genes show a circadian rhythm in at least one tissue, and most of these rhythms are organ-specific 64.

§ 7The molecular clock

The core mechanism of the mammalian clock is a transcription–translation feedback loop that takes about 24 hours to complete one turn (Figure 3) 65,66.

In the positive limb, the basic helix-loop-helix PAS-domain transcription factors CLOCK and BMAL1 form a heterodimer that binds E-box enhancer elements and drives transcription of the Period (Per1, Per2, Per3) and Cryptochrome (Cry1, Cry2) genes 16,67. Mouse Clock was the first mammalian clock gene identified, by positional cloning 16, and mice lacking BMAL1 lose circadian behaviour entirely in constant darkness 71.

In the negative limb, PER and CRY proteins accumulate in the cytoplasm, associate with each other and with casein kinase 1δ/ε, and translocate to the nucleus, where they repress CLOCK:BMAL1 activity 68,72. Mice lacking both Cry1 and Cry2 are arrhythmic, showing that the cryptochromes are essential repressors in mammals 68. The repression lasts until PER and CRY are degraded; phosphorylated PER is targeted by the ubiquitin ligase β-TrCP, and CRY by FBXL3 65,70. The rates of phosphorylation, nuclear entry and degradation therefore set the period of the clock, which is why mutations in casein kinase 1ε (the hamster tau mutation) shorten it 59.

A second loop adds robustness. CLOCK:BMAL1 also drives the nuclear receptors REV-ERBα/β and RORα/β/γ, which compete for ROR response elements (ROREs) in the Bmal1 promoter. REV-ERB represses and ROR activates Bmal1, so that Bmal1 expression oscillates in antiphase to Per 65,69. CLOCK:BMAL1 and the other clock components also control large sets of output genes, which is how the clock imposes daily rhythms on metabolism, hormone secretion and cell physiology 64,65.

Human genetics confirms that the same loop governs sleep timing in people. Familial advanced sleep phase syndrome, in which affected family members fall asleep around 7:30 pm and wake around 4:30 am, was first described in 1999 73 and traced in one family to a serine-to-glycine substitution (S662G) in PER2 that disrupts its phosphorylation by casein kinase 1ε 74. Conversely, a dominant splice-site variant in CRY1 that strengthens its repressive activity lengthens the circadian period and causes familial delayed sleep phase disorder 75.

§ 8Light, melatonin and entrainment

Because the intrinsic human period is slightly longer than 24 hours, the clock must be reset a little every day. This process, called entrainment, is driven by environmental time cues that Aschoff termed Zeitgeber ("time givers"), of which light is by far the strongest 27,31.

Photoreception for the clock

Light reaches the SCN through the retinohypothalamic tract, a direct projection from the retina first traced in the rat in 1972 76. The cells of origin are a small subset of retinal ganglion cells that contain the photopigment melanopsin, first identified in the human inner retina in 2000 77. In 2002 Berson and colleagues showed that these ganglion cells are intrinsically photosensitive, responding to light even when rod and cone input is blocked, with peak sensitivity near 484 nm 22, and Hattar and colleagues showed that melanopsin cells project to the SCN 23. Consistent with this, the human melatonin system is most sensitive to short-wavelength (blue) light around 460 to 480 nm, a spectrum distinct from that of the rods and cones that serve vision 78,79. Exposure to 460 nm light shifts the human clock about twice as much as an equal photon dose of 555 nm green light 80.

The effect of light depends on when it arrives. A phase response curve constructed from single 6.7-hour bright light pulses showed that light in the evening and early biological night delays the clock, while light in the late night and early morning advances it, with the crossover close to the minimum of core body temperature 81. The system is sensitive to ordinary indoor light: half of the maximal melatonin suppression and phase shift is reached at only about 100 lux, typical of room lighting 82. When people spent a week camping with only natural light, their internal clocks shifted about 2 hours earlier and became closely aligned with sunrise and sunset, suggesting that modern electric lighting delays human circadian timing 83.

Melatonin

Melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine) was isolated from bovine pineal glands in 1958 by Aaron Lerner and colleagues 85. The pineal gland synthesises it from tryptophan via serotonin; the enzyme arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase is the main rate-limiting step, and its activity rises at night under noradrenergic control from the SCN through the sympathetic pathway shown in Figure 4 84. Melatonin therefore acts as a hormonal signal of biological night, secreted in darkness regardless of whether an animal is diurnal or nocturnal 84. Bright light suppresses its secretion in humans, which Lewy and colleagues first showed in 1980 86.

Melatonin also feeds back on the clock through MT1 and MT2 receptors in the SCN. Its phase response curve is roughly 12 hours out of phase with that of light: melatonin taken in the afternoon or early evening advances the clock, and melatonin taken in the morning delays it 84,87. This property is exploited clinically to treat circadian rhythm disorders, including the free-running rhythms of totally blind people 88.

Chronotype and social jet lag

Entrainment produces stable individual differences in the phase of the clock relative to the solar day, known as chronotype. When a person's social schedule (work or school) conflicts with their chronotype, the difference between sleep timing on workdays and free days, termed social jet lag, can amount to several hours, and is common in later chronotypes 40,89.

§ 9Functions of sleep

Sleep has been found in every animal species carefully studied, and prolonged total sleep deprivation is lethal in rats within weeks, preceded by a debilitated appearance and by weight loss despite increased food intake 90,91. Some researchers argue that sleep is fundamentally a state of adaptive inactivity that conserves energy and keeps an animal safe when activity is unproductive 92. Others argue that sleep serves specific restorative and computational functions that cannot be achieved during waking 90,93. These views are not mutually exclusive, and several functions have strong experimental support.

Memory consolidation

Sleep after learning improves the retention of both declarative and procedural memories 94. In rats, hippocampal place cells that fire together while an animal explores an environment fire together again during subsequent sleep, as though replaying the experience 95. The active systems consolidation hypothesis proposes that during NREM sleep, hippocampal replay coordinated with sleep spindles and slow oscillations redistributes memories to neocortical networks for long-term storage 34,94.

Synaptic homeostasis

The synaptic homeostasis hypothesis proposes that waking experience strengthens synapses overall, which is costly in energy and space and eventually saturates learning, and that slow-wave sleep renormalises synaptic strength 93. In support, slow-wave activity increases locally during sleep in the cortical area engaged by a preceding learning task, and the size of the local increase correlates with overnight improvement in performance 96.

Clearance of metabolic waste

The brain lacks conventional lymphatic vessels, but cerebrospinal fluid flows into the parenchyma along spaces around arteries and exchanges with interstitial fluid, clearing solutes such as amyloid-β; this route has been named the glymphatic system 97. In mice, the interstitial space expands by about 60% during natural sleep or anaesthesia, and the clearance of amyloid-β roughly doubles 98. In humans, the slow waves of NREM sleep are followed by large pulsatile waves of cerebrospinal fluid flow in the fourth ventricle, coupling the electrophysiology of deep sleep to fluid dynamics in the brain 99.

Metabolic and cardiovascular regulation

Restricting healthy young men to 4 hours in bed for six nights impaired glucose tolerance, raised evening cortisol and increased sympathetic activity compared with a fully rested condition 100. Misaligning behaviour with the circadian clock has separate effects: when volunteers ate and slept 12 hours out of phase with their usual schedule, their post-meal glucose and blood pressure rose and leptin fell, and three of eight subjects showed glucose responses in the prediabetic range 101.

§ 10Disorders of sleep and timing

The International Classification of Sleep Disorders, third edition (ICSD-3), groups sleep disorders into seven major categories: insomnia, sleep-related breathing disorders, central disorders of hypersomnolence, circadian rhythm sleep–wake disorders, parasomnias, sleep-related movement disorders, and other sleep disorders 102. The most common and best understood are summarised below.

Insomnia

Insomnia is a persistent difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, or waking too early, despite adequate opportunity for sleep, with consequences for daytime functioning 102. In population studies about a third of adults report at least one insomnia symptom, and about 6% meet full diagnostic criteria 103. The dominant explanation is hyperarousal: people with chronic insomnia show raised physiological, cognitive and cortical arousal around the clock, including increased high-frequency EEG activity during sleep and elevated metabolic rate 104. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which combines sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive therapy and sleep hygiene education, is recommended as the first-line treatment by the American College of Physicians and the AASM 105,106. Medications, including benzodiazepine receptor agonists and orexin receptor antagonists, have a supporting role 107.

Obstructive sleep apnoea

In obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), the upper airway repeatedly narrows or collapses during sleep, causing pauses in breathing (apnoeas) or reduced airflow (hypopnoeas), oxygen desaturation and arousals. Severity is graded by the apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI): events per hour of 5 to 14 are mild, 15 to 29 moderate and 30 or more severe 108. In the Wisconsin Sleep Cohort Study, an AHI of 5 or more was found in 9% of middle-aged women and 24% of middle-aged men 109, and prevalence rose substantially over the following two decades alongside obesity 110. A global estimate published in 2019 suggested that about 936 million adults aged 30 to 69 have OSA with an AHI of 5 or more, of whom about 425 million have moderate to severe disease 111. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), introduced by Sullivan and colleagues in 1981, splints the airway open and remains the standard treatment 112.

Narcolepsy

Narcolepsy is a chronic disorder of excessive daytime sleepiness, often with intrusions of REM sleep phenomena into wakefulness: hypnagogic hallucinations, sleep paralysis and, in type 1 narcolepsy, cataplexy (sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by emotions such as laughter) 53. Its cause was discovered in quick succession around 1999. A mutation in the hypocretin (orexin) receptor 2 gene causes canine narcolepsy 19, and orexin-knockout mice show narcolepsy-like behavioural arrests 20. Human patients have undetectable orexin in the brain and a loss of about 90% of orexin-producing neurons 21,113. Cerebrospinal fluid orexin-A of 110 pg/mL or less is highly specific for narcolepsy with cataplexy 114. Most cases of type 1 narcolepsy occur in carriers of the HLA allele DQB1*06:02, which supports an autoimmune attack on orexin neurons 53,115. In the flip-flop model, the loss of orexin's stabilising influence explains the frequent, abrupt transitions between wake, NREM and REM states 51.

Circadian rhythm sleep–wake disorders

Circadian disorders arise when the timing of the internal clock is misaligned with the desired or required sleep schedule 102. Intrinsic forms include delayed sleep–wake phase disorder, common in adolescents and young adults; advanced sleep–wake phase disorder, more common in older adults; non-24-hour sleep–wake rhythm disorder, in which the clock free-runs, most often in totally blind people; and irregular sleep–wake rhythm disorder, seen in neurodegenerative disease. Treatment uses timed light and timed melatonin according to the phase response curves described above 88,116. Extrinsic forms include jet lag, in which the clock adjusts by only about an hour per day after rapid travel across time zones, and shift work disorder 117. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies night shift work as probably carcinogenic to humans (Group 2A), largely on the basis of circadian disruption 118.

Parasomnias

Parasomnias are abnormal behaviours or experiences arising from sleep. NREM parasomnias, including confusional arousals, sleepwalking and sleep terrors, typically emerge from N3 in the first third of the night and reflect incomplete arousal, in which motor and some cortical regions awaken while others remain asleep 119. REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD), first described as a distinct condition by Schenck and colleagues in 1986, is characterised by loss of normal REM atonia, so that patients physically act out dreams, sometimes violently 120. Isolated RBD is now recognised as an early stage of synucleinopathy: in a multicentre study of 1,280 patients, 6.3% per year developed Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies or multiple system atrophy, and 73.5% had done so after 12 years 121.

Sleep-related movement disorders

Restless legs syndrome is defined by an urge to move the legs, usually with unpleasant sensations, that begins or worsens at rest, is relieved by movement, and occurs mainly in the evening or night 122. Its evening predominance is itself an expression of circadian modulation.

Summary

Sleep emerges from the interaction between a homeostatic drive that tracks time awake and a circadian clock built from a transcriptional feedback loop. Brainstem and hypothalamic circuits translate these signals into rapid, stable switches between wake, NREM and REM sleep. Each layer of this system, from orexin neurons to PER2 phosphorylation, has been linked to a human disorder, which makes sleep one of the clearest cases in which molecular, cellular and systems neuroscience meet clinical medicine.
