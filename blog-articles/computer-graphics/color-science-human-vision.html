Color Science and Human Vision

Light, color representation, color processing and the perceptual limits that computer graphics has to respect

Prepared November 2026 · References follow the Vancouver (numbered) style

Abstract. Every image a computer produces is, in the end, a pattern of light that enters a human eye. Computer graphics therefore rests on two sciences at once: the physics of light, which says what a scene emits and reflects, and the science of human vision, which says what a person will actually see. Color science connects the two. This article covers the fundamentals of light (electromagnetic radiation, the visible spectrum, spectral power distributions, radiometry and photometry), the ways graphics systems represent color (RGB, RGBA, CMYK, HSV, HSL, CIE XYZ, CIELAB, Display P3, gamuts and profiles), the processing steps that turn rendered values into displayed pixels (conversion, gamma, linear-light math, white balance, chromatic adaptation, exposure, high dynamic range, tone mapping, grading and color management), and the perceptual effects that decide whether the result looks right (trichromacy, contrast sensitivity, brightness, adaptation, masking, uniformity, metamerism and quality metrics). Each section has worked examples, source code, diagrams and exercises with solutions. Eight live demonstrations run directly in this page.

1. Introduction: from photons to pixels

Color is not a property of light by itself. It is a sensation that the visual system builds from light [1,2]. A beam of light is fully described by how much power it carries at each wavelength. A person, however, reports only a few things about it: how light or dark it looks, what hue it has and how vivid it is. The step from a whole spectrum to those few attributes happens in the eye, where three kinds of cone cells each sum the incoming light with a different weighting, and in the brain, which compares and adapts those signals [3,4].

This reduction is the reason computer graphics works at all. Because human color vision has only three independent channels, a display needs only three primaries (red, green and blue) to reproduce most colors people can see, and a renderer needs to store only three numbers per pixel rather than a full spectrum [5,6]. It is also the reason color in graphics is full of traps. Three numbers mean nothing until someone says which primaries, which white and which nonlinear encoding they refer to. Arithmetic done on the wrong kind of number gives dark seams, wrong blends and hue shifts. And a scene can contain a range of light levels thousands of times larger than any screen can show, so something has to decide how to compress it [7,8].

Section 4 covers the operations that move color from a renderer to a screen. Section 5 returns to the observer and asks which differences people can see, which they cannot, and how to measure image quality in a way that agrees with them.

2. Fundamentals of light

2.1 Electromagnetic radiation

Light is electromagnetic radiation: coupled electric and magnetic fields that travel through empty space at the speed of light, c = 299 792 458 m/s exactly, a value fixed by the definition of the metre [10,11]. Electromagnetic radiation behaves as a wave when it propagates, interferes and diffracts, and as a stream of particles, called photons, when it is emitted or absorbed [11]. Each photon carries an energy

E = hν = hc / λ

where h = 6.626 070 15 × 10−34 J·s is the Planck constant (also exact since the 2019 redefinition of the SI), ν is the frequency and λ is the wavelength [10,12]. Absorption by a photoreceptor is a photon event: a single photon either isomerizes a pigment molecule or it does not, and the wavelength only changes the probability that it will be absorbed [1,3].

The electromagnetic spectrum runs from radio waves kilometres long to gamma rays shorter than an atomic nucleus. Human eyes respond to a very narrow band of it (Figure 2) [11].

2.2 Wavelength and frequency

Wavelength and frequency are tied together by the speed of propagation, c = λν in vacuum. In a medium with refractive index n the wave slows to c/n and the wavelength shrinks to λ/n, but the frequency stays the same, which is why frequency is the more fundamental label [11]. Color science nevertheless tabulates almost everything by wavelength, usually in nanometres (nm) measured in air or vacuum, and the difference is small enough to ignore in graphics [1,13]. Green light at 555 nm has a frequency of about 540 THz (5.40 × 1014 Hz) and a photon energy of about 3.58 × 10−19 J, or 2.23 eV.

Renderers almost never simulate wave effects such as interference or diffraction. They use geometric optics, which treats light as rays that carry power, and add wave effects back only for special cases such as thin films, iridescence or diffraction gratings [14,15].

2.3 The visible spectrum

The visible spectrum is usually given as about 380 nm to 780 nm. The edges are not sharp: sensitivity falls off smoothly, and the CIE tabulates its observer functions from 360 nm to 830 nm [1,13]. Within the band, monochromatic light looks violet near 400 nm, blue near 470 nm, green near 520 nm, yellow near 580 nm, orange near 600 nm and red beyond about 620 nm, although these names are perceptual labels, not physical boundaries [4]. Some colors, such as purple, magenta and pink, do not appear anywhere in the spectrum. They exist only as mixtures, for example of short and long wavelengths together [1,2].

The eye is blind outside this band for two reasons. At short wavelengths, the cornea and lens absorb ultraviolet before it reaches the retina. At long wavelengths, photons carry too little energy to isomerize the visual pigments efficiently [3,16].

2.4 Spectral power distributions

A spectral power distribution (SPD) describes how much radiant power a light carries per unit wavelength interval, written S(λ). It is the complete physical description of a light's color [1,17]. When light with SPD S(λ) falls on an opaque surface with spectral reflectance R(λ) (a number between 0 and 1 at each wavelength), the reflected light has SPD

C(λ) = S(λ) · R(λ)

This product is what reaches the eye, and it changes when either the light or the surface changes [3]. Spectral renderers carry SPDs through the whole light transport simulation; most real-time renderers approximate them with three RGB samples, which is usually adequate but fails for strongly peaked light sources and for some fluorescence and dispersion effects [14].

The most important theoretical SPD is that of a blackbody, an ideal emitter whose spectrum depends only on its temperature T. Planck's law gives its spectral radiance:

Lλ(λ, T) = (2hc² / λ⁵) · 1 / (ehc/(λkT) − 1)

where k = 1.380 649 × 10−23 J/K is the Boltzmann constant [1,12]. The peak moves to shorter wavelengths as temperature rises, according to Wien's displacement law λmax = b/T with b ≈ 2.898 × 10−3 m·K [11,12]. Incandescent lamps behave roughly like blackbodies, which is why the color of a light is often given as a correlated color temperature (CCT): the temperature of the blackbody whose color is closest to it [13].

The CIE defines standard illuminants so that colors can be computed under agreed lights. Illuminant A represents tungsten light. The D series represents phases of daylight; D65 is the reference white of sRGB, Rec. 709, Rec. 2020 and Display P3, and D50 is the reference for graphic arts and for the profile connection space of ICC color management [13,18,19].

2.6 Photometry

Photometry measures light as the standard human observer would weight it. Each radiometric quantity has a photometric twin, obtained by weighting the spectral quantity with the CIE luminous efficiency function V(λ) and multiplying by a constant [13,22]:

Φv = Km ∫ Φe,λ(λ) V(λ) dλ, Km ≈ 683 lm/W

V(λ) describes the eye's sensitivity in daylight (photopic) conditions. It peaks at 555 nm and falls to about 0.107 at 650 nm and 0.038 at 450 nm. In other words, 1 watt of 650 nm red light looks only about a tenth as bright as 1 watt of 555 nm green [1,13]. The constant comes from the SI definition of the candela, which fixes the luminous efficacy of monochromatic radiation of frequency 540 × 1012 Hz at exactly 683 lm/W [10]. In dim light the rods take over, sensitivity follows the scotopic function V′(λ), which peaks near 507 nm with a maximum efficacy of about 1700 lm/W, and in between lies a mesopic range where both work [13,23].

2.7 Luminance and illuminance

Illuminance is how much visible light lands on a surface. Luminance is how much visible light leaves a surface (or a display) toward the viewer, per unit projected area and solid angle. Illuminance describes lighting; luminance correlates with how bright something looks, and it is the quantity display specifications quote [7,22]. For a perfectly diffuse (Lambertian) surface with reflectance ρ under illuminance E, the luminance is

L = ρ E / π

so a white sheet of paper (ρ ≈ 0.9) under 500 lx of office lighting, a typical recommended level for reading and writing [24], has a luminance of about 143 cd/m² [21].

Real scenes span an enormous range of luminance (Table 1). The visual system copes by adapting. Over the whole range it can work across roughly ten or more orders of magnitude, but at any single state of adaptation it sees detail across only about four to five orders at once [8,25]. This gap between scene range and display range is the subject of high-dynamic-range imaging and tone mapping (Sections 4.6 to 4.8).

2.8 Human visual perception: the eye

The eye is a camera with an adjustable aperture (the pupil), a fixed-focus cornea plus an adjustable lens, and a light-sensitive layer at the back called the retina [3,4]. The retina contains two kinds of photoreceptor. About 92 million rods work in dim light and support no color vision. About 4.6 million cones work in brighter light and come in three types [27]. The cones are packed most densely in the fovea, a small region at the centre of gaze that provides the sharpest vision and contains almost no rods; rod density peaks some way out in the periphery [3,27].

The three cone types are named for the part of the spectrum where they are most sensitive: long (L), medium (M) and short (S) wavelength. Measurements of human photopigments put their peaks near 564 nm, 534 nm and 420 nm, with the rods near 498 nm [28]. Their sensitivity curves overlap heavily, especially L and M (Figure 5). This overlap is why no physical light can stimulate the M cones alone, which has consequences for color spaces in Section 3 [3,16].

Each cone obeys the principle of univariance: its output depends only on how many photons it absorbs, not on which wavelengths they had [3]. A single cone type therefore cannot tell a dim light at its peak wavelength from a brighter light away from its peak. Color vision emerges only by comparing the outputs of different cone types. After the cones, the retina recombines signals into one achromatic channel and two opponent chromatic channels (roughly red versus green, and blue versus yellow), as proposed by Hering and quantified by Hurvich and Jameson [4,30]. This opponent structure explains why no color looks "reddish-green" or "bluish-yellow", and it is mirrored in spaces such as CIELAB and in video's luma–chroma encodings [2,7].

Key idea. A spectrum has infinitely many degrees of freedom. A human observer reports three. Everything in color reproduction, from RGB displays to color profiles, exploits this loss of information, and every color "problem" in graphics comes from forgetting what the three numbers mean.

Exercises for Section 2

2.1 Using c = 299 792 458 m/s and h = 6.626 070 15 × 10−34 J·s, find (a) the wavelength in vacuum of light at 540 THz, (b) the energy of one photon at 555 nm, and (c) how many 555 nm photons per second carry 1 W.

Show solution

(a) λ = c/ν = 299 792 458 / (540 × 1012) = 5.552 × 10−7 m = 555.2 nm. This is why the SI candela definition, written in terms of frequency, corresponds to the 555 nm peak of V(λ).

(b) E = hc/λ = (6.626 × 10−34 × 2.998 × 108) / (555 × 10−9) ≈ 3.58 × 10−19 J (about 2.23 eV).

(c) 1 / 3.58 × 10−19 ≈ 2.79 × 1018 photons per second.

2.2 Where does the spectral radiance of a 6504 K blackbody peak? Is the peak inside the visible band?

Show solution

λmax = 2.898 × 10−3 / 6504 ≈ 4.46 × 10−7 m = 446 nm, inside the visible band in the blue. The light still looks white rather than blue, because the eye weights the whole spectrum, the spectrum is broad, and the visual system adapts to the prevailing illumination (Section 5.4).

2.3 One watt of monochromatic radiation is emitted at 555 nm, and another watt at 650 nm, where V(650) ≈ 0.107. What is the luminous flux of each?

Show solution

At 555 nm, V = 1, so Φv ≈ 683 × 1 × 1 = 683 lm. At 650 nm, Φv ≈ 683 × 0.107 × 1 ≈ 73 lm. The red source carries the same power but looks less than a ninth as bright.

2.4 A small lamp emits 1000 lm uniformly in all directions. Find its luminous intensity, the illuminance on a desk 2 m directly below it, and the luminance of white paper (ρ = 0.85) on the desk.

Show solution

I = 1000 / (4π) ≈ 79.6 cd. E = I / d² = 79.6 / 4 ≈ 19.9 lx (far below the 500 lx recommended for desk work). L = ρE/π = 0.85 × 19.9 / π ≈ 5.4 cd/m².

2.5 Explain, using the principle of univariance, why a person who has only rods working (for example, at night) cannot see color.

Show solution

All rods share one photopigment, so they all have the same spectral sensitivity. A rod's response depends only on the number of absorbed photons. Any two lights can be made to produce identical rod responses by adjusting their intensities, so wavelength information is lost and only brightness differences remain. Color needs at least two receptor types with different sensitivities whose outputs can be compared [3,4].

3. Color representation

3.1 From color matching to numbers

Thomas Young proposed in 1802 that color vision rests on three kinds of receptor [31], and Grassmann's laws of 1853 stated that color matches behave linearly: if light A matches B and C matches D, then A + C matches B + D, and scaling both sides of a match preserves it [1,32]. Linearity means that any color can be described by the amounts of three fixed primaries that match it, called its tristimulus values.

In the late 1920s, Wright and Guild measured how much of three monochromatic primaries observers needed to match each wavelength of the spectrum [33,34]. For some wavelengths, no positive mix matched; one primary had to be added to the test light instead, which counts as a negative amount. In 1931 the CIE combined these data into the CIE 1931 standard colorimetric observer and transformed them to a new set of imaginary primaries, X, Y and Z, chosen so that [13,35]:

  • all three color-matching functions x̄(λ), ȳ(λ), z̄(λ) are non-negative at every wavelength,
  • ȳ(λ) equals the photopic luminous efficiency function V(λ), so Y alone carries luminance, and
  • equal-energy white has X = Y = Z.

The tristimulus values of any light with SPD C(λ) are then

X = k∫C(λ) x̄(λ) dλ, Y = k∫C(λ) ȳ(λ) dλ, Z = k∫C(λ) z̄(λ) dλ

where k is a normalization constant, chosen for reflective objects so that a perfect white has Y = 100 (or 1) [13,17]. The 1931 observer is based on a 2° field of view, roughly the size of the fovea. A 1964 supplementary observer covers 10° fields [13].

// Wyman, Sloan and Shirley (2013) piecewise-Gaussian fit to the CIE 1931 2° observer

// Reflectance R(λ) under illuminant S(λ) → XYZ with Y(white) = 1
function reflectanceToXYZ(R, S, lo = 380, hi = 780, step = 5) {
  let X = 0, Y = 0, Z = 0, N = 0;
  for (let l = lo; l <= hi; l += step) {
    const e = S(l) * R(l);
    X += e * xBar(l); Y += e * yBar(l); Z += e * zBar(l);
    N += S(l) * yBar(l);                     // normalization: perfect white → Y = 1
  }
  return [X / N, Y / N, Z / N];
}

3.2 Chromaticity and the CIE 1931 xy diagram

Brightness and "color quality" can be separated by normalizing the tristimulus values:

x = X / (X + Y + Z), y = Y / (X + Y + Z)

The pair (x, y) is the chromaticity. Plotting the chromaticity of each monochromatic light traces a horseshoe-shaped curve, the spectral locus; the straight line joining its two ends is the line of purples. Every physically realizable color lies inside this region [1,13]. Because tristimulus values add linearly, any mixture of two lights lies on the straight line joining their chromaticities, and any mixture of three primaries lies inside the triangle they form. That triangle is the primaries' gamut (Section 3.9) [17]. The xy diagram is not perceptually uniform: equal distances in different regions correspond to very different visible differences, a problem addressed by the u′v′ diagram and by CIELAB (Section 3.7) [13,36].

3.3 RGB

An RGB color space describes a color by the amounts of three real primaries, normally a red, a green and a blue. It is fully defined only when three things are given [7,38]:

  1. the chromaticities of the three primaries,
  2. the chromaticity of the white point (the color of R = G = B = 1), and
  3. the transfer function that relates the stored values to light (Section 4.2).

The most common is sRGB, defined in IEC 61966-2-1 for typical computer monitors and the web. It uses the Rec. 709 HDTV primaries and a D65 white [18,39,40]. With linear (not gamma-encoded) components, an RGB space is related to XYZ by a 3 × 3 matrix. The matrix follows from the primaries and white point: write each primary's chromaticity as an XYZ column with Y = 1, then scale the columns so that R = G = B = 1 gives the white point [7,41]. For sRGB the result is

⎢Y⎥ = ⎢0.2126 0.7152 0.0722⎥ ⎢G⎥        ⎢G⎥ = ⎢−0.9692  1.8760  0.0416⎥ ⎢Y⎥

const xyToXYZ = ([x, y]) => [x / y, 1, (1 - x - y) / y];   // Y = 1

function inverse3(m) {
  const [a, b, c] = m[0], [d, e, f] = m[1], [g, h, i] = m[2];
  const A = e*i - f*h, B = f*g - d*i, C = d*h - e*g, det = a*A + b*B + c*C;
  return [[A/det, (c*h - b*i)/det, (b*f - c*e)/det],
          [B/det, (a*i - c*g)/det, (c*d - a*f)/det],
          [C/det, (b*g - a*h)/det, (a*e - b*d)/det]];
}
const mulMV = (m, v) => m.map(r => r[0]*v[0] + r[1]*v[1] + r[2]*v[2]);

function rgbToXYZMatrix(red, green, blue, white) {
  const P = [xyToXYZ(red), xyToXYZ(green), xyToXYZ(blue)];
  const M = [0, 1, 2].map(row => P.map(col => col[row]));  // primaries as columns
  const S = mulMV(inverse3(M), xyToXYZ(white));            // per-primary scale factors
  return M.map(row => row.map((v, j) => v * S[j]));
}

3.4 RGBA

RGBA adds a fourth channel, alpha (α), that records coverage or opacity: the fraction of the pixel covered by the object, from 0 (none) to 1 (fully). Alpha was introduced by Catmull and Smith in the 1970s and formalized as an algebra of compositing operators by Porter and Duff [42,43]. The most common operator, over, places a foreground F on a background B. With premultiplied (also called associated) alpha, in which the stored color is already multiplied by α, it is

Cout = CF + (1 − αF) CB, αout = αF + (1 − αF) αB

Premultiplied alpha makes filtering, mipmapping and repeated compositing correct, because color and coverage are scaled together; with straight (unassociated) alpha, a filter that averages a transparent pixel with an opaque one can pull in the meaningless color of the transparent pixel and produce dark or colored fringes [15,42,43]. Alpha is not a color in itself. It should be blended in linear light, like the color channels it scales (Section 4.3) [44].

3.5 CMYK

Displays add light; printing subtracts it. Printed inks absorb part of the spectrum of the white paper beneath them. Cyan absorbs mostly red, magenta absorbs mostly green and yellow absorbs mostly blue, so overprinting them approximates the subtractive complements of RGB [6,45]. In theory C + M + Y would make black. In practice the mix is a muddy dark brown, uses a lot of ink and dries slowly, so printers add a fourth ink, key (black, K). Replacing the gray component of a color with black ink is called gray-component replacement (GCR) or under-color removal (UCR), and it also keeps total ink coverage below the limit the paper and press can hold [6,45].

A textbook conversion from RGB in [0, 1] is

K = 1 − max(R, G, B), C = (1 − R − K)/(1 − K), M = (1 − G − K)/(1 − K), Y = (1 − B − K)/(1 − K)

This formula is useful for understanding the relationship but is not color-accurate. Inks are not ideal block filters, halftone dots spread and overlap, and paper differs, so the mapping from CMYK to color is nonlinear and device-specific. Production printing converts through measured ICC profiles instead (Section 4.10) [19,45].

3.6 HSV and HSL

RGB coordinates are hard to adjust by hand, because changing "how blue" a color is changes all three numbers. In 1978 Alvy Ray Smith introduced HSV (hue, saturation, value) and Joblove and Greenberg introduced HLS (hue, lightness, saturation; usually called HSL today) as cylindrical rearrangements of the RGB cube for color pickers [46,47]. Both put hue on an angle around the cube's gray diagonal (Figure 7).

For R, G, B in [0, 1], with M = max(R, G, B), m = min(R, G, B) and chroma C = M − m [46,47,48]:

  • Hue H (degrees) is 60 × ((G − B)/C mod 6) if M = R, 60 × ((B − R)/C + 2) if M = G, and 60 × ((R − G)/C + 4) if M = B; it is undefined when C = 0.
  • HSV: V = M and SV = C / V (0 when V = 0).
  • HSL: L = (M + m)/2 and SL = C / (1 − |2L − 1|) (0 when L is 0 or 1).
Listing 5. RGB to HSV, HSL and naive CMYK (JavaScript).
function rgbToHsvHsl(r, g, b) {
  const M = Math.max(r, g, b), m = Math.min(r, g, b), C = M - m;
  let H = 0;
  if (C > 0) {
    if (M === r)      H = 60 * (((g - b) / C) % 6);
    else if (M === g) H = 60 * ((b - r) / C + 2);
    else              H = 60 * ((r - g) / C + 4);
    if (H < 0) H += 360;
  }
  const V = M, Sv = V === 0 ? 0 : C / V;
  const L = (M + m) / 2, Sl = (L === 0 || L === 1) ? 0 : C / (1 - Math.abs(2 * L - 1));
  return { H, Sv, V, Sl, L };
}

function rgbToCmykNaive(r, g, b) {             // device-naive; real printing uses ICC profiles
  const K = 1 - Math.max(r, g, b);
  if (K >= 1) return [0, 0, 0, 1];
  return [(1 - r - K) / (1 - K), (1 - g - K) / (1 - K), (1 - b - K) / (1 - K), K];
}

HSV and HSL are convenient but not perceptual. They are simple geometric transforms of whatever RGB space they start from, so they inherit its primaries and gamma. Their "lightness" and "value" do not track perceived lightness: pure yellow (1, 1, 0) and pure blue (0, 0, 1) both have HSL lightness 0.5 and HSV value 1, yet their CIELAB lightness values are about 97 and 32 [2,49]. Demo 7 in Section 5.6 shows the effect.

3.7 CIE XYZ and CIELAB

CIE XYZ is the device-independent hub of color science. Its values are linear in light, so mixtures are sums, and every RGB space defines itself by a matrix to XYZ. That makes XYZ the natural connection space for conversions and color management [13,19]. It is not perceptually uniform, though: equal steps in XYZ do not look like equal steps in color.

CIELAB (formally CIE 1976 L*a*b*) was designed to be approximately uniform. It applies a compressive, roughly cube-root nonlinearity to each tristimulus value relative to a reference white (Xn, Yn, Zn), then forms one lightness and two opponent axes [2,13]:

L* = 116 f(Y/Yn) − 16, a* = 500 [f(X/Xn) − f(Y/Yn)], b* = 200 [f(Y/Yn) − f(Z/Zn)]

f(t) = t1/3 if t > (6/29)³, otherwise t / (3(6/29)²) + 4/29

L* runs from 0 (black) to 100 (reference white); positive a* is reddish and negative a* greenish; positive b* is yellowish and negative b* bluish. The cylindrical form uses chroma C*ab = √(a*² + b*²) and hue angle hab = atan2(b*, a*) [13]. The simplest color difference is the Euclidean distance ΔE*ab. A difference of about 2.3 is often quoted as a just-noticeable difference [50], but CIELAB is only approximately uniform, so the CIE later published the more accurate but much more complex CIEDE2000 formula, ΔE00 [51,52]. CIELAB is used heavily in graphic arts, in ICC profiles and for color tolerances [17,19].

3.9 Color gamuts

The gamut of a device or space is the set of colors it can represent or produce. For an additive three-primary display, the chromaticity gamut is the triangle of its primaries, and the full gamut is a three-dimensional solid, because the brightest color that can be shown depends on chromaticity [6,60]. A print gamut is an irregular solid set by inks and paper [45]. A useful reference is Pointer's gamut, an estimate of the gamut of real surface colors from measurements of 4089 samples; it reaches well outside sRGB in the cyan-green region [61].

When a color lies outside the destination gamut, something must give. Clipping each RGB channel to [0, 1] is cheap but shifts hue and lightness. Gamut mapping algorithms instead move out-of-gamut colors toward the gamut in a perceptual space, usually by reducing chroma while preserving lightness and hue, and may compress nearby in-gamut colors too so that gradations survive [60]. CSS Color 4, for example, specifies reducing chroma in Oklch until the color is inside the destination gamut or close enough that the difference is not noticeable [48]. Rendering intents in ICC color management (Section 4.10) are named policies for this choice [19].

3.10 Color spaces and color profiles

A color model is an abstract coordinate system such as "RGB" or "HSV". A color space is a model tied to physical colorimetry: specific primaries, white point and transfer function, so that each coordinate triple corresponds to a definite XYZ value [7,38]. A color profile is a data file that describes how to convert between a device's or space's values and a standard connection space. The International Color Consortium (ICC) defines the standard profile format. An ICC profile maps device values to a profile connection space (PCS), which is CIEXYZ or CIELAB relative to a D50 white, using matrices and tone curves for simple displays or multidimensional lookup tables for printers [9,19]. Images carry an embedded profile (or a tag such as "sRGB") so that software knows what their numbers mean. An untagged image is usually assumed to be sRGB [18,48].

Practical rule. Never pass RGB numbers between programs, files or shaders without knowing their color space. "RGB (255, 0, 0)" is ambiguous; "sRGB (255, 0, 0)" or "linear Display P3 (1, 0, 0)" is not.

4. Color processing

A modern rendering or imaging pipeline keeps color in a linear, scene-referred working space while it computes, then converts to a display-referred, encoded form at the end (Figure 8). The steps in this section are the stages of that pipeline [9,15,62].

4.1 Color conversion

Converting between colorimetric spaces is a chain of well-defined steps. Between two RGB spaces it is [7,19,38]:

  1. decode the source transfer function to get linear RGB;
  2. multiply by the source RGB-to-XYZ matrix;
  3. if the white points differ, apply a chromatic adaptation transform (Section 4.5);
  4. multiply by the destination XYZ-to-RGB matrix;
  5. handle any out-of-range values (clip or gamut-map, Section 3.9);
  6. encode with the destination transfer function.

Steps 2 to 4 are all linear, so they collapse into a single 3 × 3 matrix that can be precomputed. For example, linear Display P3 to linear sRGB (both D65) is

⎡R⎤       ⎡ 1.2249 −0.2249  0.0000⎤ ⎡R⎤
⎢G⎥     = ⎢−0.0421  1.0421  0.0000⎥ ⎢G⎥
⎣B⎦sRGB   ⎣−0.0196 −0.0786  1.0983⎦ ⎣B⎦P3

Each row sums to 1, so white maps to white. The negative entries show where P3 colors fall outside sRGB [48]. Conversions to and from HSV, HSL and CMYK are not colorimetric by themselves: they inherit meaning from whatever RGB space they are built on. Conversions to CIELAB, Oklab or appearance spaces go through XYZ (or LMS) and add their nonlinearity at the end [13,49].

4.2 Gamma encoding and decoding

Image files and display signals do not store light linearly. They store a nonlinear encoding, historically called gamma. The encoding function that maps linear light to a signal is the opto-electronic transfer function (OETF) or inverse EOTF; the function a display applies to turn the signal back into light is the electro-optical transfer function (EOTF) [7,63]. The reason is perceptual efficiency. Vision is roughly logarithmic in its sensitivity to luminance differences (Section 5.3), so a linear 8-bit encoding would waste codes on bright values that the eye cannot tell apart and leave visible steps (banding) in the shadows. A power-law encoding with an exponent near 1/2.2 spaces the 256 codes more evenly in perceived lightness [7,64]. CRT displays happened to have a similar power-law response, which is why the convention began, but the perceptual reason is why it remains [7].

The sRGB EOTF is a power function with a short linear segment near black [18]:

decode: L = V/12.92 if V ≤ 0.04045, else ((V + 0.055)/1.055)2.4

encode: V = 12.92 L if L ≤ 0.0031308, else 1.055 L1/2.4 − 0.055

The overall curve is close to a pure 2.2 power. Video uses different curves: Rec. 709 specifies a camera OETF, and ITU-R BT.1886 specifies a display EOTF that is essentially a 2.4 power [39,63]. The mismatch between them is deliberate, because a slightly higher end-to-end contrast looks right in the dim surround of a living room [7,63].

On GPUs, decoding and encoding are built into the hardware. A texture with an sRGB format (for example GL_SRGB8_ALPHA8 or VK_FORMAT_R8G8B8A8_SRGB) is decoded to linear automatically when it is sampled, before filtering, and a framebuffer with an sRGB format encodes automatically when it is written, after blending [15,65]. Using these formats is the simplest way to keep shader math linear.

4.3 Linear-light processing

Light adds linearly, so any operation that models light (lighting, blending, compositing, blurring, resampling, antialiasing, mipmapping) must be computed on linear values. Doing it on gamma-encoded values gives wrong results that are often obvious: dark fringes on antialiased edges, blurs that darken highlights, gradients that are too dark in the middle, and lighting that falls off too quickly [15,44,64]. The classic example is averaging black and white. On encoded values the average is 0.5, which displays at about 21 % luminance. On linear values the average is 50 % luminance, which encodes to about 0.735 (8-bit code 188). Only the second matches what the eye sees when a fine black-and-white checkerboard is viewed from far away [44].

Linear does not mean "use linear values everywhere". Perceptual operations, such as choosing evenly spaced gradient stops, quantizing for storage, dithering or measuring color difference, belong in a perceptual space such as an encoded RGB space, CIELAB or Oklab [7,49]. 4.4 White balance

A white sheet of paper reflects much more blue light at noon than under a tungsten bulb, yet people see it as white in both cases. A camera sensor does not adapt on its own; without correction, photographs taken indoors look orange and those taken in shade look blue. White balance corrects for the illuminant by scaling each channel so that a neutral surface comes out neutral [38,66]. In its simplest form it is a diagonal matrix applied to linear camera or RGB values:

(R′, G′, B′) = (R / Rw, G / Gw, B / Bw) · Gw

where (Rw, Gw, Bw) is the response to a neutral surface and the factor Gw keeps the green channel unchanged. The difficulty is estimating the illuminant. Common estimators are [66,67,68,69]:

  • Measured reference. Photograph a gray card under the scene lighting, or use the known CCT of the light.
  • Gray world. Assume the scene's average reflectance is neutral, so the average of each channel estimates the illuminant [67].
  • White patch (max-RGB). Assume the brightest area is white, an idea related to Land's retinex theory [68].
  • Learned and statistical methods that combine many cues; this remains an active research area [69].

In rendering, white balance is the same operation, applied so that a virtual camera adapts to the dominant light of a scene the way a viewer would. Doing it in camera or linear RGB is only an approximation of how the eye adapts. The more principled version is chromatic adaptation in a cone-like space, described next [2,38].

4.5 Chromatic adaptation

Chromatic adaptation is the visual system's ability to discount the color of the illumination. A chromatic adaptation transform (CAT) predicts the color that, under a destination white, looks the same as a given color under a source white. Nearly all CATs follow von Kries's 1902 hypothesis that adaptation scales each cone response independently [2]. A von Kries-type CAT has three steps:

XYZdst = M−1 · diag(ρD/ρS, γD/γS, βD/βS) · M · XYZsrc

where M converts XYZ to a cone-like response space and (ρ, γ, β)S and (ρ, γ, β)D are the responses to the source and destination whites. The choice of M matters. The Bradford matrix, derived by Lam in 1985 from corresponding-color data, uses "sharpened" responses that perform better than true cone fundamentals [2,70]:

M_Bradford = ⎡ 0.8951  0.2664 −0.1614⎤      M_CAT02 = ⎡ 0.7328 0.4296 −0.1624⎤
             ⎢−0.7502  1.7135  0.0367⎥                ⎢−0.7036 1.6975  0.0061⎥
             ⎣ 0.0389 −0.0685  1.0296⎦                ⎣ 0.0030 0.0136  0.9834⎦

CAT02 comes from the CIECAM02 appearance model, and CAT16 from CAM16; both also include a degree of adaptation factor D, because adaptation is incomplete when the illuminant is strongly colored or dim [53,54]. ICC color management uses a Bradford-type transform to adapt colorimetry to its D50 profile connection space [19].

4.6 Exposure

A renderer working in physical units can produce radiance values from starlight to sunlight. Exposure scales those values so that the part of the range the viewer cares about lands in the range the display can show. Photographers express exposure settings as an exposure value (EV), which combines f-number N and shutter time t [8,71]:

EV = log2(N² / t)

Each step of 1 EV halves or doubles the light reaching the sensor. EV is usually quoted for ISO 100 sensitivity (EV100); a reflected-light meter chooses settings so that EV100 = log2(L·100/K), where L is the average scene luminance in cd/m² and K is a calibration constant, commonly 12.5 [71,72]. Physically based engines borrow this model. The Frostbite engine, for example, converts EV100 into a scale factor using the ISO 12232 saturation-based sensitivity definition, which gives the luminance that just saturates the sensor as Lmax = 1.2 × 2EV100, and multiplies scene values by 1/Lmax [72,73]. Automatic exposure (eye adaptation) computes the scene's average log luminance each frame, often from a histogram that ignores the darkest and brightest few percent, and moves the exposure smoothly toward a target over a fraction of a second, mimicking the eye's time course of light and dark adaptation [15,25].

4.7 High dynamic range (HDR)

The dynamic range of an image or device is the ratio between the largest and smallest luminance it can represent or produce, often quoted in stops (factors of 2) or orders of magnitude. Real scenes, especially those with the Sun or light sources in view, easily exceed 105:1, while a standard dynamic range (SDR) display with 100 cd/m² white and modest black level may reach only around 103:1 in typical viewing conditions [7,8]. HDR imaging deals with this gap at three points in the chain [8]:

  • Capture and rendering. Renderers compute HDR natively. Cameras can capture it by merging several exposures; Debevec and Malik showed how to recover the camera's response curve and a radiance map from a bracketed sequence [74].
  • Storage. HDR images need floating-point or log encodings. The 16-bit IEEE 754 half-precision format, used by OpenEXR and by GPU render targets such as RGBA16F, covers values up to 65 504 with about 3 significant decimal digits [8,75].
  • Display. HDR displays reach peak luminances of 1000 cd/m² or more. They are driven with transfer functions designed for wide ranges, defined in ITU-R BT.2100: the Perceptual Quantizer (PQ, SMPTE ST 2084), which encodes absolute luminance up to 10 000 cd/m², and Hybrid Log-Gamma (HLG), a relative curve that stays compatible with SDR displays [76,77].

PQ was derived from a model of contrast sensitivity (Barten's model) so that each 10- or 12-bit code step stays just below the visibility threshold across the whole luminance range [78,79]. Its inverse EOTF is

N = ((c1 + c2 Ym1) / (1 + c3 Ym1))m2, Y = L / 10 000 cd/m²

with m1 = 2610/16384, m2 = 2523/4096 × 128, c1 = 3424/4096, c2 = 2413/4096 × 32 and c3 = 2392/4096 × 32 [76,77]. ITU-R BT.2408 recommends placing diffuse "reference white" for graphics at 203 cd/m², which is about 58 % of the PQ signal range, leaving the rest for highlights [26].

Listing 10. PQ and HLG encoding (GLSL).
// SMPTE ST 2084 inverse EOTF: absolute luminance (cd/m²) → PQ signal in [0, 1]
vec3 pqEncode(vec3 nits) {
  const float m1 = 2610.0 / 16384.0, m2 = 2523.0 / 4096.0 * 128.0;
  const float c1 = 3424.0 / 4096.0, c2 = 2413.0 / 4096.0 * 32.0, c3 = 2392.0 / 4096.0 * 32.0;
  vec3 y = pow(clamp(nits / 10000.0, 0.0, 1.0), vec3(m1));
  return pow((c1 + c2 * y) / (1.0 + c3 * y), vec3(m2));
}

// ITU-R BT.2100 HLG OETF: relative scene light E in [0, 1] → signal
float hlgOETF(float E) {
  const float a = 0.17883277, b = 0.28466892, c = 0.55991073;
  return E <= 1.0 / 12.0 ? sqrt(3.0 * E) : a * log(12.0 * E - b) + c;
}

4.8 Tone mapping

Tone mapping (or tone reproduction) compresses scene-referred HDR values into the range of a particular display while trying to preserve the appearance of the scene: its overall brightness, local contrast, detail in shadows and highlights, and colors [8,80]. Simply clipping values above 1 loses all highlight detail and makes bright colored lights turn into flat, oversaturated patches. Tone mapping operators fall into two families [8]:

  • Global operators apply the same curve to every pixel. They are fast, predictable and the norm in real-time rendering and film.
  • Local operators vary the curve with neighborhood, like the eye's local adaptation, preserving detail in very high-contrast scenes at the risk of halos. Durand and Dorsey's bilateral-filter method, which compresses a smoothed base layer and keeps the detail layer, is a well-known example [81].

Reinhard and colleagues' photographic operator borrows from Ansel Adams's zone system [80]. It first scales the image so the log-average luminance L̄w maps to a "key" value a (typically 0.18, middle gray): L = a Lw / L̄w. Then it compresses with

Ld = L (1 + L / Lwhite²) / (1 + L)

where Lwhite is the smallest value that maps to pure white. As Lwhite → ∞ this becomes the simple L/(1 + L) curve, which maps 1 to 0.5 and never quite reaches 1 [80]. Film-like ("filmic") curves add a toe that deepens shadows and a shoulder that rolls off highlights smoothly. The Academy Color Encoding System (ACES) defines a reference rendering transform of this kind, and a widely used rational-polynomial approximation to it for real-time use was published by Narkowicz [57,82].

Applying a curve to each RGB channel separately desaturates very bright colors toward white, which often looks natural (bright lights "burn out" in film too) but can shift hues, for example making bright orange fire go yellow. Applying the curve to luminance only and scaling RGB by the ratio preserves hue and saturation but can push colors out of gamut. Production systems use more elaborate hybrids and gamut compression [8,62].

4.9 Color grading

Color grading is the creative adjustment of an image's color and tone to set mood, guide attention and keep shots consistent. In film it is done by a colorist on a calibrated reference display; in games, a grade is usually authored as a lookup table (LUT) and applied in the final post-processing pass [15,62]. Common primary controls are [7,62,83]:

  • Lift, gamma, gain (or shadows, midtones, highlights), which adjust the black level, the midtone curve and the white level per channel.
  • Saturation, which scales the distance of each color from its own luminance (gray) value.
  • Curves and hue-versus-hue/saturation adjustments for finer control.

The ASC Color Decision List (CDL) standardizes a minimal, portable primary grade so that the same correction can move between on-set tools, editorial and final grading. Per channel, it computes out = (in × slope + offset)power, with the result clamped to [0, 1] before the power, followed by one saturation control that uses the Rec. 709 luma weights [83]. A full grade can be baked into a 3D LUT: a lattice (commonly 33 × 33 × 33) of output colors indexed by input RGB, interpolated trilinearly or tetrahedrally. LUTs are fast but must be built for a specific input space and range; applying one to the wrong encoding gives wrong results [62].

Listing 12. ASC CDL slope/offset/power and saturation (JavaScript).
function ascCdl([r, g, b], slope, offset, power, sat) {
  const sop = [r, g, b].map((v, i) => {
    const x = Math.min(1, Math.max(0, v * slope[i] + offset[i]));
    return x ** power[i];
  });
  const luma = 0.2126 * sop[0] + 0.7152 * sop[1] + 0.0722 * sop[2];   // Rec. 709 weights
  return sop.map(v => luma + sat * (v - luma));
}

4.10 Color management

Color management is the system that makes the same color numbers look the same on different devices, by attaching a meaning to every set of numbers and converting between meanings when color moves from one device or file to another [9,19,45]. In the ICC architecture, every device or space has a profile that relates its values to the D50 profile connection space. A color management module (CMM) links a source profile and a destination profile to build a transform (Figure 12) [19].

When the destination cannot reproduce a source color, the rendering intent says what to do [19,60]:

  • Perceptual: compress the whole source gamut into the destination, keeping relationships between colors at the cost of changing in-gamut colors too. Typical for photographs.
  • Relative colorimetric: reproduce in-gamut colors exactly (relative to the media white) and clip the rest. Typical for proofs and logos.
  • Saturation: keep colors vivid, at the expense of accuracy. Used for charts and business graphics.
  • Absolute colorimetric: like relative, but also simulate the source's white, for example the tint of a newsprint stock on a proofing printer.

Film and visual effects pipelines use the same ideas with different tools. ACES defines a scene-referred interchange space (ACES2065-1), a linear working space for rendering and compositing (ACEScg, with AP1 primaries), and standardized output transforms for each display type [57,62]. In real-time graphics, color management means knowing the color space of every texture (albedo maps are usually sRGB-encoded; normal, roughness and other data maps must be read as linear data), rendering in a linear working space, and outputting in the color space the operating system's compositor expects for the display: sRGB, Display P3, or Rec. 2020 with PQ for HDR [15,48,76].

Common bugs. (1) Treating an sRGB texture as linear, which makes colors too dark and saturated after lighting. (2) Encoding twice (shader encodes and the framebuffer is also sRGB), which washes the image out. (3) Assuming a wide-gamut display shows sRGB content correctly without conversion, which makes it oversaturated. (4) Interpreting data maps such as normals as sRGB, which distorts them [7,15,44].

5. Perceptual considerations

Physics says what light reaches the eye; it does not say what a person will see. This section collects the properties of human vision that most affect graphics: how many color channels there are, which spatial patterns are visible, how brightness is judged, how vision adapts, when one signal hides another, how evenly color differences are spaced, when different spectra look identical, and how to measure image quality in a way that agrees with people.

5.1 Trichromatic vision

Normal human color vision is trichromatic: any color can be matched by a suitable mixture of three fixed primaries, because three cone types each reduce a spectrum to one number [1,3,31]. The 1931 color-matching experiments are the operational proof (Section 3.1). Trichromacy has three direct consequences for graphics.

  • Three channels are enough to reproduce color for a human observer, which is why images store RGB and displays have three primaries [6].
  • No three real primaries can match every color. Because the cone sensitivities overlap (Figure 5), every real light excites at least two cone types, so the set of possible cone responses is a curved cone that no triangle of real primaries can enclose. Every display gamut is therefore smaller than the full range of visible colors [3,17].
  • Spectral detail is invisible as long as the three cone responses are unchanged. This is the basis of metamerism (Section 5.7).

Not everyone is a normal trichromat. About 8 % of men and about 0.4 % of women of Northern European ancestry have a red-green color vision deficiency, most often because the L or M pigment is shifted (anomalous trichromacy) or missing (dichromacy) [84]. Interfaces and visualizations should therefore never rely on a red-green hue difference alone to carry meaning; adding a lightness difference, a shape or a label makes them readable for everyone [4,84].

5.2 Contrast sensitivity

Sensitivity to a pattern depends strongly on its spatial frequency, measured in cycles per degree of visual angle (cpd). Campbell and Robson measured the smallest contrast at which observers could detect sinusoidal gratings and found that the contrast sensitivity function (CSF), the reciprocal of that threshold, is band-pass: sensitivity peaks at a few cycles per degree and falls off toward both low and high frequencies, reaching zero at roughly 50 to 60 cpd in bright light [79,85]. Figure 13 shows the CSF model of Mannos and Sakrison, which was fitted for image coding [86]. The CSF also depends on luminance (sensitivity drops and the peak moves to lower frequencies in dim light), on retinal location and on temporal frequency [3,79].

Demo 5. A Campbell–Robson chart. Spatial frequency increases from left to right and contrast decreases from bottom to top. The physical contrast at any height is the same across the whole width, but the stripes seem to reach highest in the middle: the boundary you see traces your own contrast sensitivity function. Move closer or farther and the peak shifts, because cycles per degree depend on viewing distance [85].

Chromatic contrast sensitivity is different. For red-green and blue-yellow gratings of equal luminance, the CSF is low-pass and falls off at much lower spatial frequencies than luminance does [87]. People see fine detail mainly through luminance and only coarse detail through color. Video and image compression exploit this by separating a luma channel from two chroma channels and storing chroma at reduced resolution (for example 4:2:0 subsampling, which keeps one chroma sample per 2 × 2 block) with little visible loss [7,88].

For displays, the CSF sets a target resolution. A display's highest representable frequency is half its pixels per degree (the Nyquist limit). Once that exceeds the CSF cutoff, extra pixels cannot be resolved by a viewer with normal acuity at that distance [15,79].

5.3 Brightness perception

Perceived brightness is not proportional to luminance. Over a wide middle range, the smallest detectable luminance difference ΔL is roughly a constant fraction of the background luminance L, ΔL/L ≈ constant (Weber's law), with a threshold of about 1 % under good viewing conditions [3,7]. Weber's law implies that sensation grows roughly with the logarithm of intensity (Fechner's proposal). Stevens's magnitude-estimation experiments instead found a power law, perceived brightness ∝ L0.33 for an extended target seen in the dark [89]. CIELAB's cube-root lightness L* is a compromise of this kind, and it puts 18 % reflectance close to the perceptual midpoint (L* ≈ 49.5) [2,13]. The same nonlinearity is why gamma encoding (Section 4.2) and PQ (Section 4.7) work.

Brightness also depends on context, which is why the same pixel value can look different in different parts of an image [4,90]:

  • Simultaneous contrast: a gray patch looks lighter on a dark surround and darker on a light one (Figure 14).
  • Mach bands: illusory light and dark stripes appear at the edges of a luminance ramp, where the gradient changes abruptly. They make Gouraud-shaded polygons look faceted even though the shading is continuous [4,5].
  • Lightness constancy: the visual system estimates surface reflectance, discounting illumination and shadows. Adelson's checker-shadow illusion shows that two squares with identical luminance can look like black and white paint [90].
  • Helmholtz–Kohlrausch effect: highly saturated colors look brighter than neutral colors of equal luminance, so luminance alone underestimates the brightness of vivid colors [2].

5.4 Color adaptation

Vision adapts continuously to the level and color of the light. Light adaptation to a brighter environment takes seconds; full dark adaptation takes 30 minutes or more, because the rods recover sensitivity slowly. In very dim light colors fade, acuity drops, and the peak of sensitivity moves from 555 nm toward 507 nm (the Purkinje shift), so reds darken relative to blues [3,25]. Ferwerda and colleagues built a model of these changes for realistic image synthesis, so that rendered night scenes lose color and detail in the way real ones do [25].

Chromatic adaptation adjusts the gain of each cone class to the prevailing illumination, which is the main mechanism behind color constancy, the tendency for surfaces to keep their perceived colors when the light changes [2,69]. Constancy is good but not perfect, and it depends on scene cues such as specular highlights, the range of colors present and the known colors of familiar objects [69]. White balance and chromatic adaptation transforms (Sections 4.4 and 4.5) are engineering imitations of it.

Adaptation state also changes how colors appear, beyond what tristimulus values predict. Colorfulness increases with luminance (the Hunt effect), perceived contrast increases with luminance (the Stevens effect), and hue shifts with luminance (the Bezold–Brücke effect) [2]. The surround matters too: an image viewed in a dark surround looks lower in contrast than the same image in a bright surround, which is why cinema and dim-room video are mastered with a higher end-to-end gamma than office displays [2,7]. Color appearance models such as CIECAM02 and CAM16 take the adapting luminance, white point and surround as inputs and predict appearance attributes (lightness, brightness, chroma, colorfulness, hue), so that images can be reproduced to look the same in a different viewing environment [2,53,54].

5.5 Visual masking

Visual masking is the reduction in visibility of one signal (the target) caused by another signal (the mask) at a similar location, spatial frequency and orientation. Legge and Foley measured how the threshold contrast for detecting a grating rises when it is superimposed on a masking grating of similar frequency [91]. In practical terms, errors are much harder to see in busy, textured regions than in smooth ones.

Graphics and imaging use masking in several ways:

  • Compression. Lossy codecs quantize high-frequency and textured content more coarsely, where the error is masked, and spend bits on smooth areas where it is not [88].
  • Rendering. Ferwerda and colleagues built a model of masking for computer graphics and showed that texture can hide faceting and other rendering artifacts, so a heavily textured surface can be tessellated more coarsely [92]. Bolin and Meyer used a perceptual metric to steer adaptive sampling, adding rays only where the remaining noise would be visible [93].
  • Quality metrics. Visible-difference predictors include a masking stage so that they do not over-report errors in textured regions (Section 5.8) [94,95].

5.6 Perceptual uniformity

A color space is perceptually uniform if equal distances anywhere in it correspond to equal perceived differences. MacAdam measured the variability of color matches around 25 chromaticities and found that the regions of equal discriminability, now called MacAdam ellipses, vary greatly in size and orientation across the xy diagram; they are largest in the green region [36]. The xy diagram is therefore far from uniform. The CIE 1976 u′v′ diagram, with u′ = 4x/(−2x + 12y + 3) and v′ = 9y/(−2x + 12y + 3), reduces this variation, and CIELAB extends the idea to three dimensions with lightness [1,13]. Still, CIELAB's hue lines curve noticeably in the blue region, and its ΔE*ab over-weights chroma differences in saturated colors, which led to CIEDE2000 [51,52], to appearance-based uniform spaces such as CAM16-UCS [54], and to simpler spaces like Oklab [49].

Uniformity matters whenever graphics software generates or compares colors: in gradients, palettes, color pickers, data-visualization color maps, gamut mapping and quality metrics. Interpolating hue in HSL gives bands of uneven lightness; interpolating in Oklch or CIELCh keeps lightness steady (Demo 7) [48,49].

5.7 Metamerism

Two different spectra that produce the same three cone responses, and therefore look identical, are called metamers [1,17]. Metamerism is what makes color reproduction possible: a display does not need to reproduce the spectrum of a lemon, only a spectrum with the same tristimulus values. It is also a source of trouble [1,2,17]:

  • Illuminant metamerism. Two surfaces with different reflectance spectra can match under one light and mismatch under another, because the product S(λ)R(λ) changes differently for each. A shirt and trousers bought as a match under shop fluorescent lighting can differ in daylight.
  • Observer metamerism. Two spectra that match for one observer may not match for another whose cone sensitivities differ slightly. Displays with narrow-band primaries, such as laser or some LED and quantum-dot designs, make this worse, because small shifts in cone sensitivity then matter more.
  • Rendering with three channels. An RGB renderer multiplies light and reflectance per channel, which is exact only for one particular spectrum per color. Scenes with strongly colored or narrow-band lights, or with materials whose spectra cross, can render noticeably differently from a spectral renderer [14].

Mathematically, a reflectance spectrum can be split into a fundamental component that determines its tristimulus values under a given illuminant and a metameric black, a spectral variation whose tristimulus contribution is zero under that illuminant. Adding any metameric black to a reflectance produces a metamer under that light [1]. Demo 8 does this.

5.8 Perceptual quality metrics

Rendering, compression, denoising and upscaling algorithms all need a way to measure how far an output is from a reference. The simplest metrics are pixel-wise. Mean squared error (MSE) and its logarithmic form, peak signal-to-noise ratio,

PSNR = 10 log10(MAX² / MSE) dB

are easy to compute and differentiate but correlate poorly with what people see, because they ignore everything in Sections 5.2 to 5.5: they weight all frequencies equally, ignore masking and treat luminance and chroma alike [94,96]. SSIM, the most widely used structural metric, compares local means μ, variances σ² and covariance σxy in small windows [96]:

SSIM(x, y) = ((2μxμy + C1)(2σxy + C2)) / ((μx² + μy² + C1)(σx² + σy² + C2))

with C1 = (0.01 L)² and C2 = (0.03 L)² for a dynamic range L. The image score is the mean over all windows; the original paper uses an 11 × 11 Gaussian window with σ = 1.5 [96]. Note that most metrics, including PSNR and SSIM, are defined on display-encoded values, not on linear HDR values, so they must be applied after tone mapping and encoding or with an HDR-aware variant [8,95].

Listing 13. PSNR and a simplified single-scale SSIM on grayscale arrays (JavaScript).
function psnr(a, b, max = 255) {
  let se = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < a.length; i++) se += (a[i] - b[i]) ** 2;
  const mse = se / a.length;
  return mse === 0 ? Infinity : 10 * Math.log10(max * max / mse);
}

// SSIM with an 8×8 box window and stride 4 (the reference uses an 11×11 Gaussian, σ = 1.5)
function ssim(a, b, w, h, L = 255) {
  const C1 = (0.01 * L) ** 2, C2 = (0.03 * L) ** 2, N = 8;
  let sum = 0, count = 0;
  for (let y = 0; y + N <= h; y += 4) for (let x = 0; x + N <= w; x += 4) {
    let ma = 0, mb = 0;
    for (let j = 0; j < N; j++) for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) { const k = (y + j) * w + x + i; ma += a[k]; mb += b[k]; }
    ma /= N * N; mb /= N * N;
    let va = 0, vb = 0, cov = 0;
    for (let j = 0; j < N; j++) for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
      const k = (y + j) * w + x + i, da = a[k] - ma, db = b[k] - mb;
      va += da * da; vb += db * db; cov += da * db;
    }
    const n1 = N * N - 1; va /= n1; vb /= n1; cov /= n1;
    sum += ((2 * ma * mb + C1) * (2 * cov + C2)) / ((ma * ma + mb * mb + C1) * (va + vb + C2));
    count++;
  }
  return sum / count;
}

No single metric is best for every task. Metrics trained or calibrated on one kind of distortion can fail on another, and none replaces a well-designed user study when the stakes are high. A good practice is to report a fast traditional metric, a perceptual one suited to the task, and visual comparisons [95,98,99].

5.2 Assume a Weber fraction of 1 %. How many just-distinguishable luminance steps are there between 1 and 100 cd/m²? Why does that suggest 8-bit linear encoding is not enough?

Show solution

Each step multiplies luminance by 1.01, so n = ln(100)/ln(1.01) ≈ 463 steps. PSNR = 10 log10(255²/10) ≈ 38.1 dB. A uniform brightness shift of about 3.2 codes everywhere gives MSE ≈ 10 and is almost invisible. The same MSE concentrated as blocking artifacts in a smooth sky, or as structured ringing around edges, is clearly objectionable. SSIM and other perceptual metrics distinguish the two because they respond to structure and account for masking, while PSNR does not [96].

6. Summary and further reading

Color in computer graphics is a chain of reductions and conversions. Light is a spectrum, measured radiometrically in watts and photometrically in lumens and candelas, with V(λ) translating between them. The eye reduces each spectrum to three cone signals, which is why three numbers suffice and why spectrally different lights can match. CIE XYZ turns that observation into a standard, device-independent coordinate system; RGB spaces such as sRGB, Display P3 and Rec. 2020 are defined by their primaries, white point and transfer function relative to it; CIELAB and its successors approximate perceptual uniformity; and color profiles let software convert between them reliably.

Processing should be done on linear light in a known working space, with nonlinear steps (tone mapping, gamut mapping, encoding) at the end. Exposure and tone mapping decide how an unbounded scene fits a bounded display; HDR displays and transfer functions such as PQ and HLG widen that window. Finally, the observer has the last word: contrast sensitivity, brightness nonlinearity, adaptation, masking and metamerism decide which errors matter, and perceptual metrics try to predict them.

For deeper study, Wyszecki and Stiles [1] remains the reference for colorimetric data and formulae; Hunt and Pointer [17] and Fairchild [2] cover measurement and color appearance; Poynton [7] is the standard text on video encoding and transfer functions; Reinhard and colleagues [8,38] cover HDR imaging and color imaging for graphics; Wandell [3] and Palmer [4] cover the vision science; and Pharr, Jakob and Humphreys [14] show how spectral rendering is implemented in practice.
