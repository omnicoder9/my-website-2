Ask who invented the telephone and you get a name. Ask who invented artificial intelligence and you get a crowd: mathematicians, psychologists, physicists, roboticists, statisticians, auditors, chip designers and chief executives, many of whom spent their careers disagreeing about what the field was for. This post profiles forty-eight of them. It is not a ranking. It is an attempt to take the title’s question literally—if AI is the sum of the people who made it, who are they, what did each contribute, and what does the record of their recognition say about how the field learns?

The organising claim is that AI is not one lineage but seven traditions—symbolic reasoning, neural learning, probabilistic inference, reward-driven agents, robotics, accountability and industrial scale—that have repeatedly handed the field to one another. Each plate below places a person in one tradition, names the work most associated with them, and draws a small timeline from that work to whatever recognition followed. Read together, the timelines carry the article’s main finding: credit has tracked the field’s changing fashions more closely than the dates of the work. Research done in the 1980s on neural networks and reinforcement learning waited an average of 36.3 years for a Turing Award or a Nobel Prize; AlphaFold2 waited four1.

A second finding is about the present. In the twenty-two months before this was written, eleven of the forty-eight changed employer or title and two more saw the organisation they lead change hands—thirteen in all, 27.1% of the list, eleven of them in 2026 alone. Any portrait of who AI is now has a short shelf life, and the plates say exactly when each role was last confirmed.

1How to read the plates

Selection. The forty-eight were chosen to cover the breadth of the field rather than its current centre of gravity: ten are founders whose defining work predates 1981, nine lead or built companies, and the rest span neural networks, learning theory, reinforcement learning, robotics and the critique of deployed systems. Inclusion required a contribution that changed what the field could do or how it understood itself, and a documentary record good enough to cite. Many people who pass the first test are missing; §11 names some of them.

Fields. Every plate carries the same five fields: what the person is known for; an editorial coding of the kind of contribution—an idea, a method, a system, a dataset, an institution or a critique; the landmark work used for the timeline; recognition, restricted to the Turing Award and the Nobel Prizes because they are the two honours with comparable, public, dated citations; and the latest role cited in the plate. The contribution coding is a judgement, not a measurement, and is used only descriptively.

The lifeline gauge. Each plate’s strip runs from 1930 to 2030. A filled dot marks the landmark work; a gold diamond marks a Turing Award or a Nobel Prize, and the bracket above it gives the years between the two (Simon’s economics prize is drawn hollow and left out of the arithmetic, because it honoured different work). An open ring marks a cited change of employer or title between January 2025 and November 2026—dashed when it was the organisation, not the person, that changed hands—and a dashed tick marks the date of writing. Lag is measured from one chosen landmark, so it understates the body of work a prize usually honours; read it as a proxy for when the field caught up, not as a precise interval.

Sources and currency. Facts are referenced in the Vancouver style, numbered in order of first citation. Present-day roles were checked against reports published in 2025 and 2026 and are current to 11 November 2026; where a current role could not be confirmed from a citable source, the field is left blank rather than guessed.

2The foundersLogic, search and the naming of the field

The first generation treated intelligence as the manipulation of symbols by rules and took search to be the hard part. They named the field, wrote its first programs and collected its first prizes: the Dartmouth proposal asked for a study in the summer of 19562, and the Turing Award first went to work in AI thirteen years later3. Two of the ten—Rosenblatt and Samuel—belong here by date but pointed toward the learning traditions that follow.

● 1950 Computing machinery and intelligence

Turing gave the field both its floor and its first question. His paper on computable numbers, published in 1936–37, defined what any mechanical procedure can and cannot do4. In 1950 he replaced the question of whether machines can think with a behavioural test he called the imitation game, and spent much of that paper answering objections that are still raised today5. He died in 1954, aged 416. Computing’s best-known prize, the ACM A.M. Turing Award, carries his name7.

McCarthy is the reason the field is called what it is. The proposal he drafted in 1955 with Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon asked for a ten-man summer study at Dartmouth on the conjecture that every feature of learning or intelligence could be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate it, and it is the document in which the term artificial intelligence first appears2. In 1960 he published LISP, a language of symbolic expressions and recursion that became the working medium of AI research10. He received the Turing Award in 19713.

● 1956 Logic Theory Machine  ·  ◆ Turing 1975 (+19)

With Herbert Simon, Newell built the Logic Theory Machine, a program that proved theorems from Whitehead and Russell’s Principia Mathematica by heuristic search rather than exhaustive enumeration13. 3Learning machinesNeural networks, from perceptrons to transformers

The second tradition held that intelligence should be learned from data rather than written down. For much of its history it was out of favour; ACM’s citation for the 2018 Turing Award describes Bengio, Hinton and LeCun as part of a small group who stayed committed to neural networks while many peers doubted they would work7. The twelve plates run from a 1980 self-organising vision model to the 2017 Transformer, and four of them record a change of role in 2025–26.

Fukushima’s neocognitron stacked alternating layers of feature-detecting and pooling cells so that a pattern could be recognised wherever it appeared in the input, and it organised itself from repeated exposure to patterns rather than from labelled examples22. That hierarchical, shift-tolerant design is the one convolutional networks later made trainable by gradient descent, and LeCun’s 1998 paper acknowledges the lineage23.

4Reasoning under uncertaintyStatistics, probability and learning theory

A third tradition asked what learning and inference mean mathematically: when a pattern seen in a sample can be trusted, how beliefs should change with evidence, and how cause differs from correlation. Its products—PAC bounds, Bayesian networks, support-vector machines, graphical models—are less visible than chatbots and older than most of them.

5Agents and rewardReinforcement learning, games and science

Reinforcement learning treats intelligence as behaviour: an agent acts, receives a reward signal and improves. Its formal core dates from the early 1980s72. Its public demonstrations came three decades later at DeepMind, with Atari and Go73,74, and the same laboratory’s protein-structure work earned a Nobel Prize in Chemistry1.

9What the ledger shows

Credit arrives on the field’s schedule

The plates yield 17 pairs of landmark and award for 16 people (Hinton contributes two). Grouped by when the landmark work was done, they show a pattern no single biography reveals. The founders’ work of the 1950s and 1960s waited a mean of 18.2 years for a Turing Award. Work from the 1980s waited 31.2 years, 1.72 times as long—and the excess is concentrated in two traditions. The six 1980s pairs in neural networks and reinforcement learning averaged 36.3 years, against 21.0 for the three 1980s pairs in speech systems, learning theory and probabilistic reasoning.

The extremes make the point. Hopfield’s 1982 network waited 42 years for its Nobel Prize24,25, and Barto and Sutton’s 1983 pole-balancer waited 41 years for its Turing Award72,75. Then the lag collapsed: AlphaFold2 was presented in 2020 and recognised in 20241. The likeliest reading is not that 1980s learning research was worth less than its symbolic contemporaries, but that its value could not be demonstrated until data and computation caught up with it; ACM’s 2018 citation is explicit that the trio’s ideas were doubted for years before they were vindicated7. Recognition, on this evidence, measures when the field became able to see a contribution, not when the contribution was made. Only two people in the article hold both a Turing Award and a Nobel Prize: Herbert Simon and Geoffrey Hinton.

Lineages are short, and they now end in start-ups

Figure 3 traces the doctoral and postdoctoral links that the cited theses and award citations document among the forty-eight. They are short and dense: Barto trained Sutton72; Sutton’s Alberta group produced Silver82; Hinton supervised Sutskever and hosted LeCun as a postdoc31,49; Bengio supervised Goodfellow55; Li supervised Karpathy42; Jordan supervised Ng48.

What has changed is where the students end up. Of the seven people in the student rows of Figure 3, five changed employer or title between January 2025 and November 2026. Four of those five—Sutton, LeCun, Sutskever, Silver—founded or now run a venture created since 202433,52,80,83; the other, Andrej Karpathy, joined Anthropic61. A generation trained in university laboratories is now, in large part, running companies.

Twenty-two months of churn

Figure 4 lists every cited change of role. Of the eleven people who moved, five left Google, Google DeepMind or Meta, and six founded, or became chief executive of, a venture created since 202433,52,80,83,108,120. Two more plates record organisations changing owner rather than people changing jobs: SpaceX’s acquisition of xAI118 and SoftBank’s agreement to buy the Robotics and AI Institute92. All of the movement falls in four traditions—learning machines, agents and builders with 4, 4 and 4 each, and robotics with 1; the founders, the theorists and the critics record none.

11Limits and omissions

Forty-eight people cannot represent a field now practised by hundreds of thousands. This selection leans toward North America and Western Europe and toward work recorded in English; six of the forty-eight are women; and it over-weights people whose contributions are easy to date to a single paper. Recognition is limited to two families of prize, both of which have their own histories of whom they notice. The recognition-lag analysis rests on 17 data points and an editorial choice of landmark for each, and should be read as a pattern worth testing rather than a result.

Among the many who could have had a plate and do not: Karen Spärck Jones, Joseph Weizenbaum, Terry Winograd, Hubert Dreyfus, Nils Nilsson, Douglas Lenat, Terry Sejnowski, Takeo Kanade, Cynthia Breazeal, Margaret Mitchell, Kate Crawford, Shane Legg, Koray Kavukcuoglu and Kai-Fu Lee. Others appear only as collaborators inside someone else’s plate—Seymour Papert, David Rumelhart, Sepp Hochreiter, Corinna Cortes, Alex Krizhevsky, Oriol Vinyals, Quoc Le and Ashish Vaswani among them.
