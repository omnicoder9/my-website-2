Generative AI did not introduce a new kind of data. It introduced a new kind of consumer. A dashboard tolerates a stale row; a language model asked a question it cannot answer will confidently invent one. This piece is about the engineering discipline that sits between your corpus and that consumer: how retrieval-augmented generation pipelines are built, why the vector store deserves to be treated as a first-class data source rather than a cache, and which of the old data-engineering habits survive contact with embeddings.

§ 01The model is a new consumer of your data

For twenty years the terminal consumer of a data platform was a person reading a number, or a program comparing one. Both are forgiving in a specific way: a human looking at a revenue chart notices when a region is missing, and a downstream job that expects a column either finds it or crashes loudly. Failure is legible.

A language model is a different customer. Hand it three paragraphs of retrieved text and it will produce a fluent, well-organised, plausibly-cited answer whether or not the right paragraph was among them. There is no crash, no null, no obviously empty axis. The pipeline defect — a table split across two chunks, a document whose access-control tag was dropped during ingestion, an index that has not seen yesterday's policy update — arrives at the user disguised as a confident sentence. Retrieval quality is a data quality problem wearing a conversational interface.

That single observation explains most of what follows. The components are new: embedding models, approximate nearest-neighbour indexes, rerankers. The obligations are not. You still need idempotent ingestion, immutable raw storage, schema versioning, lineage, incremental updates, deletion that actually deletes, and an evaluation gate in the deployment path. What changes is that each of these obligations now has a vector-shaped instance, and the vector-shaped instance is usually the one nobody staffed.

The framing used throughout A retrieval index is a derived dataset. It is materialised from tables you own, by a deterministic pipeline, using a pinned model version, and it can be rebuilt from scratch at any time. Every difficult operational question in this article — freshness, deletion, model migration, cost, multi-tenancy — becomes tractable once the index is treated that way, and intractable when it is treated as the place where your knowledge lives.

§ 02Before the acronym: fifty years of turning text into vectors

The idea that a document can be a point in space is older than most of the infrastructure now sold to exploit it. Salton's vector space model, published in 1975, represented documents as weighted term vectors and ranked them by similarity to a query vector, replacing Boolean matching with a continuous notion of relevance.1 Nearly everything in a modern retrieval stack is a descendant of that move, including the parts that look brand new.

The probabilistic branch of the family produced BM25, whose term-frequency saturation and length normalisation made it the default lexical ranking function for decades and remain competitive today — a fact worth remembering before ripping it out.2 The representational branch waited on machine learning: distributed word representations trained on co-occurrence,3 then contextual representations from bidirectional transformer pre-training,4 then — the step that actually mattered for retrieval — sentence-level encoders trained with a siamese objective so that cosine similarity between two independently encoded texts became a meaningful quantity.5 Without that last step, storing a corpus as vectors and comparing them at query time would not work at all.

Dense passage retrieval then showed that a dual-encoder trained on a modest number of question–passage pairs could beat a strong BM25 baseline on open-domain question answering by 9–19 points of top-20 retrieval accuracy, which was the moment dense retrieval stopped being an academic curiosity.6 Alongside it, late-interaction models such as ColBERT kept per-token representations and deferred the comparison, buying much of a cross-encoder's precision at something closer to a dual-encoder's cost.7

Retrieval was wired into generation from several directions at once. Nearest-neighbour language models interpolated a parametric model with a datastore of cached hidden states;8 REALM pre-trained a retriever jointly with a masked language model;9 and in 2020 Lewis and colleagues published the architecture that gave the pattern its name, combining a dense retriever over a Wikipedia index with a sequence-to-sequence generator and observing that this made knowledge updatable and answers attributable rather than locked in the weights.10 RETRO later demonstrated retrieval against a datastore of trillions of tokens, showing that scaling the corpus could substitute for scaling parameters.11

§ 03What RAG is actually for

It is common to justify retrieval as a hallucination fix. That is true but imprecise, and the imprecision leads to bad architecture. The original framing is better: a pre-trained model holds parametric knowledge in its weights, and retrieval adds a non-parametric memory that can be inspected, swapped and attributed independently of the model.10 Four properties follow, and they are the reasons a data team gets involved at all.

  • Currency. Weights are a snapshot; an index is a table. Updating knowledge becomes a write, not a training run.
  • Attribution. Every claim can be traced to a retrieved chunk, which can be traced to a document, version and owner. Without provenance, no regulated deployment survives review.
  • Authorisation. A model cannot be trusted to forget selectively, but a retriever can be made to never return what a user may not see. Access control belongs at retrieval time.
  • Economy. Retrieving the relevant thousand tokens is cheaper and more accurate than placing a million tokens in context and hoping for the best.

That last point is contested, and honesty requires engaging with the counter-argument. Context windows have grown enormously, and for a small corpus the simplest correct answer is to skip retrieval entirely: Anthropic's own guidance is that a knowledge base under roughly 200,000 tokens — about 500 pages — can simply be placed in the prompt, with caching making the repetition affordable.22 But long context is not free of failure modes. Models use the beginning and end of a long input far more effectively than the middle, producing a U-shaped accuracy curve that persists even in models built for long context,23 and controlled follow-up work finds performance degrading as input grows even on tasks that ought to be trivially length-invariant.24 Retrieval and long context are therefore complements: retrieval decides what goes in the window, and the window's known weaknesses decide how much and in what order.

A useful diagnostic If your quality problem is that the answer was never retrievable, more context will not save you; you have an ingestion or chunking defect. If the right chunk was retrieved and ranked third of twenty and the model still missed it, you have an assembly and ordering problem. These have different owners and different fixes, and conflating them is the most common way RAG projects stall. The survey literature on RAG variants is largely a catalogue of teams discovering which of the two they had.25

§ 04The anatomy of a pipeline: two planes, one contract

Almost every production RAG system decomposes into a build plane and a query plane. The build plane is an ordinary data pipeline that happens to emit vectors; it is measured in throughput, cost per document and correctness. The query plane is an ordinary online service that happens to call a model; it is measured in p95 latency and relevance. They share exactly one artefact — the index — and the discipline of the whole system comes from keeping that interface narrow.

Mapping the build plane onto the familiar data engineering lifecycle — generation, ingestion, storage, transformation, serving26 — is straightforward and clarifying. Parsing and chunking are transformation. Embedding is a transformation with an unusual property: it is expensive, non-local in cost, and pinned to an external model version. Indexing is serving-layer materialisation, closer to building a search index or a cube than to writing a table. And the raw zone obligation is unchanged: keep the original bytes, immutable and addressable, because you will re-derive everything downstream of them at least twice.

Two consequences deserve stating early. First, the index should never be the only copy of the chunks. Chunk text, chunk metadata and, ideally, the embedding vectors themselves belong in tables in your lakehouse or warehouse, where they can be joined, audited, diffed and replayed; the vector store holds a queryable projection of those tables.27 Second, because the index is derived, it gets a version. Not a mutable name that quietly changes meaning, but kb_v7 behind an alias that the serving layer resolves. This one decision buys you model migrations, chunker changes, A/B tests and instant rollback.

§ 05Ingestion, and the unstructured tax

The first surprise for teams arriving from analytics engineering is how much of the work happens before anything resembling machine learning. Corpora arrive as PDFs with two-column layouts and footnotes, scanned faxes, slide decks whose reading order is a lie, HTML with navigation chrome, Confluence pages with macros, spreadsheets where the header is on row seven, and email threads that quote themselves recursively. Text extraction that ignores layout produces chunks in which a table's column headers are separated from its values by three pages of body copy.

The mature approach is to parse documents into typed elements — title, section heading, paragraph, list item, table, figure caption, page header, footnote — each carrying provenance: source id, page, bounding box, reading-order index. Open toolchains built for exactly this exist and are the right starting point: element-level partitioning with per-format strategies,28 and layout-model-based document conversion that emits a structured document object with tables preserved as tables.29 Whether you buy or build, the output contract matters more than the tool: an element stream with types and coordinates is chunkable; a flat string is not.

Three properties from ordinary pipeline engineering carry over without modification, and skipping any of them is a recognisable failure mode. Idempotency: re-running ingestion over the same inputs must produce the same chunk ids and no duplicate index records, which is what content_hash is for. Immutability: never overwrite the raw object; a re-parse with a better layout model is a new derivation, not a correction of history. Replayability: the ability to rebuild every chunk and vector from the raw zone is what makes it safe to change the chunker — and you will change the chunker. These are the same disciplines that keep warehouses honest,30 and the price of neglecting them here is a corpus nobody can explain.

Carry the access tag from the first hop Retrieval-time authorisation is only as good as the metadata attached during ingestion. If the permission model lives in the source system and is not copied onto the chunk record at parse time, you will end up reconstructing it later by joining on file paths, which fails silently the first time a document is moved. Treat acl_tag as a not-null column, and fail ingestion when it cannot be resolved.

§ 06Chunking is data modelling, not string splitting

Chunking decides what a “row” means in your retrieval system, which makes it the most consequential modelling decision in the pipeline and the one most often delegated to a default. The tension is structural. Small chunks embed cleanly, because a short passage has a single topic and its vector is not an average of five topics; but a small chunk loses the surrounding context that made it interpretable. Large chunks retain context and waste the window, and their embeddings drift toward the corpus mean, retrieving everything and distinguishing nothing.

The standard library of strategies is small, and the framework documentation is honest that the choice is empirical.31,32 Fixed windows with overlap are the baseline: trivially parallel, no document understanding, and defensible for homogeneous prose. Recursive splitting tries successively finer separators — sections, paragraphs, sentences — until chunks fit a budget. Semantic splitting places boundaries where the embedding similarity between adjacent sentences dips, which finds real topic shifts but makes chunk identity depend on the embedding model, so a model change becomes a re-chunk. Structural splitting uses the parsed element tree: one chunk per leaf section, tables and code blocks kept whole, headings inherited. For technical corpora with real structure, this last one usually wins, and it has the operational virtue that chunk boundaries are stable across model versions.

Restoring the context you just destroyed

The best-documented improvement in the last two years is to stop treating a chunk's text as the only thing worth embedding. In Anthropic's contextual retrieval, a short model-generated description situating the chunk inside its document is prepended before embedding and before lexical indexing. On their evaluation across code, fiction and research papers, contextual embeddings alone cut the top-20 retrieval failure rate from 5.7% to 3.7%; combined with a contextual BM25 index the failure rate fell to 2.9%, a 49% reduction; and adding a reranking stage brought it to 1.9%, a 67% reduction against the naive baseline.22 The mechanism is worth appreciating for how mundane it is: no new retrieval model, no ranking trick, just better input to the same index — paid for once at build time, with prompt caching making the document-level context affordable.

A cheaper variant captures much of the benefit deterministically: prepend the document title and section path, which you already have from parsing, and which costs no inference. A more elegant variant is late chunking: embed the whole document with a long-context encoder, then pool token embeddings into chunk vectors afterwards, so each chunk vector has already attended to the rest of the document. It needs no additional training and applies to any long-context embedding model.33 The three approaches are not exclusive, and which one is right depends on corpus size, edit rate and how much you are willing to pay per document.

Retrieve small, generate large A pattern worth adopting by default: index small, precise chunks for matching, but store a pointer to a larger unit — the parent section, the surrounding page, the whole ticket — and expand to that unit when assembling the prompt. Matching and reading have different optimal granularities, and there is no reason a single chunk size has to serve both. The cost is one extra lookup against your chunk table, which you have because of § 05.

§ 07Embeddings are a build artefact with a version

An embedding model is a compiler. It maps text to a fixed-length numeric representation under a particular set of weights, and the output is meaningful only relative to those weights. Two vectors produced by different models, or by different versions of the same model, are not comparable, cannot share an index, and will silently produce nonsense if mixed. The single most useful habit in this part of the pipeline is therefore to treat model_id as part of the schema, exactly as you would treat a currency unit or a timezone.

Choosing the model, and reading the leaderboard properly

Model selection is genuinely empirical, and the standard instrument is MTEB, which evaluates embeddings across eight task families spanning 58 datasets and 112 languages precisely because strong performance on semantic similarity does not transfer automatically to retrieval or clustering; benchmarking 33 models, its authors found no single method dominating across tasks.34 Its multilingual successor expands the same idea to hundreds of tasks contributed by a distributed community.35 Read these as a screen, not an answer: the aggregate score tells you which models are worth trialling, and BEIR-style zero-shot retrieval evaluation is the closest public proxy for “will this generalise to my corpus?”36 Nothing substitutes for running the top handful against your own golden set, because domain vocabulary is exactly what a general benchmark averages away.

The dimension count is a cost decision disguised as a quality decision. Matryoshka representation learning trains embeddings whose leading coordinates are independently useful, so a 1,536-dimension vector can be truncated to 512 or 256 with graceful degradation rather than corruption;37 commercial embedding APIs expose this directly as a shortening parameter.18 Truncation is the cheapest lever in the whole system: it shrinks index memory, network transfer and distance computation in one move, and its cost is measurable on your own evaluation set in an afternoon.

The cache is the pipeline

Because embedding is deterministic given a model version, it should never be invoked twice for the same content. A durable cache keyed on (model_id, content_hash) converts the most expensive stage in the pipeline into an incremental one, and turns the awkward question “what does it cost to re-run ingestion?” into “roughly nothing.” Store the vectors in a table as well as in the index: it makes the index rebuildable without paying the model again, which is what makes index versioning affordable.

This is also where model migration becomes a solved problem rather than an outage. A new embedding model means: build a new collection, backfill it from the chunk tables through the cache, evaluate it against the golden set, swap the alias, keep the old collection readable for a deprecation window, delete it when nobody has rolled back. That sequence is unremarkable to anyone who has managed a schema migration, which is the point. The alternative — in-place re-embedding of a live index — produces a period during which the collection contains two incompatible geometries, and the symptom is a mysterious relevance regression that nobody can reproduce.

§ 08Inside the index: the recall, latency and memory trilemma

Exact nearest-neighbour search over a large corpus of high-dimensional vectors is a brute-force scan; the entire field exists to trade a controlled amount of accuracy for orders of magnitude of speed. Understanding the three main index families is not optional for a data engineer, because the choice determines memory footprint, build time, update behaviour and cost — all of which land on your capacity plan.

Partitioning with quantisation (IVF-PQ). Cluster the vectors, search only the nearest few clusters, and store each vector as a short code rather than raw floats. Product quantisation decomposes the space into subspaces and quantises each independently, compressing a vector to a few dozen bytes while supporting distance estimation directly on the codes.12 Anisotropic quantisation refines the objective to weight the errors that actually change ranking outcomes.38 These indexes are memory-frugal and fast to scan, need a training pass over a sample, and lose precision at the top of the ranking — which is why they are almost always paired with a reranking stage.

Proximity graphs (HNSW). Build a multi-layer graph where each node links to near neighbours and the upper layers, populated with exponentially decaying probability, provide long-range shortcuts; search is greedy descent from a sparse entry layer into the dense base layer.13 There is no training step, insertions are incremental, and recall at a given latency is generally the best available in memory. The cost is memory: you pay for the vectors and for the neighbour lists.

Disk-resident graphs (DiskANN). Lay a graph index out so that a search touches few pages, keeping compressed vectors in memory and full-precision vectors on SSD; this puts billion-point search on a single machine at a fraction of the RAM.15 GPU implementations attack the same problem from the compute side and made billion-scale indexing practical for ordinary teams;14 the library that resulted is now the reference implementation of most of these methods and a reasonable way to prototype before adopting a database.39

Two habits keep this section from becoming folklore. First, benchmark on your own vectors: published recall/QPS numbers are dataset-specific, which is exactly why standardised harnesses exist,40 and why billion-scale challenges are run on fixed public corpora.41 Second, watch filtered search specifically. Most real queries are not pure similarity but similarity subject to a predicate — this tenant, this language, effective as of today — and index behaviour under selective filters is where implementations diverge sharply. Milvus's design treats attribute filtering as a first-class query type with a cost-based choice between filtering first and searching first;16 pgvector added iterative index scans specifically to stop over-filtered queries from returning too few rows.42 If your workload is heavily filtered, that behaviour is your real benchmark.

What a managed service is actually doing

It is worth understanding the internals of the platform-native and managed options, because they explain the pricing and the latency profile. The generation of managed vector databases built for generative AI workloads abandoned the earlier scatter-gather design in which every shard held its slice of the index in memory — excellent for sustained high-throughput querying, wasteful for intermittent queries over large datasets, and awkward to update.45 The replacement separates storage from compute: object storage is the source of truth, writes go to a durable log and a memtable so they are queryable before indexing completes, an asynchronous builder writes immutable partitioned files, and a fleet of stateless executors caches those files on local SSD and scans only the partitions a query planner selects.46,43 Reads, writes and storage are then billed separately, so idle namespaces cost storage only.47,48

Object storage has since absorbed the pattern outright. Amazon S3 Vectors added native vector storage and similarity search to S3 itself, positioned as a roughly 90% cheaper tier for RAG and agent memory;49 at general availability it supported two billion vectors per index with sub-100 ms latencies for frequently queried indexes, and AWS frames it explicitly as performance tiering rather than a replacement for a low-latency vector database.21,50 The lakehouse route is equally direct: a Delta-synced index is defined against a Delta table, keeps itself incrementally up to date as that table changes, and inherits catalogue governance — with the telling requirement that the source table have change data feed enabled.51,52 That requirement is the whole thesis of the next section in one line: the index is downstream of a table, and freshness is a CDC problem.

A decision rule that holds up Start with vectors inside the engine that already holds your data — Postgres or the lakehouse. Move to a dedicated service when you can name the constraint that forces it: sustained QPS a shared engine cannot serve, tenant counts in the tens of thousands, or a latency budget under the storage-native floor. The migration is genuinely easy in that direction and genuinely painful in reverse, because what you carry over is chunks and vectors from your own tables, not an opaque index.

§ 10Freshness: change data capture for a derived index

A retrieval index is stale by construction. Between the moment a policy document is edited and the moment the corresponding vector is queryable there is a window, and the length of that window is an engineering choice with a price. Most teams discover they have made the choice implicitly, by scheduling a nightly full rebuild, and then discover that the rebuild costs more than the entire serving path.

The two paths are the ones you already run elsewhere. Incremental synchronisation consumes a change feed and applies row-level operations to the index; full rebuild replays the chunk tables into a new collection. They are complements, not alternatives: incremental sync handles content edits at minute latency, rebuild handles anything that changes the meaning of a record — a new embedding model, a new chunker, a new metadata field you now filter on.

Three details cause most of the production incidents in this area.

Distinguish content changes from metadata changes. Re-embedding a chunk because someone added a label is pure waste, and at corpus scale it is the difference between a pipeline that costs tens of dollars a day and one that costs thousands. The content hash tells you which case you are in; route on it. Payload-only updates are cheap in every serious vector store.

Deletes are a correctness requirement, not a cleanup task. When a document is removed or a user's access is revoked, the corresponding vectors must stop being retrievable immediately, and “immediately” means before the next query, not at the next compaction. Write a tombstone, filter tombstoned ids at query time if the store's deletion is asynchronous, and reconcile the live record count against the source table on a schedule. A retrieval system that returns content the source of truth has deleted is not merely stale; under a right-to-erasure request it is a compliance failure, because the index is personal-data processing in its own right.53

Late-arriving and time-effective data need modelling, not heuristics. If the answer to “what is the refund window?” depends on the date, the chunk needs effective_from/effective_to columns and the retriever needs to filter on them. Otherwise the model receives two contradictory chunks and picks one, and no amount of prompt engineering fixes an index that is temporally ambiguous. This is the same slowly-changing-dimension problem the warehouse solved decades ago; the ACID table formats underneath a lakehouse give you the snapshot semantics and time travel to implement it honestly.54

The rebuild you cannot afford is the one you will need Teams that never rehearse a full rebuild discover, at the worst moment, that it takes eleven days and exceeds a rate limit. Rehearse it quarterly on a sampled corpus, and keep the embedding cache warm — a rebuild that hits cache for unchanged chunks is a data movement exercise, not an inference bill. This is the vector-shaped instance of a much older lesson about the hidden cost of machine-learning plumbing: the model is a small box in a large diagram of pipelines and configuration.55

§ 11Retrieval quality engineering

Once ingestion is sound, quality work concentrates in a handful of well-understood moves. They compose, and the order in which you adopt them matters, because each adds latency or build cost.

Hybrid retrieval and fusion

Dense retrieval generalises across paraphrase; lexical retrieval is unbeatable on identifiers, error codes, product names, surnames and anything a vector treats as an unremarkable token. Running both and merging is the highest-value change most systems can make. The merge should almost always be reciprocal rank fusion, which sums 1/(k + rank) across result lists and thereby needs no score normalisation between incomparable scoring functions — it was shown to outperform Condorcet methods and learned rank fusion while remaining trivial to implement.56

Reranking

First-stage retrieval optimises recall at twenty; the generator needs precision at three or five. A cross-encoder that reads the query and candidate together scores far more accurately than any independent-encoding retriever can, and running it over twenty candidates is affordable where running it over a corpus is not. This is the stage that took contextual retrieval's failure-rate reduction from 49% to 67%,22 and late-interaction architectures exist precisely to sit between the two regimes.7 Budget for it: reranking adds a network hop and tens to hundreds of milliseconds, and it is the most common reason a demo that felt instant becomes a product that feels sluggish.

Query transformation and iteration

The query a user types is often not the query the index wants. Generating a hypothetical answer and retrieving against its embedding sidesteps the vocabulary mismatch between short questions and long passages.57 Decomposing a multi-hop question into sub-questions turns one impossible retrieval into several easy ones. Beyond that lie the adaptive patterns: retrieving only when the generator's confidence drops rather than on every request,58 and training the model to critique its own retrievals and decide whether to retrieve again.59 Each adds a model call, so each needs to justify itself on your evaluation set rather than on a blog post.

When flat retrieval is the wrong shape entirely

Some questions are not lookups. “What are the recurring themes in these ten thousand support tickets?” has no relevant chunk, because the answer is a property of the corpus. GraphRAG addresses this class by extracting entities and relationships at index time, clustering them into communities, summarising each community, and answering global queries from those summaries rather than from retrieved passages.60 The cost is a substantially heavier build plane; the benefit is a query class that vector search cannot serve at all. Recognising which class a requirement belongs to before building is worth more than any retrieval tuning.

Assembling the window

Finally, the retrieved set has to be laid out. Given the U-shaped position sensitivity of long contexts,23 put the strongest evidence first and last rather than in the middle; deduplicate near-identical chunks, which overlap-based chunking guarantees you will have; cap the number of chunks rather than the token count, since a handful of well-chosen passages beat twenty mediocre ones; and label every chunk with an identifier the model can cite, so that attribution is mechanical rather than generated.

§ 12Governance: the part that decides whether it ships

Retrieval systems fail security review for reasons that have nothing to do with models. Four concerns recur, and all four are data engineering work.

Authorisation at retrieval time. The only defensible design is that the retriever cannot return what the caller may not read: a mandatory metadata filter derived from the caller's identity, applied inside the index query, never as a post-filter in application code. Post-filtering leaks through result counts and through anything that logs candidates, and it interacts badly with top-k — filter after retrieval and a user with narrow permissions gets an empty result set from a corpus that contains their answer. Where tenants must be strictly isolated, use separate namespaces or collections rather than a shared index with a tenant predicate.

Sensitive data minimisation. Embeddings are derived from text and should be assumed to carry whatever the text carried; do not treat a vector as anonymised. Classify and mask at ingestion, keep the unmasked original in the raw zone under tighter access, and record in the chunk table which transformation was applied. The frameworks that reviewers will cite here are ordinary risk-management ones,61 and they reward a system that can show where each field came from.

Retrieved content is untrusted input. This is the concern most often missed. If your corpus contains anything users can write — tickets, comments, uploaded documents, crawled pages — then an attacker can place instructions in the corpus and have them delivered to the model as authoritative context, which is indirect prompt injection; the associated risk catalogues treat it and data poisoning of the knowledge base as first-order application risks.62 Mitigations are architectural: separate instructions from evidence in the prompt, never let retrieved text authorise a tool call, constrain what the generation step is permitted to do, and treat write access to the corpus as a privileged operation with review.

Lineage and provenance. When an answer is wrong, someone will ask which document caused it. Answering requires that a citation resolve to a chunk id, a document version, a source object hash and an ingestion run — the chain that § 05's tables exist to preserve. It is much cheaper to build that chain in the pipeline than to reconstruct it after an incident.

§ 13Evaluation is a pipeline, not a vibe check

The defining discipline of a production retrieval system is that it has a test suite. Without one, every change — a chunker tweak, a new model, a reranker, a filter — is evaluated by asking three questions in a chat window and forming an impression, which is how systems drift into quiet regression.

Split the measurement in two, because the failures have different owners. Retrieval metrics need only a labelled set of queries and the chunk or document that should answer each: recall@k (“was the answer available at all?”) is the gate that matters most, with MRR and nDCG@k capturing whether it arrived high enough for a reranker to save it. These are cheap, deterministic and runnable on every commit. Generation metrics need a judge, and the reference-free frameworks are now reasonable: RAGAS decomposes an answer into atomic statements and scores faithfulness against the retrieved context, alongside answer relevance and context relevance, all without human annotations;63 ARES trains lightweight judges on synthetically generated in-domain data and calibrates them against a small human-labelled validation set using prediction-powered inference.64 Use one, sample its verdicts against human review periodically, and do not confuse a judge score with truth.

Two structural points. First, build the golden set from real traffic. Retrieval traces — query, retrieved chunk ids, scores, what the user did next — are the cheapest label source you will ever have, which is why Figure 1 routes them back into the build plane. Second, gate promotion on the metrics rather than reporting them. An index version that fails recall@k should not become kb_current, and the alias-swap design in § 10 exists precisely so that this enforcement costs nothing.

§ 14Cost and capacity, worked honestly

Vector workloads have an unusual cost profile: the build is dominated by inference, the serving is dominated by memory, and neither scales the way stakeholders expect. Two arithmetic exercises are worth doing before committing to an architecture.

Build cost. Corpus tokens, not document count, drive the bill. A million documents averaging 1,200 tokens is 1.2 billion tokens of embedding input, and any per-chunk enrichment multiplies it: a contextual-retrieval prefix generated per chunk adds a generation call per chunk, which is why the technique's authors emphasise prompt caching of the parent document.22 The mitigations are all structural rather than clever: cache on content hash, use the cheapest model that passes your evaluation gate, batch aggressively, and enrich only the corpora where measurement shows it paying.

Serving footprint. Index memory is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is worth internalising because it is where architectural decisions get made.

The practical levers, in the order they usually pay off: truncate dimensions if your evaluation tolerates it;37 quantise, accepting that you now need a reranking stage to restore top-of-ranking precision;12 partition by tenant, time or language so that queries touch a fraction of the index; and move cold partitions to storage-native tiers, which is exactly the performance-tiering argument that object-storage vector search makes.50 Note the interaction that catches teams out: aggressive quantisation plus no reranker is usually a worse system at the same total cost, because the money saved on memory is spent on a larger generation context to compensate for weak retrieval.

§ 15Three reference architectures

Lakehouse-native batch. The default, and correct for the large majority of enterprise corpora. Documents land in object storage; a scheduled job parses, chunks and embeds into Delta or Iceberg tables; a managed index is defined against the chunk table and kept current from the change feed; governance, lineage and access control are inherited from the catalogue.51 The appeal is that almost nothing here is new infrastructure — the vector index is one more materialisation off the same tables that feed everything else, which is the whole promise of the lakehouse pattern.27 Freshness lands in minutes; operational surface is minimal.

Streaming. Required when staleness is a product defect: support conversations, trading commentary, operational telemetry, anything where a document five minutes old is the interesting one. Source changes flow through a log, a stream processor parses and chunks, embedding happens inline with a cache in front of it, and upserts go straight to the index — whose write path is designed for this, acknowledging writes to a durable log and serving them from a freshness layer before indexing completes. The engineering difficulty is not throughput but ordering and idempotency under retry,30 and the mitigation is the same content-addressed keying used in the batch case.

Agentic context layer. The direction of travel in 2026, and worth understanding even if you do not build it yet. As agents replace single-shot question answering, retrieval stops being one call before one generation and becomes many calls interleaved with tool use: an agent assembles context over a task, tracks what it has already seen, and decides what to fetch next. Practitioner commentary through 2025 and 2026 describes RAG evolving from a pattern into a “context engine” whose core capability is intelligent retrieval,65 and reports enterprise buying shifting toward hybrid retrieval and semantic layers built and versioned with the same discipline as a data pipeline.66 Standardised tool interfaces are part of the same story: exposing retrieval as a described, discoverable capability rather than a bespoke function call is what lets an agent choose it.67 For a data team the implication is concrete — retrieval requests per user task rise by an order of magnitude, so filter selectivity, caching and per-query cost matter far more than they did when a human was reading every answer.

§ 16A pipeline you can run

The claims in this article compress into a small program. What follows is a complete miniature retrieval pipeline in the Python standard library alone: no embedding API, no vector database, no frameworks. The embedder is a feature-hashing stand-in, deliberately weak at semantics, because the point is not retrieval quality but the shape of the pipeline — content-addressed chunks, a cache keyed on (model_id, content_hash), idempotent upserts, tombstoned deletes, hybrid retrieval fused by RRF, and a recall gate. Swap embed() for a real model and Index for a real store and the surrounding structure does not change.

minirag.py — standard library only · run: python3 minirag.py

"""minirag.py - a RAG index as a data pipeline, in the standard library only.

Demonstrates the five properties that matter operationally: content-addressed chunks,
an embedding cache keyed by (model_id, chunk_hash), idempotent upserts with tombstones,
hybrid retrieval fused by RRF, and a recall@k gate. Run: python3 minirag.py
"""
from __future__ import annotations
import hashlib, math, re, unicodedata
from collections import defaultdict

EMBED_DIM, MODEL_ID = 64, "hashmb-v1"          # model_id is part of the cache key

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 1. normalise
def normalise(text: str) -> str:
    text = unicodedata.normalize("NFKC", text).replace("\u00ad", "")
    return re.sub(r"[ \t]+", " ", text).strip()

def sha(*parts: str) -> str:
    h = hashlib.sha256()
    for p in parts:
        h.update(p.encode("utf-8")); h.update(b"\x1f")
    return h.hexdigest()[:16]

# ------------------------------------------------- 2. structural chunk + context
def chunk(doc_id: str, title: str, md: str, target=120) -> list[dict]:
    """Segment on structure, not on a character count: headings start a new section,
    blank lines end a block, table blocks stay atomic, and prose blocks are packed up
    to a token budget. Then prepend the context the chunk would otherwise lose."""
    blocks, section, buf = [], title, []

    def flush():
        nonlocal buf
        if buf:
            blocks.append((section, "\n".join(buf), all(l.lstrip().startswith("|") for l in buf)))
            buf = []

    for line in normalise(md).split("\n"):
        if re.match(r"^#{1,6}\s", line):
            flush(); section = line.lstrip("# ").strip(); continue
        if not line.strip():
            flush(); continue
        if buf and (line.lstrip().startswith("|") != buf[-1].lstrip().startswith("|")):
            flush()
        buf.append(line)
    flush()

    out = []
    for sec, body, atomic in blocks:                      # pack prose, keep tables whole
        n = len(body.split())
        prev = out[-1] if out else None
        if prev and not atomic and not prev["atomic"] and prev["section"] == sec \
           and prev["ntok"] + n <= target:
            prev["body"] += "\n\n" + body
            prev["ntok"] += n
        else:
            out.append({"doc_id": doc_id, "title": title, "section": sec,
                        "body": body, "ntok": n, "atomic": atomic})
    for i, c in enumerate(out):
        c["ord"] = i
        c["context"] = f"{c['title']} > {c['section']}"
        c["indexed_text"] = f"{c['context']}\n{c['body']}"    # contextual embedding input
        c["chunk_id"] = sha(c["doc_id"], str(i))
        c["content_hash"] = sha(c["indexed_text"])            # what the cache keys on
    return out

# --------------------------------------------------------- 3. embed with a cache
def _tokens(s: str) -> list[str]:
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s.lower())

def embed(text: str) -> list[float]:
    """Stand-in for a hosted embedding model: feature-hash word and char 4-grams,
    then L2-normalise. Deterministic, so the cache below is safe."""
    v = [0.0] * EMBED_DIM
    grams = _tokens(text) + [text[i:i + 4].lower() for i in range(0, max(len(text) - 3, 0), 2)]
    for g in grams:
        d = int(hashlib.blake2b(g.encode(), digest_size=8).hexdigest(), 16)
        v[d % EMBED_DIM] += 1.0 if d >> 8 & 1 else -1.0
    n = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in v)) or 1.0
    return [x / n for x in v]

class EmbedCache(dict):
    hits = misses = 0
    def get_vec(self, model_id: str, content_hash: str, text: str) -> list[float]:
        k = (model_id, content_hash)
        if k in self:
            type(self).hits += 1
            return self[k]
        type(self).misses += 1
        self[k] = embed(text)
        return self[k]

# --------------------------------------------------------------- 4. the index
class Index:
    """A vector 'collection' plus a lexical index over the same records. Upserts are
    idempotent on (chunk_id, content_hash); deletes write tombstones."""
    def __init__(self, name: str, cache: EmbedCache):
        self.name, self.cache = name, cache
        self.rec: dict[str, dict] = {}
        self.dead: set[str] = set()
        self.written = self.skipped = 0

    def upsert(self, chunks: list[dict]) -> None:
        for c in chunks:
            cur = self.rec.get(c["chunk_id"])
            if cur and cur["content_hash"] == c["content_hash"]:
                self.skipped += 1                     # replay-safe: nothing to do
                continue
            c = dict(c, vec=self.cache.get_vec(MODEL_ID, c["content_hash"], c["indexed_text"]),
                     model_id=MODEL_ID)
            self.rec[c["chunk_id"]] = c
            self.dead.discard(c["chunk_id"])
            self.written += 1

    def delete_doc(self, doc_id: str) -> int:
        gone = [k for k, r in self.rec.items() if r["doc_id"] == doc_id]
        self.dead.update(gone)
        return len(gone)

    @property
    def live(self) -> list[dict]:
        return [r for k, r in self.rec.items() if k not in self.dead]

    # ---- retrieval -----------------------------------------------------------
    def dense(self, q: str, k=6) -> list[str]:
        qv = embed(q)
        s = [(sum(a * b for a, b in zip(qv, r["vec"])), r["chunk_id"]) for r in self.live]
        return [cid for _, cid in sorted(s, reverse=True)[:k]]

    def bm25(self, q: str, k=6, k1=1.2, b=0.75) -> list[str]:
        docs = {r["chunk_id"]: _tokens(r["indexed_text"]) for r in self.live}
        if not docs:
            return []
        avgdl = sum(len(t) for t in docs.values()) / len(docs)
        df = defaultdict(int)
        for t in docs.values():
            for w in set(t):
                df[w] += 1
        N, out = len(docs), []
        for cid, toks in docs.items():
            tf = defaultdict(int)
            for w in toks:
                tf[w] += 1
            s = 0.0
            for w in set(_tokens(q)):
                if w not in df:
                    continue
                idf = math.log(1 + (N - df[w] + 0.5) / (df[w] + 0.5))
                s += idf * tf[w] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[w] + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(toks) / avgdl))
            if s:
                out.append((s, cid))
        return [cid for _, cid in sorted(out, reverse=True)[:k]]

    def hybrid(self, q: str, k=5, rrf_k=60) -> list[str]:
        score = defaultdict(float)
        for lst in (self.dense(q, 10), self.bm25(q, 10)):
            for rank, cid in enumerate(lst):
                score[cid] += 1.0 / (rrf_k + rank + 1)
        return [c for c, _ in sorted(score.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])[:k]]

# ------------------------------------------------------------------- 5. metrics
def recall_at_k(index: Index, golden: list[tuple[str, str]], k=3) -> float:
    hit = 0
    for q, want_doc in golden:
        docs = {index.rec[c]["doc_id"] for c in index.hybrid(q, k)}
        hit += want_doc in docs
    return hit / len(golden)

CORPUS = [
    ("d1", "Refund policy", """# Eligibility
Customers may request a refund within 30 days of delivery. Digital goods are
excluded once downloaded.

# Processing time
Approved refunds are returned to the original payment method within 5 business days.

| tier | window | fee |
| pro | 30 days | none |
| trial | 14 days | none |"""),
    ("d2", "Incident response runbook", """# Severity levels
A SEV1 is customer-visible data loss. Page the on-call engineer immediately.

# Postmortems
Every SEV1 and SEV2 requires a written postmortem within five business days."""),
    ("d3", "Embedding model policy", """# Model pinning
Every index records the embedding model id. Changing the model requires a full
rebuild into a new collection, then an alias swap.

# Deprecation
Retired model versions stay readable for one quarter so rollback is possible."""),
]
GOLDEN = [("how long is the refund window", "d1"),
          ("who is paged for customer data loss", "d2"),
          ("do we rebuild when the embedding model changes", "d3"),
          ("refund fee for the trial tier", "d1")]

if __name__ == "__main__":
    cache = EmbedCache()
    idx = Index("kb_v1", cache)
    chunks = [c for d, t, m in CORPUS for c in chunk(d, t, m)]
    idx.upsert(chunks)
    idx.upsert(chunks)                                  # replay the same batch
    print(f"chunks={len(chunks)}  written={idx.written}  skipped_on_replay={idx.skipped}"
          f"  cache_hits={EmbedCache.hits} misses={EmbedCache.misses}")

    q = "refund window for trial accounts"
    print(f"\nquery: {q!r}")
    for label, ids in (("dense ", idx.dense(q, 3)), ("bm25  ", idx.bm25(q, 3)),
                       ("hybrid", idx.hybrid(q, 3))):
        print(f"  {label}: " + " | ".join(
            f"{idx.rec[c]['doc_id']}#{idx.rec[c]['ord']} {idx.rec[c]['section'][:24]}" for c in ids))

    print(f"\nrecall@3 before edit: {recall_at_k(idx, GOLDEN):.2f}")
    edited = chunk("d1", "Refund policy", CORPUS[0][2].replace("30 days", "45 days"))
    idx.upsert(edited)
    print(f"after editing one doc: written={idx.written} (only changed chunks re-embedded)")
    idx.upsert(chunks)                                  # roll the edit back
    print(f"after rollback: cache_hits={EmbedCache.hits} (old vectors were still cached)")
    print(f"tombstoned on delete of d2: {idx.delete_doc('d2')}  live_records={len(idx.live)}")
    print(f"recall@3 after delete: {recall_at_k(idx, GOLDEN):.2f}  <- the gate that should fail CI")

Running it produces the following, and every number in it is a claim made earlier in this article:

Read the output as a checklist. Seven chunks are written on the first pass and zero on an identical replay, because the upsert compares content hashes — that is idempotency, and it is why a re-run costs nothing. Editing a single sentence in one document re-embeds two chunks rather than seven, because the hash changes only where the text changed. Rolling the edit back is served entirely from the cache: two hits, no inference, which is what makes model rollback and index rebuilds affordable. Deleting a document tombstones two records and drops recall@3 from 1.00 to 0.75 — a regression that a build gate would catch before promotion, and that no amount of manual spot-checking in a chat window reliably would.

Worth noticing in the retrieval output For the query about trial-tier refunds, the dense and lexical retrievers disagree on their second and third results, and the fused ranking keeps what they agree on. That is Figure 8 in miniature, on nine lines of code. The lexical retriever also surfaces an unrelated chunk from the model-policy document, and inspecting the overlap shows the culprit is the single shared token “for” — this implementation does no stopword removal. Hybrid retrieval is not free of noise; it is merely noise that is uncorrelated between the two retrievers, which is exactly what makes rank fusion work.

§ 17Anti-patterns, ranked by how much they cost to unwind

  • The index as the only copy of the chunks. Everything else in this list is recoverable; this one means a chunker or model change requires re-ingesting the world. Chunks and vectors belong in tables first, in the index second.
  • No model_id on the record. Mixed geometries in a single collection produce relevance failures with no error message and no way to identify affected rows after the fact.
  • Deletion handled asynchronously. A tombstone that lands at the next compaction is a data leak with a schedule.
  • Access control applied after retrieval. Post-filtering both leaks and silently starves narrowly-permissioned users of results.
  • Dense-only retrieval. Guarantees systematic failure on exactly the queries enterprise users type: ticket numbers, SKUs, error codes, surnames. Add BM25 and RRF before tuning anything else.56
  • Fixed-size chunking on structured technical documents. Cheap to implement, expensive to diagnose, because the failure looks like a model problem.
  • No golden set. Every subsequent change becomes unfalsifiable. Fifty labelled queries beat zero by an enormous margin and take an afternoon.
  • Treating retrieved text as trusted. If users can write into the corpus, they can write into the prompt.62
  • Adopting a specialist vector database before measuring. Vector search inside Postgres has supported graph indexing since pgvector 0.5.0 in 202368 and has improved its filtering behaviour since;42 many workloads never outgrow it, and the ones that do will tell you clearly.

§ 18Where this is heading

Three trends are visible and none of them is speculative.

Vectors are becoming a column type, not a product category. The trajectory from specialist system to engine feature to storage feature is nearly complete: proximity-graph indexing inside Postgres,68 indexes defined declaratively against lakehouse tables and maintained from a change feed,51 and native vector storage in object storage itself, positioned as a tier rather than a competitor.21,50 For data engineers this is straightforwardly good news: it collapses the number of systems that need governing, and it makes the index look like what it always was — a derived dataset.

The build plane is getting heavier, and that is where quality lives. Every technique that has demonstrably improved retrieval in the last two years moved work earlier: context prepended before embedding,22 pooling deferred until after a document-level forward pass,33 entity graphs and community summaries constructed at index time to answer questions no passage contains.60 Query-time cleverness has a latency ceiling; build-time cleverness is paid once and amortised over every query. Expect ingestion pipelines to keep absorbing model inference, and expect the cache in Figure 3 to become the most load-bearing component you own.

The consumer is becoming an agent. A human asks one question and reads one answer; an agent issues many retrievals across a task, and the aggregate load, filter complexity and cost sensitivity all change by an order of magnitude. The commentary describing RAG's evolution into a context engine and the procurement data showing spend moving from evaluation toward retrieval optimisation both point the same way,65,66 as does the standardisation of retrieval as a described, discoverable tool an agent can select.67 The semantic layer — which entities exist, how they relate, which source is authoritative for which question, who may see what — stops being documentation and becomes versioned, tested infrastructure. That is a data modelling problem, and it belongs to the same people who own the warehouse.

Which returns to the observation this piece began with. Nothing in a retrieval stack asks data engineers to become machine learning researchers. It asks them to apply an existing discipline to an unfamiliar artefact: a table of vectors, derived from documents, versioned by the model that produced it, kept fresh by a change feed, gated by a test suite, and governed like everything else. The parts that are genuinely new — approximate nearest-neighbour geometry, embedding model selection, the particular way long contexts degrade — are a week of reading.13,34,23 The parts that decide whether the system works were already your job.
