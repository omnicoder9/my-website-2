1The question

A job advertisement is a claim about scarcity. When an organisation invents a title it is asserting that some quantity has become expensive enough, and specialised enough, that a person should be assigned to it full time. Read that way, the recent proliferation of AI infrastructure titles is not a fashion. It is a readable trace of where the money started going.

The common reading of this list — AI platform engineer, AI infrastructure engineer, GPU infrastructure engineer, AI compute engineer, inference engineer, model serving engineer, AI reliability engineer, AI observability engineer, AI FinOps engineer, LLMOps engineer, MLOps engineer, AI operations engineer, AI cloud architect, AI infrastructure architect — is that the industry has generated more names than distinctions, and that most of them are the same job with different words in front of it. That reading is half right, and the half it gets wrong is the interesting half.

There is a way to make the question precise. Take a single accelerator-second, bought under contract, and follow it to the point where it has produced something a user wanted and got in time to use. The fraction that survives the journey is a product of seven ratios, each one the numerator of the last:

delivered ÷ purchased  =  SITED × HEALTHY × ALLOCATED × RUNNING × BUSY × USEFUL × DELIVERED

SITED is the share of what was contracted that is actually installed, powered and cooled. HEALTHY is the share of that which is fit to run right now. ALLOCATED is the share of the healthy fleet that a scheduler has handed to some job. RUNNING is the share of allocated time in which the job is making forward progress rather than starting, restarting or recovering. BUSY is the share of progressing time in which a kernel is actually resident on the device. USEFUL is the share of resident time in which the device's arithmetic units are doing arithmetic. DELIVERED is the share of finished work that somebody wanted and that arrived inside its objective.

The decomposition is not a metaphor. Each term is a ratio of two counts of accelerator-seconds, and each one is measured by a different instrument, owned by a different team, and lost for a different reason. Assembled from the measurements in this article's laboratory, the chain multiplies out to 20.78%: one purchased accelerator-hour buys 12.5 minutes of wanted work. That number is neither scandalous nor unusual. It is what a seven-stage product does.

The thesis of what follows is that this chain is the right instrument for reading the job titles. A title is a claim to own a term. Some titles move a term — they hold the actuator, and can change the number without asking anyone. Some can only measure it and must persuade someone else to act. Some inherit it as a constraint and organise their work around it. Coding all fourteen titles that way, against all seven terms, turns a vocabulary argument into an arithmetic one, and it produces four findings that are hard to reach any other way.

The first is that the fourteen titles are genuinely distinct, but only just. Asking all seven questions separates every pair; asking the right four also separates every pair, and no set of three does. The second is that the effort is allocated almost exactly backwards. The correlation between how much room a term has left in it and how many titles can move it is -0.6433 — negative, and not weakly. The largest single loss in the whole chain, 59.0 points at USEFUL, is moved by exactly one title in fourteen; the term with the least room left, 2.0 points at BUSY, is moved by three; and the term seven titles can move is worth 10.0 points. The third is that 4 titles can stop something on their own authority and 10 cannot, which turns out to matter more than any difference in their tooling. And the fourth is arithmetic rather than sociological: in a product, every term has an elasticity of exactly one, so no term is more leveraged than another by virtue of where it sits. The only thing that makes a lever worth pulling is how much slack is left in it — which is precisely the quantity the industry's hiring appears not to have consulted.

2Where the titles came from

None of these roles is new in the sense of having no ancestors. All of them are new in the sense that the thing they specialise in was, until recently, a sub-clause of somebody else's job.

The operational lineage, 1990–2015

The oldest thread is system administration, which by the early 2000s had acquired a literature and a professional identity but not much leverage [1]. Two movements changed that. The first was continuous delivery, which argued that the path from a change to production should be automated, measured, and short [2], and DevOps, which argued that the separation between the people who build and the people who run is the source of most operational pain [3,4]. The second was site reliability engineering, which took the opposite tack from the same premise: keep an operations function, but cap the operational work, staff it with engineers, and govern it with an explicit numerical objective and a budget for failing to meet it [5,6].

Both movements assumed something that stopped being true. They assumed the scarce resource was human attention, and that compute was elastic — that if you needed more, you asked for more, and the interesting question was how quickly you could deploy onto it. Every practice built on that assumption, from autoscaling to blue-green deployment to the idea that you should over-provision rather than tune, is a practice that quietly spends compute to buy reliability or velocity.

The machine-learning thread, 2014–2022

Running in parallel was a second lineage that started from data rather than uptime. Its founding observation was made in 2014 and 2015: in a production machine-learning system, the model itself is a small box surrounded by a much larger diagram of configuration, data collection, feature extraction, analysis tooling, process management and monitoring, and the surrounding diagram accrues technical debt in ways ordinary software does not [7,8]. What followed was a decade of building the missing machinery: readiness rubrics [9], engineering guidance [10], case-study surveys establishing that deployment rather than modelling is where projects die [11,12], an architecture literature [13,14], and interview studies of what practitioners actually do [15].

MLOps is the name that stuck. It is essentially continuous delivery with two additions that turned out to be hard: data as a versioned dependency, and a promotion decision that rests on a statistical claim rather than a passing test. Platform engineering arrived alongside it from a different direction, arguing that the way to give teams autonomy without giving every team an operations burden was to build an internal product — a paved road with golden paths [16,17,18].

The accelerator shock, 2022–

What changed after 2022 was not the software practices. It was the price and the divisibility of the underlying resource, and the shape of the workload that consumed it.

Three things happened at once. Compute stopped being elastic: accelerators became supply-constrained, bought in blocks against multi-year commitments, and sited in buildings whose power envelope is a hard constraint on a timescale of years. The IEA's 2026 assessment puts data-centre electricity demand growth at 17% in 2025, with demand from AI-focused sites growing 50%, and projects total consumption roughly doubling from 485 TWh in 2025 to around 950 TWh by 2030 [19,20]. Second, the workload became synchronous and gang-scheduled. A training job on sixteen thousand devices is one job: it progresses at the rate of its slowest rank and stops entirely when any rank dies [21,22]. Third, the model architecture converged. Where a general deep-learning cluster ran convolutional, recurrent and tabular workloads with wildly different resource profiles, an LLM cluster runs one architecture, which makes its utilisation profile bimodal and its failure modes uniform [23,24].

The consequences are visible in the two best public datasets. A six-month trace from a 4,704-device production LLM datacentre found that pretraining jobs were 3.2% of the job count and 94.0% of the GPU time, while evaluation jobs were 92.9% of the count and 0.8% of the resource; that median GPU utilisation read 97% and 99% against a median streaming-multiprocessor activity of about 40%; that roughly 40% of jobs failed, with completed jobs consuming only 20–30% of the resource; and that infrastructure failures were 11% of failed jobs but over 82% of the GPU time lost to failure [23]. Meta's published snapshot of a 405-billion-parameter run on 16,384 accelerators recorded 466 interruptions in 54 days, 419 of them unexpected, roughly 78% attributed to hardware, and maintained over 90% effective training time only through automation [25]. Earlier traces from general deep-learning clusters look nothing like either [26,27,28].

Those two datasets are the reason the titles fractionated when they did. Every number in them is a different term of the same product, failing for a different reason, and measurable only by a different instrument. An organisation reading its own version of those numbers concludes, correctly, that no single person can hold all seven.

A sociologist of work would recognise the pattern without needing to know what an accelerator is. Occupations form when a body of task-specific knowledge becomes scarce enough to be worth claiming and inspectable enough that the claim can be defended, and the resulting jurisdictions are contested at their boundaries rather than at their centres [29,30,31]. The boundaries in this list are exactly where the arguments are: platform against infrastructure, MLOps against LLMOps, reliability against operations. What settles such contests is rarely the merits. It is which claimant can point to a measurement that only they can make — which is why the observability role, alone among the fourteen, can exist without owning anything.

Why the names arrived in the order they did

The sequence is legible. MLOps came first, around 2018, because the first bottleneck was getting a trained artefact into production at all. Platform engineering followed, because the second bottleneck was that every team was building its own path. GPU infrastructure engineering emerged around 2021 as fleets grew past the point where a general systems administrator could keep the drivers, firmware and collective libraries coherent. Inference engineering and model serving separated in 2023, when generative workloads broke the assumptions of the model-serving systems built in 2017 [32,33,34,35]. LLMOps was coined within months of the first hosted model APIs, because the artefact being released stopped being a weight file. AI FinOps is the most recent and the most telling: 98% of surveyed practitioners now manage AI spend, against 31% two years earlier [36,37].

Each name appeared when its term became the binding constraint for enough organisations at once. That is also the best available prediction of which names will still exist in five years: the ones whose term stays expensive.

3How to read a plate

Each of the fourteen titles below gets a plate. The fields are the same on every plate, so they can be compared:

  • Moves — the term or terms the holder can change without asking.
  • Decides — the concrete decision the role closes on its own authority.
  • Runs on — the tooling, named rather than gestured at.
  • Loop — the process the role actually runs, and its period.
  • Fails as — the characteristic failure of the role, not of the person.
  • Evidence — an anchored measurement, from the literature or from this article's laboratory.

The gauge on each plate draws the whole cascade. Each of the seven bars stands for one term, and its height is that term's measured value, so the shape of the chain is the same on every plate. What changes is the shading: a solid bar is a term the role can move, a hatched bar is one it can measure but not change, and a thin dashed outline is one it inherits. Beneath the bars, a six-tick ruler marks the lead time of the role's shortest effective intervention, from seconds at the left to years at the right.

Reading the plates side by side, the useful question is not which role sounds most senior. It is which bars are solid, and how far left the tick sits.

4The fourteen

I

The site and the fleet

Everything downstream is priced in accelerator-seconds that somebody had to buy, power and cool. The four titles in this group act on the first two terms, and they act on the longest lead times in the whole system.

R01

AI Cloud Architect

evidence
Data-centre electricity demand grew 17% in 2025 and demand from AI-focused sites grew 50%; the IEA's central projection roughly doubles total data-centre consumption from 485 TWh in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030 [19,20].

The cloud architect is the oldest title on this list and the one least changed by the arrival of accelerators — which is exactly why it is worth starting here. The job has always been to convert a demand forecast into a purchasing and topology commitment, and to be wrong about it slowly enough that the organisation can adapt. What changed is the unit and the term. A general compute estate could be re-shaped in an afternoon; an accelerator estate is bought against a multi-year contract, sits in a building with a fixed power envelope, and cannot be resized when the model architecture changes underneath it. The role's centre of gravity moved from elasticity to commitment.

The tooling is unglamorous and mostly financial: quota requests, committed-use discounts, capacity blocks reserved months ahead, and a growing reliance on normalised billing exports so that three providers' invoices can be compared at all [38]. The engineering content is in the parts nobody bills for — whether the interconnect inside a purchased block is a fat tree or a torus, whether the storage tier can feed the accelerators at the rate they consume, whether the region has the power to take a second block next year. The architect's characteristic failure is not overspending. It is buying a block that is technically excellent and operationally unreachable: right silicon, wrong building, or right building with no path to the next one.

Where the cloud architect chooses what to buy, the infrastructure architect chooses what it is wired into. The lineage here is high-performance computing rather than web operations: the questions are the ones supercomputing centres have asked for thirty years [39], and the tools are still benchmarks that measure a collective, a checkpoint write and a straggler. What is new is that the design has to serve two workloads with opposite requirements on the same metal — synchronous training, which wants enormous bisection bandwidth and tolerates queueing, and interactive serving, which wants none of the first and none of the second.

Almost everything this role decides becomes an inherited constraint for someone else. The size of a failure domain sets the reliability engineer's error budget. The size of a fabric island sets the ceiling on what the scheduler can allocate. The checkpoint bandwidth sets the optimal checkpoint interval, and therefore how much of a training run is lost per failure [40]. This is the role with the widest blast radius and the fewest opportunities to be corrected: a fabric is not re-cabled because the ledger says it should be.

This title barely exists outside organisations large enough to have a single fungible pool of accelerators, and inside them it is one of the most consequential jobs in the building. The compute engineer owns the answer to "who is running on what, and why", which in an organisation whose dominant cost is accelerator-hours is a question about strategy wearing operational clothes. The work is part capacity planning, part market design: quotas, reservations, pre-emption tiers, fair-share half-lives, and the political economy of telling a research team that its exploratory sweep will be evicted at 09:00.

The tooling is thinner than the responsibility warrants. Most of it is home-grown — an allocation ledger, a burn-down against a committed number, a model that turns a research roadmap into a device-month forecast. What distinguishes the role from the platform engineer next door is the direction it faces: the platform engineer builds the mechanism by which capacity is handed out, the compute engineer decides how much of it there is and who is entitled to it. One owns the queue; the other owns the entitlements the queue enforces.

R04

GPU Infrastructure Engineer

c. 2021, from systems and HPC administration
moves
S2 HEALTHY, and S4 through fault detection and node repair
decides
whether a node is fit to run, what firmware and driver stack it carries, and when it is drained
runs on
DCGM and node-health checks, driver and firmware pipelines, the GPU operator and device plugins, NCCL pair tests, RDMA and link diagnostics, burn-in and validation suites
loop
a continuous health sweep, a rolling firmware campaign, and a repair queue measured in devices returned per day
fails as
declares a node healthy because it passes a synthetic test, while it is silently 30% slow and poisoning every collective it joins
evidence
In the Llama-3 405B snapshot, 419 of 466 interruptions in 54 days were unexpected, roughly 78% traced to hardware, with faulty accelerators and their memory the largest single category [25].

The most physical job on the list, and the one with the clearest ancestor: this is a systems administrator whose fleet happens to be accelerators. The distinguishing feature is that the failure modes are neither binary nor loud. A device that has died is easy. A device that has silently downclocked, or whose high-bandwidth memory is quietly correcting errors, or whose link has negotiated a lower width, will pass every liveness probe and slow an entire synchronous job by the amount it is slow [21].

The lab's fourth experiment is a direct statement of the role's difficulty. Two worlds — one where every rank jitters and one where a single device is sick — produce identical fleet-mean utilisation to six decimal places (0.8131 against 0.8131), identical wasted device-time, and require opposite responses. What separates them is not a mean but a per-rank order statistic: how often a given rank is the last to finish. Under distributed jitter, the busiest rank is critical on 0.0035 of steps; a single sick device is critical on all of them. The instrument that finds this is not a dashboard, it is a fleet-wide comparison of like devices against each other, run continuously, with a threshold nobody enjoys tuning. The scaling itself is a statement about extreme values rather than about hardware: the expected maximum of many draws grows with the logarithm of their number, which is why a jitter tax that is trivial at eight ranks is material at thirty thousand [41].

The tooling has consolidated quickly. Device telemetry, driver lifecycle and node labelling are now packaged rather than assembled by hand [42], and partitioning a device into smaller isolated instances is a supported operation rather than a research result [43] — though partitioning helps a fleet of small inference jobs and does nothing at all for a gang-scheduled training run.

II

The shared machine

Between a healthy fleet and a running job stands a queue. These two titles own the rules by which the machine is handed out, and the difference between them is which side of the interface they stand on.

R05

AI Platform Engineer

c. 2022, from platform engineering
moves
S3 ALLOCATED and S4 through the launch and restart path
decides
the scheduling policy — queues, priorities, gang semantics, pre-emption, backfill — and the interface engineers use to reach it
runs on
Kubernetes with dynamic resource allocation, Kueue or Volcano, Slurm, Ray, job templates and golden paths, admission and quota controllers, self-service portals
loop
a product loop: a paved road released on a version cadence, with adoption and time-to-first-job as the measures
fails as
builds an abstraction that hides exactly the thing its users need to control, and is routed around within a quarter
evidence
Dynamic resource allocation reached general availability in Kubernetes v1.34 and its gate is locked on in v1.35, replacing counted devices with attribute-matched claims [44,45,46].
solid = moves · hatched = measures · dashed = inherits

Platform engineering arrived as a named discipline around 2019, when the industry noticed that asking every team to operate its own infrastructure had produced neither autonomy nor reliability [16,17]. Its AI variant inherits the whole idea — an internal product, a paved road, a platform team whose customers are engineers — and adds one thing the original did not have to handle: the resource being scheduled is indivisible, expensive, and comes with a topology.

That single fact is why AI platform engineering is not a rebranding. A web platform can pack containers by CPU share; a training platform must place sixteen whole nodes inside one fabric island or not at all. The second experiment measures what that costs. Packing small jobs rather than spreading them is worth nothing at all in the tested mix (+0.01 points, a null result worth reporting); the island constraint costs 11.17 points; backfill returns 7.19 points. And the ceiling is flat as the gang grows to 128 devices and then falls away — the break is not at any particular job size, it is at the boundary of the failure domain the architect drew [47,48]. The platform engineer's real product is the set of defaults that keep users away from that cliff.

There is a decade of scheduler research to draw on and it is mostly not deployed: introspective migration and packing [49], least-attained-service policies for jobs whose duration is unknown [50], and heterogeneity-aware allocation across mixed device generations [51]. The reason so little of it reaches production is that most of it assumes cheap pre-emption, and a gang-scheduled training job with a terabyte of sharded state is the opposite of cheap to pre-empt. Where the newer work has landed is in the general-purpose distributed runtimes that sit above the scheduler rather than replacing it [52].

If the other thirteen titles are specialisations, this is the undifferentiated substance they were carved out of. An AI infrastructure engineer is expected to be able to take a model, a cluster and a deadline, and produce a running job — which means owning the fabric health, the launcher, the parallelism strategy, the checkpoint path, the profiler and whatever else stands between the three. In a small organisation it is the only infrastructure title that exists. In a large one it survives as the role that covers the seams between the others.

Its tooling is the deepest stack on this list, and it is the only role expected to be fluent across all of it: collective libraries [53], sharded optimisers and parallelism frameworks [54,55,56], tracing at kernel resolution, and container images that pin a working combination of driver, runtime and framework. The hazard is structural rather than technical. A title that moves four terms and owns the budget for none is a title whose holder is accountable for outcomes they can influence but not decide, which is a reliable way to burn people out — and it is the single strongest argument for the specialisation the rest of this list represents [57,58]. The right criterion for the split is the one Parnas gave for splitting a module: not the steps of the process, but the decisions each part is allowed to hide from the others [59].

The role's own history is a warning about how fast this ground moves. Horovod, which in 2018 was the standard answer to distributed data-parallel training and a genuine specialism to operate, was absorbed so completely by the collective libraries and framework-native implementations that followed it that the skill it represented no longer names a job [53,60].

III

Keeping the job alive

A job that has been given devices has not yet done any work. These two titles exist because the interval between allocation and progress is where synchronous training loses its time, and because someone has to carry the pager.

R07

AI Reliability Engineer

c. 2023, from site reliability engineering
moves
S4 RUNNING and S7 DELIVERED, through the error budget
decides
the service level objective, whether the budget is spent, and therefore whether shipping stops
runs on
SLO and error-budget tooling, incident management, alerting on symptoms rather than causes, postmortem practice, load-shedding and degradation policy
loop
a burn-rate loop measured in hours, a budget loop measured in weeks, a postmortem loop measured per incident
fails as
writes objectives for the things that are easy to measure — availability, latency — and none for the thing users actually notice, which is whether the answer was any good
evidence
The lab's serving experiment shows goodput peaking at 2.556 req/s and collapsing to 0.011 req/s while the replica is still below its saturated capacity of 6.49 req/s: throughput never fails, the objective does L5.
solid = moves · hatched = measures · dashed = inherits

Site reliability engineering gave the industry two durable ideas: that reliability is a quantity you choose rather than maximise, and that the way to choose it is an explicit objective with an explicit budget for violating it [5,6]. The AI variant keeps the machinery and inherits a harder measurement problem, because the thing being made available is not a page but a generation, and a system that returns a fluent wrong answer inside its latency budget has not failed by any metric the classical practice would recognise.

The role's real authority is negative: it is one of the few on this list that can stop something. That is why the load-shedding result in the fifth experiment belongs here rather than with the serving engineer. Capping admission does not rescue an overloaded replica — it converts a per-token violation into a queueing violation, and the best goodput a cap alone achieves is 0.677 req/s. Abandoning requests that have already waited past the objective lifts it to 3.063 req/s, a 4.52× improvement, by refusing 10.1% of arrivals outright. That is not an engineering decision. It is a policy decision about who gets told no, and it needs someone whose job is to own it.

R08

AI Operations Engineer

c. 2023; often a renamed operations or NOC role
moves
S2 and S4, by executing the response rather than designing it
decides
what happens in the next ten minutes: drain, restart, fail over, roll back, escalate
runs on
runbooks, on-call rotations and paging, ticketing, cluster and job dashboards, restart automation, change calendars
loop
the shortest loop on the list — minutes — and a weekly review that turns repeated incidents into automation
fails as

evidence
At 16,384 devices, moving detection and restart from an automated 4.94 minutes to a human's 30 minutes costs 13.5 points of effective training time — 2,213 device-days of the run per day L3.

This is the title most likely to be a rename, and the one most worth examining for that reason. Operations as a distinct function predates everything else here, and the last twenty years of practice have been a long argument about whether it should exist at all: DevOps said the people who build should run [3], SRE said operations work should be capped and converted into engineering [5]. The AI variant reappeared anyway, because accelerator fleets generate a volume of physical, unglamorous, time-critical work that neither of those arguments makes go away.

The lab puts a number on the role's value and, in the same breath, on its obsolescence. The gap between a one-minute automated recovery and a thirty-minute human one is 15.6 points of a training run at frontier scale. That gap is the entire economic case for the role — and it is also the reason the role should be pushing itself toward zero, because the response that a runbook describes precisely enough for a person to follow at 03:00 is a response a program can execute in a tenth of the time [6]. An operations function whose incident count grows linearly with the fleet has not been automated; it has been staffed.

The field has unusually candid primary sources on what this work felt like before it was automated. The training logbooks published alongside two early open large models record, in the researchers' own words, nights of manual restarts, loss spikes chased by hand, and hardware replaced mid-run [61,62]. They are the closest thing this occupation has to a founding document, and what they describe is precisely the gap the lab measures.

IV

Making the silicon work

The two terms with the least in common: one is nearly saturated and one is the largest loss in the chain. Both are owned by people who read assembly, and neither is visible on a cluster dashboard.

Of all the titles here this is the one whose skills are oldest and whose demand is newest. Kernel-level performance engineering is a discipline with a fifty-year lineage; what changed is that a single fused attention kernel became worth a measurable fraction of a company's compute bill [63]. Everything this role does is an attack on the gap between the arithmetic a device can issue and the arithmetic it is actually issuing — memory-bound decode, padding, recomputation, precision left on the table.

The ledger's sharpest finding sits on this row. When the seven stages are assembled from the lab's own measurements, the worst term by a wide margin is S6, at 0.4100 — and it is moved by one title in fourteen while the term with the least headroom left in it, S5 at 0.9800, has three. Across all seven stages the correlation between a stage's headroom and the number of titles that can move it is -0.6433. Attention is allocated in inverse proportion to where the loss is. The first experiment explains why this is not merely unfortunate: in a product, every term has an identical elasticity of exactly one, so the only thing that distinguishes a lever is how much room is left in it. Closing half the gap at S6 multiplies end-to-end delivery by 1.72×; closing half the gap at S5 multiplies it by 1.0102× — a factor of 71 between the two interventions, which is a fair description of how badly effort is misallocated in this industry.

The field does have a shared vocabulary for the term, even if it has no shared owner. Model FLOPs utilisation — the ratio of the arithmetic a model theoretically requires to the arithmetic the hardware could have issued in the same wall-clock time — was named and reported in the PaLM paper and has been the standard figure of merit since [64]. On the serving side the same accounting explains why speculative decoding works at all: a memory-bound decode step has arithmetic capacity going spare, so verifying several draft tokens costs almost nothing more than producing one [65].

The sixth experiment shows why that is harder than it sounds. A single device in a decode loop is honestly 98.00% kernel-resident and honestly 40.18% SM-active: a wedge of 57.82 points between two correct measurements of the same silicon, and the reason a fleet dashboard can read 99% while more than half the machine does nothing. Model serving as a named job is old — TensorFlow Serving and Clipper are both from 2017 [32,33] — but the generative workload broke every assumption those systems were built on. A request is no longer one forward pass of predictable cost; it is a prefill whose cost scales with the prompt and a decode whose cost scales with the output, run against a cache that grows with both. Continuous batching [34] and paged key-value memory [35] were the two ideas that made the workload tractable, and the current generation of stacks pushes further by separating the two phases onto different devices entirely [73,74,75,76].

What makes this a distinct engineering role rather than a configuration exercise is the shape of the objective. Time to first token and time per output token pull in opposite directions across the batch size, so there is an interior optimum and it is not where the throughput number is largest. In the lab the capacity-optimal batch is 512 and the goodput-optimal batch is 40; chunking the prefill so that it cannot block a decode step moves the fraction of requests inside the objective from 0.9740 to 0.9872 while costing time to first token. Every one of those is a trade the serving engineer makes and the reliability engineer signs.

The stack itself is consolidating around a two-layer shape: an engine that owns a device's batching and cache, and an orchestration layer above it that owns routing, cache-aware placement and the split between prefill and decode workers. The Kubernetes-native expression of that pattern and the vendor one have converged closely enough to share components [76,77].

FinOps arrived as a named practice for cloud spend, with a foundation, a certification ladder and a framework built around a simple observation: in a consumption-priced system, engineering decisions are purchasing decisions, and the two functions have to share a vocabulary [80,81]. 5The distinctions that survive

Seven questions separate all fourteen titles. Four of the seven also do — there are 8 such four-question sets — and no three questions suffice L9. That is the formal answer to whether these titles are real: they are distinguishable, but the margin is thin, and most of the pairs that people find confusing differ in only two of seven terms. Here are the six confusions worth resolving explicitly.

Platform engineer against infrastructure engineer

The platform engineer builds a product for other engineers; the infrastructure engineer builds the thing the product runs on. Put operationally: if the answer to "how do I run this job" is a documented interface with defaults, someone is doing platform engineering. If the answer is "come and talk to me", someone is doing infrastructure engineering. The coding shows the platform role moving two terms and observing three, and the infrastructure role moving four — the widest span on the list. The infrastructure engineer is not more senior. They are less specialised, which in a large organisation is a different thing.

Infrastructure engineer against compute engineer

Both operate on the fleet, and they face opposite directions. The compute engineer decides how much capacity exists and who is entitled to it; the infrastructure engineer makes a specific job run on whatever capacity was granted. One owns entitlements, the other owns execution. The tell is what happens when there is not enough: the compute engineer's job is to decide whose work stops, and the infrastructure engineer's job is to make the remaining work fit.

Reliability against observability against operations

These three are routinely merged and should not be. The observability engineer moves nothing — the coding gives that row seven observations and zero moves, which is the job rather than a deficiency. The reliability engineer sets the objective and owns the budget, which is an authority to stop. The operations engineer executes the response, on the shortest loop of any title here. In organisations that merge them, the predictable outcome is that measurement work is deprioritised whenever there is an incident, because the same person is carrying the pager — and measurement is the input to everything else.

Inference engineer against model serving engineer

The cleanest split on the list, and the most often conflated. The inference engineer works inside one device: kernels, fusion, precision, attention implementation. The model serving engineer works across devices and requests: batching, cache placement, routing, replica shape, overload behaviour. Both move BUSY; only the inference engineer moves USEFUL, and only the serving engineer moves DELIVERED. A useful diagnostic question at interview is what the candidate would do about a p99 latency regression. The inference engineer profiles a kernel; the serving engineer looks at the queue. Both answers are right, for different jobs.

MLOps against LLMOps

Two terms of seven separate them, which is the narrowest real gap here. The substantive difference is the artefact and its cadence. MLOps promotes a retrained model, a heavyweight artefact on a weekly or monthly cycle, gated by a statistical comparison. LLMOps promotes a prompt, a context policy, a tool schema or a routing rule — lightweight artefacts that can reach production in minutes, often changed by people who are not engineers, and typically covered by no release machinery at all. In most organisations these are one person. The argument for splitting them is not scale but blast radius: a prompt change is a production change with no build, no artefact and frequently no record.

Architect against engineer

The two architect titles are distinguished from everything else by lead time rather than by scope. Both sit at the months-to-years rung, and both make decisions that become inherited constraints for people who will never meet them. The cloud architect commits money and location; the infrastructure architect commits topology and failure domains. The lab supplies the clearest illustration of what that means in practice: the fabric-island constraint, which is a one-time architectural choice, costs 11.17 points of allocation forever after, and the allocation ceiling is flat as gang size grows to 128 devices and then falls to 0.7957 at 512 — the break sitting exactly at the island boundary the architect drew, not at any property of the jobs L2.

The distinction that actually matters

Cutting across all six: 4 of the fourteen titles can stop something on their own authority — evict a running job, block a promotion, drain a node, refuse a release — and 10 cannot. The 4 are the AI Compute Engineer, AI Reliability Engineer, AI Operations Engineer, MLOps Engineer; that coding is the author's, and it is the line in this article most open to dispute, which is exactly why it is worth arguing about in a specific organisation rather than in general. That line does not follow seniority, tooling, or how technical the role is. It follows whether the organisation gave the role an actuator or only an opinion. Titles on the wrong side of it are the ones that burn people out, and they are identifiable in an advertisement: they list the responsibilities of a term-mover and the authority of an observer.

6Background, and what passes for a credential

What the work actually requires

There is no accredited path into any of these roles, and the honest description of the common background is a systems education plus exposure to a machine large enough to have taught the person that averages lie. Concretely, four things recur across every plate above.

The first is operating-system and hardware fluency: what a page fault costs, what a PCIe topology looks like, why a device that has negotiated a lower link width is worse than one that has failed. The second is distributed-systems literacy of the ordinary kind — partial failure, queueing, tail latency, the fact that the mean of a distribution is rarely the number that matters [21,85,86]. The third is enough numeracy to treat a measurement as an estimate with an error bar, which is the skill that separates a promotion gate from a ritual [78]. The fourth is the ability to read a profiler and believe it over one's own model of the code [87].

Notably absent from that list is machine learning. Of the fourteen roles, three require a working understanding of model architecture — inference engineer, model serving engineer, and to a lesser extent LLMOps engineer — and the rest require knowing what a training step does and nothing more. This is the single most common misconception in hiring for these positions: they are systems roles that happen to be pointed at a model, not modelling roles that happen to touch systems.

The specific technical ground

By group, the material that is genuinely load-bearing:

  • Site and fleet: power and cooling envelopes [88,89], rack and fabric topology, storage tiering, procurement and commitment structures, driver and firmware lifecycle, device telemetry [90], and failure statistics at fleet scale [91,92].
  • The shared machine: batch scheduling theory — gang scheduling, backfilling, fair share, pre-emption [47,48,93] — plus Kubernetes internals to the level of the scheduler and the device layer [44,94,95], and the cluster-management lineage that produced them [96,97].
  • Keeping the job alive: checkpoint and restart economics [40,98], collective communication and its failure modes [53], parallelism strategies [54,55,56,99], and incident practice [5,100].
  • The silicon: the memory hierarchy and roofline reasoning, kernel programming, attention implementations [63], quantisation, and enough compiler knowledge to know when the graph is the problem [101,102].
  • What is delivered: queueing theory to the level of Little's law and the M/G/1 intuition [103,104], serving-system internals [34,35,73,74,105], evaluation design and statistical power, telemetry standards [66,68,106], and cost allocation [38,81,107].

Credentials, such as they are

The credential landscape is thin, young, and almost entirely vendor-supplied, which is what one expects of an occupation that has not yet been recognised by any classification system.

NVIDIA operates the only ladder aimed squarely at this work. The associate certification in AI infrastructure and operations is an entry-level examination covering GPU fundamentals, the software stack, and monitoring and sharing [108]. Above it, the professional certification in AI operations is a 120-minute, 70-to-75-question examination priced at 400 US dollars, valid for two years, with a stated prerequisite of two to three years of data-centre experience; its published syllabus is cluster management software, Slurm administration, Kubernetes administration and system troubleshooting tools, and its stated audience is MLOps engineers, DevOps engineers, solution architects, system architects and AI infrastructure engineers [109]. That list of audiences is itself evidence for this article's thesis: a single examination addressed to five titles is an examination that has not decided which term it is testing.

For the platform group, the Kubernetes administration certifications remain the baseline [110], and the CNCF's Kubernetes AI Conformance programme — expanded in March 2026 to cover disaggregated inference, LLM traffic routing and allocation-aware networking — is becoming the closest thing to a portable standard for what an AI-capable cluster must do [111]. For the cost group, the FinOps Foundation launched a dedicated AI certification with its examination live from March 2026 [82], alongside its billing specification [38] and framework [81]. For governance- adjacent work there are management-system and risk frameworks [112,113], though none of them is a technical credential.

One thing that does function as a shared measurement, if not as a credential, is the benchmark suite. MLPerf established a common definition of a training run and an inference deployment, with rules about what may be tuned and what must be reported [114,115], and its 2026 rounds are a reasonable proxy for what the industry currently considers a representative workload: the training suite added mixture-of-experts benchmarks [116], and the inference suite added an interactive, latency-constrained scenario — the first standard treatment of the two-sided objective this article's fifth experiment measures [117]. Being able to read a submission critically, and to say why a headline number does not transfer to one's own request mix, is a more useful qualification than most certificates.

Two further bodies of standards sit adjacent rather than central. Secure deployment practice for the surrounding infrastructure is well documented [118], and the process standards for addressing ethical concerns during system design [119] matter to the governance roles that neighbour this list without appearing on it.

The absence that matters most

The US Standard Occupational Classification, the instrument by which occupations are counted, has no code for any of these fourteen titles. The 2018 structure predates all of them [120], and the revision now in progress will not take effect until reference year 2028, with its final structure due in 2027 [121]. Until then there are no official employment counts, no wage series, and no way to distinguish any of these roles from the general category they are currently swept into. What exists instead is job-posting analysis and survey work [122,123], which measures what employers advertise rather than what people do — a distinction this article's central argument depends on.

There is a precedent worth studying before anyone accepts one of these titles. The chief data officer role was created in 2002, spread rapidly, and then spent two decades with a median tenure well under half that of the executives around it — largely because the role was defined as a portfolio of several distinct jobs that no single person had the authority to do [124]. The titles on this list that move four terms and control the budget for none are structurally the same bet.

The practical consequence for anyone entering: the credential will not get the job, and the classification will not exist to defend the title. What transfers is a demonstrated measurement. Someone who can say which term they moved, by how much, and how they knew, is legible across all fourteen of these roles. Someone who can only list the tools they have used is legible in none of them, because every one of these roles runs on approximately the same tools.

L2's first version varied the gang size and the offered load together, so it confounded the cost of indivisibility with the cost of having more work; it was rebuilt to hold offered load constant by scaling the gang class's arrival share inversely with its size. L3's first version asserted that its model reproduced Meta's published effective training time when it did not — it gave 0.863 against a published figure above 0.90 — and was rebuilt to solve the model for the detection-and-restart time that reproduces the published number instead, which is the more useful question and yields 4.94 minutes. L5's first version ran the server above its capacity, so nothing met the objective and the whole sweep read zero; rebuilt at a stable load, it produced the goodput-collapse result that is now the experiment's headline, and a second rebuild added abandonment after a cap alone was shown not to help. L7's first version had a scheduling bug that meant new replicas never became ready, and once fixed had no dynamics worth measuring, because a smooth diurnal curve never stresses a cold start; it was rebuilt around a compressed ramp plus two unannounced spikes.

One claim was withdrawn. An earlier draft of L2 reported that packing small jobs rather than spreading them recovers a meaningful share of the allocation ceiling. Four results survive the assembly, and none of them is about vocabulary.

The titles are real, and thin. All fourteen are distinguishable, but 4 of the seven questions are needed and no fewer, 8 different four-question sets do the job, and the closest pairs differ in two terms of seven. An organisation with fewer than a few hundred engineers has no business staffing fourteen of these. It has business knowing which four questions it is answering when it writes one advertisement.The effort is allocated backwards. The correlation between a term's remaining headroom and the number of titles that can move it is -0.6433. The largest loss in the chain sits at USEFUL, 59.0 points, and one title in fourteen can move it. The smallest, 2.0 points at BUSY, has three. Seven titles can move RUNNING, worth 10.0 points. A production line is governed by its constraint rather than by its average, and improving a station that is not the constraint improves nothing at all [125,126]. Since every term in a product has an elasticity of exactly one, that distribution has no defence in arithmetic: it is a distribution of attention, not of leverage. Closing half the remaining gap at USEFUL would multiply end-to-end delivery by 1.72×; the same operation at BUSY multiplies it by 1.0102×.Measurement and authority have come apart. One title observes all seven terms and moves none. 10 of the fourteen cannot stop anything. The two instruments that would let anyone check the chain end to end are still unfinished: the OpenTelemetry generative-AI conventions have no stable attribute and no tagged release [66,67], and the occupational classification that would let anyone count these jobs does not acknowledge them [120,121]. The industry has more names for this work than it has ways of measuring it.And the number at the end is not the scandal it looks like. A chain of seven terms each at 90% delivers 47.83%; each at 95% delivers 69.83%. To reach 50% end to end, every one of the seven has to average 0.9057. The assembled cascade in this article gives 20.78% — 12.5 minutes of wanted work per purchased accelerator-hour — and there is no single failure anywhere in it. That is the strongest argument for the specialisation these fourteen titles represent, and the strongest argument against expecting any one of them to fix the number alone.

The question to ask of any of these advertisements, then, is not what the role is called or what it will use. It is: which term does this role own, does it hold the actuator or only the instrument, and how much room is left in the term by the time it gets there.
