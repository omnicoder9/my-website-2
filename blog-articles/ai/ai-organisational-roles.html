Schedule 1, clause 1

A title is a claim about authority

Between 2023 and 2026 a dozen job titles appeared that had barely existed before, all of them built from the same two letters and a business noun. Chief AI Officer. Head of AI. AI Transformation Lead. AI Transformation Architect. AI Centre of Excellence Lead. AI Program Manager. AI Enablement Lead. AI Adoption Consultant. AI Workforce Strategist. AI Operations Manager. AI Governance Manager. AI Risk Manager. Read their job advertisements side by side and you will find the same twenty verbs — drive, embed, partner, scale, upskill, govern, align — distributed almost at random. Read their responsibilities and you will find heavy overlap. Read their required qualifications and you will find, as this article measures below, that they do not separate at all.

The obvious conclusion is that most of these are the same job under different names, and that conclusion is partly right. But it is not the interesting part, and taking it as the answer stops the enquiry one question too early. The question worth asking is why an organisation invents a title in the first place. It is not to describe work; work gets described in a job description, which is a different document. A title is a public claim about where a decision now sits. It tells the rest of the organisation whom to go to, whose agreement is needed, and — the part usually left unstated — who will be asked to account for it afterwards. When a firm creates a Chief AI Officer it is not announcing that someone will think about artificial intelligence. It is announcing that a class of decision has been moved.

So this article reads the twelve titles as claims about authority, and tests each claim against two things that can actually be checked. The first is what the role can decide without asking anyone: not what it influences, not what it is consulted on, but what it can close alone. The second is what holds the role in place when the organisation chart is redrawn — whether anything outside the firm's own preferences requires the role to exist. Those two properties turn out to be nearly independent of each other, which is itself a finding, and the gap between them explains most of what looks like confusion in this labour market.

The organising question is deliberately narrow, and it is the crudest possible test of authority: what can this role stop? Not what it can start, which is easy to claim and hard to verify, but what it can bring to a halt when it is already running and something has gone wrong. Stopping is the decision that cannot be faked. It costs money, it embarrasses people, it requires someone to be willing to be wrong in public, and it is the one moment when the difference between an advisory role and a real one becomes visible in a single meeting. The answer, for the reference organisation modelled here, is that ten of the twelve titles containing the letters AI cannot stop an AI system.

That is not an argument that the other ten are useless. It is an argument that they are something other than what their titles imply, and that the difference matters when you are deciding which one to create, which one to apply for, or which one to believe when it tells you a system is safe. The remainder of this article works through the history that produced these roles, the responsibilities and tooling each actually carries, the processes they share, the backgrounds and credentials the market asks for, and finally a set of measurements over a coded model of all twelve. The measurements are not decorative: three of the article's central claims are ones I did not expect and would not have made without running them.

Schedule 1, clause 2

Where these roles came from

New coordinating roles are not a novelty of the AI era and they do not appear because a technology is impressive. They appear when a technology changes the pattern of interdependence inside a firm faster than the existing structure can absorb. Chandler's finding that administrative structure follows from the strategy a firm adopts is the background condition [1], and Pfeffer and Salancik's resource-dependence account supplies the trigger: organisations restructure in response to the external parties they depend on, which is why a regulation reliably produces an office [2]. Lawrence and Lorsch established the general shape of this in 1967: as environments differentiate, organisations differentiate to match, and differentiation forces the creation of integrating roles whose entire job is to hold the pieces together [3]. Galbraith turned it into a design rule — as uncertainty rises, an organisation must either reduce the information it needs to process or increase its capacity to process it, and lateral roles are one of the standard ways of buying that capacity [4]. Thompson had already given the vocabulary for why some interdependencies are cheap to coordinate and others expensive [5], and Mintzberg named the resulting layer: a technostructure of people who do not do the work and do not manage those who do, but standardise it [6].

What that literature does not settle is which way the causation runs, and the answer turns out to be both ways at once. Barley's study of two hospitals adopting identical CT scanners is the decisive case: the same machine produced different departmental structures depending on the existing relations between radiologists and technologists, so the technology was an occasion for restructuring rather than a cause of it [7]. Orlikowski generalised this into the duality of technology — technology is both produced by human action and a constraint on it [8] — and Leonardi's later work traced the same imbrication through flexible routines meeting flexible software [9]. Zuboff's distinction between automating and informating supplies the mechanism most relevant here: a technology that also generates a record of the work reorganises who can see what, and therefore who can decide [10]. Every register, log and inventory in section 9 is an informating artefact, and the fight over who owns them is a fight over decision rights.

The lag, and why it is always long

The most useful historical fact about technology-driven reorganisation is that it is slow, and that the slowness has a known cause. Factories electrified decades before they were rebuilt around electricity. Devine's account of the shift from shafts to wires shows plants replacing the steam engine with an electric motor while keeping the line shafts, the belts and the multi-storey layout that the steam engine had required — capturing almost none of the available gain until the whole plant was relaid around unit drive [11]. David used exactly this to explain the computer productivity paradox that Solow had put so memorably [12]: general-purpose technologies pay only after the complementary reorganisation, and the reorganisation takes a generation [13].

That is not merely a historical analogy. It was measured directly for information technology. Brynjolfsson and Hitt showed that computer investment paid off only in firms that also changed work organisation, decision rights and skills [14]; Bresnahan, Brynjolfsson and Hitt found the three to be complements, not substitutes [15]; Milgrom and Roberts had given the theory of why bundles of complementary practices must move together or not at all [16]. The productivity J-curve puts a shape on it: intangible complementary investment is expensed as it is made and shows up as output only later, so a genuinely transformative technology first makes measured productivity look worse [17]. Every role in this article is, economically speaking, a line item in that intangible investment.

Four predecessor roles, and what each one teaches

The AI titles have four direct ancestors, and each one contributes a lesson that is being relearned rather than learned.

These are not the only precedents — the business process reengineering wave of the 1990s produced its own coordinating offices and its own literature of failure [18,19] — but they are the four whose lessons transfer most directly.

The chief information officer was proposed as a concept in 1981, in a book by two consultants who argued that information should be managed as a resource with an executive owner [20]. It took roughly fifteen years for the role to acquire a stable definition, and the definition it acquired was not the one proposed; by the early 1990s researchers were describing a role whose content had already turned over completely [21], and a twenty-five year review found continuous redefinition rather than convergence [22]. Lesson: a title invented by consultants stabilises, if at all, around whatever budget it captures, not around its founding description.

The chief information security officer appeared in 1995 as a response to a specific incident, and remained a discretionary role for two decades. What changed it was regulation. New York's Part 500 does not suggest that a covered entity should have a security executive; it requires the designation of a chief information security officer, with defined reporting duties [23]. The SEC then required registrants to describe their cybersecurity governance in an annual filing [24]. Lesson: the fastest route from a title to a durable role runs through an instrument that names it.

The chief data officer was first appointed at Capital One in 2002 and spent two decades failing to stabilise. Davenport and Bean, studying the role across a decade, identified seven distinct jobs being asked of one person, each demanding enough that no individual could do them all well [25]; the observed consequence was a mean tenure of roughly two and a half years against about seven for a chief executive [26,27]. Lee and colleagues had proposed a three-dimensional framework precisely because the role could not be described in one dimension [28]. Lesson: a role defined as the union of everything a new asset class touches is not a role.

The data protection officer is the counterexample that makes the pattern legible. Articles 37 to 39 of the General Data Protection Regulation require designation, specify the tasks, forbid instructions on how to perform them, and forbid dismissal or penalty for performing them [29]; the Article 29 Working Party guidance sets out what independence means in practice [30]. That is not a title. It is a protected office with a statutory job description, and it did not drift. Lesson: the thing that stops a role from being reorganised away is not seniority, budget or a good business case. It is an external requirement with a name in it.

The AI wave

The immediate ancestry of the twelve runs through the AI ethics teams of 2016 to 2021, which are unusually well documented because researchers studied them while they existed. Metcalf, Moss and boyd's interviews with the people they called ethics owners found a recurring structure: a role created in response to external pressure, given responsibility for outcomes it could not control, and forced to translate ethical commitments into whatever the firm's existing metrics could carry [31]. Rakova and colleagues asked practitioners what would actually change organisational practice and got answers about incentives, ownership and escalation paths rather than about principles [32]. Meanwhile the principles themselves proliferated: Jobin, Ienca and Vayena counted eighty-four guideline documents converging on a handful of themes [33], and Mittelstadt argued that principles alone could not do the work because AI development lacked the professional structures — fiduciary duties, licensure, a body that can strike you off — that make principles bite in medicine [34,35].

Then the instruments arrived, and they arrived in a specific order that shaped which roles hardened. NIST published the AI Risk Management Framework in January 2023 with GOVERN as its first and cross-cutting function [36], followed by a generative-AI profile [37]. ISO published a certifiable management-system standard, ISO/IEC 42001, in December 2023, whose clause 5.3 requires top management to assign and communicate roles, responsibilities and authorities [38], and in July 2025 published ISO/IEC 42006, which tells accredited bodies how to audit it and what competence their auditors need [39]. The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 [40]. And in the United States, an executive order [41] and then OMB Memorandum M-24-10 [42] required every covered federal agency to designate a Chief AI Officer — an order retained, with a reset clock and a changed emphasis, when M-24-10 was rescinded and replaced by M-25-21 under a new administration [43,44,45].

That last sequence is worth pausing on, because it is the only case in this entire field of a role being created by fiat and then surviving a change of government that reversed the surrounding policy. The emphasis moved from managing risk to accelerating adoption; the requirement to designate a named officer did not move. Agencies published compliance plans naming the post and, in several cases, giving it explicit approval authority over high-impact uses [46,47]. US state legislation moved on a separate track and mostly named duties rather than officers: California's frontier-model transparency statute took effect at the start of 2026 [48], and Colorado repealed and replaced its own 2024 act before it ever applied [49]. Whatever one thinks of the policy, the institutional lesson is unambiguous: of the twelve titles in this article, exactly one has ever been required by name, and it was required by an administrative instrument rather than by any market discovery about what the job should be.

Why did all twelve appear everywhere at once, in nearly the same form? The mechanism is not mysterious and it is not primarily technological. DiMaggio and Powell's account of institutional isomorphism — coercive from regulation, mimetic under uncertainty, normative through professions and consultancies — describes it exactly [50]. Meyer and Rowan add the part that matters most here: formal structures adopted for legitimacy tend to be decoupled from the actual work, so a firm can carry the structure without the structure carrying anything [51]. Edelman's study of how organisations respond to ambiguous law is the sharpest version: faced with a rule whose requirements are unclear, firms build visible symbolic structures — offices, officers, policies — and those structures are later treated by courts and regulators as evidence of compliance, whether or not they changed any outcome [52]. If you want a single sentence explaining why twelve titles arrived in three years, it is that: the law became legible enough to demand a response and not specific enough to determine one.

Schedule 1, clause 3

Two axes, and how each role is read

Each of the twelve roles is set out below on the same six fields, in the same order, so that they can be compared rather than merely described: what it decides, what it answers for, what it runs on, what it is anchored by, what it fails as, and the evidence. Two of those fields are also scored, and the scores are what the closing ledger is built from.

The delegation strip

Every AI programme has to make four decisions, whatever it calls them. Someone commits the money (FUND). Someone settles build against buy, the platform and the model (BUILD). Someone releases the system to real users making real decisions (DEPLOY). And someone, eventually, has to be able to take it down (STOP). Against each of the four, a role holds one of four authorities: none, recommend, veto — able to block but not to start — or decide, meaning able to close the question alone. Twelve roles by four decisions gives forty-eight cells, and the article's central measurements are audits over that grid.

The distinction between veto and decide is the one that does the work, and it is borrowed from a literature that has thought about it carefully. Aghion and Tirole's separation of formal from real authority is the cleanest statement: the right to decide and the ability to determine what is decided are different things, and an agent with the formal right routinely rubber-stamps because the informed party controls the option set [53]. Jensen and Meckling put the complementary constraint — decision rights have to be co-located with the specific knowledge needed to exercise them, and moving one without the other destroys value either way [54], which is Hayek's problem restated inside the firm [55].

The anchor ladder

The second axis asks what would have to change in the world for the role to disappear. Six rungs, from N0 to N5, each defined by the kind of thing that requires the role to exist. At the top, N5, an instrument requires that a named individual hold the duty: the data protection officer under the GDPR, the chief information security officer under Part 500, the Chief AI Officer under M-25-21. At N4 an instrument names the duty but leaves the organisation to decide who discharges it — which is where most AI Act obligations sit, and where six of the twelve roles sit with them. N3 is a management-system standard or supervisory guidance requiring the function. N2 is a load-bearing internal artefact: a register, a reference architecture, a control set that other people's work depends on. N1 is a budget line and nothing else. N0 is a job posting.

The ladder is not a quality ranking. A role at N1 can be excellent and a role at N5 can be a sinecure. It is a durability estimate, and it is the closest available proxy for the question a candidate should actually ask at interview, which is what happens to this job at the next reorganisation. The predecessor roles supply the calibration: the chief data officer never rose above N1 in most firms and churned at two-and-a-half-year intervals [26]; the data protection officer went straight to N5 and stopped moving [29]; the chief information security officer climbed from N0 in 1995 to N5 in the regulated sectors and its definition stabilised as it climbed [23].

What the reference organisation is, and why it has to be stated

Authority is not a property of a title; it is a property of a title inside a particular organisation. So the coding below is made against one stated reference: a large enterprise that is a deployer rather than a provider of high-risk AI systems, operating an ISO/IEC 42001 management system [38], organised on the Institute of Internal Auditors' three lines — management owning and managing risk in the first line, risk and compliance functions providing expertise and challenge in the second, internal audit providing independent assurance in the third [56]. Move to a model-developing laboratory, a bank under model-risk supervision [57], or a federal agency under M-25-21 [44], and several cells change. Where they change in an interesting direction, the plates say so.

One more caution, stated once and meant throughout. The duty inventory, the decision matrix, the anchor rungs and the qualification matrix are codings: my reading of published obligations, standards and position descriptions into fixed vocabularies. They are printed in full in the appendix precisely so that a reader who disagrees can change a cell and rerun the argument. What the experiments in section 12 measure is the structure of the coding — gaps, overlaps, asymmetries, separability — not the world directly. The five experiments that involve no coding at all (L4 through L7, and L9) are pure models and are labelled as such.

The distinction is about which way they face. The Chief AI Officer faces outward and upward: to the board, to a regulator, to the public. The Head of AI faces inward and downward: to a platform, a roadmap, a team. Read the two anchor fields and the difference is stark. M-25-21 requires a designated Chief AI Officer in every covered US agency, at Senior Executive Service level in cabinet departments, and requires an agency AI governance board to be convened [44]; the Federal Reserve Board's own compliance plan states that high-impact uses need explicit approval from that officer [46]. Nothing anywhere requires a Head of AI. The Head of AI exists because engineering work needs a manager, which is a completely sufficient reason and a completely undurable one.

Outside the US public sector the Chief AI Officer's anchor evaporates and the role reverts to whatever the firm's own governance grants it. This is the point at which the chief data officer's history becomes uncomfortable rather than merely instructive. The seven-jobs-in-one problem that Davenport and Bean diagnosed [25] is visible in the Chief AI Officer's plate: strategy, portfolio, budget, board convening, high-impact approval, termination, escalation, and external reporting is eight duties spanning four organisational functions. The CDO's answer to that load was a mean tenure of about two and a half years [26]. There is no structural reason for the Chief AI Officer's to be longer, and one structural reason for it to be shorter: the CDO at least owned an asset. The Chief AI Officer owns a portfolio of other people's systems.

There is a survey result that bears directly on this and is easy to misread. McKinsey finds that chief-executive oversight of AI governance is, of twenty-five attributes tested, among those most strongly associated with self-reported bottom-line impact, and simultaneously that only 28 per cent of organisations report the chief executive holding that responsibility, with 17 per cent reporting the board [62]. The tempting reading is that chief executives should personally run AI governance. The structural reading is more modest and more useful: what correlates with impact is that the decision sits somewhere that can actually close it. A Chief AI Officer with the chief executive's delegated authority is the same arrangement; a Chief AI Officer without it is not.

The Stanford AI Index reports that AI-specific governance roles grew 17 per cent in 2025 and that ownership shifted away from data and analytics functions toward dedicated governance roles, while the share of organisations with no responsible-AI policy at all fell from 24 per cent to 11 per cent [63]. Both movements are real. McKinsey's 2026 trust-maturity survey, taken across roughly five hundred organisations whose respondents hold direct responsibility for AI governance or investment, reports the same direction of travel and the same gaps in strategy and risk management [64]. Neither tells you whether any of those roles can stop anything, which is exactly the measurement that no survey in this field currently makes.

There is one further structural reason to expect these two roles to keep pulling apart rather than merging, and it is the oldest argument in the strategy literature about running two clock speeds at once. Tushman and O'Reilly's ambidextrous organisation exists because exploration and exploitation need different structures, metrics and tolerances for failure, and putting both under one manager collapses toward whichever has the nearer deadline [65]. A Head of AI shipping against a roadmap and a Chief AI Officer holding a five-year portfolio are that split made into two job titles. Where firms merge them, the roadmap wins, because the roadmap has dates.

Schedule 2, clause 5

Coordination: three roles with no lever

The second group is the largest and the least differentiated. All three roles exist for the reason Lawrence and Lorsch identified sixty years ago — the work crosses functions and somebody has to hold it together [3] — and all three are, in the coding, advisory across every one of the four decisions. Between them they own twenty-seven duties. Between them they hold ten points of authority out of a possible thirty-six.

Why the centre of excellence saturates

The Centre of Excellence Lead deserves separate treatment because its failure mode is arithmetic rather than political, and the arithmetic is old. Brooks's observation that communication paths grow as the square of the group is the starting point [70]; Galbraith's lateral-relations argument is the response, that a liaison role converts a mesh into a hub [4]. A hub spanning n functions replaces n(n−1)/2 direct channels with n. At twenty functions that is twenty channels instead of a hundred and ninety, an 89.5 per cent reduction, and on channel arithmetic alone the hub is better without limit.

The limit is the hub itself, and it is invisible on an organisation chart. Model the centre as a single server: each function raises requests at some rate, the centre serves at some rate, and the wait is the standard single-server result [72,73]. In the lab's parameterisation the centre holds up to ten functions at utilisation 0.80; it saturates completely at fourteen; and going from ten functions to twelve multiplies the mean wait by 2.50 while the organisation chart shows only two more dotted lines. This is Kingman's heavy-traffic behaviour showing up in a governance process [74]: nothing degrades gradually near saturation, and the last two units of demand cost more than the first eight.

The standard fix is federation — pods with their own coordinators under a coordinator of coordinators — and the lab confirms it works, holding both tiers under 0.80 utilisation at twenty-four functions with three pods. But note what federation costs, because it is the reason firms resist it: it converts one role that everybody can name into four roles that nobody outside the pod can name. The visible-legitimacy benefit that motivated the centre in the first place [51] is exactly what federation gives up.

It is worth noting what the whole group is for, in the oldest terms available. Coase's answer to why firms exist at all is that internal coordination is sometimes cheaper than transacting in a market [75]. A coordination role is a bet that this remains true for a particular class of work — that holding AI capability inside one internal hub beats letting each function buy its own. The bet is defensible while the capability is scarce and the platform decisions are expensive. It stops being defensible at exactly the point where a business unit can buy a competent system with a corporate card, which is roughly where most organisations now are.

The AI Program Manager is the clearest instance of the pattern this article is about. It owns six duties, a middling load, and scores second-lowest on authority of all twelve. The project-management literature is candid about this: the multi-phase PMO research programme found offices being reconfigured on roughly two-year cycles, with the reconfiguration usually a response to dissatisfaction rather than to failure [67]. An office whose value is coordination is very hard to defend in a budget review, because coordination is only visible when it is absent.

The architect's problem is that architecture is advice

The AI Transformation Architect has the lowest self-description score of the twelve in the lab's keyword test — the title's own words predict its duties with an F1 of 0.222, worst in the set — and that is diagnostic rather than trivial. The word "transformation" points at change management; the word "architect" points at buildings; the job is data contracts, integration boundaries, evaluation harness design and the question of what the model is allowed to be responsible for. Conway's observation still governs the outcome: the system will mirror the communication structure of the organisation that builds it, whatever the architecture document says [77].

What makes AI architecture different from ordinary architecture is a specific class of failure that the literature has documented well. Sculley and colleagues named the underlying property: machine learning systems erode abstraction boundaries, because a model's behaviour depends on everything upstream of it, so changing anything changes everything [58]. Amershi and colleagues found that the resulting engineering practice differs from conventional software in exactly the places where architecture usually holds — model management, data dependencies, and the customisation of components that cannot be treated as modules [59]. Simon's argument that complex systems are manageable because they are nearly decomposable into subsystems whose internal interactions dominate their external ones is the design assumption being violated [81]: a machine learning system is decomposable in the code and not in the behaviour. Widder and Nafus show the accountability consequence: modularity in the AI supply chain lets each participant sincerely believe that responsibility lies elsewhere [78]. The architect's plate says the role fails as a diagram, and that is why: architecture that no build gate enforces is discovered to have been ignored at the first incident.

The operations manager, and the only workable model of a stop

The AI Operations Manager is one of only two roles in the twelve that can halt a running system alone, and the reason it can is worth stating precisely, because it is the only mechanism in this entire article that reliably converts a measurement into an authority. Site reliability engineering solved this problem twenty years ago with the error budget: an agreed reliability target implies an agreed allowance for failure, the allowance is measured continuously, and when it is exhausted a pre-agreed policy halts releases automatically [79]. Nobody argues in the moment, because the argument happened when the target was set. The authority is not personal; it is a consequence of a number that both parties agreed to in advance.

No equivalent has been established for AI systems, and the operational research explains why it is hard. Shankar and colleagues, interviewing engineers who run machine learning in production, found that behaviour in production is genuinely not knowable in advance, and that practitioners cope with continuous ad hoc validation rather than with thresholds [60]; Paleyes and colleagues catalogue the same across deployment case studies [61]. Agentic systems make it harder again: the MAST taxonomy attributes a large share of multi-agent failures to specification and inter-agent misalignment rather than to model capability, which is to say to exactly the interfaces an error budget would have to be defined over [80,82,83].

The law is now pushing in the same direction from the other side. The AI Act requires deployers of high-risk systems to monitor operation, to keep automatically generated logs for at least six months, and to suspend use and inform the provider where a risk is identified [40]. That is a named duty, which puts this role at N4 — and it is the reason the AI Operations Manager is the only role below executive level in the twelve with a genuine post-deployment lever.

What enablement can and cannot move

The enablement role has the best-named title in the set — the lab's keyword test gives it a perfect F1 of 1.000, the only role whose title predicts its duties exactly — and the narrowest mandate. Three duties: training, community, change communications. It is worth being careful about what that mandate can achieve, because the evidence base is strong in one direction and silent in another.

That individual productivity gains from generative AI are real and large is now well established across settings. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond found substantial gains among customer-support agents, concentrated among the least experienced [88]; Noy and Zhang found the same shape for professional writing [94]; Peng and colleagues for code [95]; Dell'Acqua and colleagues found gains inside a task's competence frontier and losses outside it, which is the finding practitioners most need and most often skip [96,97]. Against that, METR's controlled study of experienced open-source developers found no speed-up and a plausible slowdown on their own repositories, with participants believing they had been faster [98]. The honest summary is that the gains are real, unevenly distributed by task and experience, and not reliably self-reported — which makes measurement, not enthusiasm, the enablement role's actual product.

The structural limit is separable from all of that. Model adoption as a diffusion process [86,87], in which training, campaigns and office hours raise the external-influence coefficient, and let the ceiling be the share of work the tool genuinely fits. The lab's result is clean: a sixteen-fold increase in enablement effort pulls the half-way point from 19.2 months to 8.1, a 2.37-fold speed-up, and the gap between a well-enabled and an unenabled rollout peaks at 39.0 percentage points at month twelve — and has fallen to 0.0 points by month forty-eight. Raising the ceiling instead, from 0.62 to 0.80, with no enablement at all, is worth 18.0 points at four years. Enablement buys time-to-value, which is worth a great deal, and buys nothing at all at the end of the curve. What the ceiling is made of — which work the tool is allowed to touch, which process it is embedded in — is a design decision, and on the evidence of the decision matrix, it is not in this role's authority set.

The consultant, and the isomorphism engine

The AI Adoption Consultant is the only role in the twelve anchored at N0, and the plate's second failure mode is the more interesting one. Consultancies sell target operating models, and a target operating model sold to every firm in a sector produces the same structure in every firm in that sector. That is DiMaggio and Powell's normative and mimetic isomorphism operating through a specific industry [50], and it is a substantial part of why twelve titles appeared nearly simultaneously across unrelated organisations rather than being discovered independently. The article you are reading is possible only because the answer converged, and it converged partly because the same firms wrote it.

The workforce strategist's asymmetry

The AI Workforce Strategist is the sharpest single instance of the accountability gap this article is about. Its anchor is genuine and enforceable: the AI Act requires deployers to inform workers' representatives before putting a high-risk system into workplace use [40]; the European framework directive on information and consultation applies more broadly [90]; and German co-determination attaches earlier still, since a system capable of monitoring employee performance triggers works-council rights under the Works Constitution Act regardless of whether anyone intended monitoring [89]. So there is a counterparty with legal standing, a duty with a deadline, and a role that owns the consultation record.

And the role has no authority over the deployment that triggers all of it. It can recommend on DEPLOY; it cannot veto, and it holds nothing at all on STOP. Kellogg, Valentine and Christin's survey of algorithmic control at work describes the terrain this role is standing on [93], and Pachidi and colleagues' study of an organisation adopting algorithmic knowledge shows what happens when the affected occupational group has no formal channel: resistance moves into symbolic action, and the change is absorbed rather than negotiated [99,100]. Christin's fieldwork adds the detail that matters for anyone measuring adoption: professionals routinely comply with an algorithmic system in the record while working around it in practice, so the telemetry a workforce or enablement role reports can be exactly inverted from what is happening [101]. The macroeconomic literature meanwhile makes the stakes concrete — the distributional consequences of automation depend heavily on whether displaced tasks are replaced by new ones [91,92,102] — and none of that literature has anything to say about who inside the firm gets to decide.

The distinction that is actually a distinction

AI Governance Manager and AI Risk Manager look interchangeable and are not. The difference is the line they sit on. In the three lines model, management owns and manages risk in the first line; risk and compliance functions in the second line provide expertise, support, monitoring and challenge; internal audit in the third line provides independent assurance to the governing body, and its independence is structural rather than cultural [56]. A governance manager who builds the control framework, runs the intake, tiers the use cases and assembles the evidence is a second-line function that is deeply entangled with the first. A risk manager whose defining product is an independent opinion on somebody else's model is performing a role with a twenty-five-year supervisory pedigree: SR 11-7 requires validation to be performed by staff independent of model development, with the independence backed by incentives, competence and influence [57].

Collapse the two and you have not saved a headcount; you have removed the independence that made one of them worth having. This is the oldest known failure mode in assurance and Power named it decades ago — an audit society generates rituals of verification that are optimised for producing evidence of control rather than control [110]. The AI-specific version is well documented: Raji and colleagues' internal auditing framework exists precisely because end-to-end accountability was absent [105]; Metcalf and colleagues show impact assessments co-constructing the impacts they measure [103]; Selbst argues that an impact assessment's value depends almost entirely on the institutional arrangements around it rather than on the document [104]; Costanza-Chock, Raji and Buolamwini find an auditing ecosystem with no agreed standards and severe access constraints [111,112,113,114].

The gate that closes before launch and never after

Here is the finding I did not expect and would not have made without the audit. In the coded matrix, five of the twelve roles hold binding authority over DEPLOY — three can decide, two can veto. Two hold binding authority over STOP. The pre-launch to post-launch ratio is 2.50 to one, and the roles that lose their teeth at the moment of launch are precisely the control functions: the Head of AI, the AI Governance Manager and the AI Risk Manager all hold a gate before a system goes live and hold nothing but a recommendation afterwards.

That asymmetry is not an accident of my coding; it is what the surrounding apparatus is built to produce. Conformity assessment, impact assessment, model documentation and sign-off are all pre-market instruments [115,116,117,118,119]. They are inherited from product safety, where the object being certified does not change after it is certified. An AI system changes after it is certified — because the model is updated, because the input distribution moves, because the users learn what it will accept. The governance apparatus is aimed at the moment of least uncertainty in the system's life.

The AI Act's own text shows the drafters saw this. Article 26(2) requires deployers to assign human oversight to natural persons who have the necessary competence, training and authority [40]. That clause is doing something unusual for a technology regulation: it names authority as a required personal attribute, alongside competence and training, and thereby makes the article's central question a matter of legal compliance rather than of organisational preference. It is, as far as I can determine, the only place in a major AI instrument where an organisation is told not merely to designate someone but to give them the power to act. Whether that clause is enforced against firms that designate an overseer with no lever is the single most consequential open question for every role in this article — and the timeline for finding out has moved, since the Digital Omnibus deferred the high-risk obligations for stand-alone Annex III systems to 2 December 2027 and for AI embedded in regulated products to 2 August 2028 [84].

Schedule 3, clause 9

Tooling: what these roles actually operate

Job advertisements for all twelve roles list tools, and the lists are close to useless because they name products rather than functions. It is more informative to ask what artefacts the work produces, since an artefact is the thing that survives the person and the thing an auditor asks for. Across the twelve, six families of tooling recur, and they cluster by group in a way the plates already imply.

The register family

A use-case inventory, a model registry, and a risk register. These are the load-bearing artefacts of the whole apparatus and the reason the Governance Manager sits at N4 rather than N1: M-25-21 requires agencies to inventory AI use cases and to determine which are high-impact [44]; ISO/IEC 42001 requires the organisation to know what it is operating [38]; the AI Act's obligations cannot be discharged by anyone who does not know which systems they have [40]. In practice the inventory is the first thing a new governance function builds and the first thing it discovers to be incomplete, because shadow adoption runs ahead of registration by a wide margin. The register is also the article's clearest example of an artefact conferring authority: whoever maintains the inventory decides what counts as a system, and that classification decision determines which obligations attach.

The evaluation family

Evaluation suites, regression gates, red-team exercises and canary deployments. This is where the Head of AI, the Architect and the Operations Manager meet, and it is the least mature family by a distance. Holistic evaluation frameworks give breadth across scenarios and metrics [120], sociotechnical evaluation frameworks make the point that model-level tests do not measure system-level harm [121], and transparency reporting proposals aim at what a deployer can even find out about what it has bought [122]. Practitioner studies have been saying the same thing for years: Holstein and colleagues asked industry teams what they actually needed to improve fairness and got answers about data collection, domain-specific auditing and organisational support rather than about algorithms [123]. The practical constraint remains the one Shankar and colleagues documented: teams do not have a validation set that predicts production, so they run continuous ad hoc evaluation and treat production as the test [60]. Every governance process in this article assumes an evidence base that this family is not yet able to supply.

The documentation family

Model cards [115], datasheets [116], impact and fundamental-rights assessments [103,117], architecture decision records, and the technical documentation the AI Act requires of providers [40]. This family is the most complete on paper and the most vulnerable to Power's critique [110]: it produces exactly the evidence that an auditor will ask for, which is a different objective from producing a system that behaves. Madaio and colleagues' work on co-designing fairness checklists is instructive about the conditions under which a document does change behaviour — it has to be built with the teams that will use it and tied to an existing decision point, otherwise it becomes a filing step [124]. Kroll's traceability argument is the useful corrective — documentation is worth something when it makes a decision reviewable after the fact, and worth little when it merely records that a step was performed [118,119,125].

The operations family

Drift detection, incident management with severities and on-call rotations, log retention meeting the AI Act's six-month floor [40], rollback via the model registry, and cost and quota management. This family is inherited nearly intact from site reliability engineering [79] and is the most mature of the six, which is not surprising: it is the only one with a twenty-year head start and the only one where the tooling and the authority were designed together.

The delivery family

Portfolio and dependency management, stage gates, benefits tracking, vendor and licence management [71]. Nothing here is AI-specific and that is precisely the complaint most often made about it. Its distinctive difficulty is that benefits tracking for AI programmes has to survive the J-curve [17]: the complementary investments are expensed now and the returns arrive later, so an honest benefits model reports a loss in the period when the programme is most in need of defending.

The people family

Learning paths, prompt and pattern libraries, champion networks, licence telemetry, skills taxonomies and job architectures, and consultation records. Telemetry deserves a warning: licence activation and weekly active use are the metrics these roles are most often measured on, and they measure neither capability nor value. The productivity literature is unanimous that effects are heterogeneous by task and experience [88,96,97], and METR's result shows self-report can point the wrong way entirely [98].

Schedule 3, clause 10

Process: the loop all twelve are standing on

Underneath the twelve titles there is one process, and every framework in the field is a different rendering of it: NIST's four functions of GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE, with GOVERN cross-cutting [36]; ISO/IEC 42001's plan-do-check-act management system [38]; ISO/IEC 5338's life-cycle processes [76]; ISO/IEC 38507's governance-level view for boards [126]. Stated plainly, the loop is: something is proposed, it is registered, it is classified by risk, it is designed and built against a standard, it is evaluated, it is approved or refused, it is released, it is monitored, and — the step that is usually drawn and rarely wired — it is stopped.

Intake and tiering, where most of the leverage is

Two of the lab's experiments bear directly on the intake and tiering steps, and both say the same counter-intuitive thing: the classification rule matters more than the review capacity.

The first is a queueing result. A single undifferentiated approval gate behaves like any single server: pushing utilisation from 0.50 to 0.95 multiplies the mean wait by exactly ten [72]. Introduce tiering — a fraction of intake gets a full review, the rest a screening pass — and the improvement is dramatic but front-loaded. Moving from reviewing everything to reviewing 60 per cent removes 0.714 weeks of end-to-end time; moving from 60 per cent to 25 per cent removes a further 0.183; moving from 25 per cent to 10 per cent removes only 0.054 more. Almost all of the benefit of a risk-tiering rubric arrives in its first coarse cut. The elaborate five-tier rubrics that governance functions build in their second year are, on this model, buying very little.

The second is a triage result and it is sharper. Hold the review team fixed at forty full reviews per quarter, let a cheap pre-screen with 90 per cent sensitivity and a 20 per cent false-positive rate feed it, and grow the intake. Capacity is exceeded once intake passes 200 proposals per quarter. By 1,600, coverage of genuinely high-risk cases has fallen from 90.0 per cent to 8.8 per cent, and the number of unreviewed high-risk cases per quarter has gone from 0.8 to 116.8. Now hold intake fixed at 800 and improve the pre-screen instead of the team: cutting the false-positive rate from 0.20 to 0.01 lifts coverage from 17.6 per cent to 55.4 per cent with no additional reviewers, a 3.15-fold gain bought entirely by the classification rule. Sensitivity, meanwhile, caps coverage at 0.90 no matter what else is done.

The organisational reading is direct. A governance function under intake pressure has two moves: ask for more reviewers, or make its own tiering rule more precise. The first is a budget conversation it will usually lose. The second is entirely within its authority, produces a larger effect in this model, and is the thing least often prioritised — because a sharper rubric is invisible to everyone outside the function, while a bigger team is visible to everyone.

The step that is drawn and not wired

The last step of the loop is where the process diagrams and the decision matrix disagree, and the disagreement is the article's core finding. Every framework includes a decommission, suspend or terminate step. NIST's MANAGE function includes it. The AI Act requires deployers to suspend use where a risk is identified [40]. M-25-21 requires agencies to terminate non-compliant high-impact uses [44]. But an obligation to stop is not the same as an authority to stop, and in the coded matrix only two of twelve roles hold the latter.

The cost of that gap is measurable. If the Operations Manager can halt alone, the expected delay is one desk's turnaround. If the halt has to be recommended by a governance manager and decided by a chief AI officer sitting on a body that meets far less often, it is two desks in series, and the lab puts the difference at 4.083 weeks — a 4.27-fold increase, from an organisation chart alone, with no change to the system, the evidence, or the decision itself. On a system taking two thousand decisions a week, that is roughly 8,167 further decisions taken by a system already believed to be faulty. And a third desk added in series costs another full turnaround while adding no authority at all, because there is still exactly one decider: the count of desks sets the delay, and the count of deciders sets nothing.

There is a second cost to the extra desk that the delay figure does not capture. March and Simon called it uncertainty absorption: when evidence is summarised and passed up a chain, the inferences travel and the evidence does not, so the person who finally holds the authority is deciding on someone else's conclusion rather than on the data [127]. Every desk added between the measurement and the decision absorbs a little more, which is why the operations manager's error-budget-style halt is not merely faster but better informed.

This is the problem of many hands in its textbook form [109]. Bovens's definition of accountability — a relationship in which an actor is obliged to explain and justify conduct to a forum which can pose questions and pass judgement, with consequences [108] — is worth holding against every one of the twelve plates. Several of these roles have a forum. Several have an obligation to explain. Almost none have consequences that attach to them rather than to somebody else.

Schedule 3, clause 11

Background, qualifications and credentials

The market's answer to what these roles require is easy to state and hard to use: everything. Coding sixteen commonly named requirements across the twelve roles from published position descriptions produces a matrix with a mean of 5.08 requirements per role, a mean pairwise cosine of 0.29, and — the result that matters — no requirement asked by more than seven of the twelve. The single most commonly named requirement is experience presenting to a board or a regulator, which is a proxy for seniority rather than a description of the work. Only three of sixteen requirements are asked by two roles or fewer, and those three are the only credentials in the set that would tell any two of these jobs apart.

The stronger test is whether qualifications predict duties. Recover four duty groups by clustering the ownership vectors, then try to predict a role's group from its qualification vector by leave-one-out nearest centroid. The result is 6 of 12 correct — 50.0 per cent — against a majority-class baseline of 66.7 per cent. The qualification vector performs 16.7 points worse than ignoring it and guessing the largest group every time. Separately, a role's nearest neighbour by required background agrees with its nearest neighbour by duty in only 6 of 12 cases. Requirements and responsibilities in this market point at different partitions of the same twelve jobs.

What the credentials are, and what they are for

Four credential families are now visible in postings. The IAPP's Artificial Intelligence Governance Professional launched in April 2024 and reached version 2.1 of its body of knowledge in February 2026 [128]; ISACA layered AI audit and AI security management credentials on top of its existing CISA and CISM tracks in 2025 [129]; ISO/IEC 42001 lead-auditor and lead-implementer schemes proliferated, and ISO/IEC 42006 now constrains what an accredited certification body's auditors must be able to demonstrate [39]; and the change-management credentials, principally Prosci's [130], carried over intact from the digital transformation wave.

It is worth being clear-eyed about what a two-year-old credential can and cannot certify. Wilensky's account of professionalisation sets out the sequence — full-time occupation, training school, professional association, licensure, code of ethics — and observes that most occupations claiming professional status never complete it [131]. Abbott's more precise framing is that professions compete for jurisdiction over problems, and that jurisdiction is won by demonstrating an abstract knowledge base that lets one profession's members claim a problem that another's cannot solve [132]. On that test, AI governance is at an early and contested stage: the IAPP's credential claims the jurisdiction from privacy and law, ISACA's from audit, the ISO schemes' from quality management. They are three different professions each arguing that this problem is a special case of the one they already own.

The market data is consistent with genuine demand and does not tell you the credentials are what is being bought. PwC's 2026 barometer, over more than a billion advertisements in twenty-seven territories, finds AI-skilled postings growing 69 per cent since 2019 against 9 per cent for the market as a whole, and an average wage premium for AI skills of 62 per cent, up from 57 per cent [133]; Lightcast finds AI skills spreading well beyond technical occupations [134]. Those are premia for AI skills in general, measured on advertisements, not controlled for education, experience or location — and none of them isolates the value of a governance credential.

The honest advice

Read across the plates and the pattern is legible. For the direction roles, what is being bought is the ability to hold a decision in a room full of people who outrank you, which is why board exposure and budget ownership dominate. For construction, it is engineering judgement about a class of system whose failure modes are not those of ordinary software [58,59]. For control, it is the ability to write down an opinion that someone will be angry about, backed by a method that survives challenge [57]. For capability, it is measurement discipline and enough process knowledge to argue about the ceiling rather than the training. And for coordination — the three roles with no lever — what is being bought is the ability to work without one, which is a real skill and one that no credential in the market attempts to certify.

There is also a structural fact worth knowing before choosing a title. The US Standard Occupational Classification, which every federal statistical agency is required to use, contains no occupation corresponding to any of these twelve. The 2018 revision took over five years; the 2028 revision's structure is not due to be published until spring 2027 and does not enter use until reference year 2028 [135,136]. Whatever these roles are, the statistical system will not have a name for them for at least another two years, and it will name at most a fraction of them then.

Schedule 4, clause 12

The lab: nine measurements

Nine deterministic experiments, standard library only, no network and no data beyond what is printed in this article. Their complete output is reproduced verbatim below, and every number quoted anywhere in this article comes from it. Four of the nine (L1, L2, L3, L8) run over coded matrices — my reading of published obligations and position descriptions into fixed vocabularies — and measure the structure of that coding rather than the world. The coding is printed in the appendix. Five (L4, L5, L6, L7, L9) are pure models with stated parameters and no coded input at all.

What each one asks

L1 — what the title tells you. The twelve titles decompose into nine rank tokens and ten domain tokens, generating ninety nameable positions of which the brief occupies twelve. Then a mechanical test: a duty counts as predicted by a title if any word in the title's stated synonym set appears in the duty's inventory text. No judgement is exercised at match time. Mean precision 0.796, mean recall 0.453, mean F1 0.549 — titles are right about what they mention and silent about half of what the role does. The best is AI Enablement Lead at 1.000, the worst AI Transformation Architect at 0.222. The rank token carries nothing: the three roles called Lead overlap on duties at 0.256 against a set-wide mean of 0.130, and the four called Manager at 0.152.

L2 — are these twelve distinct jobs? Pairwise Jaccard over thirty coded duties [137]. Mean 0.130, median 0.100, and 21 of 66 pairs share nothing at all. The highest pair is Chief AI Officer with Head of AI at 0.4286. Single-linkage clustering at threshold 0.30 collapses twelve titles into four groups, one of which contains eight of them. The most contested single duty is resolving cross-functional dependencies and escalations, owned by six of the twelve.

L3 — the decision-rights audit. Twelve roles by four decisions. Only one role can decide FUND alone. Five hold binding authority over DEPLOY; two over STOP; the pre-to-post ratio is 2.50. Three roles hold a gate before launch and nothing after it. And the correlation between a role's anchor rung and its authority score is +0.154 — effectively nothing — while the correlation between duty count and authority score is +0.694. Authority tracks how much a role does, not what requires it to exist.

L4 — the coordination surface. A hub replaces n(n−1)/2 channels with n, and at twenty functions that is an 89.5 per cent reduction. Modelled as a single server it holds ten functions at 0.80 utilisation, saturates at fourteen, and going from ten to twelve multiplies the wait by 2.50. Federation into pods holds both tiers under 0.80 at twenty-four functions with three pods and twenty-seven channels.

L5 — the approval gate. Utilisation 0.50 to 0.95 multiplies the wait by exactly 10. Tiering is front-loaded: 100 to 60 per cent full review removes 0.714 weeks, 60 to 25 removes 0.183, 25 to 10 removes 0.054.

L6 — enablement against the ceiling. Bass diffusion in months with q = 0.25 and ceiling m = 0.62. Sixteen-fold enablement moves the half-way point from 19.2 to 8.1 months. The enabled-versus-unenabled gap peaks at 39.0 points at month twelve and reaches 0.0 by month forty-eight. Raising the ceiling to 0.80 with no enablement is worth 18.0 points at four years.

L7 — capacity-constrained triage. Fixed capacity of forty reviews per quarter. Coverage of true high-risk cases falls from 90.0 per cent to 8.8 per cent as intake grows sixteen-fold; unreviewed high-risk cases rise from 0.8 to 116.8 per quarter. L8 — do qualifications separate the roles? Leave-one-out nearest centroid predicting duty group from the sixteen-item qualification vector: 6 of 12, or 50.0 per cent, against a 66.7 per cent majority baseline. A negative result, and the strongest single piece of evidence in the article that the credential market is not tracking the job market.

L9 — what a missing decision right costs. Parallel sign-off grows logarithmically in the number of signers; serial sign-off grows linearly. The specific case: 1.250 weeks for a single desk that touches the system daily, 5.333 weeks for a recommend-then-decide chain through two desks, a difference of 4.083 weeks and a factor of 4.27.

What was rebuilt, and one thing that was withdrawn

Two experiments were rebuilt after their first run and the rebuilds are disclosed here rather than quietly absorbed. L6's first version used yearly units with an imitation coefficient an order of magnitude too small, producing a time-to-half-adoption of 130 months — absurd for a technology that the AI Index records reaching 53 per cent population adoption in three years [63] — and it measured the ceiling effect at the wrong point on the curve, reporting enablement as dominant when the whole point is that its dominance is temporary. Recalibrated to monthly units it produces the crossing result reported above, which is the opposite conclusion from the first run at long horizons. L5's first parameterisation made the untiered queue unstable, so the comparison it was built to make did not exist; the service rates were raised until the baseline was a queue rather than a divergence.

One claim was withdrawn. An earlier draft asserted that duty count and authority are unrelated — that these roles are given work in inverse proportion to power. The measurement does not support it: the correlation is +0.694, which is substantial. The true version is narrower and more interesting, and it is the one the article now makes: authority tracks workload, and it does not track anchoring (+0.154). A role acquires power in this market by accumulating responsibilities, not by being required to exist.

This is the only distinction the clustering finds unaided. Cutting the duty tree at 0.30 isolates the AI Workforce Strategist and the AI Operations Manager as singletons — the two roles whose objects are, respectively, employment relationships and running processes — and leaves eight of the remaining ten in one undifferentiated group. That is the honest measurement of how much the titles carve reality: two clean separations, one two-member control cluster, and a blob of eight.

Second: the three lines

AI Governance Manager and AI Risk Manager differ by which line they sit on, and the difference is structural, not stylistic [56,57]. A second-line function that builds the control framework and assembles the evidence is not independent of the thing it is assessing. A validation function whose product is an independent opinion is. Every other apparent distinction inside the control group is a naming preference; this one is load-bearing, and collapsing it is the most consequential organisational mistake available in this whole design space.

Third: permanent against terminating

Transformation Lead, Adoption Consultant and Program Manager are terminating roles: their mandate ends when the programme does, and their anchor ends with it. Governance Manager, Risk Manager and Operations Manager are standing functions attached to obligations that do not end. This distinction is invisible in the titles, obvious in the anchor rungs, and it is the single most useful thing a candidate can establish before accepting one of these jobs. A terminating role is not a worse job; it is a differently shaped one, and the exit is part of the design rather than a failure.

Fourth: anchored against unanchored

Exactly one of the twelve — the Chief AI Officer — has ever been required by name, and only inside one jurisdiction's public sector [44]. Six sit at N4, where an instrument names the duty and leaves the organisation to choose who carries it: Enablement, Workforce, Operations, Governance, Risk, and in the wider private sector the Chief AI Officer too. Four sit at N1, held by a budget line alone. One sits at N0. The N4 band is the interesting one, because it is where the next few years of churn will happen: a duty with no named owner is a duty that will be reassigned repeatedly until either a regulator names someone or a serious incident does.

And where there is no distinction at all

Head of AI against AI Centre of Excellence Lead is a naming preference in most organisations; their duty overlap is 0.375, their qualification vectors are each other's nearest neighbour at cosine 0.866, and the only real difference is whether the platform team reports to them. AI Transformation Lead against AI Adoption Consultant is the same job inside and outside the firm. AI Program Manager against AI Transformation Lead differs by scope and not by kind. And the rank token — Lead, Manager, Head, Chief — is close to noise: L1 finds the three Leads no more alike than any random pair, and the four Managers barely more so.

The middle of the organisation — precisely where eleven of these twelve titles are usually placed — holds none of it.

The second is that the anchor column and the authority column do not move together (r = +0.154). The AI Enablement Lead sits at N4, anchored by an obligation in a major regulation, and scores zero on authority. The Head of AI sits at N1, anchored by nothing but a budget line, and scores eight. What anchoring predicts is not power but survival, and those are different properties that this labour market persistently conflates.

The third is the FUND column, which has exactly one decider in the whole set. Eleven of twelve roles created to reorganise a firm around artificial intelligence cannot commit a pound to it. That is not a scandal — most roles in most organisations cannot commit money — but it is worth holding next to the finding that the single attribute most associated with bottom-line impact in the largest survey of the field is that someone senior enough to close a decision owns the governance of it [62].

Schedule 5, clause 15

What holds

The honest forecast for these twelve titles is not that they will all disappear, and not that they will all consolidate into a Chief AI Officer. It is that they will sort by anchor, and the sorting is already legible in the ledger.

The roles at N4 will persist in some form, because the duties persist. The AI Act's monitoring, logging, oversight and worker-information obligations do not go away when a transformation programme ends; they are attached to systems, and the systems stay [40]. Whether they persist under these names is a different question, and the history says probably not: the chief information security officer got its name from regulation naming it [23], and the AI Act names duties rather than officers. The likelier outcome for the N4 band is absorption — the AI Governance Manager's work migrating into a privacy or compliance function that already has a designated officer, the AI Operations Manager's into site reliability engineering, the AI Workforce Strategist's into employee relations. Absorption is not failure. It is what happened to every one of the electrification-era coordinating roles, and it is what a technology looks like when it stops being a special case.

The roles at N1 will churn on the schedule that budget-anchored roles always churn on. The PMO literature's roughly two-year reconfiguration cycle [67] and the chief data officer's two-and-a-half-year tenure [26] are the base rates, and there is nothing in the AI versions that should beat them. A Head of AI, a Centre of Excellence Lead and a Transformation Lead in the same firm are three claims on the same territory backed by three budget lines, and budget lines are the easiest thing in an organisation to consolidate.

The Chief AI Officer is the genuinely uncertain case, and it is uncertain for a reason worth naming. It is the only title with an N5 anchor and the anchor is administrative rather than statutory: a memorandum, revocable by the next memorandum, which has already survived one reversal of the surrounding policy [42,44]. In the private sector it has no anchor at all and inherits the chief data officer's structural problem intact — eight duties across four functions, ownership of other people's systems, and an accountability that attaches at the moment of failure [25].

Which leaves the question the article has been circling. If the apparatus is this elaborate, the instruments this numerous, and the titles this many, why does so little of it converge on the authority to stop? Part of the answer is inheritance: conformity assessment and impact assessment come from product safety, where the certified object does not change after certification, and they are aimed at the moment of least uncertainty in an AI system's life [103,104]. Part is the decoupling that Meyer and Rowan predicted and Edelman documented — a structure adopted for legitimacy under ambiguous law is optimised for being seen, and a stop is by construction the thing nobody sees when it works [51,52]. And part is simply that stopping is expensive and someone has to be willing to pay, which is a question about incentives rather than about organisational design [53].

There is one mechanism in the whole field that has demonstrably solved this, and it is not a governance mechanism. The error budget works because the authority to halt is a consequence of a number that both parties agreed to before anyone was under pressure [79]. Nobody has to be brave. Nobody has to win an argument in the week that the argument is most expensive to have. That is the design pattern the twelve titles are missing, and it is missing for a concrete and solvable reason: no equivalent measurement exists yet for AI systems that is agreed in advance, monitored continuously, and specific enough to trip a pre-agreed policy. The evaluation family is the least mature of the six precisely where the whole apparatus most needs it [60,121].

So the practical advice is short. If you are creating one of these roles, the useful question is not what to call it. It is which of the four decisions it can close, whether that includes the one that comes after launch, and what would have to change in the world for it to still exist in three years. If you are being hired into one, the same three questions, asked out loud, will tell you more than the job description will. And if you are trying to work out whether an organisation's AI governance is real, there is a single test that is cheap to apply and hard to fake: ask who, by name, can take a running system down this afternoon without asking anyone. If the answer is a committee, there is no answer.

The AI Act, for what it is worth, asks the same question in its own register. It requires deployers to assign oversight to people with the necessary competence, training and authority [40]. Two of those three are things you can buy with a training budget. The third is the whole subject of this article, and there is no course for it.
