01The question this article is asking

Seven job titles have appeared in the last three years that did not exist before it, and they overlap so heavily that most published guidance to them is a list of shared tools. An AI security engineer and an AI red team engineer both know what indirect prompt injection is. An AI security architect and an AI governance engineer both draw boundaries around a retrieval corpus. An AI safety engineer and an AI risk engineer both write down a number and attach a threshold to it. Read the postings and the seven look like one job with a hiring manager's preference attached.

They are not one job, and the difference is not seniority, tooling, or which part of the stack a person touches. It is what each of them is on the hook to say. Every one of these roles exists to produce a claim about a system nobody can fully test: a sentence somebody else will act on. The sentences have different logical shapes. An exploit is an existence proof. An access rule is a universal statement. A refusal rate is an estimate with an interval. A threat model is a conditional. An evidence pack is a procedural assertion that says nothing at all about outcomes. These shapes are not stylistic. They decide what it would take to refute the claim, how long it stays true once the system moves, and what weight it can bear when a deployment decision is made on top of it.

So the organising question here is the one Popper asked of any assertion that wants to be taken seriously [1]: what would falsify it? Applied to a job rather than a theory, the question has a sharper edge, because the answer determines the shape of the role. It is also, as Lakatos pointed out about scientific practice, not a question anyone answers once: what gets protected from refutation and what gets exposed to it is a running decision, and inside an engineering organisation it is a decision about reporting lines [2]. A person whose output is existence proofs can never be asked to certify absence, however senior they get. A person whose output is procedural evidence can be perfectly diligent and still tell you nothing about whether the system is safe. Confusing the two is not a communication problem. It is a category error with a signature failure mode, and most of the trouble organisations are having with AI security right now is an instance of it.

What follows is an attempt to be precise about this. It covers where the seven roles came from, what each of them actually does day to day, the tools and processes each has settled on, the backgrounds and credentials that lead into them, and then a set of measurements — computed in a small deterministic lab whose output is reproduced in full below — of how far apart they really are. Some of the results were not what I expected. Claims held by the two roles with the highest public profile turn out to have the shortest lives. The two roles whose duties are most easily absorbed by others are the two that recruiters compete hardest over. And the ordering of roles by claim durability, which is the first thing you would want to know, turns out not to be robust to a modelling assumption I had to make and could not verify.

02Where these titles came from

The work is old; only the labels are new. Penetration testing as a named practice dates to the RAND and Air Force studies of the late 1960s and early 1970s, which set out the idea that you learn what a system's security is worth by having someone competent try to break it, under authorisation, and write down what happened [3,4]. The Morris worm of 1988 turned that practice into an industry with an incident-response arm attached [5]. Everything the AI red team does now inherits its rules of engagement, its reporting conventions, and its central epistemic limitation from that lineage. The defensive half of the inheritance is younger and equally direct: the security development lifecycle and the build-security-in programme of the mid-2000s established that security is a property of a development process rather than a test at the end, which is the argument the AI-specific guidance is now making again [6,7].

Machine learning acquired an adversary early. Dalvi and colleagues modelled spam classification as a game against an opponent who edits the input in 2004 [8]; two years later Barreno and colleagues asked the field's founding question directly and produced the first taxonomy of attacks against learners [9]. Biggio and colleagues demonstrated gradient-based evasion against deployed detectors in 2013 [10], and Szegedy and colleagues independently found adversarial examples in deep networks the same year [11,12]. What followed over the next decade was a research programme with a stable structure: an attack class is described, defences are proposed, the defences are broken by adaptive attackers, and the attack class survives as a permanent property of the technology [13,14]. Model extraction [15], membership inference [16], targeted misclassification through crafted perturbations [17], backdoors in the training pipeline [18] and memorisation of training data [19,20] all arrived in this period and all are still live; the surveys consolidating this material now run to hundreds of pages [21].

The gap between that literature and industrial practice was measured once, carefully, and the result is worth keeping in mind whenever anyone claims the field is mature. In 2020, Siva Kumar and colleagues interviewed twenty-eight organisations building or using machine learning, and found that twenty-five of them did not have the tools to secure it, with several explicitly asking for guidance on where to even start [22]. The same group had already published a failure-mode taxonomy separating intentional attacks from unintentional failures [23]; MITRE turned that work into the Adversarial ML Threat Matrix and then into ATLAS, which is now the standard shared vocabulary for AI-specific adversary behaviour and is maintained in the same idiom as ATT&CK [24,25].

Generative models changed the shape of the problem rather than the substance of it. The decisive move was architectural: once a model's context window is filled from sources the user does not control — a retrieved document, an incoming email, a web page, a tool's return value — the boundary between instruction and data, which operating systems have policed since the 1970s [26,27], stops being enforced by anything. Simon Willison named the resulting failure prompt injection in September 2022 [28]; Greshake and colleagues generalised it to the indirect case a few months later and showed it working against real deployed applications [29]. The prehistory of the jailbreak is older than the term: universal adversarial triggers against language models predate instruction tuning [30], and the mechanism by which safety training fails was characterised before the automated methods arrived [31]. Zou and colleagues then demonstrated that adversarial suffixes found against open models transfer to closed ones [32], and a sequence of automated jailbreak methods followed [33,34,35], along with the consistent finding that published defences fall to attackers who adapt to them [36,37].

Two events in 2023 turned red teaming from a laboratory practice into an expectation. OpenAI published a system card describing external red teaming before deployment [38], and at DEF CON that August a public exercise put several thousand participants against eight vendors’ models, producing the largest published account of distributed adversarial testing [39]. The US executive order that October made red teaming a named obligation for dual-use foundation models and directed NIST to define the term [40]; the Bletchley declaration in November committed twenty-eight countries and the EU to a shared programme on frontier risk [41].

The state response was quick and, unusually, coordinated. CISA and the NCSC published joint guidelines for secure AI system development in November 2023, co-sealed by twenty-three agencies and organised around four lifecycle stages — secure design, secure development, secure deployment, and secure operation and maintenance [42]. NIST released the AI Risk Management Framework in January 2023 and a generative-AI profile the following year [43,44], finalised a secure-development community profile for foundation models [45,46], and updated its adversarial machine learning taxonomy in March 2025 to cover generative systems, agents, and a new category of misuse violations, drawing on work with both the US and UK institutes [47,48]. The EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024 [49]; California enacted the first US frontier-AI statute in September 2025 [50]; ENISA had already published a multilayer framework for AI cybersecurity practice in 2023 [51]; ISO published a certifiable management-system standard for AI in December 2023 and the accreditation rules for auditing against it in 2025 [52,53]. NIST is now producing control overlays that map AI system types onto the existing catalogue rather than replacing it [54,55].

Job titles arrived last, and they are still not official. The most consequential fact about the US workforce taxonomy is that it has not created any of the seven roles this article is about. The NICE Framework describes cybersecurity work as fifty-two work roles across seven categories [56]; when NICE addressed AI, it added a competency area rather than a work role — a cluster of knowledge and skill statements that can be applied across many existing roles [57,58]. The AI Security competency area defines the capability as securing AI against cyberattacks, containing it adequately where it is used, and mitigating the threat AI presents where it or its users are malicious [59]. Proposed updates went out for comment in June 2025, and the framework components have moved through versions 2.0.0, 2.1.0 and 2.2.0 between March 2025 and April 2026 without an AI security work role appearing [60]. That is the correct reading of the whole landscape: the market has produced seven job titles for what the reference taxonomy still treats as one competency spread across many jobs.

03Five things a security claim can be

An existential claim says there is an input that does something. An exploit is the paradigm case. It is cheap to verify — run it again — and it cannot be refuted by any amount of testing, because no set of trials that fails to reproduce it establishes that no such input exists. This is the strongest kind of claim in security and the least generalisable. It licenses exactly one inference: the system is not safe in the way you thought. It licenses nothing about the system being safe in any other way. The asymmetry is the one Hempel made precise for confirmation in general: instances accumulate without ever closing the gap to a universal, while one counter-instance closes it at once [61].

A universal claim says something holds for every request, path or actor. An access rule is the paradigm case: retrieval returns only what the caller's token already grants. One counterexample kills it, which makes it falsifiable in the strict sense, and it is the only form of the five that supports a design argument. Its characteristic weakness is that it holds only where it is enforced. A universal claim about a system is really a claim about a mechanism, and the mechanism has a scope.

A statistical claim states a rate with an interval: refusal at 98.6 per cent, transfer at some measured proportion. It is falsified by a bigger or a different sample, which means it is never falsified outright, only revised. Statistical claims are what evaluations produce, and they carry a distinctive hazard: the interval is usually reported and the power is not, so a claim of no change between two checkpoints is frequently a report about sample size rather than about the model.

A conditional claim states that something holds given assumptions. Every threat model is one, every scope decision is one, and every risk appetite statement is one. Conditionals are the most useful claims in architecture and the most dangerous in operation, because they are voided silently: nothing fires when assumption A stops holding. The assumption that untrusted content cannot reach a component with tool-call authority is a true and load-bearing conditional on Monday and a false one on Wednesday when somebody adds a connector.

A procedural claim says the process ran and the record exists. Governance work produces these, and they are frequently disparaged for it. The disparagement is misplaced but the limitation is real: a procedural claim is falsified by a missing record and by nothing else, which means it is epistemically silent about outcomes. Knowing that every deployed model version has a completed impact assessment on file tells you about the completeness of a filing system. It is still worth having — a system whose decisions cannot be reconstructed cannot be corrected — but it is a claim about the organisation, not about the model.

Two teams can be equally competent, equally well resourced, and looking at the same system, and produce claims of different shapes that do not compose into a single statement about safety. That is the situation most enterprises are actually in.

04The seven roles, as they are actually practised

What follows describes each role in terms of the claim it signs, the work that produces the claim, and the tooling that has become conventional. The descriptions are drawn from published guidance and from the standard artefacts each role produces; where a practice is contested I say so.

SECAI security engineer

The claim is universal: a stated property holds for every request through this system. The work is building and operating the mechanisms that make it true. This is the closest of the seven to conventional product and platform security, pointed at a new class of system, and it is the role with the most duties in the register below.

In a week: reviewing and constraining a tool manifest so that no tool can write outside its volume and every tool's schema is on an allowlist; wiring retrieval so entitlement is evaluated at query time against the calling user's token rather than at index time; giving the agent its own identity with short-lived, narrowly scoped credentials instead of letting it borrow a user's session; putting a gateway in front of model calls that does classification, rate limiting, cost accounting and logging in one place; hardening the training and fine-tuning pipeline, which is where data-integrity practices from the NIST secure-development profile bite — the profile adds an explicit practice for confirming the integrity of training, tuning and alignment data before use [45]. Output handling gets its own attention, because a model's output reaching a browser, a shell or a SQL string is an injection sink of the oldest kind [62].

Tooling is a mix of the general and the specific: an AI gateway or proxy, policy engines for authorisation, secrets and identity infrastructure adapted to non-human principals, guardrail libraries at the input and output, and sandboxing for anything the agent executes. The reference documents are the OWASP top ten for LLM applications and its agentic companion [63,64], the AI security verification standard for requirements-level checking [65], ATLAS for adversary behaviour [24], zero-trust architecture for the identity model [66], and the ordinary control catalogue and management system underneath, which do not stop applying because the workload became a model [55,67].

REDAI red team engineer

The claim is existential: here is an input, or a chain of them, that makes the system do something it should not. The work is finding it and making it reproducible. Microsoft's account of red teaming more than a hundred generative AI products is the best public description of the practice, and its third lesson states the boundary of the role exactly: AI red teaming is not safety benchmarking [68]. A benchmark measures known harms on a fixed dataset. A red team explores the space where the harm categories are not yet known.

In a week: building and rotating a corpus of attack prompts and testing against the deployed configuration rather than the bare model; chaining tools to construct an exfiltration path that no single tool permits; testing indirect injection through whatever channel the system ingests — documents, calendar invitations, ticket comments, retrieved web pages [29]; attempting multimodal injection where images or audio carry the instruction [69]; probing for memorisation and extraction [20,70]; and writing the finding up so that an engineer can reproduce it in one step. The second lesson from the Microsoft account is worth repeating to anyone recruiting for this role: you do not have to compute gradients to break an AI system [68].

Tooling is younger than the practice. PyRIT and garak are the two open frameworks with real adoption, one oriented to orchestrated risk identification and one to probe-based scanning [71,72]; AgentDojo provides a dynamic environment for evaluating injection attacks against agents [73]; the OWASP red teaming guide organises engagements into model evaluation, implementation testing, infrastructure assessment and runtime behaviour analysis [74], and the Cloud Security Alliance has a companion for agentic systems [75]. The critical literature is worth reading alongside the guides: Feffer and colleagues argue that AI red teaming has been asked to carry more assurance weight than its structure can bear [76], which is a restatement of the existential-claim problem.

ARCAI security architect

The claim is conditional: given these assumptions, these boundaries hold. The work is deciding where the boundaries go before anything is built, and recording the assumptions in a form somebody can check later. This is the role with the least tooling and the most leverage.

In a week: threat modelling a proposed feature, in practice using STRIDE per element on a data-flow diagram that has been redrawn to show where untrusted content enters and where authority sits [77]; deciding whether the planner that chooses tool calls may ever see untrusted content, which is the single highest-leverage architectural decision in an agentic system and the one the CaMeL design makes explicit by separating a privileged planner from a quarantined interpreter [78]; specifying the identity model for agents; setting which actions require human approval and which are irreversible; and writing the reference pattern that other teams instantiate. Architecture descriptions have a standard [79] and lifecycle processes for AI systems have one too [80]; both are more useful here than their reputation suggests, because the architect's problem is precisely that different stakeholders need different views of the same system.

GOVAI governance engineer

The claim is procedural: the control exists, it was applied, and here is the record. The work is turning policy text into machine-checkable controls and durable evidence. This role is frequently and wrongly described as compliance with a keyboard. What distinguishes the engineering version is that the controls are implemented in the pipeline rather than asserted in a spreadsheet.

In a week: maintaining the inventory of models, versions, use cases and their owners, which is the precondition for everything else; mapping controls to whichever frameworks apply and keeping the mapping current as they revise; instrumenting logging, lineage and retention so a decision can be reconstructed — the AI Act's deployer obligations require monitoring and at least six months of automatic log retention for high-risk systems [49]; producing evidence packs for audit or certification against the AI management-system standard [52,53]; and running impact assessments to the ISO template [81] or the risk-management guidance [82]. In the US federal context the equivalent scaffolding is the OMB memorandum's governance structure [83]; in financial services it is model risk management, which has required independent validation and effective challenge since 2011 and is the closest mature precedent for the whole family of roles [84].

SAFAI safety engineer

The claim is conditional or statistical, depending on the day: this checkpoint does not cross a stated capability threshold, or it refuses at a measured rate. The work is designing evaluations, running them against candidate models, and holding the line on what the result licenses.

In a week: writing and maintaining behavioural evaluations with explicit thresholds; running dangerous-capability elicitation, where the standard method is to try hard to make the model do the thing rather than to sample from a fixed set; distinguishing a genuine behaviour change from a prompt or scaffold change, which is a causal question and usually the hardest thing on the list; and signing or refusing a ship decision. Two results from 2024 define the difficulty of this role better than any framework does: a backdoored model can survive the safety training designed to remove it [85], and a model can behave differently when it infers that it is being evaluated [86]. Both mean an evaluation measures behaviour under observation, which is not the quantity of interest. State evaluation bodies have converged on the same methodological answer, which is to elicit hard and report what could not be elicited [87]. The public scaffolding is the family of frontier safety policies: Anthropic's responsible scaling policy with its safety levels and capability thresholds [88], OpenAI's preparedness framework and its statutory companion [89,90], and DeepMind's frontier safety framework, whose 2026 revision added tracked capability levels below the critical ones to catch less extreme risks earlier [91]. METR maintains a comparison of what these policies have in common, which is the most useful single document for anyone specifying the role [92].

The role's characteristic difficulty is that its claims are about a checkpoint, and checkpoints are replaced. An evaluation result is a measurement of an object that will not exist in three months, which is why the survival strip below puts this role in the middle of the range rather than at the top where its rigour would suggest.

RSKAI risk engineer

The claim is statistical with a conditional wrapper: exposure is this large, under these assumptions, and it is inside or outside appetite. The work is quantification and aggregation.

In a week: building loss models for AI-mediated exposure with explicit distributions rather than heat-map colours, in the tradition of quantitative security risk work [93,94]; classifying systems against regulatory regimes, which after the EU Digital Omnibus is a moving target — the regulation defers the Annex III high-risk obligations to December 2027 and the Annex I ones to August 2028 while leaving the transparency and general-purpose obligations broadly in place [95]; aggregating across systems for a committee that will read one page; and deciding, with the safety engineer, whether something ships. Where a frontier developer is in scope, the reporting obligations are now statutory: California's Act requires published frontier frameworks, transparency reports, quarterly catastrophic-risk summaries to the state's emergency services office and critical-incident reporting within fifteen days, with penalties up to a million dollars per violation [50]; New York's RAISE Act sets a shorter incident window and higher penalties [96].

RESAI security researcher

The claim is about a class rather than an instance: this attack works against this family of systems, or this defence does not generalise. The work is producing knowledge that outlives the system it was found on.

In a week: designing an attack that isolates a mechanism rather than exploiting a configuration; measuring transfer across model families; evaluating whether a proposed defence survives an adaptive attacker, which is the single most common failure in the published defence literature [36]; and publishing. The role's distinctive value is that its claims are the longest-lived of the seven, because a claim about a class does not die when a checkpoint does. The distinctive risk is that the class-level claim is the easiest to overstate: a defence evaluated only against a static injection set looks far stronger than it is, and a 2026 adaptive evaluation of the published agent defences argues exactly that about several headline numbers [97,78,98].

05The claim register

Below are twenty claims. They are written the way they get written in practice — as a sentence somebody could put in a report — and each carries a fixed set of fields: who holds it, what one observation would falsify it, which classes of change it survives, and which classes kill it. The small chart on each plate is that claim's survival curve, and the vertical mark is its half-life. Both are explained in the next section.

Six classes of change are enough to cover what happens to a deployed AI system between one month and the next: a prompt or template is edited; the retrieval corpus or a data source changes; a tool or connector is added or altered; the model checkpoint is updated within its family; a policy, configuration or entitlement changes; the model family is swapped. A claim's rung on the ladder is how many of those six it survives. Rung five is a claim touched by only one class of change. Rung zero is a claim that any change at all can void.

06How long each role's claims stay true

Rung counts breadth of immunity. It does not price it, because the six change classes do not occur equally often. Prompt edits are constant; model family swaps are rare. If each change event is drawn independently from a mix of the six classes with weights P 0.34, D 0.22, T 0.18, M 0.14, G 0.09, F 0.03, then a claim depending on a subset with total weight w survives k events with probability (1 − w)k, and its half-life is ln(0.5) / ln(1 − w). No simulation is involved; the survival curves on the plates and in the gauge are exact.

The weights are an assumption, and they are the weakest part of this analysis. They are a stipulation about how enterprise deployments change, not a measurement, and I know of no published dataset that would let me replace them with one. What the model does buy is a way to see the difference between breadth of immunity and expected lifetime, and the two turn out to disagree in specific, interpretable places. Across the twenty claims, Spearman's rho between rung and half-life is 0.9042 — strong, but not one. C08, the claim that every deployed version has an impact assessment on file, sits at rung three and yet outlives most rung-four claims, because the three things that kill it (checkpoint updates, family swaps, policy changes) are the three rarest. C11, the causal claim that a behaviour change came from the prompt rather than the checkpoint, sits at rung four and dies faster than claims two rungs below it.

Read across the strips and the order is: the security engineer at 2.84 changes, the researcher at 2.64, the governance engineer at 2.13, the safety engineer at 1.53, the architect at 1.12, the risk engineer at 0.89 and the red team at 0.87. The spread is 3.26-fold. The security engineer comes top because a universal claim about a mechanism is immune to whatever the model does; the red team comes bottom because an existence proof is a statement about a configuration that no longer exists once the configuration moves.

That ordering should be treated with suspicion, and the lab was built to test it. Reweighting the mix so that checkpoint updates dominate — a reasonable description of a team consuming a fast-moving frontier model — moves 6 of the 7 positions, with Kendall's tau between the two orderings at 0.714. What survives the reweighting is the top and the bottom: GOV, RES, SEC stay in the top three under both weightings and RED, RSK stay in the bottom three. The middle of the table is an artefact of my assumptions and should not be quoted.

A red team finding is the most reliable sentence in the report and the shortest-lived. Both facts follow from the same feature of its logical form.

The practical consequence is a scheduling one rather than a status one. Claims with short half-lives need re-establishing on a cadence tied to change, not to the calendar; claims with long half-lives can be established once and carried. An organisation that red-teams annually and treats the report as current for a year has, on this model, a claim that expired within the first week of the next sprint. An organisation that verifies its gateway invariants after every tool change and never re-runs its red team has the opposite problem.

07What the evidence actually supports

Dijkstra’s remark that testing shows the presence of bugs and never their absence is quoted often enough to have lost its force [99]. It is worth recovering, because it is not a caution about diligence. It is a claim about the logical form of a test result, and it has an exact quantitative expression that reports of security testing almost never use [100].

Start with the cleanest case. A red team runs N independent trials against a deployed configuration and finds nothing. What may be claimed? The exact one-sided upper bound on the true failure rate at 95 per cent confidence is 1 − 0.051/N, which is the Clopper–Pearson interval specialised to a zero numerator [101] and the same arithmetic behind the clinical rule of three [102,103]. Three hundred clean trials bound the rate at 0.9936%. To claim the true rate is under one in a thousand takes 2,995 clean trials; under one in ten thousand, 29,956.

Independence is doing all the work in that formula, and red-team corpora are rarely independent. A corpus of 4,812 prompts generated by mutating 120 seeds has an effective sample size closer to 120 than to 4,812, and the bound moves from 0.062 per cent to 2.466 per cent — a factor of 40 in what the same afternoon's work entitles anyone to say. This is not a criticism of corpus generation, which is how the work scales [104,105]. It is an argument for reporting the seed count alongside the prompt count, which almost no published red-team summary does. The general form of the point — that the probability here is a statement about a state of knowledge, and that the state of knowledge is a count of independent attempts — is standard and routinely ignored in this field [106].

The second limit is the base rate. Put a prompt-injection classifier on the request path with a true-positive rate of 0.95 and a false-positive rate of 0.01 — numbers most vendors would be pleased with — and evaluate it against a population where one request in ten thousand is really an injection. Precision is 0.94 per cent. At two million requests a day that is 19,998 false alerts against 190 true ones. Tightening to a false-positive rate of one in ten thousand lifts precision to 49.98 per cent. This is the base-rate fallacy that Axelsson formalised for intrusion detection in 2000, and it has not become less binding since [107,108].

The engineering consequence is that a classifier is not a control. It is a prioritiser feeding a control, and the control has to be something that holds whether or not the classifier fires — which is exactly why the architectural claims in the register are universal and the detector claims are not. Designs that separate a privileged planner from quarantined data, or that constrain what a tool call may do rather than what a prompt may say, are attempts to convert a statistical claim into a universal one [78,98].

The third limit is evaluation power. Take a safety suite with a refusal rate of 99.0 per cent on the current checkpoint. To have an 80 per cent chance of detecting a drop to 98.5 per cent, at the conventional five per cent level, using an exact binomial test, the suite needs 3,295 prompts. A drop to 98.0 needs 960; a drop from 99.9 to 99.5 needs 1,102. Suites of a few hundred prompts, which is the common size, can reliably detect a fall from 99.0 to 95.0 — 110 prompts suffice for that — and nothing finer.

So an evaluation report that says a new checkpoint shows no regression is, in the usual case, reporting that the regression was smaller than the suite could see. That is a defensible thing to say if it is said that way. It is not a defensible thing to convert into a conditional claim about capability thresholds, which is what happens when the number travels upward through an organisation and loses its interval on the way [109].

08The surface a tool-by-tool review cannot see

The single feature that separates agentic deployments from the chatbots that preceded them is that capabilities compose. A tool that reads HR records is not dangerous. A tool that posts to a webhook is not dangerous. An agent holding both, with a shared context between them, holds a capability that neither tool grants and that no review of either tool would surface.

The lab enumerates this exhaustively for a small, realistic manifest: twelve tools over six resource classes, four of them able to reach the network. Counting a capability as a pair — a resource class read, a sink reached — there are 24 reachable pairs. 4 of them need a single tool call. The other 20, which is 83.3 per cent of the surface, need two, with a shared context in the middle acting as the transfer medium.

The asymmetry in remedies is the point. Reviewing all twelve tools individually removes none of the 24 pairs, because every tool is individually reasonable. Removing two of the four egress-capable tools removes 50 per cent of them. Capability composition is governed by the topology of the manifest, not by the properties of its members, and topology is an architect's object.

This is the structure behind the incidents that have actually happened. EchoLeak, disclosed in June 2025 and assigned CVE-2025-32711 at critical severity, was a zero-click indirect injection in Microsoft 365 Copilot: an attacker's email entered the retrieval context through the ordinary path, its hidden instructions were followed, and data left through a channel the content security policy already permitted. Aim Labs described it as a scope violation rather than a filter failure, which is the correct diagnosis: the classifier, the link redaction and the policy were all present and all irrelevant to a chain that stayed inside permitted edges [110,111,112]. Microsoft patched it server-side and reported no exploitation in the wild, and has published its layered approach to the class since [113]. OWASP's first-quarter round-up for 2026 records the same shape in a different setting: an agent inheriting excessive default permissions through a managed service account, mapping to identity and privilege abuse and tool misuse in the agentic top ten [114,64].

The Five Eyes agencies' joint guidance on agentic services in May 2026 makes the same argument in policy language, and the OWASP agent cheat sheet reduces it to a rule that is easy to state and hard to implement: least agency as well as least privilege — autonomy is a capability that has to be granted deliberately, not a default that comes with the framework [115,116]. Multi-agent arrangements make the topology worse rather than better: the largest annotated study of multi-agent failures locates most of them in specification and inter-agent misalignment rather than in the base models, which is a finding about composition [117]. Nor does a long context window hold everything safely; effective context falls short of advertised context on every model that has been benchmarked [118]. Where the data crossing these boundaries is personal the boundary is also a legal object, and purpose limitation applies to a retrieval corpus exactly as it applies to a database [119]. The supply-chain version of the problem is newer still: OWASP's agentic skills project documents registry-level poisoning of installable agent skills during 2026, which is the composition problem with an untrusted third party supplying one of the components [120].

09Two numbers and what they conceal

A steering committee gets one number for AI risk. Usually it is an expected annual loss: incidents per year times cost per incident. Model that properly — Poisson counts, lognormal severity, 200,000 simulated years — and the point estimate turns out to be almost exactly right and almost useless. The naive product is $1,074,570; the simulated mean is $1,078,656. The median year costs $572,579, 9.0 per cent of years cost nothing at all, the 95th percentile is $3,751,122 and the 99th is $7,663,295 — 13.38 times the median. The worst five per cent of years carry 29.7 per cent of all modelled loss.

The mean is not wrong; it is answering a question nobody asked. A committee deciding whether to hold a control, buy insurance or accept an exposure needs the tail, and the tail is where the modelling assumptions are least constrained by data. That is the honest position for this role: quantification is better than a colour on a heat map because it makes the assumptions explicit and arguable [93,94], and it remains a conditional claim whose conditions are mostly unverified. The banking precedent is instructive here, because model risk management has spent fifteen years on exactly this problem and its answer was governance rather than better models: independent validation, effective challenge, and a documented account of what the model is not for [84].

The governance engineer's number is different in kind and no more comfortable. Evidence has a shelf life. A control carries an attestation valid for some window and is invalidated early by any change that touches it. If the changes arrive as a Poisson process at rate λ per year and the attestation is valid for V days, then at an instant chosen uniformly in the cycle the probability that the evidence still describes the deployed system is (1 − e−L) / L with L = λV/365. For ten controls with plausible windows and change rates, the mean is 22.1 per cent. The worst is the system prompt and policy text at 10.1 per cent, because it is edited most often and attested least often.

The closed form has a consequence worth stating plainly, because it disposes of an argument that comes up in every programme. The function depends only on the product λV. Halving every attestation interval and halving the rate of change produce the identical figure — 38.5 per cent in this configuration. Twice the audit work and half the shipping velocity buy exactly the same amount of evidence currency, which means the choice between them is a business question and not a control question. The third option, which the formula also permits, is to make attestation a function of change rather than of the calendar: attest on the event, and the concept of a stale window disappears.

10Seven titles, how many jobs?

The register of duties is the direct way to answer the question. Twenty- eight duties, drawn from the role descriptions, the NICE competency area, the OWASP and NIST guidance and published postings. Each duty is marked twice: the roles accountable for it in the ordinary case, and the wider set of roles whose skills would let them perform it. 16 of the 28 duties have exactly one accountable role.

On accountability the seven are almost disjoint. Mean pairwise Jaccard overlap is 0.067; 10 of the 21 pairs share no duty at all; the highest overlap in the register is between the safety engineer and the researcher at 0.33, which is what you would expect of two roles that both investigate behaviour to its cause. Because every role solely owns at least one duty, all seven appear in the only minimal covering set — a result that is close to a tautology, and I report it as one.

On capability the picture inverts. 3 roles suffice to cover all 28 duties, in 3 different combinations: SEC + GOV + RES; SEC + RSK + RES; ARC + GOV + RES. The researcher appears in every one of them. The red team engineer and the safety engineer appear in none. That is the sharpest finding in this article and it needs stating carefully: it does not mean those two roles are unnecessary. It means that on the coding used here, every duty either of them holds is one that some other role could plausibly perform, whereas the security engineer's tool-manifest work and the governance engineer's evidence work have no such neighbour. The two most visible titles are the two most absorbable ones.

Why keep them separate, then? For the same reason model risk management separates validation from development, and for the same reason financial audit is not performed by the finance team. A red team that reports to the engineers it is testing produces existence proofs whose non-appearance is uninformative, because the incentive to look hard is the thing under test. A safety engineer who also owns the ship date will not refuse the ship. The separation is not a capability boundary; it is an independence boundary, and independence is a property of reporting lines rather than of skills [84]. This is the one place where the AI-specific literature has less to say than the older disciplines, and it is where most organisations are getting it wrong: they hire for the skill and then place the role where its claims cannot bite.

One more result, and it is a small embarrassment for the taxonomy. Compared on the shape of their claims rather than their content, the safety engineer and the risk engineer are indistinguishable: identical profiles, total-variation distance 0.00, the only such pair in the register. Both sign conditionals with statistical bodies. They differ in the object — one is talking about a checkpoint, the other about a portfolio — and in the audience, and not at all in the epistemic structure of what they say. The most distant pair is the security engineer and the safety engineer at 1.00, which is the whole distance from universal to conditional, and a good argument for not merging those two into a single 'AI security and safety' headcount, which is what job adverts increasingly do.

11Backgrounds, qualifications and credentials

There is no accredited path into any of these roles and there will not be one soon. The US occupational classification does not contain a code for any of them: the 2018 structure has nothing close [121], the 2028 revision is mid-process with a final structure due in 2027 [122], and the workforce framework has deliberately chosen a competency area over a work role [59,60]. What exists instead is a set of feeder disciplines and a credential market that grew very fast between 2024 and 2026.

The feeders, in rough order of how well they transfer:

  • Application and product security transfers best to the security engineer and architect roles. The mental model — untrusted input, trust boundaries, output encoding, least privilege — is the model these systems need, and the main thing to unlearn is the assumption that a parser exists between data and instruction.
  • Offensive security and penetration testing transfers best to the red team role, with the caveat that the work is more linguistic and less binary than the source discipline. The published account from the largest industrial team is explicit that gradient computation is rarely needed and that the human element remains decisive [68].
  • Machine learning engineering and research transfers to the safety engineer and researcher roles, and much less well to the others. The gap to close is adversarial thinking, which is harder to acquire than the mechanics of how a model consumes context.
  • Model risk, internal audit and quantitative risk transfer directly to the risk engineer, and the transfer is underrated. Banking has been doing independent validation of models used in consequential decisions since 2011 [84], and almost every mistake being made in AI risk quantification now was made and corrected there.
  • Privacy, compliance engineering and platform engineering converge on the governance engineer role. The distinguishing skill is the ability to implement a control in a pipeline rather than assert it in a document.

Anthropic-, OpenAI- and DeepMind-style safety work is a partial exception: those teams recruit from ML research and increasingly from evaluation science, and their published frameworks describe an internal function with sign-off authority that has no obvious analogue in a non-frontier enterprise [88,89,91]. Enterprises copying the title without the authority get the artefact and not the function.

On the market itself: the credential boom is real and the demand signal behind it is real, but the published figures come mostly from vendor and aggregator sources and should be treated as indicative. The Stanford index reports AI-specific governance roles growing sharply through 2025 with ownership shifting from analytics functions to dedicated ones, and organisations lacking any responsible-AI policy falling from roughly a quarter to about one in ten [128]. ISC2's own survey has more than a third of security professionals naming AI as the largest skills gap on their teams [130,131]. Survey work on where AI governance sits organisationally suggests ownership is still contested, typically split across two leaders rather than held by one [132] — the pattern that made the chief data officer role unstable when it was new, because too many distinct jobs were bundled into one title [133]. Sectoral regulators have an answer the AI field has not yet adopted: New York’s financial services rule simply requires a named officer, which converts an organisational question into a compliance one [134]. Underneath all of this the demand-side picture remains what CyberSeek has shown for a decade: many more openings than qualified applicants, and titles that mean different things at different employers [135].

12The problem the enterprise actually has

Enterprise AI adoption is generating a specific, repeatable set of problems, and it is worth restating them as claims so that the right role picks them up.

Agent permissions. The recurring failure is an agent that borrows a human's session and therefore holds that human's full entitlement for the duration of a task that only needed a fraction of it. The claim wanted is universal — the agent's credential grants exactly the scope of the current task and expires with it — which makes it a security engineer's mechanism and an architect's design decision, not a policy statement. Least privilege is the old half of it; the new half is least agency, the deliberate grant of autonomy as a separate axis from access [116,64]. Where an approval gate exists, the claim to hold is that no irreversible action can occur without it, and irreversibility has to be enumerated in advance because the agent will not know which of its actions are one-way doors.

Data boundaries. Retrieval systems fail by evaluating entitlement at index time rather than query time, which produces a corpus that is correct on the day it is built and progressively wrong afterwards. The claim wanted is again universal and again a mechanism: retrieval returns only what the caller's own token grants, evaluated on each query. The subtler boundary is the one inside the context window, where retrieved content and system instruction sit in the same undifferentiated buffer. There is no reliable filtering answer to this — the research position is that input filtering alone does not solve indirect injection and that defences evaluated only against static attack sets overstate their protection [29,97] — so the answer has to be structural: untrusted content reaches a component that has no authority, and authority sits in a component that never sees untrusted content [78].

Governance. The failure is not absent policy; it is policy whose evidence is stale, which section nine quantifies. The design response is to bind attestation to change events rather than to the calendar, and to instrument lineage and retention while the system is being built rather than before an audit. The regulatory context is now specific enough to design against: the AI Act's deployer obligations, the Digital Omnibus deferrals, and for frontier developers the Californian reporting regime [49,95,50].

Cost control. This one is usually filed under finance and belongs in the register, because unbounded spend is a denial-of-service condition with an invoice attached. An agent in a retry loop, a prompt that grows without bound through accumulated context, a sub-agent fan-out with no depth limit: each is a resource-exhaustion failure that presents as a bill rather than an outage. The claims that hold it are universal ones a security engineer can enforce at a gateway — a per-task token ceiling, a maximum tool-call depth, a spend limit per principal per window — and they are the same mechanisms that limit the blast radius of a compromised agent. Cost control and containment are the same control surface, and organisations that build them separately build them twice. The published breach economics are the usual argument for spending here [136]; the better argument is that the ceiling is cheap and the incident it prevents is not.

Underneath the four sits a familiar systems-safety point. Tightly coupled, interactively complex systems fail through interactions between components that were each behaving correctly [137]; safety is a control problem at the level of the system rather than a reliability property of its parts [138]; and the capacity to keep working while degraded is designed in rather than inspected in [139]. An agent with tools is a textbook instance of all three.

The pattern across all four is that the durable claims are mechanisms and the perishable ones are observations. An enterprise that wants a defensible position should be spending its architectural attention on the small number of universal claims it can actually enforce, and treating everything its red team, its evaluations and its detectors produce as evidence with a stated expiry date.

Four limitations, in descending order of how much they should worry a reader.

The change weights are stipulated. The half-lives, and therefore the ordering of roles in the survival strip, depend on a mix of change classes I asserted rather than measured. The sensitivity sweep shows the ordering is fragile: 6 of 7 positions move under a different but equally defensible mix. Only the top and the bottom of that ranking should be relied on, and I would withdraw the middle entirely if someone produced deployment telemetry that contradicted it.

The duty matrix encodes my judgements. Which role is accountable for a duty, and which roles are capable of it, are assignments I made from published descriptions and standard artefacts. The capability cover of three roles is the most interesting result here and also the most sensitive to that coding: move two or three capability marks and the answer changes. The matrix is printed in full in Figure 11 precisely so that a reader can disagree with a specific cell.

The accountability cover is a tautology and is reported as one. Because every role solely owns at least one duty, all seven necessarily appear in the minimal cover. That result carries no information beyond the sole-ownership counts, and I have avoided drawing anything from it.

The claim register is a construction, not a sample. The twenty claims were written to be representative of what these roles sign, and they are not drawn from a corpus of real reports. A different twenty would give different half-lives. What I do not think would change is the structural result — that existence proofs are the shortest-lived claims and mechanism claims the longest — because that follows from the logical forms rather than from the selection.

What would change my mind about the article's central argument is a case where a role's usefulness clearly did not track the shape of its claims: a red team whose findings held their value across model replacement, or a governance function whose procedural claims turned out to predict outcomes. Both are imaginable. Neither is what the published record currently shows.

EXPERIMENT 5.  How many prompts to notice that a model got worse
==============================================================================

An evaluation suite of n prompts, refusal rate p0 on the old checkpoint.  How
large must n be before a one-sided exact binomial test at alpha = 0.05 has
80 per cent power to detect a drop to p1?  Computed by exact enumeration of the
binomial tail, no normal approximation.

     p0     p1    drop   n for 80% power   expected misses
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  0.990  0.980   1.00%               960              19.2
  0.990  0.985   0.50%             3,295              49.4
  0.999  0.995   0.40%             1,102               5.5
  0.950  0.930   2.00%               885              61.9
  0.990  0.950   4.00%               110               5.5

A half-point regression from 99.0 to 98.5 per cent needs 3,295 prompts before
it is distinguishable from noise at conventional thresholds.  Most published
safety suites are two to three orders of magnitude smaller than that, which
means an evaluation that "shows no regression" between checkpoints is usually
reporting the width of its own confidence interval.
