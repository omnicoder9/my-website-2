01What question does this answer?

There is no shortage of AI security frameworks. There is a shortage of people who can say, out loud and without hedging, what any particular one is for. An organisation that has adopted the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, certified against ISO/IEC 42001, mapped its threat model to MITRE ATLAS, and put the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications on a slide has not thereby done four versions of the same thing. It has done four different things, only one of which produces a testable claim about whether its systems can be attacked.

The confusion is understandable, because the documents look alike. They arrive as PDFs with numbered sections, control identifiers, and lifecycle diagrams. They cite each other. Vendors sell products against all of them indiscriminately. But underneath the shared format, each document answers exactly one of four questions, and the answer to one is never an answer to another.

What can go wrong? This is the threat layer: MITRE ATLAS, the NIST adversarial machine learning taxonomy, the OWASP Top 10 lists, Google's SAIF risk map. These are enumerations of adversary behaviour. They are descriptive. Nothing in them can be passed or failed.

What must be verifiably true? This is the control layer: the OWASP Artificial Intelligence Security Verification Standard, NIST's control overlays for AI systems, the secure-development profile in SP 800-218A. Every entry is a claim about a system that an assessor can retest. These documents are useless for understanding your adversary and excellent for arguing with an auditor.

What must be managed, and by whom? This is the governance layer: ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, the Cyber AI Profile. They allocate responsibility, mandate records, and define review cycles. They produce evidence about an organisation. They are close to silent about whether any particular control works.

What is legally required? This is the obligation layer: the EU AI Act, a churning set of US state statutes, sector rules, procurement clauses. Unlike everything above it, this layer has penalties. It is also, characteristically, the layer least willing to tell you how to comply.

This article walks the landscape one layer at a time — history, structure, what each document actually contains, and where the seams are. It ends with a small piece of runnable code that models a six-system portfolio against a twenty-six-threat corpus, and the results are uncomfortable in a specific way: an organisation can hold a passing management-system certificate while seven of those twenty-six threats have no implemented control behind them at all.

Two framings are worth fixing before we start. The first is that security of AI and AI for security are different subjects that these documents increasingly bundle. NIST's Cyber AI Profile splits the bundle into three explicitly: securing AI systems, using AI for cyber defence, and defending against adversaries who use AI[1,2]. Canada's Cyber Centre orders its ten actions the same way, and puts adversarial use of AI first, before attacks on the AI you run[3]. That ordering is a claim about where the losses currently are, and it is worth taking seriously.

The second framing is that this field's foundational technical document says, in its own executive summary, that the problem is not solved. NIST's adversarial machine learning taxonomy pairs every attack class with mitigations and then discusses the limitations of those mitigations, because for several classes no mitigation is complete[4,5]. Every control standard downstream of it inherits that admission whether or not it repeats it.

02Before there were frameworks

The research literature on attacking machine learning ran roughly two decades ahead of the compliance literature, and the gap explains a good deal about why the frameworks look the way they do.

The recognisable starting point is 2004, when Dalvi and colleagues modelled spam filtering as a game between a classifier and an adversary who could modify inputs at test time — the first formal treatment of classification under adversarial pressure[6]. Two years later Barreno and colleagues asked the question in its general form, can machine learning be secure?, and produced the taxonomy that most later ones descend from: attacks distinguished by their influence on the learner, the security property they violate, and their specificity[7]. Both papers predate deep learning entirely.

The field acquired its emblem in 2013. Biggio and colleagues demonstrated gradient-based evasion against detectors at test time[8], and Szegedy and colleagues reported that imperceptible perturbations reliably flipped the predictions of neural networks that were otherwise highly accurate[9]. Goodfellow, Shlens and Szegedy followed with the linear explanation and the fast gradient sign method, which made adversarial examples cheap enough to be everyone's problem[10]. By 2017 the attack surface had widened past inference: BadNets showed that a supply-chain compromise could plant a backdoor triggered by a specific pattern while leaving benchmark accuracy untouched[11], and membership inference showed that a model's outputs leak facts about who was in its training set[12].

What is striking in retrospect is how little of this reached practice. Biggio and Roli's 2018 retrospective observed that the security community and the machine learning community had been rediscovering each other's results for a decade[13]. Papernot and colleagues' systematisation the same year noted that almost no deployed system treated its model as an attack surface[14].

The event that changed the conversation was not a paper. In March 2016 Microsoft launched a conversational bot on Twitter and withdrew it within a day after coordinated users taught it to produce racist output; the company's public post-mortem framed it as a lesson about adversarial interaction rather than a model defect[15]. It was the first time a mainstream organisation had to explain in public that its AI system had been attacked through its intended interface, and that the intended interface was the vulnerability.

03The taxonomy years, 2021 to 2023

Institutions responded first with taxonomies, which is what institutions do when a field is moving faster than they can write requirements for it.

MITRE launched ATLAS — Adversarial Threat Landscape for Artificial-Intelligence Systems — in 2021, deliberately modelled on ATT&CK so that anyone who could read one matrix could read the other[16,17]. ENISA published a multilayer framework of good cybersecurity practices for AI in June 2023[18]. The OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications began the same year as a grassroots effort to catalogue prompt injection and its relatives, which the web application Top 10 did not address[19].

Then, in a remarkable eleven months, the governance layer arrived more or less at once. NIST released the AI Risk Management Framework in January 2023, built on four functions — govern, map, measure, manage — and explicitly voluntary[20,21]. In November, CISA and the UK's NCSC published Guidelines for Secure AI System Development, co-sealed by twenty-three organisations across eighteen countries, structured around four lifecycle stages: secure design, secure development, secure deployment, and secure operation and maintenance[22,23,24]. In December, ISO and IEC published ISO/IEC 42001, the first certifiable management system standard for artificial intelligence[25,26].

None of these documents contained a requirement you could fail a penetration test against. All of them contained a structure you could organise a programme around. That was the trade the period made, and it was probably the right one: the threat surface was moving too quickly for anyone to write durable technical requirements, and the organisational surface was not moving at all.

04The agentic turn, 2024 to 2026

The second wave was driven by a change in what AI systems do. A model that answers questions has a bounded blast radius. A model that holds credentials, invokes tools, retains memory across sessions and initiates actions in other systems does not.

The documents track the shift almost month by month. NIST's Generative AI Profile arrived in July 2024 alongside SP 800-218A, a secure-development community profile for generative AI and dual-use foundation models[27,28]. The 2025 edition of NIST's adversarial ML taxonomy, published in March 2025, extended a report that had previously been about predictive systems to cover generative ones, adding supply chain attacks, direct and indirect prompt injection, misuse violations, and a section on the security of AI agents[4,5,29]. OWASP's Agentic Security Initiative published a Top 10 for Agentic Applications in December 2025, with ten risk categories prefixed ASI and running from agent goal hijack to rogue agents[30,31].

In 2026 the pace accelerated further. On 1 May, six national cybersecurity agencies — CISA, the NSA, and their counterparts in Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the United Kingdom — jointly published Careful Adoption of Agentic AI Services, the first coordinated Five Eyes guidance aimed specifically at autonomous agents[32,33,34]. On 24 June, OWASP released version 1.0 of the Artificial Intelligence Security Verification Standard, the first widely adopted document in this field consisting entirely of testable requirements[35,36]. On 4 August, the 2026 edition of the LLM Top 10 became the first in its three-year history to check the community vote against a corpus of documented incidents[37,38].

That last date is five days before this article was written, which is the correct way to understand this landscape: it is not settled, and any document you rely on should be checked for a newer edition before you cite it in an assessment.

05NIST publishes a family, not a framework

The most common error in this space is to speak of "the NIST AI framework" as though it were one document. NIST's approach has been the opposite: rather than build a standalone AI security framework, it overlays AI-specific considerations onto instruments that organisations already operate[2].

The structure matters practically. If your organisation already runs a CSF-based programme, the Cyber AI Profile is an extension of it rather than a replacement[2]. If you already select controls from SP 800-53, the COSAiS overlays tailor that catalogue rather than introducing a parallel one[39]. If you already follow the SSDF, SP 800-218A adds AI-specific practices to the lifecycle you have[28]. NIST's own framing is that CSF 2.0, the AI RMF and the Cyber AI Profile are meant to be used together[2].

06The AI Risk Management Framework and its profiles

AI RMF 1.0, released in January 2023, is a governance instrument. Its four core functions — govern, map, measure, manage — decompose into categories and subcategories describing outcomes rather than controls[20,40]. It is voluntary, and its influence has come from adoption rather than obligation: it has become the de facto vocabulary for AI risk in the United States, and at least one state statute treats substantial compliance with it as a safe harbour[40,41].

The Generative AI Profile, NIST AI 600-1, was published on 26 July 2024 in response to Executive Order 14110[27,21]. A profile in NIST's sense is a prioritised selection from the framework core for a particular context, and this one identifies twelve generative-AI risk categories — including confabulation, information integrity, information security, data privacy, harmful bias and homogenisation, intellectual property, and value chain and component integration — with more than two hundred suggested actions organised by RMF function[42,43].

What AI 600-1 is not is a security control set. Its information security category names risks; it does not tell you how to verify that your retrieval pipeline treats retrieved documents as data rather than instructions. That is deliberate. The AI RMF addresses trustworthiness considerations — validity, safety, accountability, transparency, explainability, fairness — that sit outside the scope of security and privacy controls entirely[44].

07NIST AI 100-2e2025: the vocabulary everything else borrows

If one document in this field deserves to be read cover to cover by engineers rather than skimmed by compliance teams, it is the adversarial machine learning taxonomy, published in final form in March 2025[4,45].

Its contribution is a conceptual hierarchy rather than a list. Attacks are located along several axes at once: the type of system targeted, the lifecycle stage at which the attack occurs, the attacker's goal in terms of which property is violated, the attacker's capabilities and access, and the attacker's knowledge of the learning process[5]. For predictive systems the taxonomy covers evasion, poisoning and privacy attacks; for generative systems it extends to supply chain attacks, direct and indirect prompt injection, misuse violations, and the security of AI agents[5,45].

Two things make this document unusually honest. The first is that it documents real-world consequences rather than staying in the laboratory — autonomous vehicles induced to cross into oncoming lanes, stop signs classified as speed limit signs[5]. The second is its treatment of mitigations. Each attack class is paired with mitigation strategies and an assessment of their limitations, and the report is explicit that current techniques cannot fully prevent all attacker techniques[5,46]. That is a strange sentence to find in a national standards document, and it is the correct one.

The taxonomy's influence runs downstream through the whole NIST family: the COSAiS overlays draw their attack model from it, alongside SP 800-218A, draft AI 800-1 and the SP 800-53 catalogue itself[47,44].

08The Cyber AI Profile: NIST IR 8596

NIST released a preliminary draft of the Cybersecurity Framework Profile for Artificial Intelligence on 16 December 2025[48,49]. It is the clearest statement so far of how a mature cybersecurity programme should absorb AI, and its structure is the interesting part.

The profile applies CSF 2.0's six functions — govern, identify, protect, detect, respond, recover — across three focus areas[2]:

  • Secure: securing the AI systems the organisation builds, buys or uses.
  • Defend: using AI to conduct cyber defence.
  • Thwart: defending against adversaries who use AI against you.

The draft's scope of "AI" is deliberately broad, covering stand-alone systems and applications as well as infrastructure incorporating large language models, predictive analytics, generative AI, agentic AI and search[2]. Like CSF 2.0 itself, it does not prescribe how to achieve an outcome; it supplies informative references and assigns each subcategory a suggested priority — high, moderate, or foundational[48].

Three details are worth noting. First, the profile explicitly recognises AI as a cybersecurity governance concern rather than a purely technical one[2]. Second, its informative references reach outside government, citing technical papers, ENISA's threat landscape work, NIST SP 800-218, and at least one vendor framework[48]. Third — and this is the part most likely to matter — although the profile is voluntary, it is the kind of document regulators and litigants reach for when they need a benchmark for reasonable cybersecurity diligence[2].

NIST's development process for the profile ran through public workshops, and a separate but related discussion draft of the control overlays was released to feed the second of these in January 2026[47,2].

09COSAiS: overlays on the control catalogue

The Control Overlays for Securing AI Systems project is the piece of the NIST family that will matter most to engineers, and it is the least finished. NIST launched it in July 2025 and released a concept paper for comment on 14 August 2025, with the resulting publications anticipated under the NIST IR 8605 designation[47,50].

An overlay, in SP 800-53 terms, is a tailoring of the control catalogue for a specific technology, mission or operating environment. The premise of COSAiS is that the security of an AI system is inseparable from the security of the infrastructure it runs on, that many organisations already implement SP 800-53 controls and have processes for assessing them, and that AI-specific risk is therefore better handled by customising that catalogue than by inventing a parallel one[39].

The concept paper proposes a library of overlays for distinct use cases — generative AI, predictive AI, AI agents, and AI developers — which organisations can apply individually or in combination[44,51]. Two design decisions are worth flagging. The overlays are explicitly not a comprehensive control set for an enterprise; they assume organisation-wide policies, access control for datasets and services, and account management are already in place[44]. And the attacks and mitigations informing the tailoring are drawn from AI 100-2e2025, with SP 800-218A and draft AI 800-1 also feeding in[44,47].

As of January 2026 an annotated outline of the predictive AI overlay was circulating as a discussion draft ahead of an initial public draft, with feedback requested by 13 February 2026[50,47]. Anyone planning a control baseline for AI systems in a federal or federal-adjacent context should be tracking this series rather than waiting for it.

10ISO/IEC 42001: the certifiable one

ISO/IEC 42001:2023, published in December 2023, is the first international standard specifying an artificial intelligence management system, and its distinguishing property is that an accredited body can audit you against it and issue a certificate[25,52].

Structurally it is a conventional management system standard. It uses the harmonised high-level structure shared with ISO 9001, ISO 27001 and ISO 14001: clauses 4 to 10 cover organisational context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation and improvement, arranged as a plan-do-check-act loop[26,53,54]. Annex A supplies a reference library of thirty-eight AI-specific controls grouped under nine control objectives, from AI policy and internal organisation through impact assessment, system lifecycle, data, information for interested parties, responsible use, and third-party relationships[55,52,26].

The standard does not require every control. It requires a risk assessment and an impact assessment, and then controls proportionate to what those assessments found — with the Statement of Applicability recording the reasoning[26,55].

Its commercial significance now exceeds its technical significance. It is voluntary, but it is increasingly requested in procurement, particularly by government buyers and enterprise customers assessing AI supply-chain risk[52]. Major cloud providers have pursued certification and publish their status[25].

What it does not do is tell you whether your system is secure. The Annex A controls are governance and accountability controls, not security controls[52]. OWASP's AISVS puts this boundary in writing from the other side, listing AI governance and risk management — impact assessments, fairness documentation, model cards, transparency reports, risk-management process design — as explicitly out of scope and defined by ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 23894 and the NIST AI RMF instead[36]. Two standards agreeing on where the line falls is about as good as this field gets.

11The EU AI Act: the layer with teeth

Everything above this point is voluntary. The EU AI Act is not, and that single fact reorganises how most large organisations actually behave.

The Act classifies systems by risk. Practices deemed unacceptable are prohibited outright. High-risk systems — split between stand-alone use cases listed in Annex III and AI embedded as a safety component of products already regulated under EU product law in Annex I — carry the heavy regime: risk management systems, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, conformity assessment and registration[56]. Article 50 imposes transparency duties on a wider set of systems, and general-purpose AI model providers have their own obligations, in force since 2 August 2025[57,58].

The timeline has moved, and the way it moved is instructive. The European Commission proposed a Digital Omnibus on 19 November 2025, principally to defer the high-risk compliance deadline, because the implementation machinery — national competent authorities, harmonised standards, compliance tooling — was not ready[59,57]. After trilogues that failed and then succeeded, the Parliament and Council reached political agreement on 7 May 2026; Parliament formally endorsed the package on 16 June 2026 and the Council gave its final approval on 29 June 2026[60,59].

The outcome: high-risk obligations for stand-alone Annex III systems move from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and for AI embedded in Annex I regulated products to 2 August 2028[61,62]. The final position dropped the earlier proposal to make application conditional on the completion of harmonised standards, replacing it with fixed dates[62].

It is easy to overstate the relief. Three things did not move. The Article 4 AI literacy duty has applied since 2 February 2025. The Article 50 transparency obligations still take effect on 2 August 2026. The GPAI obligations remain in force, with only a documentation grace period for models already on the market[56,57,58]. The Omnibus also added prohibitions — on AI generating non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material — effective 2 December 2026[61,58]. And the penalty ceilings were untouched: up to €35 million or 7% of global turnover for prohibited practices, and up to €15 million or 3% for other obligations including high-risk and transparency duties[58].

For a security engineer the practical reading is narrow but real. The Act does not specify controls. It specifies duties — risk management, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness — and leaves the means to harmonised standards that were, as the deferral concedes, not finished. In the gap, organisations reach for whatever produces defensible evidence, which is exactly why documents in the layers above have acquired quasi-regulatory weight they were never designed to carry.

12Canada's ten actions: the most usable single page

The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security's Top 10 artificial intelligence security actions: a primer (ITSAP.10.049) is the shortest genuinely useful document in this landscape. It is explicitly positioned as an addition to the Centre's existing top ten IT security actions rather than a replacement, on the reasoning that AI security fails first where ordinary security fails[3,63].

The Centre followed this with edge-specific guidance organised around the same three pillars, and in June 2026 issued a statement urging Canadian organisations to act on risks from frontier AI models, following a joint Five Eyes statement[64,65].

13Five Eyes: careful adoption of agentic AI

On 1 May 2026, CISA, the NSA, Australia's ACSC, the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security, the UK's NCSC and New Zealand's NCSC jointly released Careful Adoption of Agentic AI Services — the first time these six agencies had coordinated on guidance aimed specifically at autonomous agents rather than generative AI broadly[32,33,66].

The guide is directed at developers, vendors and operators, and addresses the risks of deploying agentic systems particularly in critical infrastructure and defence settings[34,32]. It organises its analysis into five categories of risk. Secondary summaries render the category names slightly differently, but the substance is consistent across them: privilege compromise, design and configuration failures, behavioural misalignment, structural or cascading failure, and a fifth concerning accountability and supply chain[67,68,69].

Prompt injection receives specific emphasis as a mechanism that drives both behavioural misalignment and privilege escalation: an attacker who embeds instructions in data the agent reads can redirect its objectives while it continues to operate with the permissions it was given[67].

The recommendations that follow are notably conservative. Avoid granting broad or unrestricted access, especially to sensitive data or critical systems. Begin with low-risk, non-sensitive use cases[32]. The guidance expects each agent to carry a verified, cryptographically anchored identity with short-lived credentials, and operators to maintain traceability for every decision and action an agent takes[66,70].

That last expectation is where the guidance bites hardest, because it is the one most organisations cannot currently meet. Industry survey data circulated alongside the guidance suggested that a majority of organisations could not enforce purpose limitations on AI agents, quickly terminate a misbehaving agent, or isolate AI systems from broader network access[70]. Guidance of this kind does not create obligations directly, but six agencies publishing jointly converts "best practice" into something auditors cite and litigants attach to discovery requests[70].

14The United States, minus a federal statute

The US has no comprehensive federal AI statute. What it has is a patchwork of state law operating against an active federal preemption push, and the patchwork changes fast enough that any snapshot needs a date attached. This one is August 2026.

Several significant state laws took effect on 1 January 2026. California's SB 53, the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, requires developers of the largest models to publish risk frameworks, report critical safety incidents, and provide whistleblower protections, with penalties up to $1 million per violation[71]. California's AB 2013 requires training-data disclosure far more broadly. Texas's Responsible AI Governance Act took effect the same day, narrowed substantially during passage to focus on categorical prohibitions and government use, with a regulatory sandbox and an AI council[72,73]. Illinois's HB 3773 addresses AI in employment[74].

Colorado is the instructive case. SB 24-205, passed in May 2024, was the first comprehensive state regime for high-risk AI in consequential decisions, imposing duties on developers and deployers around risk management, impact assessments and disclosures[75,76]. Its effective date was delayed from February to June 2026, and then the legislature repealed and replaced it: SB 26-189 was signed on 14 May 2026, substituting a narrower automated-decision-technology regime with substantive obligations commencing in 2027[73,41]. The original act never took effect.

Layered over this is a federal preemption effort. An executive order signed on 11 December 2025 proposed preempting state AI laws deemed inconsistent with federal policy, named Colorado's act as an example of excessive state regulation, carved out child safety, compute and data-centre infrastructure, and state procurement, and was accompanied by a Department of Justice litigation task force[77,76,73]. As of mid-2026, no federal statute or court decision had actually preempted or paused a state AI law[73,78].

For practitioners the useful move is not to track statutes but to build one inventory and one risk programme and map obligations onto it — the overlap across regimes on impact assessments, disclosure and risk-management policy is substantial, and at least one state grants explicit safe-harbour status to substantial compliance with the NIST AI RMF[79,41].

15MITRE ATLAS

ATLAS is a living knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques against AI-enabled systems, built on real-world attack observations and demonstrations from AI red teams[17]. Launched in 2021, it borrows ATT&CK's matrix structure and lifecycle framing, and most of its tactic names, so that a SOC analyst can read it on day one[80,16].

Its two genuinely AI-specific tactics are AI Model Access and AI Attack Staging; the November 2025 v5.1.0 release also added a Command and Control tactic[80]. As of that release the knowledge base contained sixteen tactics, eighty-four techniques, fifty-six sub-techniques, thirty-two mitigations and forty-two documented case studies[81].

The recent growth has been overwhelmingly agentic. A spring 2025 release expanded generative-AI coverage with techniques including retrieval-augmentation poisoning and prompt crafting[82]. A late-2025 collaboration added agent-focused techniques covering context and memory poisoning, agent configuration tampering, credential harvesting, and exfiltration via agent tool invocation[80]. By early 2026 real-world exploitation of agentic platforms was driving further technique contributions[83].

What ATLAS is not is a control framework. It is the threat side of the house, and it pairs with the NIST AI RMF, the OWASP lists and ISO/IEC 42001 rather than replacing any of them[80]. Its practical value is in threat modelling, red-team scoping and detection engineering: a substantial share of its mitigations map to security controls organisations already run, which is what makes it integrable with existing SOC workflows rather than a parallel universe[81].

One subtlety matters for coverage work. A single OWASP category can map to several ATLAS techniques at different tactic stages — prompt injection, for example, involves both the staging activity of crafting adversarial content and the injection itself. A testing programme that treats such a category as one technique will systematically miss the staging half[84].

16OWASP draws a boundary: component or actor

OWASP's GenAI Security Project now maintains three flagship documents, and the most useful thing the 2026 edition did was state where each one stops.

The Top 10 for LLM Applications covers risk when the model is a component inside an application: it receives input and produces output, and nothing it says reaches the world except through code someone wrote. The Top 10 for Agentic Applications, first published on 9 December 2025, covers risk when the model becomes an actor — with tools it can invoke, memory it carries between sessions, and consequences it sets in motion downstream. The two are complementary, and the authors say plainly that neither covers the picture alone[85,30].

The LLM Top 10, 2026 edition

The 2025 edition, version 2.0, was published on 18 November 2024 and reorganised the original 2023 list around how LLM applications are actually built and attacked, adding vector and embedding weaknesses and splitting several overlapping categories[86,19].

The 2026 edition, released during Black Hat week in early August 2026, changed the methodology rather than the format[87,37]. Every prior edition rested entirely on practitioner consensus. This one checked that consensus against a corpus of 7,714 documented LLM-related incidents drawn from public vulnerability databases and an AI-harm database, of which 6,639 carried enough detail to classify. The weighting was deliberately conservative — the community vote kept three quarters, the incident data one quarter — but a quarter was enough to move an entry a tier where belief and evidence diverged sharply[85,38].

Two divergences are worth dwelling on, because they are the most useful findings in the document.

The first is prompt injection. Practitioners voted it first; the raw incident record alone would have dropped it out of the top ten entirely. The authors attribute this to a defence effect: prompt injection is the oldest and best-known LLM vulnerability class, mature teams fight it continuously, and meaningful mitigations exist, so fewer clean exploits reach public databases. The low count measures defensive spend, not shrinking risk — and meanwhile the realistic attack surface is expanding through cross-modal injection, every new tool connection, and injections written into persistent memory or a retrieval corpus that taint every later interaction reading from that store[85].

The second is the mirror image. Practitioners placed misinformation near the bottom; the incident record placed it near the top, the widest gap in the list and in the more dangerous direction. The 2025 framing treated the risk as users over-trusting fluent output. That framing is now too narrow: model outputs drive tool calls, generate production code, authorise actions and coordinate other agents, so a wrong answer becomes a wrong action in a downstream system[85].

Structurally, the 2026 edition also replaced the per-entry framework links that appeared throughout the 2025 edition with a single version-pinned appendix mapping all ten risks to nine external frameworks — MITRE ATLAS, ATT&CK and CWE, NIST AI 600-1 and the AI RMF, the OWASP Agentic Top 10, OWASP GenAI Data Security, the CSA AI Controls Matrix, and the OWASP AI Vulnerability Scoring System — with every mapping drawn from a pinned version so the references do not drift[85]. For anyone maintaining a crosswalk, that is the most immediately useful change in the document.

The Agentic Top 10

The agentic list uses the prefix ASI, for Agentic Security Initiative, and runs ASI01 through ASI10: agent goal hijack, tool misuse and exploitation, agent identity and privilege abuse, agentic supply chain compromise, unexpected code execution, memory and context poisoning, insecure inter-agent communication, cascading agent failures, human-agent trust exploitation, and rogue agents[31,88,89].

It was developed with more than a hundred contributors and built from incidents observed in real systems rather than research projections, which is why the entries read like a news summary of the preceding year[30,90]. The recurring theme across all ten categories is that agent security cannot live inside the agent: identity, per-action authorisation, in-band content inspection, resource governance and tamper-evident audit are all control-plane properties[89].

17AISVS: the first document you can fail

OWASP published version 1.0 of the Artificial Intelligence Security Verification Standard on 24 June 2026, at Global AppSec Vienna[35,91]. It is an open catalogue of testable security requirements for AI-enabled systems, modelled on the long-running Application Security Verification Standard, and it fills a gap the other documents left open by design: technical, verifiable controls at the AI layer[35,92].

The design commitment is visible in the grammar. Every requirement begins with the words Verify that, which the standard says is deliberate, to emphasise testability[36]. Each requirement carries a unique identifier and a level, and requirements are grouped into chapters by control family: training data integrity, input validation, model lifecycle, infrastructure, access control, supply chain, model behaviour, memory and vector databases, orchestration and agentic action, tool-protocol security, adversarial robustness, and detection and logging. Three appendices support the core — a glossary, a cross-cutting inventory of every defence technique mapped back to requirement identifiers, and controls for the safe use of AI coding tools during development[36]. Published accounts of the release put the requirement count around five hundred; secondary summaries differ on the chapter count, and the standard's own text describes twelve requirement chapters[93,36].

The levels

AISVS defines three ascending verification levels, and the framing is more careful than most level schemes[36]:

  • Level 1 holds the most critical and foundational requirements, focused on attacks that do not depend on other preconditions. Most Level 1 controls are either straightforward to implement or important enough to justify the effort.
  • Level 2 addresses more advanced or less common attacks, and layered defences against widespread ones. These requirements may involve more complex logic or target specific attack prerequisites.
  • Level 3 holds controls that are harder to implement or situational — defence-in-depth mechanisms and mitigations for niche, targeted or high-complexity attacks.

The important detail is what the levels are indexed to. They are not a maturity ladder. They are aligned with ASVS levels and are intended to be applied at the matching level: verifying an application against AISVS Level N assumes it has also been verified against ASVS Level N[36]. Level 1 is the baseline for any AI application handling untrusted input or data of any sensitivity; Level 2 for sensitive business data, regulated data, or adversarial operating contexts; Level 3 for high-assurance applications — life-safety decisions, critical infrastructure, highly sensitive personal data[36].

What it refuses to cover

AISVS is unusually explicit about its own boundaries, and the exclusions are the most useful part of the document for anyone assembling a programme. It defines only requirements specific to AI and ML systems, or general controls with AI-specific nuances worth restating. It assumes the underlying application, infrastructure and organisational practices are already verified against general-purpose standards. Out of scope, and named as belonging elsewhere: general application security (ASVS), general software supply chain security (SCVS, SLSA, CIS Controls), infrastructure and platform hardening (CIS Benchmarks, SP 800-53, SP 800-190, CSF), general data protection operations (ASVS, ISO/IEC 27001, applicable privacy law), general logging and monitoring, and AI governance and risk management (ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 23894, NIST AI RMF)[36].

It is also vendor-neutral by policy: it specifies what to verify, not which product to use[36,92].

Chapters are control families, not threats

One structural consequence deserves emphasis, because it is where most coverage arguments go wrong. AISVS organises by control family rather than by attack or component. Defending against a given threat therefore requires requirements from several chapters at once — the standard's own example is that prompt injection in an agentic application combines input validation, model behaviour, orchestration, tool-protocol, robustness and logging requirements[36]. The appendix cross-referencing defence techniques to requirement identifiers exists precisely because no single chapter is a threat's home.

18Vendor frameworks, and why they are not automatically suspect

Vendor-published frameworks carry an obvious conflict of interest and are worth reading anyway, because vendors see incident data that standards bodies do not.

Google SAIF

The Secure AI Framework is a conceptual framework built around six core elements, presented as a way to make AI systems secure by default[94,95]. Its more useful artefact is the SAIF Map, which divides the picture into four component areas — data, infrastructure, model and application — and locates each risk on the development path[96].

That introduced-versus-exposed distinction is the map's real contribution. Data poisoning is introduced early, during ingestion and processing, and exposed later through model behaviour; model source tampering is introduced where code, frameworks or weights are insufficiently hardened against supply-chain attack and exposed either at the source or through modified behaviour in use[97]. The map also makes explicit the observation that in AI development, data takes on a role previously played by code, which is why attacks on model weights can be as damaging as attacks on source[98]. Google contributed the risk assessment, map and associated descriptions to the Coalition for Secure AI, and has extended the framework to agents[96,99].

Cisco's integrated framework

Cisco published an Integrated AI Security and Safety Framework in December 2025, and its motivating argument is the one this article opened with. Existing frameworks — MITRE ATLAS, the NIST adversarial ML taxonomy, the OWASP Top 10 lists — each cover a slice of a multi-dimensional space, leaving organisations to piece guidance together from disparate sources[100,101].

The framework's response is a single lifecycle-aware taxonomy with two components: technical threats to AI systems, including LLMs, agentic systems and the AI supply chain from data pipelines through model artefacts to deployment environments; and harmful or toxic content that users might input or systems might generate[100]. Merging security and safety in one taxonomy is the deliberate move — a security attack such as injecting instructions or corrupting training data often culminates in a safety failure such as harmful output or leaked confidential information[102]. It carries embedded sub-taxonomies for tool-protocol, agentic and supply-chain threats, on the reasoning that protocols governing how models interpret tools, prompts and metadata become an attack surface when their components are tampered with or impersonated[100,102].

The ADG framework

EC-Council Global Services publishes ADG — adopt, defend, govern — a three-pillar operating model that positions itself explicitly as a consumer of the threat catalogues rather than a competitor to them. Its stated design has governance surfaces inheriting detection rules, evaluation scenarios and runtime guardrails from published threat catalogues, with the resulting controls mapped onto the NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001[103,104]. It also carries a four-class harm taxonomy intended to cover harms beyond security vulnerabilities, and coverage beyond LLMs into diffusion, multi-modal and composite systems[103].

Read structurally, ADG is a governance-layer document that has solved the layering problem by declaring it: threat catalogues in, controls out, mapped to the governance standards a board already recognises. Whether the mapping holds is an empirical question about a specific implementation, which is true of every crosswalk in this field.

19The crosswalk problem

Every organisation operating more than one of these documents eventually builds a crosswalk: a matrix mapping the categories of one framework onto the controls of another. Crosswalks are necessary and they are also where most assurance arguments quietly break.

Three failure modes recur.

Version drift. A mapping built against one edition of a framework decays as both sides revise. The 2026 LLM Top 10 addressed this directly by replacing scattered per-entry links with a single appendix in which every mapping is drawn from a pinned framework version and cited in a sources section[85]. That is the right pattern, and it is rare.

Cardinality collapse. Crosswalks are usually drawn as one-to-one because tables are easier to read that way, but the underlying relationships are many-to-many. One OWASP category maps to several ATLAS techniques across different tactic stages[84]; one threat requires requirements from several AISVS chapters at once[36]. A one-to-one table records the strongest link and silently drops the rest.

Layer confusion. The most consequential failure is mapping across layers as though the mapping were an equivalence. A governance control that requires you to consider data provenance is not the same object as a requirement that data provenance is recorded and testable, which is in turn not the same object as a statutory duty to document training data. Each may appear in a crosswalk row together. Only one of them can be failed by a test.

The following section makes that last point quantitatively.

20A lab: what the layers actually do to a portfolio

The code below models a fictional organisation — Halcyon Grid, an energy utility running six AI systems — against a corpus of twenty-six abstracted failure modes, a set of thirty-three verification requirements across twelve chapters and three levels, and twelve management-system controls. It is stdlib-only Python and it runs in under a second. It is not a compliance tool; the corpora are abstractions of the shape of published taxonomies rather than reproductions of them. The point is the structure of the result, not the specific integers.

One modelling decision drives most of the findings and is worth stating plainly: control efficacies compose multiplicatively. Two controls that each remove 40% of a threat leave 36%, not 20%. Nothing reaches zero. That is the whole argument for defence in depth and simultaneously its ceiling, and it matches what the NIST taxonomy says about the limits of current mitigations[5,46].

E1 — no catalogue names the whole corpus

The first experiment asks a simple question: for each published threat catalogue, what share of the twenty-six failure modes does it carry a category for?

The integrated vendor framework scores highest at 85%, which is unsurprising and not a criticism: it was explicitly designed as an integration of the others, so scoring well on a union-style test is what success looks like for it. Below it, coverage falls quickly — 50% for the adversarial-ML matrix, 42% each for the two OWASP lists, 38% for the AML taxonomy, 31% for the vendor risk map. Two catalogues suffice to reach the union; one never does.

The most interesting cell is the empty one. Exactly one failure mode in the corpus is named by no threat catalogue at all: shadow AI outside the inventory. That is not an oversight. It is not a technique, and no adversary performs it. It is an organisational fact, and it lives one layer up — which is precisely where Canada's Cyber Centre puts it, under mapping sanctioned and unsanctioned models on the network and governing their use by policy[3].

E2 — one threat, many requirements

The second experiment measures fan-out: how many verification requirements, across how many chapters, actually bear on a single threat.

  threat                              reqs  chapters  residual
  Indirect prompt injection via RAG      6         5    12.5%
  Agent goal hijack                      4         3    28.8%
  Malicious model from public hub        4         3    17.1%
  Unbounded consumption / cost abuse     1         1    45.0%

  mean requirements per addressed threat : 2.69
  threats needing exactly one requirement: 6 of 26
  chapters touched by indirect injection : C2, C7, C9, C11, C12

Indirect prompt injection needs six requirements spread across five chapters, and even with all six in place a 12.5% residual remains. This is the concrete form of the point the standard makes in prose: a threat has no home chapter[36]. Any programme that assigns "prompt injection" to one team and one control has mis-modelled the problem before it has started.

Note the contrast with unbounded consumption, which has exactly one requirement bearing on it and a 45% residual as a result. Six of the twenty-six threats are in that position. Single-control threats are the ones where a crosswalk drawn one-to-one looks accurate and is nonetheless the whole story — which is a different and more dangerous kind of correct.

The third experiment prices the levels.

The shape is the familiar one, and it justifies the standard's own advice that organisations may begin at Level 1 and adopt higher levels as exposure increases rather than treating Level 3 as a goal[36]. The second step returns about two thirds as much per engineer-day as the first.

The line that matters most is the last one. At full Level 3 implementation the mean residual across the corpus is 33.9%, and nineteen of twenty-six threats remain above a 25% residual. The worst are evasion at 55%, vector-store inversion at 50%, and unbounded consumption, insecure inter-agent messaging and cross-modal injection at 45% each. A complete verification programme does not produce a secure system. It produces a system whose residual risk you can name.

E4 — the conformity gap

The fourth experiment is the one worth showing to a board. Halcyon has done what most organisations do first: produced every artefact the management system asks for, and implemented the straightforward technical requirements.

  management-system controls with an artefact on file : 12/12  -> certification: PASS
  Level 1 requirements implemented                    : 11/11
  Level 2 requirements implemented                    :  4/13
  Level 3 requirements implemented                    :  0/9

  threats still above 50% residual risk               : 15 of 26
  mean residual, Halcyon as certified                 : 63.3%

Twelve of twelve management-system controls have an artefact on file, so the certification passes. Behind it, fifteen of twenty-six threats sit above 50% residual risk, and seven have no implemented control bearing on them at all: vector-store inversion, unbounded consumption, membership inference, evasion, deepfake impersonation, cross-modal injection, and confabulated output driving downstream action.

None of that is a failure of the management system standard. ISO/IEC 42001 does not claim to answer this question, and AISVS says in writing that governance is somebody else's chapter[36]. The failure mode is entirely in the reading: a certificate that answers is there a system for managing AI being offered in response to is this system attackable.

E5 — evidence has a shelf life

The fifth experiment introduces the detail that separates an assurance argument from a spreadsheet: evidence ages. A penetration test result is not a permanent property of a system, and a control attested eleven months ago against a codebase that has since been rewritten is a claim, not a finding. The model assigns validity windows by verification method — ninety days for test-derived evidence, a hundred and eighty for mixed, a year for documentary — and re-evaluates Halcyon's fifteen implemented requirements at an audit date.

  audit date: 2026-08-03

  claimed implemented : 15
  evidenced at audit  :  7  (47%)
  claim without proof :  8  (53%)

  mean residual on claimed posture : 63.3%
  mean residual on proven posture  : 80.5%
  the gap between the two is the audit finding: 17.2pp

Fifty-three per cent of the claims have expired. The delta between the claimed posture and the demonstrable one is 17.2 percentage points of mean residual risk, and that delta is the entire content of the audit. Re-evidencing at the implied cadence — roughly four times a year for test-derived controls — is not an administrative overhead on top of the security programme. For anything with a testable requirement behind it, it is the security programme.

E6 — obligations attach to systems, not to companies

The last experiment maps the regulatory layer onto the portfolio. Each of the six systems carries attributes — placed on the EU market, listed in Annex III or embedded in an Annex I product, trained at frontier scale, processing personal data, agentic, supporting critical infrastructure, operating in particular US states — and each attribute pulls in obligations.

Twelve distinct regimes bind a six-system portfolio, at a mean of 5.3 per system. Exactly one applies to everything. Four apply to a single system each. The widest exposure is the field-crew scheduling agent, which manages to be simultaneously high-risk under Annex III, agentic, personal-data-processing and critical-infrastructure-supporting, and picks up seven regimes as a result.

The operational conclusion is the reason to run this experiment at all. Because obligations attach to system attributes rather than to the organisation, a company-level policy cannot answer the question of what applies. Only an inventory keyed to systems, with attributes recorded per system, can. That is also why shadow AI — the one failure mode no threat catalogue names — is a compliance problem before it is a security problem: a system nobody has inventoried has attributes nobody has evaluated.

21What none of them can do

Four limits are structural rather than incidental, and a programme that does not account for them will be surprised by them.

No mitigation set is complete. The most authoritative technical document in the field pairs its attack taxonomy with mitigations and then discusses the limitations of those mitigations, because for several classes the defences are partial[5,46]. This is not a temporary state pending better research. It follows from the fact that a model's input surface is wherever it reads untrusted content, and that a model cannot reliably distinguish instructions from data[85]. The 2026 LLM Top 10's closing advice reflects it: build the surrounding system so that when the model is fooled, nothing important breaks[85].

Coverage counts are Goodhart bait. ATLAS technique counts, requirement counts and control-mapping percentages are all measurable, which makes them all targets. A programme optimising for coverage against a published catalogue is optimising against a document that was assembled from what was observed elsewhere, not from what is exposed here. The E1 result cuts both ways: catalogues are incomplete relative to a corpus, and any corpus is incomplete relative to a real system.

Certification is a statement about an organisation. The E4 result is not a criticism of ISO/IEC 42001, which does exactly what it says. It is a warning about substitution. The number of AI governance certificates in the world is growing faster than the number of AI systems with tested controls, and the two facts are easy to confuse in a procurement questionnaire.

The regulatory target is moving. Between November 2025 and June 2026 the EU deferred its heaviest regime by sixteen months while adding new prohibitions; Colorado repealed and replaced its comprehensive statute before it took effect; a US executive order proposed preemption that has not yet been enacted[61,73,78]. Any programme whose design depends on a specific date should have a second design that does not.

The through-line is that these documents are instruments, not policies. An instrument answers one question well and returns noise when asked a different one. The discipline the field currently lacks is not more frameworks — the last three years produced plenty — but the habit of asking, before opening any of them, which of the four questions is actually being asked.

23Resources

Read first, in this order. NIST AI 100-2e2025 for vocabulary; the OWASP LLM and Agentic Top 10s for what is landing in production; AISVS 1.0 for what to verify; ISO/IEC 42001 clauses 4–10 for how to run the programme; the EU AI Act's Article 50 and Annex III for what binds you.

Threat modelling. ATLAS at atlas.mitre.org, with the Navigator and Arsenal tooling for red-team scoping; the SAIF map for locating where a risk enters and where it shows[81,97].

Verification. The AISVS repository publishes each stable release as a locked numbered folder, mirroring the ASVS approach, with development continuing in the next minor version's folder[105]. Appendix B — the cross-cutting controls inventory — is the entry point for anyone building a crosswalk[36].

Government guidance. The Cyber Centre's ITSAP.10.049 is the best single page for a small organisation[3]. The Five Eyes agentic guide is the reference to cite when arguing for narrower agent scopes[33]. The NCSC/CISA secure development guidelines remain the clearest lifecycle framing[23].

Tracking change. NIST's COSAiS project page carries the overlay drafts and the comment deadlines[47]. For US state law, the National Conference of State Legislatures maintains a legislation database, and several firms publish trackers; treat any of them as a floor rather than a ceiling[79].

The lab. The code in this article runs standalone with no dependencies. It is worth re-parameterising with your own threat corpus and your own requirement set; the interesting output is not the numbers but which of your threats turn out to have exactly one control behind them.
