A.1

The question

In May 2026, within eight days of each other, the two largest builders of frontier language models each announced that they were putting the work of installing their products into a separate company. OpenAI's vehicle launched on 11 May with more than four billion dollars of committed investment from nineteen investment firms, consultancies and systems integrators, majority-owned and controlled by OpenAI, and it bought a London consultancy on the same day to have roughly a hundred and fifty engineers on the first morning[1,2,3]. Anthropic's, announced on 4 May and named in July, was capitalised at about one and a half billion dollars by Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman and Goldman Sachs, built on an applied-AI services firm acquired in May[4,5,6,7]. Trade coverage at the time read both as the same bet placed twice[8,9].

Read those two announcements as organisational documents rather than as press releases and they say something precise. Both companies concluded that the work of getting the model to do something useful inside a particular firm is large enough, different enough and capital-hungry enough that it should not sit on the same balance sheet as the model. Neither concluded that the work could be avoided. That is the shape of the thing this article is about.

There is a boundary running through every enterprise software business. On one side sits a general capability: a model, a platform, a set of abstractions, something that is the same for every buyer and is priced as though it were. On the other side sits one organisation's particular world: its schema, its undocumented exceptions, its approval routes, its regulator, the four people who actually know why the field is null. The general thing has to work in the particular world. Something has to cross.

Every title in this article — forward-deployed engineer, AI solutions engineer, AI solutions architect, AI deployment engineer, AI implementation engineer, AI adoption engineer, AI enablement engineer, AI transformation engineer, AI product engineer, AI product manager, AI technical product manager, AI strategist, AI architect — names a way of standing on that boundary. The obvious question to ask about them is what they do, and the answer is disappointing, because what they do overlaps enormously and the overlap is measurable. A more useful question is this one:

What does this role carry across the boundary, and in which direction?

The question decomposes into two independent permissions. The first is how deep the role can write into the customer's estate: nothing, advice, a sandbox, or their production system. The second is how deep it can write into the vendor's product: nothing, a feature request, a shared internal asset, or the shipped artefact itself. Call them the customer key and the product key. Almost every one of the thirteen titles is cut deep on one and flat on the other. On the coding used throughout this article, three of thirteen can change something in the customer's production estate, four can commit to the product, and exactly one does both.

That one is the forward-deployed engineer, and it is the reason the title has spread. The role is not remarkable for being customer-facing; the industry has had customer-facing engineers continuously since the 1950s. It is remarkable for being the only one with an obligation running back the other way — for treating what happens at a customer site as product discovery rather than as delivery. Marty Cagan, who is not a Palantir partisan, puts the condition plainly: an organisation that sends engineers out and does not generalise what they find ends up maintaining thousands of bespoke systems for ever[10,11]. The sending is the cheap half. The generalising is the business, and it is the same distinction between a project organisation and a product one that Cagan has spent a decade arguing[12].

This article takes the thirteen titles as its subject and asks the question of each one in turn. It covers where they came from, what published evidence says they do, what tools and processes the work runs on, whether the distinctions between the titles survive measurement, and what background and credentials the evidence actually supports. Nine deterministic experiments, described in full in §A.11 and reproducible from a stated seed, supply every quantity in the text. Where the model behind a number is a model rather than a measurement, it says so.

A.2

Where the line came from

The boundary between a product and the service of making it work is not a fact about software. It is a decision that has been made, unmade and remade roughly once a generation, and each time the instrument that made it was commercial rather than technical.

1931: someone owns the thing

The first move is not about computing at all. On 13 May 1931 a twenty-six year old at Procter & Gamble wrote three pages arguing that the company should hire people accountable for a single brand end to end rather than for a function[13,14]. The memo is usually cited as the origin of product management, and it is, but the part worth re-reading is the method: the accountability was to be discharged by field work, by going and looking at how the product was actually bought and used. Ownership of a thing and presence where the thing is used were, in the founding document, a single idea. Most of the subsequent history consists of prising them apart.

1969: the line gets a price

The line acquired an invoice on 23 June 1969, when IBM announced that it would stop supplying software, systems engineering and training free with the hardware and would price them separately[15,16]. Unbundling was undertaken under the shadow of antitrust rather than out of conviction, and its consequences are usually told as the birth of the independent software industry. The consequence relevant here is narrower. Before 1969 the engineer who sat with the customer was part of the product. After 1969 he was a line item, billed by the hour, with a margin profile and a utilisation target, and the accounting distinction hardened into an organisational one. Systems engineering work was priced on an hourly basis except where it supported sales[15]. That exception — free if it helps close, billable otherwise — is the direct ancestor of the modern pre-sales engineer, and it is why the American occupational classification files sales engineers under sales rather than under engineering, explicitly excluding engineers whose primary function is not selling[17,18].

The classification is worth pausing on, because it is an official statement that the boundary this article is about is a real one and that a person must choose a side. There is no occupation code for anyone who is on both sides. There is still none: the 2018 structure contains nothing corresponding to any of the thirteen titles here, and the next revision is not due for use until reference year 2028[19].

2003–2016: the requirement that could not be gathered

Palantir's early customers were intelligence and defence organisations whose data was classified, whose schemas were undocumented and whose actual working practices could not be described in a document that left the building[20,21]. The company's response was to put its own engineers inside those organisations, and Shyam Sankar — employee thirteen, later chief technology officer — named the resulting role the forward deployed engineer, borrowing a term from military disposition, and was the first person to hold it[22].

Internally the role was called a Delta, and Palantir's own published contrast with a product engineer remains the sharpest formulation in the literature: a Dev builds one capability for many customers, while a Delta builds “one customer, many capabilities”[23]. Deltas sat in business development, not in engineering, and their mandate was expressed as a technical outcome for the customer rather than as a deliverable[23]. Until roughly 2016 the company had more Deltas than ordinary software engineers; the ratio inverted when Foundry shipped, and field engineers moved back onto the platform carrying what they had learned[24,25]. Palantir's own published day-in-the-life accounts describe the resulting week as alternating between customer work and platform work rather than choosing between them[26].

That inversion is the whole model in one event. The field work was not the product; it was the process that discovered what the product should be. Cagan's reading is the same: what makes Palantir a product company rather than a consultancy is a platform organisation whose standing job is to generalise what the deployed engineers keep running into[10].

2023: the capability arrives without the craft

In June 2023 an essay argued that a class of work which had recently required a research team and several years could now be done by a software engineer against an API in an afternoon, and gave the resulting practitioner a name[27]. The claim was contested at the time and remains partly overstated, but the naming stuck and a conference series grew around it[28,29]. What matters for this article is the consequence: the scarce skill stopped being model construction and became knowing what to point the model at, which is a question about somebody else's workflow rather than about machine learning. It is also a question non-experts turn out to be systematically bad at: studies of people writing prompts without AI backgrounds find them designing by intuition and failing in patterned ways[30], which is a large part of why a specialist role emerged at all.

2025–2026: the boundary gets a balance sheet

Hiring for the forward-deployed title then rose steeply. The most-quoted figure is an eightfold increase in postings between January and September 2025, reported from Indeed data by the Financial Times and repeated by Salesforce, which committed to building a thousand-person forward-deployed engineering function of its own[25,31,32]. The figure should be handled with care: Business Insider later corrected its own reporting of the underlying Indeed series to note that the numbers are index values against a January 2025 baseline rather than counts of postings[33], and several secondary outlets still present them as absolute openings[34,35]. The direction is not in doubt, and it is corroborated by broader labour-market work showing AI skills commanding a widening wage premium and postings growing far faster than the market[36,37]. The magnitude, as usually quoted, is an index. The wider pattern is the ordinary one: automation displaces tasks and creates new occupations around the residue[38,39].

Then came May 2026 and the two joint ventures. It is worth being precise about what changed, because it was not the work. Forward-deployed engineers already existed at both companies; OpenAI's function was stood up at the start of 2025 with two people and had passed ten across eight cities by that August[24]. What changed is that the boundary-crossing work was given its own capital structure, its own investors, and in OpenAI's case its own acquisitions budget for buying more of it[1]. Fifty-seven years after IBM was pushed into pricing the field engineer separately from the machine, the industry has arrived at the same separation voluntarily, and for the opposite reason: not because the service was an anticompetitive giveaway, but because it is the scarce input.

A.3

Why the boundary moved again

Customer-facing engineers are not new. The question is why this particular arrangement — embedded, code-writing, with a duty to report back — became the default answer for AI products specifically, when configure-and-hand-over had been the industry's answer for two decades. The honest answer is that something about these systems breaks the requirements process, and it can be stated exactly.

The acceptance criteria are not knowable in advance

Conventional software engineering assumes that requirements are elicited and then satisfied. The assumption is old, contested and load-bearing: it survives in the standards[40], it was already being attacked in the 1970s and 1980s[41,42], and the agile movement's central claim was that the elicitation should be spread over time rather than done once[43,44]. Brooks put the underlying difficulty best: the hard part of building software is deciding what to build, and no notation removes that difficulty[45,46]; the estimation literature has spent forty years pricing the consequences of getting it wrong[47]. But even the iterative version assumes the criteria exist and are merely hard to extract.

For systems whose output is generated rather than computed, that assumption fails in a specific and demonstrated way. A study of practitioners building evaluation criteria for language-model pipelines found what its authors named criteria drift: people need criteria in order to grade outputs, but grading outputs is how they work out what their criteria are. Worse, some criteria turned out to depend on the particular outputs observed rather than being statable independently of them[48]. This is not a failure of interviewing technique. It is a claim that a whole class of acceptance criteria cannot exist before the system has produced something in the setting where it will be judged.

Practitioners arrive at the same place from the other direction. Consultants who specialise in this work report spending sixty to eighty per cent of development time on error analysis and evaluation rather than on building[49,50], and the standing advice is to build an evaluation system specific to the problem rather than adopt a generic one[50]. The design-research literature reached a compatible conclusion earlier: machine learning is difficult to design for because capability uncertainty and output complexity make it impossible to know what the material can do until it is prototyped against real data[51,52]. A parallel complaint runs through the machine-learning literature itself: results reported on benchmarks routinely fail to connect to any consequence anyone cares about[53], and the documentation practices developed to address it — datasheets, audit frameworks — all turn on recording the context a dataset or system was built for[54,55].

L5 · criteria drift

The lab models this directly. Suppose forty acceptance criteria govern a delivered system, and suppose — generously — that thirty-five per cent of them can genuinely be elicited in interviews before anything is built. The remainder become visible only by grading real outputs. A remote engagement captures 35% of the criteria and no amount of additional budget raises that number, because the budget buys building rather than grading. An embedded engagement that alternates grading with revision reaches 75.1% at its optimum of 4 rounds, a gap of 40.1 percentage points. Reaching ninety per cent of the output-dependent criteria costs about 576 graded outputs before the final revision — all of them drawn from this customer's own traffic, which is precisely the supply a vendor benchmark cannot provide.

The shape of that curve is worth reading. Too few rounds and there is no time to act on what grading revealed; too many and the per-round overhead eats the grading budget. Even if one assumes that eighty per cent of the criteria are statable up front — an assumption the empirical work does not support — the embedded protocol still wins by 12.3 points.

The failure is concentrated where the average cannot see it

The second structural reason is a measurement problem. Aggregate accuracy on a vendor's own benchmark is a weighted average over a population that is not the customer's population. Distribution shift is the most thoroughly documented failure mode in applied machine learning[56,57,58,59], and the standard mitigations — behavioural testing against named capabilities[60], disaggregated evaluation across subpopulations[61] — all require knowing in advance which slices matter. That knowledge is local. It is exactly the sticky information that Eric von Hippel identified thirty years ago as determining where problem solving has to physically happen: when the information needed to solve a problem is costly to move, the solving moves to the information[62,63,64]. The companion finding is that this learning is situated in a strong sense: engineers identify problems in novel equipment by being physically present when it misbehaves, and the problems are frequently not identifiable any other way[65]. Long-context retrieval has its own version of the same asymmetry — information present in a system is not thereby used[66].

L4 · the invisible subpopulation

Quantify it. Take a system that is right ninety-two per cent of the time on the vendor's population, and one subpopulation inside a customer — a document type, a dialect, a category of exception — on which it is thirty points worse. If that subpopulation is four per cent of the customer's traffic, the aggregate accuracy falls to 90.80%, a drop of 1.20 points. A thousand-item benchmark has a ninety-five per cent margin of about 1.68 points, so the drop is invisible. Detecting it in the aggregate needs roughly 4,171 graded examples. Detecting it by testing the subpopulation directly needs 14 — an exact binomial calculation, not an approximation — which is about 298 times fewer.

The ratio is the argument for the role, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not show. It does not show that being on site makes anyone a better engineer. It shows that stratifying an evaluation by the right variable is worth two to three orders of magnitude of sample size, and that nobody outside the customer's building knows what the right variable is. Even drawing at random from inside the customer's traffic — better than the vendor benchmark, but still unstratified — needs 350 examples at that subpopulation share. The saving comes from knowing where to look, and that is a fact about the customer's organisation rather than about statistics.

Two decades of empirical software engineering point the same way. The classic field study of large system design found that the scarce resource was not engineering skill but thinly spread application-domain knowledge, and that projects succeeded when a small number of people held both the domain and the system in one head[67]. Organisational theory has said the same thing in its own vocabulary for as long: knowledge that matters is enacted in practice rather than held as a stock[68,69], action is situated in circumstances that plans under-describe[70], and technology's effect on work is settled locally by the people who have to operate it[71,72]. The machine-learning version of that finding is now well documented: the model is a small fraction of a deployed system[73], data problems compound upstream of everything else[74], and practitioners consistently report that the hard part is everything around the model[75,76,77].

The bottleneck was never the model

None of this is peculiar to artificial intelligence. General-purpose technologies deliver their productivity late, because the returns require complementary investment in process, skills and organisational form that takes years to accumulate; electrification is the canonical case[78,79,80,81]. What is peculiar is the speed at which the industry has decided to buy that complementary investment rather than wait for it, and the price it is willing to pay.

The most-cited evidence for the deployment gap deserves a caution. A 2025 preliminary working paper from an MIT-affiliated project reported that ninety-five per cent of surveyed organisations saw no measurable profit-and-loss return from generative AI[82], and the figure became the industry's standard bear case. It has been fairly criticised: the paper describes itself as preliminary, was not peer reviewed, rests on a small base of interviews and survey responses, and its headline is frequently restated as “95% of pilots fail”, which is not what it measured[83,84,85]. Use it as an indication of direction, not as a measurement. The direction is corroborated elsewhere: organisational adoption is near-universal while workflow redesign is not[86,87].

A.4

Thirteen keys

What follows is one plate for each of the thirteen titles, grouped by where on the boundary the role stands. Each plate carries a fixed set of six fields — what it carries in, what it carries out, what it may write to, which budget pays for it, how it degenerates, and the evidence — and a gauge.

The gauge is a key, cut on two edges. The upper edge is the customer key: how deep the role can write into the customer's estate, from K0 (no access) through K1 (advisory, reads but does not change), K2 (builds in a sandbox, a trial tenancy, or against an offline cut of the data) to K3 (commits to their production system). The lower edge is the product key: P0 (nothing), P1 (files a request), P2 (contributes to internal assets reused on other accounts), P3 (commits to the shipped product, the SDK or the model). A role that can only turn one lock has one flat edge. Exactly one plate in the set is cut deep on both.

The codings are the article's, made from the published role descriptions and practitioner accounts cited on each plate, and they are the input to the measurements in §A.5 and §A.10. They are judgements, and a reader who disagrees with one can recode it and rerun the argument; the matrix is printed in full so that this is possible.

R03

AI Solutions Architect

Customer engineer; technical account manager

At OpenAI the distinction from the forward-deployed engineer is drawn precisely, by the person who ran both functions: solutions architects advise, rarely write code on customer infrastructure, and build their prototypes against anonymised or offline cuts of the data; forward-deployed engineers write code directly on the customer's own systems, using the customer's tooling[24]. There is no ladder

The first thing people assume about a set of overlapping titles is that they form a progression: that a solutions engineer is a junior architect, that an implementation engineer is a forward-deployed engineer with less rope. If that were true, the duty sets would nest. One title's job would sit wholly inside another's.

They do not. Of the seventy-eight pairs, exactly 1 is a containment — AI Enablement Engineer inside AI Adoption Engineer, which is the sensible one, since adoption is enablement plus instrumentation. The other 77 pairs are incomparable: each has at least one duty the other lacks, in both directions. Mean pairwise overlap is 0.3239, so the titles are neither the same job nor separate ones. They are overlapping regions of a single territory, and the territory has no vertical axis.

Where overlap is high it is high for readable reasons. The strongest pair is AI Solutions Engineer and AI Solutions Architect at 0.7857, which is to say that the two most common pre-sales titles in enterprise software are very nearly the same job — the difference is one rung of write access, not a difference in duties. Single-linkage merging makes the point structurally: at an overlap threshold of 0.5 the thirteen titles collapse into three groups, and at 0.4 they collapse into one connected blob. A hiring market cannot distinguish thirteen things that connect at that threshold.

The head noun carries almost nothing

If the titles do not nest, perhaps they at least mean something individually. Test it. For each pair of titles, measure how much vocabulary they share and how much duty they share, and correlate the two.

The correlation is real but weak: Pearson +0.360 across the seventy-eight pairs, Spearman +0.266. Words carry some information about work. The interesting result is where it lives. Decompose by token and the signal is entirely in the qualifier, not the head noun.

Eight of the thirteen titles end in “Engineer”. Sharing that word raises expected duty overlap by +0.0329 — from a baseline of about a third to about a third. A permutation test on the label with a hundred thousand shuffles gives p = 0.108: not significant, and not close. Meanwhile the word “Product”, shared by three pairs, moves overlap by +0.2454, and “Solutions”, shared by one, by +0.4678.

The concrete cases are sharper than the statistics. AI Deployment Engineer and AI Enablement Engineer share a head noun, sit adjacent in every list of these titles, and have a duty overlap of 0.1333 — the lowest of any pair that shares a word at all. One holds a production key and carries a pager; the other writes courseware. Meanwhile AI Product Manager and AI Strategist share no vocabulary whatsoever and overlap at 0.5455. If you are reading a job advertisement, the noun at the end is close to noise and the adjective in front of it is the signal. If you are writing one, the same thing is true and it is your problem. Occupational sociology would call this a jurisdictional contest: new titles are claims staked on contested work, and their proliferation is a symptom of a boundary nobody has yet won[103,105].

So what does distinguish them?

Not the duties, mostly. The two things that separate these roles cleanly are the two key depths, and they are close to independent of each other: across the thirteen, the correlation between customer-key depth and product-key depth is +0.214. Knowing that someone can commit to a customer's production system tells you almost nothing about whether they can commit to the product, and that is the whole finding. Eight of thirteen cannot change anything in the customer's estate at all. Nine of thirteen cannot commit to the shipped product. The intersection of the two full permissions has one member.

There is a second, less flattering regularity. Duty count and total key depth correlate at +0.767: the roles with the most permissions also have the longest job descriptions. Whatever else these titles are distributing, it is not workload relief.

A.6

Tooling

Tooling for this work divides along the same boundary as everything else, and the division is more informative than any particular product name. On the vendor's side of the line sit the tools the role brings; on the customer's side sit the tools it must adopt, because they are already there and are not going to move.

What crosses in

The characteristic inbound toolkit is unglamorous and mostly generic: a model API and its SDK, an agent framework, a retrieval layer, a tracing and observability stack that can record a full request and its intermediate steps, and something to run experiments in. The specific choices churn fast enough that naming them dates an article within months. Two things about the toolkit are stable and worth stating.

First, the tracing layer is load-bearing in a way it is not for conventional software. If a system's acceptance criteria are discovered by grading outputs[48], then the ability to retrieve, replay and annotate arbitrary past outputs is not observability hygiene, it is the primary instrument. Teams that cannot get at their traces cannot do error analysis, and error analysis is where the majority of the time goes[49,106].

Second, the evaluation harness is the deliverable that survives. Of everything a deployment produces — prompts, glue code, connectors, dashboards — the artefact with the longest half-life is a labelled set of this customer's own cases with agreed verdicts attached. Prompts are rewritten every time a model changes. An eval set outlives the model it was built to test. This is the closest thing the field has to a boundary object in the classic sense: a single artefact that is legible to the customer's domain experts, to the delivery engineer and to the product team, while meaning something slightly different to each[107,108].

What is already there

On the customer's side the toolkit is whatever the customer has: an identity provider that will not federate, a data warehouse with a decade of accreted schema, a ticketing system that defines what counts as work, a change advisory board that meets on Thursdays. The role does not choose these and cannot replace them. The competence being purchased is largely the ability to operate inside them without either breaking them or being stopped by them.

This is where the write-access ladder becomes concrete rather than conceptual. K1 means reading the warehouse. K2 means a project in a sandbox subscription and an offline extract with the identifiers stripped. K3 means a service account in production, a place in the deployment pipeline, and a name on the on-call rota. Each rung is a separate procurement conversation, a separate security review, and in regulated sectors a separate regulatory exposure. The reason so few of these titles reach K3 is not that the work does not require it; it is that granting it is expensive for the customer and few vendors are worth the cost.

The artefacts a deployment leaves behind

It is useful to enumerate what actually persists, because this is what the return path has to carry. In rough order of durability: the evaluation set and its labelling guidance; the integration code and its configuration; a runbook and an escalation policy; a set of prompts or agent definitions; a dashboard nobody looks at after month three; and a body of undocumented understanding in one or two people's heads about why the third exception exists. The last of these is the most valuable and the only one that cannot be checked into a repository, which is the standing risk of the model and the reason handover is an acceptance criterion rather than an afterthought[109,110,111,112].

A.7

Process

Two processes matter, and they run in opposite directions. The outbound one is well documented. The inbound one is where the model succeeds or fails, and it is almost never designed.

Outbound: scope, validate, deliver

The clearest published account of the outbound process is OpenAI's, described by the head of the function and reported in detail[24]. It runs in three phases, and the middle one is the unusual part.

Scoping is a short period on site: sitting with the people who do the work, mapping their process, identifying where value might be, and prototyping against synthetic data. Validation — which comes before delivery, not after — asks whether the thing scoped is genuinely the most valuable thing available, and answers it by building evaluations with the customer's own labelling, scaling that labelling, improving against the evaluations, and then presenting eval performance against the stated objective. Only then does delivery begin. The stated rationale is that what a customer describes during scoping frequently does not match the data and systems as they actually are, so it is better to find the immovable obstacles early and re-scope around them[24].

Put beside the criteria-drift result[48], this sequence stops looking like a process preference and starts looking like the only sequence available. If a material share of the acceptance criteria can only be discovered by grading real outputs, then any process that fixes scope before grading has committed to delivering against criteria it has not yet found. Validation-before-delivery is what a requirements phase looks like once you accept that requirements are partly unobtainable in advance.

The onsite proportion varies and is worth knowing before taking one of these jobs. Palantir has expected roughly a quarter of a forward-deployed engineer's time on the customer's premises; some healthcare and industrial companies expect up to half; some roles amount to working on a factory floor or in an air-gapped facility[24].

Inbound: the return path, and what a cadence costs

The inbound process is the one that distinguishes a product company from a consultancy, and it is mostly plumbing. OpenAI's published mechanisms are four: an always-open written channel where field engineers post observations; fortnightly readouts to the head of product and product managers; regular knowledge-sharing sessions with the research team; and quarterly gatherings that bring a geographically scattered team together[24]. Ramp's version embeds its forward-deployed engineers inside core product teams so that the transfer is structural rather than scheduled[24]. Practitioner write-ups converge on the same short list of mechanisms and on the same warning that they are the first thing to lapse[113,114].

The consequence that is rarely noticed is that a channel's period is a tax on the observation it carries. A field observation is perishable: the person who made it forgets the context, the customer moves on, the model changes underneath it. Model that decay with a forty-five-day half-life and the arithmetic is immediate.

L8 · return-path cadence

An observation routed to a fortnightly channel waits a week on average and arrives worth 90.0% of what it was. One routed to a quarterly gathering waits six and a half weeks and arrives worth 53.8% — a quarterly channel destroys nearly half the value of everything it carries, before anyone has failed to listen. The always-open channel loses almost nothing to waiting, but it has a different leak: nobody is obliged to read it. Setting the two against each other gives a clean threshold. The written channel beats the fortnightly readout only while the probability that the right person actually reads a given post exceeds 90.3%.

That is a demanding bar, and it is the argument for scheduled bandwidth rather than against it. But scheduled bandwidth does not scale. Counting slots — six items in a fortnightly readout, four in a research session, forty at a quarterly gathering — gives about 105 scheduled slots per quarter. One engineer generating forty observations a quarter fits comfortably. Ten engineers do not: 26.2% of their observations get a scheduled slot and the rest fall back to the lossy channel. The slot counts are the article's assumption, not a published figure, but the shape does not depend on them. Any fixed-cadence review has fixed bandwidth, and a field organisation generates observations in proportion to its headcount. The return path congests before the delivery organisation does.

This is the mechanism behind the failure mode everyone describes anecdotally: a company hires forward-deployed engineers, they do good work, and two years later the product has not moved and the delivery backlog has. Nobody decided that. The channel simply filled up.

A.8

The arithmetic

The case for these roles is usually made qualitatively, and the case against them is made on gross margin. Both can be put on the same footing. What follows is a model, not a measurement: a vendor sells one general capability to a sequence of customers, and each deployment turns out to need some set of capability atoms — a connector, an authentication mode, a data quirk, an escalation rule, a regulatory constraint. Atoms are Zipf-popular: a handful are needed by everyone, most by almost nobody. The parameters are stated in §A.11 and the whole thing runs from a fixed seed.

How much of the next customer have you already seen?

L1 · field coverage

The first quantity is reuse: the share of a new deployment's needs that a library built from prior deployments already covers. It rises steeply and then slowly. Against a library from five deployments, expected reuse is 44.1%. From twenty-five, 64.8%. From a hundred, 82.0%. Expected reuse first reaches ninety per cent at deployment 214, and ninety-five per cent at 412.

Two features of that curve matter more than its level. The first is that the realised per-deployment rate is far noisier than the expectation — at deployment one hundred the sampled rate is 91.9% while the trailing ten-deployment mean is 80.5% — which is why a delivery organisation's felt experience of “we keep building the same thing” and “every customer is a snowflake” can both be true in the same quarter. The second is the tail. After two hundred deployments, 829 of the 1,745 distinct needs encountered have been seen exactly once: 47.5% of everything met, accounting for 10.6% of all requirement-instances. That fraction does not go to zero. It is the irreducible bespoke residue, and no amount of platform work removes it.

The threshold that discards the field's best findings

L2 · the prioritisation threshold

Now put a prioritisation bar on the return path, which is what every product organisation does: a request gets built when enough accounts have asked for it. Set the bar at three accounts and the arithmetic is stark. Only 29.5% of the distinct needs the field encountered ever get built. Demand-weighted, that covers 79.1% of all requirement-instances, which is why the bar looks reasonable on a dashboard. From inside one account it looks different: the mean account gets 79.3% of what it needs from the product, the tenth-percentile account gets 72.2%, and the worst gets 60.5%. The remainder is bespoke work or an unhappy customer.

There is a delay term as well. Among the needs that do eventually clear a three-account bar, the median wait between first sighting and third is 58 deployments, and the ninetieth percentile is 135. The engineer who first hit the problem has left the account, and probably the company, before the product catches up.

This is the precise thing a product key buys. A role at P1 files a request and it enters the threshold. A role at P3 can build the thing, and the threshold does not apply. That is not a licence to build everything: at m = 1 the product accumulates every one-off anybody ever met, which is Cagan's warning about thousands of bespoke systems[10]. It is a licence to override the threshold with judgement, exercised by the one person who has seen the case.

When to generalise

L3 · generalisation break-even

Judgement can be given a floor. If building an atom bespoke for one account costs one unit, generalising it costs g once and r per subsequent account, then generalising pays from occurrence n* = g/(1−r). At g = 3 and r = 0.15 that is 3.53, so generalise from the fourth occurrence. Applied to the demand distribution, 345 of 1,745 atoms qualify — 19.8% of them — but they carry 72.6% of all requirement-instances. Generalising exactly those and building the rest bespoke costs 37.8% less than never generalising and 21.0% less than generalising everything on first sight. Both errors are expensive; the second is the one that platform teams make.

What the return path is worth in margin

L6 · gross margin

Finally, the number the board asks about. Suppose each account pays one unit, the software itself costs 0.20 to serve, and standing up the first account costs 0.55 in delivery labour. With a return path, delivery cost falls in proportion to reuse; without one, it does not fall at all.

Without a return path the business runs at 25% gross margin for ever, which is a good consultancy and a bad software company. With one, blended margin crosses fifty per cent at account 17 and sixty per cent at account 64, and the incremental margin on a single account first clears seventy per cent at account 38, settling near 74.2% as reuse saturates. The gap at two hundred accounts is 42.8 points.

The most useful line in that experiment is the sensitivity. If only half of what the field learns is generalised, blended margin over two hundred accounts is 46.4% rather than 67.8%. The return path is not a binary. It is a rate, it is the single largest lever on the economics of this whole class of business, and it is the thing that no job title on its own guarantees. This is the servitization literature's oldest finding[94,115,116,117,118] arriving in a new industry: adding services to a product business is easy and adding them profitably is not. It is also the integration-versus-modularity question in its usual form: work integrates where the interfaces are not yet good enough to modularise, and it migrates outward once they are[92,119]. What makes the return path a durable advantage rather than a temporary cost is that the accumulated generalisations are hard for a competitor to assemble any other way[104,120].

A.9

Background, qualifications, credentials

The question of what qualifies a person for this work has an unusually clean answer, because the credential market has been running for long enough to have produced a natural experiment and the experiment failed.

What the evidence supports as necessary

Three things recur across every published description and practitioner account, and nothing else does.

The first is the ability to write and ship production software. Not to have written it once: to be doing it. Palantir's own framing of the forward-deployed job compares its responsibilities to those of a startup chief technology officer — small team, end-to-end ownership of high-stakes work[24]. Experience thresholds vary widely and are not a good filter: Ramp prefers five years for senior posts but hires exceptional new graduates, and Palantir has hired people a year out of university[24].

The second is tolerance for undefined problems. OpenAI's advertisement asks for people who can co-develop solutions in problem spaces that are undefined or still moving[24]; Palantir's early hiring reportedly favoured free thinkers, mathematicians and people who had been awkward in their own fields over conventional corporate engineers. This is the trait that cannot be certified, and it is the one every account puts first.

The third is the willingness to be in the room. That sounds soft and is not: it is the operational form of the sticky-information result[62,64] and the reason §A.3's factor of 298 exists. A person who will not sit with the people doing the work will not learn which subpopulation matters, and everything downstream of that is guesswork with a confidence interval.

What is desirable, and what it substitutes for

Domain fluency is the strongest desirable, and it is worth more than any technology on the list. The classic field study of large-system design found application-domain knowledge to be the binding constraint rather than engineering skill[67]; nothing since has overturned it, and for AI deployments the domain expert is also the only available source of ground truth. Palantir's team structure encodes this by pairing engineers with people who bring the customer's own field expertise.

After that: evaluation literacy — error analysis, labelling design, the discipline of looking at data before building metrics[48,49,50]; data engineering, because integration is where deployments actually die[73,76]; and enough security and identity knowledge to survive a customer's review process, which is what stands between K2 and K3.

The trait most often mistaken for a qualification is seniority. The matrix in §A.5 shows why that is a category error: the roles do not nest, so there is no rung to be senior on. A person moves between these titles sideways, by acquiring a different key, not upwards.

Credentials: a market that churns faster than the work

Formal credentials in this area fall into three groups, and their track record is instructive.

Cloud platform certifications are the largest group. The current shape is AWS's restructured ladder from AI Practitioner through Machine Learning Engineer – Associate[121] and the long-standing Solutions Architect – Professional[122]; Google Cloud's Professional Machine Learning Engineer[123]; Microsoft's Azure line; and Databricks' machine-learning track[124]. They certify platform-specific competence, which is real but is not the competence at issue. They are a filter for a recruiter, not an argument to a customer.

The instructive case is Microsoft's. The Azure AI Engineer Associate certification — the credential whose name most nearly matched one of the thirteen titles in this article — was retired on 30 June 2026 along with its exam, and replaced by a differently scoped credential built around generative applications and agents[125]. That is the natural experiment. A role-named certification in this area lasted a handful of years before the role it named had moved out from under it. Any credential in this space is depreciating from the day it is issued, which is an argument for treating them as evidence of recent study rather than of standing capability.

Governance credentials are the second group and are aimed at a different job: the IAPP's AI Governance Professional[126], ISACA's AI audit credential[127], and the management-system standards they sit against, ISO/IEC 42001 and the conformity-assessment requirements in ISO/IEC 42006[90,128]. These matter for the transformation, architecture and governance-adjacent titles and hardly at all for the engineering ones. Their value is rising for a straightforward reason: regulation has begun naming personal attributes. The EU AI Act requires deployers of high-risk systems to assign human oversight to people with “the necessary competence, training and authority”[129] — the only place in a major AI instrument where authority is named as a required attribute of a person — though the associated literacy obligation was softened in the 2026 Digital Omnibus from ensuring literacy to supporting its development[95]. In the United States the equivalent instrument names an office rather than a skill: federal agencies must designate a chief AI officer[130].

Vendor-issued AI literacy credentials are the third and newest group. OpenAI has committed to certifying ten million Americans by 2030 through its Academy and has begun shipping courses[96,131]. Whatever this turns out to be, it is not a qualification for any of the thirteen roles here. It is a literacy programme, and treating it as a hiring signal for embedded engineering work would be a category error of the same kind as treating a driving licence as evidence of mechanical skill.

What a portfolio looks like

Given all of that, the strongest available evidence for a candidate is not a certificate but an artefact: a system this person put into production inside an organisation that was not their own, an account of what the evaluation set measured and how it was built, and a specific description of something the deployment taught that changed what got built next. The third item is the one that separates a forward-deployed engineer from an implementation engineer, and it is the one almost nobody is asked for.

Three conclusions follow, and one caution.

The distinctions between these thirteen titles are mostly not real as duty descriptions, and the market should stop pretending otherwise. Seventy-seven of seventy-eight pairs are incomparable, the whole set connects into one cluster at an overlap threshold of 0.4, and the head noun that eight of them share predicts nothing. A candidate reading these advertisements is being asked to distinguish things that measurement cannot distinguish.

The distinctions that are real are permissions, and permissions are negotiable and rarely negotiated. Whether a role can commit to the customer's production system and whether it can commit to the vendor's product are nearly independent of each other. They are the two facts that determine what the work actually is, and neither of them appears in any of the thirteen titles.

The forward-deployed engineer is not a delivery role that got promoted. It is a product-discovery role that acquired a second key, and it only works while the return path is open. Everything in §A.8 says the same thing from a different direction: the difference between a twenty-five per cent gross margin and a sixty-eight per cent one is not the quality of the engineers but whether what they learn gets generalised, and the difference between generalising half and generalising all of it is 21.4 points of blended margin.

The caution is about the copying. In the year to mid-2026 a very large amount of capital was committed to reproducing a model that one company spent twenty years building, on the evidence of that company's share price. The model's costs are well known to the people who have run it — it does not scale linearly, it concentrates knowledge in individuals, it invites the customisation trap, and it can leave a customer dependent on the vendor's staff for the operation of their own system[88,132,133]. A separately capitalised delivery entity solves the funding problem. It does not, by itself, open a return path; if anything, putting the field organisation in a different company with a different balance sheet and different investors makes the return path harder to keep open, not easier. That is the thing to watch over the next two years, and it will be visible in exactly one place: whether the products get better in ways that the field discovered.

A.11

Method and lab

Nine experiments supply every quantity in this article. All are deterministic, use only the Python standard library, and run from seed 20260902. Four of them (L1, L2, L3, L6) share one demand process; two (L7, L9) run over the coded matrix printed in §A.5; three (L4, L5, L8) are self-contained models. The full console output is reproduced below the descriptions.

What the lab is and is not

Three of these experiments are calculations — L4's statistical power, L3's break-even, L7's partial order — and their outputs are facts given their inputs. The other six are models with parameters the article chose, and their outputs are conditional on those choices. Where a number in the text comes from a model, the parameters are stated at the point of use. Nothing in the lab is an empirical measurement of any real company's operations, and no claim in this article should be read as one.

The coded matrix of twenty-six duties against thirteen titles is the article's own judgement, made from the sources cited on each plate. It is the weakest link in the chain and it is printed in full for exactly that reason. The two experiments that run on it, L7 and L9, are robust to individual cell changes in the sense that no single duty drives the results, but a reader who codes the matrix differently will get different numbers, and should.

L1 — field coverage

A universe of 3,000 capability atoms with Zipf-1 popularity, scaled so that a deployment requires forty atoms in expectation. Two hundred deployments are drawn. The measured quantity is reuse: the share of a deployment's atoms already met in an earlier one. The analytic expectation against a library of n prior deployments is the demand-weighted probability that an atom has been seen at least once, and it is reported alongside the sampled curve because the per-deployment realisation is a small binomial sample and is very noisy.

L2 — the prioritisation threshold

The same demand process, with a rule that a discovered need reaches the product only after m distinct accounts have asked for it. Reported three ways: the share of distinct needs built, the demand-weighted share of all requirement-instances covered, and the share of a single account's own needs covered. The three diverge sharply, which is the point of the experiment.

L3 — generalisation break-even

Closed form: with bespoke cost 1, one-off generalisation cost g and residual per-account cost r, generalising pays from occurrence g/(1−r). Swept over g in {2, 3, 5, 8} and r in {0.05, 0.15, 0.30}, then applied to L1's realised recurrence distribution to price three policies over two hundred deployments.

L4 — the invisible subpopulation

Statistical power for a one-sample binomial test of an observed accuracy against a known baseline, at α = 0.05 two-sided and 80% power. The normal approximation is used for the large-n aggregate case and exact enumeration for the small-n stratified case, because the approximation is poor at n ≈ 10: it gives 9.8 where exact enumeration requires 14. The exact power curve is not monotone in n — discrete critical values saw-tooth, so n = 10 reaches 79.83% while n = 12 falls back to 72.96% — and the article quotes the exact figure throughout. The aggregate figures are lower bounds: testing against a known baseline is cheaper than comparing two samples.

L5 — criteria drift

Forty acceptance criteria, of which a share a can be elicited a priori. Each still-unknown criterion is discovered by grading with hazard λ = 0.004 per graded output. Total effort is 400 outputs and each round burns 20 of it on overhead, which is what creates an interior optimum in the number of rounds: too few and there is no revision after discovery, too many and overhead consumes the grading. A criterion counts as acted on only if it was found before the final revision. The optimum is flat: R = 4 and R = 5 both give 75.11%.

L6 — gross margin

Each account pays 1.00; the software costs 0.20 to serve; the first deployment costs 0.55 in delivery labour. With a return path, delivery cost for account k is 0.55 × (1 − reuseₖ) using L1's realised curve; without one it never falls. The q-sweep generalises this to a partial return path where only a share q of what is learned reaches the product. The zero-return-path case is deliberately extreme — real delivery organisations learn something even with no product changes at all — which is why the q-sweep rather than the binary comparison is the honest headline.

L7 and L9 — the matrix

L7 computes the containment order and the Jaccard overlap structure over the thirteen duty sets, then single-linkage merges at four thresholds. L9 correlates lexical similarity of title pairs (token Jaccard, with the token “AI” removed as it appears in twelve of thirteen) against duty similarity, decomposes the signal by token, and runs a 100,000-shuffle permutation test on the “Engineer” label.

L8 — return-path cadence

An observation arrives at a uniformly random point in a channel's period T, so its wait is Uniform(0, T) and its expected surviving value under exponential decay with half-life h is (1 − e^(−kT))/(kT) with k = ln2/h. Half-life is set to 45 days. The capacity figures — six slots in a fortnightly readout, four in a research session, forty at a quarterly gathering — are the article's assumption and are stated as such in the text; the qualitative conclusion (fixed cadence gives fixed bandwidth, demand grows with headcount) does not depend on them.

Two rebuilds and one withdrawal

L5 was rebuilt. Its first parameterisation used a discovery hazard five times larger, with the consequence that a single round of grading found 99.98% of the latent criteria and the experiment measured nothing: every round count from two upwards was tied at the ceiling and there was no optimum to find. The rebuild lowered the hazard and added the per-round overhead that makes the trade-off real. The rebuilt version is the one reported.

L1's reporting was rebuilt. The first version searched for the deployment at which a trailing-ten mean reuse rate crossed ninety per cent and found none within two hundred deployments, because the expectation itself only reaches 89.37% at that point. Reporting a threshold that the experiment cannot reach is a way of implying a result that is not there; the analytic crossing points are now computed directly and the sampled curve is shown as a realisation rather than as the finding.

One claim was withdrawn. An earlier draft asserted that the words in these job titles carry no information about the work, on the strength of the “Engineer” result. L9 does not support that: lexical and functional similarity correlate at +0.360 across the seventy-eight pairs, which is weak but real. The claim was narrowed to what the decomposition actually shows — that the signal sits in the qualifier and not in the head noun — and the figure was rebuilt to display the per-token decomposition rather than the aggregate correlation.
