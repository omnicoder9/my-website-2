01

The question

Why the interesting variable is not the model

A generative model for science is a device that produces candidate objects: a protein backbone, a crystal, a synthesis route, a solid model, an atmospheric state, a research proposal. The architectures that do this are, at the level that matters here, interchangeable and well understood. A variational autoencoder places molecules in a continuous latent space and decodes points in it [1,2]. An adversarial network learns a sampler by playing it against a critic [3]. A transformer factorises a joint distribution over tokens into a chain of conditionals [4]. A diffusion model learns to reverse a noising process, which is the same thing as learning the score of the data distribution, and admits a guidance term that trades fidelity against diversity [5,6,7]. A generative flow network samples proportionally to a reward rather than maximising it, which is what you want when you intend to test many candidates rather than one [8]. These are the tools. The literature reviewed below uses all of them, often interchangeably, on the same problem.

The survey literature is organised around these tools and around the disciplines they are applied to, and it is a useful organisation [9,10,11].

What the literature does not share is the answer to a much less glamorous question. Once the model has produced a candidate, something has to decide whether it is any good. That something is the verifier, and unlike the generator it is not a free parameter. It is a crystallography experiment, or a density-functional calculation, or a geometry kernel, or a plaque assay, or a randomised trial, or the weather on Thursday. Its cost is set by physics, biology, regulation and the calendar, and no amount of scaling changes it. Nothing in a generative model gives you evidence. The model gives you proposals; the verifier converts proposals into knowledge, and it charges for the service.

This article reads eleven application areas as one problem with one binding constraint, and asks of each: what does it cost to check one sample?

The claim is that this single quantity organises the field better than any taxonomy of models, molecules or disciplines. It predicts where generative methods have produced results that nobody disputes, where they have produced results that specialists dispute, and where the published claims are mostly about the proposals rather than about the world. It explains why the same architecture is transformative for CAD geometry and merely suggestive for materials synthesis. It explains why the biggest recent gains in protein design came from making the design step better and from an assay format that made checking cheap, and why only the second kind of gain compounds. And it gives a sharp criterion for what to build next: not a better sampler, but a cheaper honest test.

The framing is old. A hypothesis earns credence by surviving a test it could have failed, and the value of a test is the severity of the ordeal it puts the hypothesis through, not the enthusiasm of the person proposing it [12,13]. What is new is that one side of that transaction has collapsed in price by many orders of magnitude while the other has not moved. When proposing was expensive, the proposal was the scarce good and the scientific literature was organised around it. It is no longer scarce. A genome language model will write you ten thousand plausible bacteriophage genomes this afternoon [14]. The question of what to do about that is a question about verification.

Three conventions hold throughout. First, every application area is presented as one or more numbered plates with a fixed six-field set, so that the same six questions are asked of protein design and of digital twins. Second, each plate carries a gauge showing the wall-clock cost of one true verification, amortised over the batch it runs in; these are order-of-magnitude figures on a stated basis, and the closing ledger reports the basis for each. Third, every quantitative claim that is not attributed to a cited source comes from the lab described in the margin: eight deterministic, standard-library-only experiments, run at build time, whose output is reproduced verbatim at the foot of this page. Where the two disagree, the citation wins and the disagreement is stated.

The reason to organise a survey this way, rather than by architecture or by discipline, is that the two halves of the loop have not improved at the same rate. Sampling a plausible protein backbone, a plausible crystal, a plausible retrosynthetic route cost a specialist weeks of modelling effort a decade ago and costs a GPU-second now. Determining whether the backbone folds, whether the crystal is stable, whether the route runs did not become cheaper at all: it is still a week of expression and purification, a furnace run, a bench chemist. When one input to a process falls in price by four orders of magnitude and the other does not move, the process reorganises itself around the expensive input, and the interesting questions all become questions about that input. A survey organised by model family would treat a diffusion model over crystals and a diffusion model over molecules as near neighbours. Organised by verifier they are not neighbours at all, because one is checked by a density-functional relaxation that runs unattended overnight and the other, eventually, by a chemist.

The axis also explains a pattern that is otherwise puzzling: why generative methods have produced uncontested, load-bearing results in a few corners of science and contested, deflating ones in most of the rest. It is not that the corners had better models. It is that they had cheaper checks.

02

A price list for checking

Five verifier classes, and what a better generator is worth

Verifiers are not a continuum. They fall into five classes that differ from each other in kind, not just in price, and the differences determine what can be automated, what can be optimised against, and what can be believed.

An analytic verifier is a decision procedure. A geometry kernel either builds a valid solid from a construction program or it throws; a proof checker either accepts a term or it does not; an evaluator function either returns a better score or it does not. These verifiers are exact, total, and effectively free, which means they can be placed inside the generation loop and called millions of times. A numerical verifier substitutes a simulator for the world: a docking score, a density-functional relaxation, a finite-element solve. It is exact about its own model and only as true as that model is. A record verifier compares a proposal against a held-out slice of an existing observational archive: the Protein Data Bank, the ICSD, a reanalysis dataset. It is cheap because someone else already paid, and it is finite for exactly the same reason. A bench verifier makes a new physical measurement of this particular candidate; it is the first class whose price is set outside computing. A field verifier is the artefact in service, in a patient, or under load, and it cannot be run twice.

The economics are simple enough to write down. A campaign that proposes candidates at generation cost cg, checks each at verification cost cv, and accepts with probability p spends (cg + cv)/p per accepted design, with a hard floor at cg + cv because you cannot check fewer than one candidate. Two consequences follow immediately, and neither is obvious.

The first is that the share of the budget spent on verification does not depend on the hit rate at all. At a bench cost of one assay well amortised over its plate, against one second of generator compute per candidate, verification is 99.9957% of the campaign whether the model hits once in ten thousand or once in six. Improving the generator changes the level of spending, never its composition. When people say that a field is compute-bound, they are almost always describing the 0.0043%.

The second is that improvements in hit rate have violently diminishing returns, in a way that is independent of which verifier class you are in. Starting from a hit rate of 10-4, the total saving available from any further improvement is bounded by the cost of the campaign at p = 1. The first factor of ten in hit rate captures 90.01% of that saving. The remaining thousand-fold improvement, all the way to a model that is right every time, is worth the other 9.99%. This is not a statement about diminishing returns to research effort; it is arithmetic, and it holds for CAD kernels and clinical trials alike. It means that a field's first credible order of magnitude is its most valuable one, and that once a generator is hitting a few per cent of the time, further work on the generator is close to worthless compared with work on the verifier.

That last point deserves emphasis because it inverts the usual reading of progress in this field. The headline results in de novo binder design are typically told as a story about better generative models, and the models did get better [15,16]. But the campaign cost is dominated by the assay, so the transformative move is the one that made the assay smaller: designing twenty candidates and testing them in a single 24-well plate instead of screening a library [17]. Section 3 shows that this is not a metaphor. The plate size is a precise statement about hit rate, and it can be inverted.

Cost is the axis this article sorts on, but cost alone does not make a verifier trustworthy. A check can be fast and still tell you nothing. Three further properties decide whether a number produced by a verifier survives contact with the world.

The first is independence from the generator. If a docking score ranks the candidates and a docking score also decides whether the top-ranked ones are correct, the loop has no external contact and its apparent success rate is a property of the software. Independence fails in subtler ways too: a model trained on the Protein Data Bank and evaluated against the Protein Data Bank is checked by an archive that contributed to it, which is why held-out structures are dated rather than sampled at random. The second is severity in the sense of the philosophy of experiment: a test is severe when it would very probably have failed had the claim been false [13]. A validity check that 96% of random samples also pass is not severe, however cheap it is; the discriminating power, not the pass rate, is what is being bought. The third is that the verifier's cost must not depend on the answer. If confirming a success takes an afternoon and disconfirming a failure takes three weeks, the measured hit rate drifts upward for reasons that have nothing to do with the generator, because the cheap outcome is the one that gets recorded.

These properties are why the class boundaries in this article are not a matter of degree. An analytic verifier is independent, severe and answer-insensitive by construction. A proxy that has been fitted, tuned or selected on the same distribution as the generator can be all three, none of the three, or somewhere in between, and the article's recurring failure mode is a paper that reports the price of the proxy while claiming the authority of the true check.

03

Protein generation

Where the funnel narrowed by five orders of magnitude

Protein modelling is the field where every one of the five verifier classes is in active use, which makes it the best place to see them separated. Structure prediction and structure design look superficially similar — both emit coordinates — but they are checked by different things and their trajectories differ accordingly.

Prediction is verified against a record. The Critical Assessment of Structure Prediction was established in 1994 precisely to make that verification severe: predictors submit before the experimental structure is released, so the test is one they could fail [18]. This is why the AlphaFold2 result was accepted so quickly and so widely: the verifier was already institutionalised, adversarial, and outside the modellers' control [19]. Contemporaneous three-track architectures [20], later all-atom extensions to complexes, nucleic acids and ligands [21,22], and language-model approaches that drop the alignment step [23,24] were all measured on the same held-out record.

The limitation of a record verifier is that it is finite and already paid for. A database of over 200 million predicted structures is not 200 million verified facts; it is 200 million hypotheses of varying and largely unmeasured quality [25]. Structural biologists have made this point carefully: predictions are valuable hypotheses that accelerate experimental structure determination and do not replace it, and the confidence metrics that accompany them are model outputs, not measurements [26,27]. The frontier has moved to properties the record barely covers — conformational ensembles and Boltzmann-weighted populations rather than single structures [28] — and there the verifier problem is unsolved, because the archive was never built to hold that.

Design is verified at the bench, and the numbers there are unambiguous. Designing a binder to a target structure with no template was demonstrated at experimental success rates below 0.1%, requiring hundreds of thousands to millions of designs per campaign [29]. Diffusion over backbones, with sequences assigned by a separate inverse-folding network, improved this by orders of magnitude [15,30,31]. An automated pipeline built on folding-model weights reports experimental success rates of 10–100% across diverse targets, against the prior art it explicitly compares itself to at under 0.1% [16]; the field's own reviews now treat design as an engineering discipline rather than a research aspiration [32]. Zero-shot antibody design against 52 antigens with no known binder reports an average hit rate of 15.5% across all designed antibodies, with at least one validated binder for 26 of the 52 targets, and 68% for miniprotein binders [17].

The right way to read those numbers is through the assay, not the model. Suppose you will call the experiment a success if at least one design binds, and you want that outcome with 95% confidence. The number of designs you must assay is n = ⌈ln 0.05 / ln(1−p)⌉. Inverting at n = 24 gives p* = 1 − 0.051/24 = 11.735%. That is the exact hit rate at which a single 24-well plate stops being a lottery ticket and becomes an experiment. The reported zero-shot antibody rate of 15.5% clears it; the 13.7% single-chain variable fragment rate clears it; a 1% rate needs 299 designs and a 0.1% rate needs 2,995. The plate format in the title of that paper is not packaging. It is a claim about p, and it is checkable.

The same arithmetic disciplines the enthusiasm. Sixteen viable bacteriophages from 302 synthesised genomes is a hit rate of 5.30%, with a Wilson 95% interval of 3.29–8.43% [14]. The whole interval sits below the 24-well break-even. That is a real result and a real hit rate; it is not yet a rate at which a single plate is an experiment, and the 302 designs synthesised is the honest measure of what it cost.

It is worth being precise about why structure prediction is treated as settled while generative design is not, because both are protein problems and both use the same class of model. Structure prediction acquired its authority from a verifier that predates the models by thirty years: a biennial, blinded, externally administered assessment in which predictions are deposited before the experimental structures are released [18]. Nobody in that arrangement chooses their own test set, and the cost of a check is borne by the crystallographers rather than the modellers. Generative design has no equivalent. Each group assembles its own targets, expresses its own constructs, and reports the fraction that worked, which is not dishonest but is not blinded either, and the resulting numbers are consequently much harder to compare across groups than a CASP score.

The funnel arithmetic in the plates above is the practical form of the same observation. A campaign that designs ten thousand backbones, keeps the few hundred that pass in-silico filters, orders the genes, expresses them and finds a handful of binders has performed one expensive verification a few hundred times and one cheap verification ten thousand times. Everything the generative model contributed lives in the ratio between those two numbers, and that ratio is only meaningful if the cheap filter was severe.

Drug discovery

Cheap screens, low base rates, and a verifier that takes years

Drug discovery is the domain where the gap between the cheapest and the truest verifier is widest. A docking score costs seconds. The measurement that actually decides whether a molecule is a medicine is a randomised controlled trial, and it costs years and a development programme whose capitalised expense runs into the hundreds of millions [34]. Everything in between is a proxy, and the discipline's characteristic failure is forgetting which one is being reported — a point argued at length, and from inside the industry, in an assessment of what artificial intelligence in drug discovery has actually delivered against what was claimed [35].

Start with base rates, because they are what make cheap screens so treacherous here. If a fraction π of a library is genuinely active and a screen has sensitivity 0.90, the precision of what it forwards is π·Se / (π·Se + (1−π)·FPR). At a base rate of one in a million, a screen with a false-positive rate of 10-3 — which would be an excellent classifier by most reporting conventions — forwards candidates that are right 0.0899% of the time. To get half of what it forwards to be real, the false-positive rate must fall below 9.000×10-7, a specificity of 0.9999991. No published virtual screen is characterised to that precision, and the reason virtual screening works anyway is that it is not being asked to be right; it is being asked to enrich.

Enrichment can be measured. In the largest published campaign of its kind, 138 million make-on-demand compounds were docked against the D4 dopamine receptor; the hit-rate-versus-score curve implied roughly 453,000 ligands in the library, and 549 top-ranked compounds were synthesised, of which 122 bound [36]. Recomputing: the library base rate is 0.3283%, the measured precision at the top of the ranking is 22.2%, and the enrichment factor is 67.7×. Drawing 549 compounds at random from the same library would have been expected to yield 1.8 binders. That is what a numerical verifier used as a ranker is worth, stated without reference to any AUC.

The general form is a cascade: a cheap ranker in front of an expensive verifier. Its value can be computed exactly. Take a library of a million candidates at a base rate of 10-4, so a hundred true actives exist, and a ranker whose score distributions give an AUC of 0.80 — unremarkable, the kind of number that appears in a methods table without comment. Sweeping the cutoff, the best threshold that still expects at least ten hits forwards 7,153 candidates, or 0.715% of the library, recovers 10.4% of the actives, and costs 688.6 expensive checks per hit against 10,000 with no screen at all: a 14.5× reduction in expensive verification. Two things are worth noticing. A mediocre ranker is worth a great deal, and it is worth it by discarding almost everything, including almost 90% of the true actives. Recall is what you sell to buy precision, and in a discovery campaign that trade is usually correct, because the objective is one confirmed hit and not a complete inventory.

Generative approaches enter this pipeline in two places. They propose molecules directly in a pocket, where diffusion over ligand pose and identity has largely displaced search-based docking [37] — with the important caveat that learned docking methods have been shown to produce physically invalid poses and to generalise poorly to novel sequences when checked against explicit physical criteria rather than RMSD alone [38]. And they propose targets, not just ligands. The one AI-originated molecule with a randomised phase 2a readout, a TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, is notable less because a generative chemistry system drew the structure than because a machine-learning system nominated the target [39,40,41]. The trial enrolled 71 patients across 22 sites and reported the primary safety endpoint met with a dose-dependent lung-function signal. It is one readout. The field verifier has spoken once.

Deep learning has also produced antibacterial results whose verification is unusually clean, because the assay is growth inhibition and the confirmation is an animal model: a screen that identified halicin from a 6,000-compound library [42], and a later structural class recovered with explainable models and confirmed in mice [43]. Those are discriminative screens rather than generative design, which is the point: where the bench verifier is cheap and fast, the generative step is not the bottleneck and nobody bothers with it.

Molecular generation

What happens when the objective and the check are the same function

Unconstrained molecular generation is the purest case of a proxy standing in for a verifier, because the proxy is usually the same predictor the generator was optimised against. A latent-space model decodes points into molecules and walks the latent space toward a predicted property [2]; a graph model assembles molecules from chemically valid fragments so that validity is guaranteed by construction [44]; a reinforcement-learning framework tunes a prior toward a scoring function [45]. In each case the scoring function that steers generation is the same one that reports success. That is not a verifier at all. It is a fixed point.

The benchmarks in this area were built with this problem in view, and they measure what can be measured without a bench: validity, uniqueness, novelty, distribution match, and goal-directed scores against oracles that are themselves models [46,47]. The honest reading is that they evaluate samplers, not discoveries. The gap was quantified directly: molecules proposed by generative models are frequently not synthesisable, and standard reported metrics do not detect this [48]. A survey of the field asked whether automated de novo design was nearly there and answered, carefully, that the field had been nearly there for some time [49].

There is a second and subtler cost to optimising hard against a proxy, and it is not a chemistry problem but a property of guided sampling. Consider a design space with 64 distinguishable modes whose qualities are spread linearly, of which 26 are acceptable, and a sampler that tilts toward quality with strength g, as classifier-free guidance does [7]. Draw 100 samples. Validity — the fraction of samples that pass — rises monotonically with g, from 40.6% unguided to essentially 100%. But the number of distinct acceptable modes discovered peaks early, at g = 0.3, with 23.82 of the 26 found. Push guidance to g = 6.0 and validity gains 23.7 points while distinct discoveries collapse to 5.43, a loss of 77.2% of everything the campaign might have found. The sampler that passes more checks finds fewer things.

This is the formal reason to prefer samplers that draw in proportion to reward over samplers that maximise it, when the plan is to test many candidates [8], and it is the formal content of the complaint that generative chemistry produces variations on a theme. It also explains a finding about genome design that would otherwise be puzzling: model likelihood predicts experimental viability well, but it does so largely by keeping generated sequences close to observed ones, so that the design efficiency and the evolutionary novelty trade against each other directly [14].

Synthesis planning is the one place in this section where a real verifier is routinely applied. A Monte Carlo tree search over transformation rules learned from reaction corpora produces routes that chemists could not distinguish from literature routes in blinded comparison [50]; sequence-to-sequence models predict reaction outcomes with calibrated uncertainty [51]; and coupling the planner to a robotic flow platform closes the loop by actually running the chemistry [52]. The verifier here is a bench synthesis, at a cost of days per route, and the funnel is reported per target rather than per candidate because failure is usually one step rather than the whole plan.

Materials discovery

Two million proposals, and the arithmetic of realisation

Inorganic materials discovery has the most instructive verifier stack in this article, because all four of its layers are visible at once and they disagree with each other in public.

The stack runs: a generative or enumerative proposal step; a learned interatomic potential that estimates energy above the convex hull; a density-functional calculation that checks the potential; and a synthesis with structural refinement that checks the calculation. Each layer is roughly three orders of magnitude more expensive than the one above it, and each is a proxy for the one below. The reference databases that anchor the whole stack were built for exactly this purpose [53].

The scale achieved at the top is real. Graph networks trained on relaxation trajectories, used to filter candidate compositions and structures, produced 2.2 million predicted structures of which about 380,000 were reported as stable, with 736 independently experimentally realised [54]. Read as a funnel, that is 2,989 generated structures per externally realised material. Diffusion models over the periodic crystal representation improved the proposal step itself, generating structures more than twice as likely to be new and stable as prior methods and more than ten times closer to the local energy minimum, with property conditioning on chemistry, symmetry and mechanical response [55,56].

The bottom of the stack tells a harder story, and it is where this article's thesis is most directly tested. An autonomous laboratory synthesised and characterised targets from these pipelines, reporting 41 novel compounds from 58 attempts over 17 days of continuous operation — 1.4 attempts per reported success, a remarkable throughput [57]. A detailed solid-state chemistry critique of that work then argued that the automated powder-diffraction analysis contained substantial errors and that the evidence for the claimed new compounds was insufficient, with several products better explained as known or disordered phases [58]. A parallel critique of the generative model's flagship experimental validation showed that the compound presented as novel is isostructural with a phase reported in the early 1970s and present in the training data, a recurring failure to recognise disordered crystals as known [59]. A commentary on the large-scale prediction work made the adjacent point that novelty and stability claims at that scale need to be read against what crystallography already knows [60].

None of this makes the underlying work wrong, and the field's own reviews treat computational design, synthesis planning and autonomous experimentation as one workflow for good reasons [61]. But it makes the shape of the problem unmistakable. The proxy layers scaled by six orders of magnitude; the verifier did not; and when an automated Rietveld refinement was pressed into service as a cheap stand-in for a trained crystallographer, the stand-in was where the errors entered. Self-driving laboratories that optimise a directly measurable physical quantity — thin-film conductance, say — have a much easier time, because there the instrument reading is the answer rather than an inference from it [62].

The proxy chain in materials discovery is longer than in any other section, and each link in it loses something. A generative model proposes a structure. A machine-learned interatomic potential estimates its energy. A density-functional relaxation refines that estimate. The relaxed energy is compared with the convex hull of known compounds to give a distance-to-hull, which is called stability. Stability is then treated as a claim that the compound can be made. Only the last step involves a furnace, and only the last step is the thing anybody wanted to know. Distance-to-hull is a statement about thermodynamics at zero temperature and zero pressure with no kinetics in it; a great many stable compounds have no known route and a great many made compounds are metastable. The word carries far more weight in these papers than the calculation supports.

The record verifier at the end of the chain has its own bias. Checking a proposed structure against the Inorganic Crystal Structure Database or the Materials Project asks whether the compound is already known [53], which is a well-defined question with a fast answer, and it is precisely the wrong question for a discovery claim: a match means the model reproduced the literature, and a miss means either a genuine novelty or a gap in the archive. The archives are also not uniform. They are dense in oxides and intermetallics that were interesting to twentieth-century solid-state chemistry and sparse elsewhere, so novelty rates measured against them partly report where crystallographers have historically looked.

Synthetic biology

The one place where the bench got cheap

Everywhere else in this article, bench verification costs between an hour and a month per candidate. Regulatory DNA is the exception, and the exception is the most important single fact in the article.

A massively parallel reporter assay synthesises a library of designed sequences with barcodes, transfects them together, sequences the output, and returns an activity measurement for every member of the library in one experiment [63]. The marginal cost of checking one more designed sequence is close to zero. This turns a bench verifier into something with simulator-like economics without turning it into a simulator: the measurement is still a measurement of the real thing.

The consequences are exactly what the framing predicts. Models trained on millions of measured random promoters learn the sequence-to-expression map well enough to design promoters with specified expression and to reason about the evolvability of natural regulatory DNA [64]. Cell-type-directed enhancer design produces synthetic enhancers with tissue specificity confirmed in vivo, in two independent systems reported in the same issue [65,66]. These are among the most convincing generative-design results in biology, and the reason is not that the generators are better than the ones used for proteins or crystals. It is that here the funnel is free.

Genome-scale design is the opposite extreme, and it has just been demonstrated. Genome language models trained across the tree of life [67,68] were prompted with a template phage and guided by predictors of genome architecture and host tropism; 302 designs were synthesised, 285 could be assembled, and 16 booted into viable bacteriophages with substantial sequence and structural novelty, one of which uses an evolutionarily distant packaging protein [14]. A cocktail of the generated phages overcame resistance in E. coli strains that the template phage could not. The same group has shown related design at gene scale [69].

The verifier here is a reboot assay: synthesise the genome, put it in a host, look for plaques. It cannot be pooled the way a reporter library can, because the readout is whether this particular genome runs. So the funnel stayed narrow and expensive, and the honest summary of the result is the one the authors give: a blueprint, at 5.30% viability, with the biosafety and biosecurity implications stated explicitly in the paper and the recommendation that safety and security professionals be consulted throughout the project lifecycle [14]. That recommendation is a verification argument too. The same asymmetry that makes generative design attractive — proposals are cheap and checking is not — means that screening regimes built on sequence homology to known agents can be blind to functional equivalents that share little sequence, which is the specific dual-use concern the field has been raising since generative chemistry made the analogous point about toxins [70].

The reason regulatory DNA stands out is worth stating plainly, because it is the strongest form of the argument this article is making. Nothing about the samplers used for enhancers and promoters is technically ahead of the samplers used for proteins or crystals; if anything they are simpler. What is different is the assay. A massively parallel reporter assay measures the transcriptional output of tens or hundreds of thousands of designed sequences in a single pooled experiment, reading them out by sequencing, so the marginal cost of checking one more design is close to the cost of adding one more barcode. That moves a bench verifier into territory where its economics resemble a simulator's: high fixed cost, near-zero marginal cost, and a batch size that makes a real hit rate measurable rather than estimable from a handful of constructs.

The consequence is that the field can do what the rest of this article says is hard. It can put a true verifier close enough to the sampling loop to iterate against it, and it can report hit rates with intervals narrow enough to distinguish one method from another. The leverage came from the assay format, not from the generative model, and the design lesson generalises: where a pooled, barcoded, sequencing read-out exists for a phenotype, generative design will look good, and where each candidate needs its own vessel it will not.

Engineering design

A simulator that is the objective, the check, and the reason the answer is only as good as the mesh

Structural and mechanical design is the domain where the numerical verifier has been trusted longest, and the trust is largely earned. Topology optimisation solves for a material distribution over a domain subject to loads and a volume fraction; the methods are mature, well characterised, and comparatively reviewed [71]. Generative models entered this field promising to replace the iterative solve with a single forward pass, and diffusion models over the density field outperform adversarial approaches on the standard benchmarks [72], within a broader literature of deep generative models for engineering design [73].

The critical review of that literature by the topology optimisation community is worth reading as a verification argument. Its objection is not that neural networks fail; it is that in this domain the finite-element solve is both the objective and the check, so a learned model that approximates the solve is being compared against a baseline that already computes the exact answer, and the comparison is almost always made without accounting for the cost of generating the training data with that same solver [74]. When the verifier is cheap and exact relative to the thing being learned, learning it is a compression exercise, not a discovery one. That can still be valuable — a surrogate that runs in milliseconds enables interactive design and large sweeps — but the value has to be argued in those terms.

The plate for this section records the field verifier as a physical test to failure, and marks the funnel as one: generated candidates are re-solved, not re-built. That is an accurate description of practice and also a limitation of it. The gap between a finite-element model and a manufactured part — residual stress, surface finish, tolerance stack-up, material anisotropy from the process — is not in the objective, so nothing in the generative loop is optimising against it.

Chip floorplanning is the interesting comparison case, because there the numerical verifier is unusually good. Placement quality is scored by commercial sign-off tools reporting wirelength, congestion and timing slack, and those tools are the same ones that gate the tape-out, so the proxy and the decision criterion coincide by construction [75]. The field verifier — silicon that meets timing at temperature — is reached once per design. Control problems on physical plant sit at the same end: a learned magnetic controller for tokamak plasmas was trained entirely in simulation and then validated on the actual machine, which is exactly the structure this article recommends and exactly as expensive as it sounds [76].

Engineering design is the section where the baseline question is sharpest, because the incumbent is unusually strong. Density-based topology optimisation has been solving the same problem for three decades, it is convergent, it comes with sensitivities, and on the canonical benchmarks it produces structures that are optimal to within the discretisation [71]. A generative model that produces a structure a few per cent worse, but instantly, is doing something genuinely useful; a generative model that produces a structure a few per cent worse and is compared against a topology optimiser stopped after twenty iterations is doing nothing at all. The review literature on generative design in engineering makes this complaint in almost these terms, noting that evaluation practice lags the modelling work [73,74].

The deeper problem is that the objective being optimised is not the objective the part has to meet. Compliance under a single load case is analytic and cheap; a real bracket must survive several load cases, a fatigue spectrum, a corrosion environment, a tolerance stack and a manufacturing process, and most of those are bench or field checks. The generative model is therefore optimising against the one term in the requirement that happens to be differentiable, which is a reasonable engineering compromise and a poor basis for a claim of superhuman design. Where the loop closes on something closer to the real objective — a placement whose wirelength and congestion are scored by the same tools that sign off the chip [75], a plasma controller whose reward is measured on the tokamak itself [76] — the results are correspondingly more durable, and both of those are reinforcement-learning systems with an unusually faithful verifier rather than generative models with a proxy.

CAD generation

The cheapest honest verifier in the article

Computer-aided design is where generative modelling meets a verifier that is exact, total, automatic and effectively free, and the result is the least contested set of claims in this survey.

The representational choice is what makes it work. Rather than generating geometry directly, these models generate the construction program: a sequence of sketches, extrusions, revolutions, fillets and booleans, which is how mechanical CAD is authored in the first place. The first generative model over this representation was trained on a corpus of parametric command sequences [77]; a companion dataset captured human design sequences with their construction history [78]; boundary-representation models generate the solid topology directly [79]; language-conditioned models generate the same programs from prose descriptions [80]; and reverse-engineering models emit executable code from point clouds [81].

In every case a geometry kernel runs the program. It either produces a valid, watertight, manufacturable solid or it raises an error, and it does so in milliseconds. This has three consequences that no other section of this article can claim. Invalid outputs are filtered before anyone measures anything, so validity rates near 100% are meaningful rather than definitional. The verifier can be placed inside the sampling loop, so rejected candidates cost nothing but compute. And the model's output is inspectable and editable by the engineer who receives it, because a construction program is the artefact the profession already uses.

The residual difficulty is that a valid solid is not a correct one. Chamfer distance and intersection-over-union against a reference solid say that the geometry matches; they say nothing about whether the part performs, fits its assembly, or can be made on the available machine. The kernel verifies syntax, not intent. That is a much smaller gap than the one in every other section, and it is the reason CAD generation is where a working engineer is most likely to encounter a generative model that simply works.

The honesty of the kernel has an underappreciated consequence: it relocates the difficulty rather than removing it. When validity is free, validity stops being the metric anybody cares about, and the reported numbers move to geometric agreement with a reference solid. Chamfer distance and intersection-over-union are proxies of exactly the kind this article keeps flagging — cheap, differentiable, and severe about the wrong thing. Two construction programs can score identically against a reference while one exposes a clean parameter that an engineer can drive and the other hard-codes every dimension, and it is the first that is worth having. The profession's actual criterion, whether the feature tree is editable in the way a downstream change will require, has no automated check at all.

The other relocation is upstream. A model that reliably converts an unambiguous specification into a valid solid makes the specification the bottleneck, and specifications in mechanical design are mostly tacit: they live in interface drawings, in what the shop can hold, in what the last revision of the assembly assumed. Text-conditioned CAD generation is therefore best read not as a design automation result but as an authoring speed-up, and it is a real one [80]. The reverse direction, in which a scan of an existing part is turned back into an editable construction program [81], is arguably the more valuable capability precisely because its verifier is the tightest in the article: the recovered program can be executed and its output compared, point for point, with the object that was measured.

Simulation

Learning the solver you are graded against

Learned simulators predict the next state of a discretised physical field: particle systems with graph networks [82], mesh-based dynamics with adaptive remeshing [83], turbulent flows where a learned correction lets a coarse grid track a fine one [84]. The verifier is a converged run of the numerical solver being replaced, which means the ceiling on measured accuracy is the solver's accuracy and the training data is the solver's output.

This arrangement has a specific pathology, and it has been measured. A systematic review of machine learning applied to fluid-related partial differential equations found that of the articles claiming to outperform a standard numerical method, 79% (60 of 76) compared against a weak baseline, and that outcome-reporting and publication biases were widespread [85]. The failure is not fabrication. It is that the verifier is chosen by the same people choosing the method, from a family with a free accuracy parameter, and that a solver run at a resolution nobody would use in production is very easy to beat.

The methodological remedy is the one this article keeps arriving at: make the verifier external. Physics-informed formulations attempt something adjacent by putting the residual of the governing equation into the loss, so that the model is penalised for violating the physics rather than for disagreeing with a particular solver [86]. That converts part of the verification into an analytic check, which is a genuine gain in class, though it buys accuracy on the residual rather than on the quantity of interest, and it does not by itself resolve stability over long rollouts.

What a fair comparison would require is not mysterious, and the review that documented the problem also documented the remedy [85]. The baseline must be a numerical method that a practitioner would actually use, at the resolution they would use it, with the same error tolerance on the same quantity of interest, and the comparison must be at matched wall-clock rather than matched grid. That last condition is the one most often dropped, and dropping it is what makes a learned simulator look fast: a neural network evaluated on a coarse grid is being compared against a solver run on a fine one, and the speed-up being reported is mostly the grid.

There is also a stability question that accuracy metrics do not capture. A learned update rule is a discrete dynamical system, and nothing in the training objective constrains its long-run behaviour. Single-step error can be very small while rollouts drift, blow up, or settle into an attractor that has no physical counterpart, which is why the mesh-based and particle-based simulators go to some trouble with noise injection and remeshing during training [83,82] and why the turbulence work is framed as a learned correction to a coarse solver rather than a replacement for it [84]. Keeping a real solver in the loop is not a concession; it is what converts an unbounded extrapolation into a bounded one, and it is the same move as putting a geometry kernel inside the CAD sampler.

The class boundary here is unusually clear. Where the learned model replaces the solver entirely, the verifier is the solver and the ceiling is the solver's own accuracy on the solver's own distribution. Where the learned model corrects a solver that still runs, part of the verification is analytic again, and the claim being made is correspondingly more modest and more likely to survive.

Surrogate models

Why accuracy buys horizon additively, not multiplicatively

A surrogate is a model queried in place of a solve, usually many times, inside an outer loop that is doing optimisation, uncertainty quantification or forecasting. Operator learning generalises this from functions to mappings between function spaces: Fourier neural operators learn resolution-invariant solution operators [87], and branch-trunk architectures learn nonlinear operators with universal-approximation guarantees [88]. The verification question for a surrogate is not whether it is accurate on its training distribution — it is, that is what training does — but whether it is accurate at the specific point in parameter space where the design sweep has wandered, and that point is by construction the one nobody solved.

Autoregressive rollout adds a second problem with an exact answer. A surrogate with relative one-step error ε, rolled into a system that amplifies perturbations by a factor a per step, accumulates error ε(aT−1)/(a−1). Setting a 10% tolerance and solving for the horizon gives a result that ought to be better known. When a = 1 — a neutral system that neither grows nor damps perturbations — the horizon is proportional to 1/ε, so a ten-fold better surrogate buys a ten-fold longer rollout. When a > 1, the horizon becomes logarithmic in ε and each decade of accuracy buys a fixed additive number of steps: 231.4 steps at a = 1.01, 116.3 at 1.02, and 24.2 at 1.10, against measured values of 226.7, 115.1 and 24.1 in the asymptotic regime. Concretely, at a = 1.02 a thousand-fold better surrogate extends the usable rollout from 55.5 steps to 383.9 — a factor of 6.92, not 1000. To double the horizon of such a surrogate you must square its accuracy.

Whether accuracy compounds or merely adds is therefore a property of the system, not of the model, and it is the single most useful thing to establish before investing in a surrogate. It also explains why medium-range weather emulation has been such a favourable case. Graph-based forecasting [89], three-dimensional transformer architectures [90], diffusion-based ensembles [91] and Earth-system foundation models [92] all operate at lead times where the atmosphere's error growth is still modest, on a state space where a reanalysis archive provides a dense, standardised record verifier [93], with a shared benchmark that fixes the evaluation protocol so that comparisons are between models rather than between evaluation choices [94], and an operational-context statistical assessment run by a forecasting centre rather than by the modellers [95].

That combination — a public archive, a fixed protocol, an outside assessor, and a verifier that arrives on a schedule whether or not you are ready — is as close as any field in this article gets to the institutional arrangement that made structure prediction credible. The verifier for a forecast is the weather at the lead time. You cannot argue with it, you cannot tune it, and it costs exactly one lead time.

Weather is the exception that makes the rule legible, and it is worth being explicit about why. Every other surrogate in this section is graded against the numerical model it replaces, so its accuracy is bounded by that model and its errors are correlated with it. Weather forecasting is graded against the atmosphere. Forecasts are scored, after the fact, against what actually happened, on a schedule nobody controls, by centres that did not build the models and have been running the same scoring protocols since long before machine learning arrived [95]. The reanalysis used for training is itself an assimilation of observations rather than a free-running simulation [93], and the community maintains a common evaluation harness so that competing systems are compared on identical terms [94].

That is a record verifier with the two properties that matter most: it is external to every group being evaluated, and it refreshes continuously, so a model cannot be tuned against it without the tuning showing up as degraded performance on the next fortnight of weather. The result is that headline claims in this corner of the field have held up under scrutiny in a way that claims in most other corners have not. The lesson is not that atmospheric dynamics is easier than protein folding or crystal growth. It is that meteorology built the scoreboard first and the models second, and that the order matters.

Digital twins

A verifier with a sample size of one

A digital twin is not a simulation of a class of things; it is a set of virtual constructs coupled to one particular physical counterpart, updated by data from that counterpart, with predictive capability, informing decisions that realise value — and the bidirectional flow between virtual and physical is what distinguishes it from modelling [96,97]. Formalised probabilistically, the twin is a graphical model over the asset's state, parameters, observations and control inputs, updated as the asset ages [98], and the engineering problem is scaling this from bespoke artisanal constructions to industrial practice [99].

This definition creates a verification problem with no analogue elsewhere in the article: the population is one. There is no held-out set of other instances of this bridge, this engine, this patient. A twin's predictive claims can be checked against the asset's own future, which arrives at the rate of one operating interval per operating interval, and against hindcasts over its own past, which is a record verifier constructed from a single trajectory and therefore weakly independent of the fitting.

Generative methods enter twins in two roles. They provide the fast surrogates that make real-time inference tractable — the Gordon Bell-winning tsunami early-warning twin performs real-time Bayesian inference at extreme scale for exactly this reason [100] — and they provide priors over states and parameters that data alone cannot identify. Both roles inherit the verification properties of their class, which is why the National Academies report puts verification, validation and uncertainty quantification at the centre of its research agenda rather than treating them as a downstream concern [96].

The practical consequence is that a twin's trustworthiness is not established by its accuracy on anything. It is established by whether its uncertainty is honest at the operating point where a decision will be taken, and that is an argument about the model's structure and its data assimilation, not a score.

What can actually be verified about a twin, given a population of one, is narrower than the literature's enthusiasm suggests, and it is worth setting out. Three things are checkable. The components can be validated on populations: the material model, the sensor model, the surrogate all came from somewhere with more than one instance, and they can be held to ordinary standards. The assimilation can be checked by hindcast, by withholding a stretch of the asset's own history and asking the twin to reconstruct it, which is weak evidence because the same trajectory informed the fit but not no evidence. And the twin's uncertainty can be checked for calibration against whatever routine measurements the asset produces, which is the only one of the three that continues to accumulate evidence for as long as the asset runs.

What cannot be checked is the counterfactual that the twin exists to support: what would have happened had the decision gone the other way. That is the same structure as a clinical decision made for a single patient, and it is why the discipline's serious treatments put verification, validation and uncertainty quantification at the centre rather than reporting predictive accuracy [96], and why the probabilistic formulations insist on carrying a full posterior over the asset's state rather than a point estimate [98]. A twin that is confidently wrong at the operating point is worse than no twin, because it will be believed.

Scientific hypothesis generation

Cheap ideas, expensive tests, and what a list is actually worth

Literature-based discovery is old: reading two disconnected literatures and proposing the link between them produced a testable clinical hypothesis in 1986 [101], and unsupervised embeddings of the materials literature were later shown to encode latent structure-property knowledge that anticipated subsequent discoveries [102]. Language models made proposal generation essentially free, and a large body of agentic systems now does it at scale [103,104,105].

The verification evidence here is unusually good, because someone paid for it. A controlled study recruited over 100 NLP researchers to write ideas and blind-review both human and machine ideas, and found that machine-generated ideas were rated significantly more novel than expert ideas, though slightly weaker on feasibility [106]. The same authors then did the expensive thing: 43 expert researchers each executed a randomly assigned idea, spending over 100 hours implementing it and writing it up, after which the projects were blind-reviewed again. The scores of the machine-generated ideas fell significantly more than those of the expert-written ideas on every metric — novelty, excitement, effectiveness and overall — closing the ideation-stage gap, with rankings flipping on several metrics so that human ideas scored higher [107].

That is the cleanest demonstration in this article of the difference between a proxy and a verifier. Blind expert rating of a proposal is a proxy. Execution is the verifier. They disagreed, and they disagreed in the direction that a cheap proxy always disagrees: in favour of whatever is optimised against it.

There are real successes with genuine bench verification. A hypothesis-generating system's top-ranked proposal for how a family of phage satellites spreads matched a mechanism that a laboratory had already confirmed experimentally but not published [108], and an agent team designed nanobody candidates for SARS-CoV-2 variants that were then validated experimentally [109]. Agentic systems coupled to real instruments and tools — a language model driving a cloud laboratory to plan and execute reactions [110], or one orchestrating chemistry tools [111] — are verified by the chemistry actually working, which is the right standard and the reason those results are narrower than the systems' generality would suggest. The oldest version of this argument is a robot scientist that formulated and tested its own hypotheses about yeast metabolism with no human intervention [112]; that it was possible in 2009 and is still notable is itself informative about where the constraint lives.

What, then, is a generated list of hypotheses worth? Not its length, and not its average plausibility. Take sixteen candidate hypotheses of which one is true, and experiments that answer yes/no questions about arbitrary subsets. Testing the current favourite — the confirmatory strategy, and the one a ranked list invites — costs 8.50 experiments in expectation under a flat prior, against 4.00 for an optimally discriminating sequence: a factor of 2.125. Under a peaked prior in which one idea is clearly favoured, the same comparison is 4.73 against 3.43, a factor of only 1.379. The value of a hypothesis generator is the probability mass it concentrates, not the plausibility it spreads. Sixteen equally good ideas cost 3.77 more experiments than one good one plus a test that halves the field — and at 100 hours per execution, that difference is nine working weeks.

The exception that proves the framing is program and proof search, where the verifier is analytic. A search that evolves programs scored by an evaluator function found new constructions in extremal combinatorics and improved bin-packing heuristics [113]; its successor improved on a 50-year-old bound for small matrix multiplication and made progress on dozens of open problems [114]; reinforcement learning over tensor decompositions found faster matrix multiplication algorithms [115]; and formal systems reached olympiad-level geometry and then medal-level performance across olympiad problems with the proofs machine-checked in Lean [116,117]. Millions of candidates are generated per accepted improvement, and nobody minds, because each check costs microseconds and cannot be wrong. This is what a field looks like when the verifier is free. It is also why these results transfer poorly as evidence about science generally: the formal-mathematics results in particular required expert human formalisation of the problem statements before the system could work on them, which is the step that in every other domain in this article is called the experiment.

When the verifier is the thing that fails

Leakage, weak baselines, and audit by proxy

Everything above assumes the verifier is at least honest. The characteristic failures of this field are failures of that assumption, and they have a common structure: a cheap thing is substituted for an expensive thing, the substitution is not stated, and the resulting number is reported as though the expensive thing had happened.

Leakage. The most common form is a test set that is not independent of the training set. Consider a library organised into families — scaffolds, homologues, crystal prototypes — with 200 families of 25 members, labels agreeing within a family 94% of the time, and a marginal base rate of 50%. A pure nearest-neighbour lookup, which has learned nothing, scores 94.00% under a random split and 50.00% under a family-held-out split. The gap of 44.0 points comes entirely from how the data were cut. It does not require large families either: at three members per family and a 50/50 split, 75.0% of test items still have a family-mate in training and the lookup still scores 83.00%. This is the measured mechanism behind the finding that most ligand-based classification benchmarks reward memorisation rather than generalisation [118], the reason scaffold splits became standard in molecular property prediction [119], and one instance of a leakage problem documented across machine-learning-based science as a whole [120].

Weak baselines. The second form is comparing against a version of the classical method that nobody would use. This is the documented pattern in machine learning for fluid-related partial differential equations, at 79% of the articles that claim to beat a numerical method [85], and its analogue in materials is the misidentification of known disordered phases as novel ordered compounds because the automated analysis was not held to the standard a crystallographer would apply [58,59].

Optimising the proxy. The third form is structural and unavoidable: once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure [121]. The PROXY field on every plate in this article marks where this pressure acts. Docking scores optimised directly produce poses that fail explicit physical-validity checks [38]. Property predictors optimised directly produce molecules that cannot be made [48]. Blind novelty ratings optimised directly produce ideas that lose their advantage on execution [107]. In each case the model is doing exactly what it was asked.

Audit at the meta-level. The failure mode extends to claims about the field. A widely covered preprint reporting that an AI tool in a materials laboratory produced 44% more materials and 39% more patents was cited by major outlets and praised by senior economists before the institution concluded it had no confidence in the provenance, reliability or validity of the data or in the veracity of the research, and formally requested withdrawal [122,123]. The lesson is not about that paper. It is that the productivity of generative AI in science is itself a claim that requires a verifier, and that the field's demand for good news about itself is a base rate to be reckoned with, exactly as in section 4.

The mechanism by which these failures occur is not fraud and rarely even carelessness. It is that a proxy which is used to select becomes a proxy that has been optimised, and a measure under optimisation stops measuring [121]. Every step in a modern pipeline applies gentle selection pressure to the evaluation: the split is chosen, the filter threshold is tuned, the target list is assembled from targets where something worked before, the failed campaign is not written up. None of these is a decision anybody would defend individually, and together they can manufacture an effect out of nothing. The memoriser experiment in the appendix is the extreme case in miniature — a lookup table that has learned nothing scores 94.00% under a random split and 50.00%, which is chance, under a family-held-out split, a gap of 44.0 points produced entirely by the choice of split.

The defence is structural rather than moral. Fix the evaluation before the method is chosen; make the split reflect the generalisation actually being claimed; report the denominator, including the campaigns that were abandoned; and where possible let somebody else hold the answer key. The corners of this article where results have proved durable — blinded structure assessment, operational weather scoring, a geometry kernel that does not care who wrote the program — are all corners where one or more of those conditions was already institutionalised before the generative models arrived.

The ledger

Twenty-one plates, sorted by what a check costs

The plates collected below are sorted by the cost of one true verification. Read in that order the field resolves into something quite orderly, and three claims can be read directly off the table.

Uncontested results cluster at the cheap end. The two analytic-verifier plates — CAD program synthesis and program and proof search — carry the results in this article that no specialist disputes, and they are the two where millions of candidates per success is a non-issue. Moving down the ledger, disagreement rises monotonically with the price of a check: numerical-verifier plates carry methodological disputes about baselines, bench plates carry disputes about whether the thing was made, and the two field plates carry a single readout each.

The one bench plate that behaves like a simulator is the one where the assay was pooled. Regulatory DNA design sits four orders of magnitude below every other bench plate, not because the biology is easier but because a reporter library measures the whole design set in one experiment. It is also, not coincidentally, the plate with the strongest experimental confirmation relative to the sophistication of its generative model. This is the article's central practical claim, and the ledger is where it is visible: the leverage is in the assay format, not the sampler.

Every plate has a proxy, and the proxy is where the field's errors live. Only one plate of twenty-one — program and proof search — needs no proxy, because its verifier is already cheap enough to serve as the objective. On the other twenty, something cheap stands in for something expensive during the optimisation, and section 14 is a catalogue of what happens next.

The prescription follows without much rhetoric. If you want to make a domain in this table move, the question to ask is not which generative architecture to adopt. It is: what is the cheapest measurement that could genuinely falsify a candidate here, can it be pooled, can it be automated without a non-expert's judgement standing in for an expert's, and can the protocol be fixed by someone who is not you. Structure prediction had that arrangement before the models arrived, which is why the result was believed within a year [18,19]. Weather has it now [94]. Regulatory DNA has it because of an assay format [63]. The rest of the table is a list of places where somebody could build one.

Read down the cost column and three practical rules fall out of it. First, the value of a better generator is bounded above by what a check costs, so the return on model improvement is largest exactly where checking is cheapest — and where checking is dear, the same effort spent making the check cheaper, or making a cheap screen more severe, dominates it. Second, a cheap proxy is worth having even when it is poor, provided its errors are not the generator's errors: a ranker at AUC 0.7998 cuts expensive checks per confirmed hit from 10,000 to 688.6, a factor of 14.5, and pays for it by discarding nine tenths of the true actives, which is a trade worth making whenever the expensive check dominates the budget. Third, the batch size of the true verifier is a design parameter and usually the most important one in the system; the pooled assay is the reason the strongest bench-verified result in this article is a bench-verified result at all.

None of this is an argument against generative models in science. It is an argument about where to expect them to pay. The plates that are settled are settled because somebody could check the output cheaply, severely and independently. The plates that are contested are contested because the check is expensive, and the expensive check has been quietly replaced by a cheap one that answers a different question. That substitution, and not any property of the samplers, is what distinguishes the two halves of this ledger.
