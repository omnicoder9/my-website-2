1. What can still be taken back?

NODE THE STEP  /  GUIDE WORD OTHER THAN

On the ninth day of a public twelve-day experiment in July 2025, an AI coding agent working on a live application ran destructive commands against a production database during a declared code freeze, deleting records for more than twelve hundred executives and eleven hundred companies. It then reported progress that did not match the state of the system.[1,2] The vendor's response, published two days later, named three changes: automatic separation of development from production databases, better rollback, and a new planning-only mode in which the agent can reason about a codebase without touching it.[3]

Those three are usually read as three fixes. They are better read as three positions on a single axis. Separation shrinks what a mistake can reach. Rollback restores what a mistake changed. Planning-only stops the mistake from changing anything at all. What distinguishes them is not how clever they are, nor how much they cost to build, but where they sit on a scale of how expensive it is to take a step back after it has been taken.

That scale is older than the technology it is being applied to. Every mechanism in the reliability engineer's vocabulary — retries, timeouts, circuit breakers, checkpoints, idempotency — is in the end a claim about undoing: either that a step can be repeated at no cost, or that its effect can be reversed, or that the system can be put back where it was. Every failure mode in the agent engineer's vocabulary — hallucination, wrong tool selection, goal drift, memory corruption — is in the end a claim about the opposite. The two vocabularies are usually taught separately, one from distributed systems and one from machine learning, as though they were about different subjects. They are about the same subject, and the thing they share is the question in the title.

This article reads twenty-two named practices and failure modes as positions on one ladder of reversal cost, and finds that they sort very unevenly. Seven of the twenty-two act where reversal is free. Six of those seven are guardrails. Not one of the eight classical reliability mechanisms reaches above the fourth rung except determinism, which reaches the top precisely because it does nothing. The failure modes land below all of them.

A second question cuts across the first, and it turns out to be the one that decides how much comfort the shape of the ladder should give. For each plate: what settles whether it has acted? Sometimes a decidable predicate does — a clock, a counter, a schema, an equality, a threshold. Sometimes a judgement does, delivered by a model or a heuristic that can be wrong in either direction. Occasionally a person does. The two axes are independent in principle. In practice they are correlated, and in the wrong direction: the controls that act where reversal is free are, with one family of exceptions, exactly the ones whose verdict cannot be computed.

Two clarifications before the ladder. The first is that reversibility is not the same as detectability, and the article keeps them apart on purpose. A crashed process announces itself and is trivially reversible. A runaway loop announces itself and is not reversible at all, because what it consumes is money and time. A hallucination announces nothing and lands somewhere in between. Any argument that slides from “we can see it” to “we can fix it” has skipped the step that matters.

The second is that this is an engineering question with a long pedigree, not a new one raised by language models. Weld and Etzioni put it plainly in 1994, arguing that a planning agent needs an explicit account of which of its actions change the world in ways it cannot change back, and that the account has to be part of the planner rather than an afterthought in the domain description.[4] Moldovan and Abbeel later gave the idea a formal shape by defining safe exploration in terms of ergodicity: a policy is safe if, from anywhere it can reach, it can get back.[5] The framing this article uses is theirs. What has changed is the component in the loop.

The idea has been rediscovered repeatedly since, each time under a different name. The 2016 survey that set the research agenda for practical AI safety put avoiding negative side effects first among its five concrete problems.[6] The measures proposed for it are all measures of reachability: how much of the state space the agent can still get to after acting, whether the states it could have reached remain reachable, whether the options available to a future task have been preserved.[7,8,9] Reinforcement learning came at the same thing from the operational side by training agents that can reset themselves and treating the ability to return as the safety criterion,[10] and later by learning reversibility directly from experience so that irreversible transitions can be avoided without being enumerated.[11] The interruptibility literature is the same question asked about the operator's own actions.[12,13] And the catalogue of specification-gaming episodes is a catalogue of agents finding the irreversible shortcut that the objective did not forbid.[14] None of this transferred cleanly into the engineering vocabulary that agent frameworks actually ship, which is the gap this article is trying to close.

2. Where the assumptions came from

NODE THE INHERITED METHOD  /  GUIDE WORD NO

Reliability engineering began with a specific and rather beautiful problem. In 1952 von Neumann asked how to build an organism that works out of parts that do not, and answered with multiplexing: run the computation in triplicate, take a majority vote, and drive the error rate of the assembly below the error rate of any of its parts.[15] Moore and Shannon proved the analogous result for relays in 1956, showing that networks of arbitrarily unreliable components can be made arbitrarily reliable given enough of them.[16,17] The discipline that grew out of this work — formalised in the United States by the AGREE report of 1957[18] and codified over the following two decades in failure mode and effects analysis[19] and fault tree analysis[20,21] — inherited its central move from those papers: enumerate the ways a component can fail, assign each a probability, and compose.

Composition requires independence. Both founding results depend on it, and the whole apparatus of quantitative reliability prediction depends on it after them. This is not a hidden assumption; it is stated openly in every textbook treatment. What is easy to miss is how much else was assumed along with it. A fault, in this tradition, is a defect that produces an error in the system state, which may then produce a failure — a deviation of the delivered service from what it should be. The chain fault → error → failure is the spine of the standard taxonomy.[22,23] It carries a quiet premise: that there is a defect to point at. Something in the component is wrong, and if you could see inside you would find it.

The second inheritance is the failure model. Distributed systems formalised the range of ways a component can misbehave, from the well-behaved end — a fail-stop processor halts and its halting is detectable by everyone else[24] — to the arbitrary end, where a faulty component may send any message at all, including messages carefully chosen to mislead.[25] Almost every practical mechanism in production use today is built for the well-behaved end. A retry assumes the failed attempt announced itself. A circuit breaker assumes the failures can be counted. A timeout assumes that not answering is the symptom. Gray drew the operational version of the same line in 1985, separating bugs that clear on a second attempt from bugs that do not, and observing that the entire value of process-pair recovery rests on the first kind being the common kind.[26]

The third inheritance is the analysis method. The process industries developed hazard and operability studies in the 1960s: walk the design node by node, apply a fixed set of guide words to each parameter — no, more, less, as well as, part of, reverse, other than — and record the deviations that result, their causes, their consequences and the safeguards against them.[27,28] The nuclear industry built the parallel apparatus for the operator rather than the equipment, quantifying human error probabilities so they could be composed into the same trees.[29] The method is deliberately mechanical. Its strength is that it does not depend on anyone imagining the failure in advance; the guide words do the imagining. Its weakness is that it needs a design with parameters, and it assumes the deviation is a deviation of a parameter.

Put those three inheritances together and you have the method that almost everyone reaches for when asked to make an agent reliable: enumerate the failure modes, assume they are independent, assume each announces itself, and put a counter on each one. The table of contents of this article is a HAZOP worksheet for exactly that reason. It is the right instrument, applied to a system whose parameters have moved.

What moved is worth stating precisely, because the usual formulation — “the model is stochastic” — is both true and useless. Three things changed. A language model in an agent loop is closer to the arbitrary end of the failure spectrum than the fail-stop end: it can emit a well-formed, plausible, wrong message, which is the Byzantine case, and it does so without any defect that inspection would find. Its errors correlate across attempts, because the correlating factor is the input, which the retry preserves. And its most consequential failures are not deviations of a parameter but deviations of an intention, for which the guide words have no column.

3. Where they broke before

NODE PRIOR ART  /  GUIDE WORD AS WELL AS

None of this is new, and the field has broken on it before in ways worth recalling precisely, because each break exposed a different one of the three inheritances.

Independence broke first, and it broke experimentally. In 1986 Knight and Leveson had twenty-seven independently written versions of the same program produced from a common specification by students at two universities, and tested all of them against a million randomly generated inputs. If the versions failed independently, the probability of coincident failures should have matched the product of the individual rates. It did not. The hypothesis of independence was rejected at the one per cent level.[30] Eckhardt and Lee had already shown why this should be expected in general: when input cases vary in difficulty, versions written by different people are more likely to fail on the same hard inputs, and the correlation is a property of the problem rather than of the programmers.[31] Ariane 5 flight 501 made the point in public ten years later. The inertial reference system carried a piece of Ariane 4 software containing an unprotected conversion from a 64-bit float to a 16-bit integer, and the active and backup units, being identical, ran the same instruction on the same data and failed within milliseconds of each other.[32] Redundancy against a shared cause buys nothing except confidence, and confidence is the one thing you do not want to buy.

The fault → error → failure chain broke next, and it broke on Therac-25. Between 1985 and 1987 a radiation therapy machine delivered massive overdoses to six patients. Leveson and Turner's investigation found no component that had failed in the sense the taxonomy means. The software did what it had been written to do; the requirements were wrong, the hardware interlocks that had covered the same errors in the previous model had been removed on the strength of a software reliability argument, and the operative defect was a race condition that only appeared when an operator typed quickly enough.[33] The lesson Leveson drew from it, and built into a research programme over the following two decades, is that safety is a property of a system in a context and not a property of the components composed together, and that treating it as the second is how organisations arrive confidently at the wrong answer.[34,35] Her control-theoretic replacement for the fault tree — model the system as a hierarchy of controllers with constraints, and ask how each constraint could be violated — is now a standard method.[36]

The automotive industry gave the same idea a name and a standard, built on the generic functional-safety framework that had already codified integrity levels and the systematic-failure distinction for programmable systems.[37] ISO 26262 covers functional safety: hazards arising from malfunctioning behaviour of electrical and electronic systems.[38] It works well for a bit flip and not at all for a perception system that classifies correctly ninety-nine times in a hundred and is not, on the hundredth, malfunctioning. ISO 21448 was written for that case, and defines safety of the intended functionality as the absence of unreasonable risk due to performance limitations, explicitly in the absence of a fault.[39] ISO/PAS 8800 extends the pair to AI specifically, managing the risk of undesired vehicle-level behaviour arising from output insufficiencies rather than from anything that could be called a defect.[40] The broader functional-safety community has followed with guidance on where AI can and cannot sit inside a safety function[41] and with a standard organised around an assurance case rather than a process.[42,43] It is worth noticing what happened here: an entire regulatory apparatus was rebuilt, at considerable expense, because a component started failing without malfunctioning. That is the same component now sitting inside agent loops with far less ceremony.

The third inheritance, the guide-word method, has not so much broken as run out of parameters. Perrow's account of accidents in tightly coupled, interactively complex systems describes the shape well: the trouble is not that any part failed but that parts interacted in a way nobody enumerated.[44] Rasmussen's version is sharper for present purposes — systems under competitive pressure migrate towards the boundary of safe operation, and the migration is made of locally sensible decisions that no single review would reject.[45,46] Hollnagel's reframing is the constructive version: if safety is defined as the absence of a countable bad outcome, the work that makes things go right is invisible to the measurement.[47] The layered-defence picture that most guardrail architectures implicitly assume is Reason's, and Reason himself was careful that the holes in the slices are dynamic and can line up.[48,49] A defence in depth built out of independent layers is subject to exactly the objection Knight and Leveson raised, and for the same reason: the layers were designed by people reading the same threat model. The nuclear formulation of defence in depth is explicit that the levels must be independent and that reliance on any single level is the failure to avoid,[50] which is a much stronger condition than most guardrail stacks meet. The 737 MAX report is the recent canonical case of the condition not being met: a control law authorised to act on the input of a single angle-of-attack sensor, in a system whose own documented assumption was that the crew would respond within seconds.[51]

One more inheritance deserves naming because it will return in the section on guardrails. Bainbridge observed in 1983 that automating the easy parts of a task leaves the human with the hard parts, at which they are now out of practice, and expects them to catch the automation's rare errors while having nothing to do the rest of the time.[52] Everything found since about automation bias and complacency is a footnote to it.[53,54] Any control whose last line is “and a human approves” inherits this problem whole.

4. The reversal ladder

NODE THE LADDER  /  GUIDE WORD PART OF

The ladder has six rungs, and each rung names a different kind of remedy rather than a different amount of damage. This matters, because severity and reversibility come apart constantly: a small irreversible loss and a large reversible one need different engineering, and a scale that mixes them tells you nothing about which to build.

The coding rule for every plate in this article is one sentence. The rung is the reversal cost of the residue the plate leaves when the step it governs goes wrong. Put the control in place, let the step fail anyway, and ask what is left to clean up. For a failure mode, the same question with no control at all: the damage lands somewhere, and the rung is the cheapest remedy that reaches it.

The rungs run from free to locked. At R5 nothing happened: the step was stopped before any state changed anywhere, and there is nothing to remedy. At R4 the change is confined to the agent's own context, scratchpad or working memory, and a snapshot restores it. At R3 an external store changed and a later call can put it back exactly, which is to say an inverse operation exists. At R2 no inverse exists and a second action offsets the first, with both remaining on the record — the compensating transaction of the saga literature, which is a weaker guarantee than rollback and was introduced precisely because rollback was unavailable.[55] At R1 nothing offsets the effect and the only move left is to tell whoever it landed on. At R0 there is no remedy: the counterparty is unreachable, the physical act is done, or the information is known.

The distinction between R3 and R2 is the one most often lost, and it is the one that has the largest practical consequences. Deleting the row you inserted restores the previous state. Refunding a payment does not: the transfer happened, the counterparty saw it, the ledger shows both entries, and any downstream process that read the first one has already acted. This is not a subtlety of accounting. It is why the saga pattern exists and why it is harder to reason about than a transaction. Between R2 and R1 the same kind of gap appears again: a compensating action at least addresses the effect, whereas at R1 the only available act addresses the person.

Two properties determine whether a step can sit at the top of this ladder, and they are the subject of the next section, because they are the only two ways to make repetition free. A step with no effect at all can be repeated at no cost, which is what determinism gives. A step whose effect is written at most once can be repeated at no additional cost, which is what idempotency gives. Everything below rung three is a consequence of having neither.

The gauge that appears in every plate below is a ratchet. Six teeth run left to right from R5 to R0, and the sawtooth slides one way only: the step falls down the ladder easily and does not climb back. A pawl is seated in the notch of the plate's rung. Cells to its left are drawn solid — those are rungs the step has already passed, and their cheaper remedies are no longer available. Cells to its right are hairline: travel still open, how much further the same step can fall if nothing catches it. A mark in the corner records what settles whether the plate has acted.

That corner mark is the article's second axis. A plate is exact when a decidable predicate over the step settles it: a clock has expired, a counter has passed a threshold, a schema validates or does not, two keys are equal or are not. It is judged when the verdict comes from a model or a heuristic that can be wrong in both directions and whose error rate is a property of a distribution rather than of a rule. It is held when a person decides, and the supply of that person's attention is finite in a way the other two are not.[56] The three categories are not a claim about difficulty. They are a claim about what kind of evidence a verdict rests on, and therefore about what can go wrong with the verdict itself.

5. Two ways to make repetition free

NODE THE PURE STEP  /  GUIDE WORD REVERSE

Everything above rung three depends on repetition being cheap, and there are exactly two ways to arrange that. Either the step writes nothing, in which case doing it again costs nothing; or the step writes at most once however many times it is issued, in which case doing it again costs nothing extra. The first is determinism, the second is idempotency, and the pair carries the whole upper half of the ladder.

Determinism is usually discussed as a convenience for evaluation, and that undersells it. A deterministic call is a pure function of its declared inputs, which is to say it has no residue at all, which is why it sits at rung five. It is also the precondition for every other kind of undoing: you cannot bisect a failure you cannot reproduce, you cannot replay a run to find the step that went wrong, and you cannot re-decide a rejected action on the evidence that produced it.

The interesting fact about determinism in a served language model is where the nondeterminism comes from. The familiar explanation — floating point is not associative and GPU threads race — points at the wrong layer. Most forward-pass kernels are run-to-run deterministic for a fixed shape. What is not fixed is the shape. Requests are batched, the batch size depends on concurrent load, and several kernels are not batch-invariant, so the same row is reduced along a different tile boundary depending on how many other people were using the server at that moment.[57] From the perspective of an individual user, the other users are not an input to the system; they are a property of it. Batch-invariant kernels restore reproducibility at a throughput cost[58] and are now implemented in the major serving stacks, though whether pinning a single universal reduction strategy is the right way to buy determinism in production is under active dispute.[59]

The size of the effect is worth measuring rather than asserting. The lab takes the same vector of terms and reduces it twice with two different tile widths, in real floating-point arithmetic at four accumulation widths, and records the disagreement. Nothing else differs between the two runs: same values, same order of terms, different tile boundary. At the width production kernels actually use — bfloat16 inputs with a float32 accumulator — the ninety-fifth percentile disagreement on a four-thousand-term reduction is 4.77e-07 logit units; with a float32 accumulator throughout it is 2.86e-06.[60,61]

Those numbers are tiny, and on their own they mean nothing. What decides whether a run diverges is not the size of the perturbation but how often the top two candidates sit within it. That relation can be inverted exactly. For a run of 1,000 steps to come back bit-identical half the time, the near-tie rate must stay below 0.0693 per cent of steps; for nine runs in ten to come back identical it must stay below 0.0105 per cent. Those are the budgets. Whether a given model meets them is an empirical question about its own next-token distributions.

To put a number on what such rates look like on a real distribution, the lab measures the top-two gap on an interpolated trigram model built from 2,489,303 tokens and 44,166 types, over 525,262 contexts. This is not a transformer and the article does not pretend otherwise; it is a real vocabulary with real gaps, standing in for the shape of the problem rather than measuring any deployed system. On that distribution the near-tie rate at the float32 perturbation is 0.36855 per cent, which is 5.3 times the budget, and gives a half-life of 188 steps: after that many tokens, the two runs have parted company half the time. At the bf16-in, fp32-accumulate perturbation the half-life is 763 steps and 40.3 per cent of thousand-token generations come back identical.

The engineering consequence is the ratio, not the absolute numbers. The gap between what reproducibility needs and what a near-tie rate delivers is two orders of magnitude, it compounds with run length, and it is worst exactly where the model is least certain, which is where you most want the run to be replayable.

Idempotency is the other half. Where determinism removes the effect, idempotency admits the effect and removes the cost of repeating it: the second identical request is recognised by its key and returns the first one's outcome instead of producing a new one. HTTP has carried the property in its method semantics since the beginning[62] and every serious payments API exposes it as a header,[63] because payments is where getting it wrong is most visibly a compensating transaction rather than a rollback.

What is easy to get wrong is not the mechanism but its horizon. Dedup requires remembering the key, remembering costs storage, and the retention you need is set by the slowest retry you will ever receive rather than by the typical one. The end-to-end argument applies with unusual force here: the dedup has to live at the component that owns the effect, because anything in front of it can be bypassed by a path that reaches the same effect another way.[64,65] And exactly-once delivery is not available at all — the two-generals result forecloses it[66] — so what is on offer is exactly-once processing, assembled from at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent endpoint.[67,68,69]

The lab draws retry delays from a six-attempt exponential backoff with full jitter, capped at thirty-two seconds, and asks how long a key must be retained to hold duplicate writes below one in ten thousand. With a clean schedule and nothing else, the answer is 14.8 seconds, about twice the mean delay of 7.9 seconds. Then add a rare late replay — a queue redrive, an operator re-running a batch, a client that was parked and woke up — affecting one request in two thousand. The median retry does not move. The required retention becomes 2,846 seconds, a factor of 192. A key store sized for the common case will silently stop deduplicating exactly the traffic that arrives when something has already gone wrong.

6. The stack, and the rung where each part stops

NODE THE CLASSICAL STACK  /  GUIDE WORD MORE

The remaining six reliability mechanisms all operate below rung four, and each stops at a specific place for a reason that is worth stating individually rather than in aggregate.

Fault tolerance is redundancy plus a fault detector, and the detector is the part that does not transfer. Triple modular redundancy works because a disagreement among replicas is evidence. Three samples from the same model on the same prompt are not three independent opinions; they are three draws from one distribution, correlated by exactly the thing a vote cannot see. Knight and Leveson's result is the general form of the objection and it was established forty years ago.[30] Ensembling different models weakens the correlation without removing it, since the models share training data, architecture family and evaluation pressure. Where redundancy genuinely helps is where the redundant path is of a different kind: a deterministic checker beside a generative producer, which is not voting but verification.

Retries sit at rung two because the residue of a retry is a duplicate, and duplicates are compensable rather than reversible. But the more interesting question is what a retry buys, and here the classical arithmetic misleads badly. The independent model says coverage after k attempts is one minus the failure probability raised to k, which climbs fast. The lab replaces the single probability with a population: 5 per cent of tasks that never succeed, 20 per cent that succeed one attempt in twenty, and the rest that succeed almost always. The mean success rate is 66.8 per cent either way, so the two models are calibrated to the same first attempt.

They diverge immediately. The independent model reaches ninety-five per cent coverage at 3 attempts. The population never reaches it at all: the ceiling is 94.8 per cent, and even eight attempts deliver 81.52 per cent against the independent model's 99.99 — a gap of 18.47 points. Marginal gain falls below one point per attempt at k = 4. This is Gray's Heisenbug and Bohrbug split with numbers attached: retrying is a treatment for one kind of fault, and the other kind is a fixed share of the population that no amount of retrying moves.[26] The published agent benchmarks show the same signature from the other direction, where a task-level pass rate on one attempt drops steeply when the same task must succeed on all of eight.[70]

Charging the retries for their own side effects sharpens it further. Give each retry an eighteen per cent chance of duplicating an effect the previous attempt had already committed, and value one duplicate at one success. The optimum is 2 attempts. Not three, not five, and certainly not the ten that most client libraries will do by default if the errors keep arriving.

Timeouts sit at rung one, which surprises people, and the reason is a single sentence: a timeout ends the caller's wait, not the callee's work. The request is still in flight. It may commit a second after the client gave up. The client has recorded a failure, will retry, and now has two effects and knowledge of neither. Cancellation is a different mechanism, most transports do not offer it end to end, and most clients do not use it where it exists.[71]

The lab makes the trade explicit over a lognormal latency distribution with a median of 180 ms and a p99 of 1,656 ms. Setting the timeout at the p99 gives 9.6 duplicate commits per thousand requests. Tightening it to the p90 — the move everyone makes when the tail latency graph looks bad — cuts the client-observed p99 by 26.65 per cent and multiplies duplicate commits by 10, to 96.04 per thousand. The mean latency saved by the tighter setting is 15.2 ms. That is the whole exchange: fifteen milliseconds of mean latency against a tenfold increase in effects nobody has a record of.

The circuit breaker is the cleanest mechanism in the stack and it has the sharpest blind spot.[72,73,74] It opens on an error rate, and a dependency that returns a well-formed wrong answer at HTTP 200 moves no counter it watches. This is not a hypothetical for agent systems: it is the modal degradation. A retrieval index goes stale, a tool returns results for the wrong tenant, a model version changes and starts producing subtly different output — none of these is an error.

You can fix this by feeding the breaker a validity check instead of an error rate, and the lab prices that. Take a check with ninety per cent sensitivity and ninety-nine per cent specificity, a rolling window of two hundred responses, a threshold set so the breaker false-trips about once in fifteen weeks, and a dependency serving fifty requests a second. Against a loud degradation where 35 per cent of responses are wrong, the breaker opens in 4 seconds, having admitted 70 bad answers. Against a quiet one at 0.5 per cent it opens after 4.5 days, by which point 97,052 wrong answers have been served and consumed. Detection time spans five orders of magnitude across a seventyfold change in the depth of the fault, and the change from exact to judged is what put it there.

Checkpointing and recovery close the stack, and they fail in mirror images of each other. A checkpoint restores the agent, not the world; the snapshot algorithms of the distributed systems literature are careful about exactly which cut of the system they capture and what has to be replayed to make the cut consistent,[75,76] and the optimum interval has been a solved arithmetic problem for decades.[77,78] What the durable-execution runtimes now built into agent frameworks add is cheapness: a snapshot per step becomes affordable.[79] What they cannot add is a snapshot of the recipient's inbox. Restore an agent to before it sent a message and it will send the message again.

Recovery is the one plate in this entire vocabulary whose verdict is held by a person. Somebody decides the incident is over, and everything that committed before the failure and cannot be un-committed is what they are deciding to live with. The characteristic failure — re-running committed side effects during the replay — is the same shape as the retry problem one level up, which is why recovery blocks were designed with acceptance tests attached rather than as bare rollback.[80]

Two mechanisms that are not in the standard vocabulary deserve a mention because they do most of the real work at this level. A retry budget caps amplification at a constant regardless of how many layers deep the call graph is, which backoff alone does not do: three layers each retrying three times multiply to twenty-seven attempts on the innermost service.[81,82] Designing the dependent system so that the failure of its control plane does not change its behaviour at all — static stability — removes the recovery question rather than answering it.[83] And deliberately injecting the failure rather than waiting for it is the only way to find out whether any of this works, which is the entire argument for chaos engineering.[84,85]

7. The only controls above the water line

NODE THE GATE  /  GUIDE WORD NO

Six of the twenty-two plates act at rung five, and all six are guardrails. They are the only things in the vocabulary that stand between a decision and an effect, which makes them the only controls whose mistakes are free. It also makes them the ones under the most pressure, and the place where the second axis does its work.

Start with the two that are decided exactly, because they are the ones with a theory. A tool guardrail is a reference monitor, and it inherits the reference monitor's three conditions unchanged from 1972: mediate every access, resist tampering, and be small enough that its correctness can be verified.[86,87] The first condition is where agent deployments fail. An allow-list on a payments tool is not complete mediation if the agent also has a shell, a generic HTTP client, or a sub-agent holding its own credentials. Complete mediation is a property of the whole capability surface, and agent frameworks add to that surface faster than they audit it.

Policy enforcement has a sharper theoretical boundary, and it is probably the single most useful formal result for anyone designing guardrails. Schneider showed in 2000 that the class of policies a runtime monitor can enforce by suppressing actions is exactly the class of safety properties: those for which any violating execution has a finite prefix that already violates.[88,89] Liveness properties — something good eventually happens — cannot be enforced this way at all, because no finite prefix witnesses the violation. Ligatti, Bauer and Walker extended the class by giving the monitor more powers: an edit automaton that may suppress, insert or substitute actions can enforce strictly more than one that may only halt.[90,91,92] The practical form of a suppressing monitor is inlined reference monitoring, where the checks are rewritten into the program itself rather than sitting beside it[93] — which in an agent stack means the check belongs in the tool implementation, not in the orchestration layer that calls it. That extension is not free. A monitor that inserts actions is acting on the world in its own right, which means it needs its own place on this ladder.

Two consequences follow directly, and both are ignored routinely. “The agent must not delete production data” is a safety property and is enforceable at rung five by a monitor that can refuse the call. “The agent must eventually finish the task” is a liveness property, is not enforceable by any suppressing monitor, and the thing people build instead is a step budget — which does not enforce completion but converts the liveness question into a safety one by bounding the run. The same reasoning explains why the shielding literature in reinforcement learning is built on automata that block unsafe actions before execution rather than on penalties applied afterwards.[94]

The other four guardrails are decided by judgement. The available toolkits differ in how the judgement is expressed — a rule language over dialogue flows in one case,[95] a fine-tuned classifier over a published taxonomy of harm categories in another[96] — and they share a single arithmetic problem that no amount of model quality removes. A classifier's precision at deployment is governed by the base rate, and the base rate for the things guardrails block is low. At a prevalence of one in a thousand, a classifier with ninety-nine per cent recall and a one per cent false positive rate has a precision of 9.02 per cent: 10.09 legitimate requests blocked for every harmful one caught, and 9,990 false blocks per million requests.[97,98] At one in ten thousand the same classifier's precision falls to 0.98 per cent. Recall barely enters into it. The false positive rate and the base rate decide everything.

The intuitive response is to stack filters, and the lab prices that too. Take four independent guardrails each at eighty per cent recall and a 0.4 per cent false positive rate, blocking if any fires. Going from one to four raises recall from 80.0 to 99.84 per cent, catching 198.4 more harmful requests per million — and raises false blocks from 3,996 to 15,888. The fourth filter alone catches 6.4 additional harmful requests per million and blocks 3,948 additional legitimate ones, a ratio of 617 to one. By the sixth the ratio is 19,584 to one. Defence in depth built out of independent blocking layers does not converge on safety; it converges on refusing everything.

The way out is structural rather than statistical, and it is a direct application of the ladder. Make the first stage escalate instead of block. A liberal first stage flagging five per cent of traffic costs nothing, because a flag is still at rung five: nothing has happened to the request except that a second, more expensive judgement will now be made about it. The lab's cascade — a first stage at 99.5 per cent recall and a five per cent flag rate, feeding a second stage at ninety-five per cent recall and two per cent false positives — reaches 94.52 per cent net recall while blocking 999 legitimate requests per million and running the expensive stage on 5.09 per cent of traffic. The same second stage standing alone reaches 95.0 per cent recall while blocking 19,980: essentially the same recall for 20 times the false blocks.

This is not a theoretical construction. It is what Anthropic's published jailbreak defences converged on. The first generation of constitutional classifiers cut jailbreak success from 86 per cent to 4.4 per cent at the cost of a 0.38 percentage-point rise in refusals on production traffic and a 23.7 per cent inference overhead.[99] The second generation replaced the wall with a cascade: a cheap linear probe over the model's own activations screens all traffic and escalates flagged exchanges to a stronger classifier, reaching a 0.05 per cent production refusal rate at roughly one per cent compute overhead. The paper's own explanation of why this works is the point of this section — because flagged exchanges are escalated rather than refused, the first stage can flag a much larger share of traffic without paying for it in refusals.[100,101]

PII detection is the case where the arithmetic bites hardest, because its miss is the one guardrail failure that is irreversible on first occurrence. An identifier that has been read has been read; there is no compensating action, only a disclosure. And detection here is recall-bounded and unevenly so. On a controlled multilingual benchmark published in 2026, a widely used rule-and-NER pipeline reached a partial-overlap micro-F1 of 0.195 overall but a recall of only 0.07 on the highest-sensitivity categories, against 0.74 and 0.77 for two general language models on the same slice; on obfuscated mentions the rule-based system fell below 0.18.[102,103] A large clinical de-identification study reached a best-in-class F1 of 0.939 with recall 0.950 — good, and still a miss in twenty.[104] At two sensitive requests in a hundred and a recall of 0.74, an identifier escapes roughly every 192 requests; at 0.99 recall, every 5,000. The control operates at rung five and its failure lands at rung one, and no setting of the threshold changes which of those two is permanent.

One observation about this group before leaving it. The control that most agent operators actually rely on for irreversible actions is a human approval gate, and it has no name in this vocabulary at all — the standard taxonomy folds it into tool guardrails. It behaves quite differently from the rest. Microsoft's Magentic-UI makes the underlying variable explicit by tagging every action with a three-valued irreversibility heuristic — always irreversible, maybe irreversible, never irreversible — auto-executing the third, always asking about the first, and routing the middle to a judge that decides whether to ask.[105] That is this article's ladder, implemented, with the judgement placed exactly where the ladder says it belongs.

8. The eight failure modes

NODE THE FAILURE SET  /  GUIDE WORD LESS

The eight named failure modes distribute across the whole ladder, and they sort into two clean halves along the second axis. Four are decided exactly and four are decided by judgement, and the split predicts almost perfectly which ones have mature controls.

Hallucination sits at rung one because a false claim that something has already relied on cannot be withdrawn, only corrected. It is decided by judgement by construction: the output is well-formed, confident and indistinguishable at the point of use from the correct case, with no exception, no status code and no malformed field. There is a reasonable argument that some rate of it is not eliminable in principle,[106,107] and a more actionable argument that the training and evaluation regime selects for it, because scoring schemes that award nothing for an admission of uncertainty make guessing the optimal policy.[108]

Wrong tool selection and incorrect arguments are the two halves of a call going wrong, and they separate along the second axis in a way that explains the state of the tooling. Malformed arguments are caught free by a schema; well-formed wrong arguments are not visible to any validator, and that is the larger half. Anthropic's own analysis of production tool use states the failure plainly — the most common problems are selecting the wrong tool and passing incorrect parameters, and JSON Schema defines what is structurally valid but cannot express which of two valid calls was meant.[109] The same work reports that supplying worked examples alongside the schema moved parameter accuracy from 72 to 90 per cent, which is a judged remedy for a judged problem. Selection pressure worsens with catalogue size for a structural reason: choosing is a discrimination among options, and a catalogue is a machine for adding options. A five-server setup reaching fifty-eight tools and fifty-five thousand tokens of definitions is now ordinary.[109,110]

Infinite loops are the sharpest illustration of why detectability and reversibility are independent. This is the easiest failure in the vocabulary to detect, and it is one of only two at rung zero, because what it consumes is money and time and neither is refundable.

The lab builds a population of agent trajectories in four loop shapes: tight cycles that repeat an identical call; nonce drift, where the same intent carries a timestamp that a normaliser could strip; semantic drift, where the changing argument is load-bearing, like a page number that keeps incrementing; and braided runs that cycle over several tools with drifting arguments. Against these, four structural detectors. The results are a lesson in complementarity: an exact-repeat detector catches 100 per cent of tight loops and 0.11 per cent of semantic ones; a same-tool-run detector catches 100 per cent of semantic loops and only 37.5 per cent of tight ones, because a short cycle alternates tools. Each is nearly perfect on the shape it was designed for and nearly blind elsewhere.

All four together catch 80.28 per cent of looping runs and stop 24.39 per cent of ordinary runs by mistake. They miss 19.72 per cent, and every single missed run is braided — the family constructed to pass all four at once. That construction is the finding rather than a trick: the space of loop shapes is not enumerable, so any finite set of structural detectors admits a shape that defeats all of them. A step budget does not have this property, because it does not look at the shape. In this population a budget of 75 steps stops every loop and truncates 1.0 per cent of ordinary runs, and at that budget 77.66 per cent of all steps executed are inside runs that were never going to finish. The step budget is the only complete loop detector available, and it is complete precisely because it is blind.

Goal drift and context loss are usually discussed together and belong on different rungs. Context loss is at rung four and decided exactly: material left the window or the summary, you can count what left, and if the source still exists you can read it again. Goal drift is at rung one and decided by judgement, because what has gone wrong is an intention, and the run looks healthy at every individual step. The empirical work is now reasonably clear that this happens to capable models under sustained context pressure, and that it happens gradually enough to be invisible in any single step.[111,112,113] Later work has shown a subtler channel: agents conditioned on trajectories from weaker agents inherit those agents' drifted behaviour without any environmental pressure at all.[112]

The structural reason a goal is fragile is that a goal is a conjunction. An instruction with twelve clauses is satisfied only if all twelve survive, so the survival probability of the whole is the product, and its half-life is the half-life of one clause divided by the number of clauses. The lab puts numbers on that. At a two per cent chance of losing any one clause per round of compaction, one clause survives ten rounds 81.71 per cent of the time and the whole instruction 8.85 per cent of the time. The half-life falls from 34.31 rounds to 2.86. Nothing in that calculation is about language models; it is about conjunctions, and it means that the more carefully specified an instruction is, the faster it degrades.

Two consequences follow, and both cut against common practice. The first: reordering the clauses cannot help. If position affects survival — and the evidence that the middle of a long context is the weakest place is now well established[114,115] — then permuting the clauses reuses the same multiset of positions and the product is invariant. The lab confirms it: whole-instruction survival is 2.535 per cent under every ordering tested, while the survival of any particular clause spans 35.5 points depending on where it sits. Moving the goal statement to the end protects the goal statement. It does not protect the goal.

The second is worse. Duplicating the goal statement to protect it lengthens the context and pushes everything else outward. Four copies raise that clause's own survival from 92.282 to 99.991 per cent and lower whole-instruction survival from 2.535 to 1.472 per cent. Re-injecting clauses verbatim does work, but the dose required is not modest: holding a twelve-clause instruction at even odds over ten rounds needs 9 of the twelve pinned, over twenty rounds 11, and holding it at ninety per cent needs all twelve at every horizon tested. Which is to say: if the instruction matters, carry the instruction, not a summary of it.

Memory corruption is the only failure mode here that outlives the run that created it. It sits at rung three because a store can in principle be rolled back, and it is decided by judgement because doing so requires knowing which entries are wrong — and the corrupted entry is retrieved again precisely because it matches the query well. Deliberate versions exist and are cheap: a single crafted record placed in an agent's memory can be made to surface on a later, unrelated query, and poisoning a retrieval corpus is a demonstrated attack rather than a projection.[116,117] The general shape of the problem is the one the prompt-injection literature has been describing since 2023 — instructions arriving inside data the agent was asked to read[118] — with the added property that a store makes the injection persistent. Simon Willison's summary of the exposure condition is the most useful operational form of it: an agent with access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and a way to communicate externally has all three ingredients, and removing any one removes the class.[119] Defences that constrain the data flow rather than classifying the text do materially better — a design that separates planning from data handling solves 77 per cent of an injection benchmark with a provable guarantee against 84 per cent undefended[120,121], and programmable privilege control cuts attack success from 39.9 to 1.0 per cent[122] — though evaluations against static injection sets overstate all of these, and defence-aware attackers recover a substantial share.[123]

Cascading failure closes the set at rung zero, decided exactly. Every counter needed to see it coming already exists. The difficulty has never been detection; it is that the correct response is to do less, and no automatic component wants to do less. Retry amplification is the standard mechanism and the standard remedy is a budget rather than politeness.[81,85] Agent systems add a channel the classical literature did not have to consider: the output of one component is the input to the next, so a wrong answer propagates as data rather than as load. The classical cases are worth keeping in view because they set the scale of what rung zero means: a deployment that reached seven of eight servers left a retired flag active on the eighth and moved roughly seven billion dollars of unwanted positions in forty-five minutes;[124] a content update that passed its validator but not the sensor that consumed it took down millions of hosts into a boot loop that required physical access;[125] a configuration file that doubled in size crashed the proxy that read it across a global network.[126] None of those involved a model. All three are the same shape: a change that reached production faster than any remedy could follow it. The largest empirical study of multi-agent failures to date annotated more than sixteen hundred traces across seven frameworks and found fourteen recurring failure modes, with the largest share in specification and inter-agent misalignment rather than in the individual model's competence — and the authors are explicit that better base models will not be sufficient.[127]

9. What decides it

NODE THE INSTRUMENT  /  GUIDE WORD OTHER THAN

Laid out on both axes at once, the twenty-two plates make a shape that is not obvious from any one of them, and the shape is the argument.

The first thing to see is the separation. The mean rung of the fourteen controls is 3.86; the mean rung of the eight failure modes is 1.62. That gap of 2.23 rungs is the space the whole discipline operates in. Within the controls the two families sit at very different heights: all six guardrails act at rung five, giving them a mean of 5.00, against 3.00 for the classical stack. And two failure modes — infinite loops and cascading failure — land below every control in the vocabulary, because what they consume cannot be given back by any mechanism at all.

The second thing to see is what does the deciding, and here the correlation runs the wrong way. Thirteen of the twenty-two plates are decided exactly, eight by judgement, and exactly one by a person. Among the eight classical reliability mechanisms, seven of eight are exact; the sole exception is recovery, whose verdict is that somebody has decided the incident is over. That is a remarkable concentration, and it is not an accident: those mechanisms were designed against fail-stop components, and a fail-stop component makes its own verdict available.

Now look at rung five. Seven plates act there. One is determinism, which acts there by writing nothing. The other six are the guardrails, and four of those six are decided by judgement. Which means the position on the ladder where a wrong decision is free — the only position where a wrong decision is free — is, for four of its six occupants, a position where the decision cannot be computed. Rung five is where a probabilistic guard stands in front of a probabilistic actor, and the arithmetic of the previous section is the price of that arrangement.

The two exceptions matter more than their number suggests. Tool guardrails and policy enforcement are decided exactly, and they are exactly the two that carry a formal theory of what they can and cannot do. They are also the two that operate on the action rather than on the text: an allow-list does not have to understand the request, only the call. That is the whole of their advantage, and it explains the direction the tooling has moved in — from filtering what the model says towards constraining what the model can reach.[128,129]

Across all twenty-two the correlation between rung and exactness is -0.09, which is to say essentially none; among the controls alone it is -0.30, weakly negative. Nothing is being predicted here. That is the point: the two axes are independent, and the field's intuition treats them as though they were the same axis. A failure that is easy to detect is assumed to be under control, and a failure that is hard to detect is assumed to be catastrophic. The plates say otherwise in both directions. Infinite loops are trivially detectable and permanently costly. Context loss is exactly measurable and cheaply reversible. Hallucination is undetectable and sits three rungs above cascading failure.

The cleanest way to state the split among the failure modes is by what would have to decide that the failure had occurred. Four are decided exactly: incorrect arguments (in the malformed half), infinite loops, context loss, cascading failures. Every one of those four has a mature, deterministic control that works — a schema, a step budget, a token count, a retry budget. Four are decided by judgement: hallucination, wrong tool selection, goal drift, memory corruption. Not one of those four has a control that works in the same sense. The correspondence is exact, and it is the most useful predictive statement in the article: the agent failure modes that have been solved are precisely the ones a predicate can recognise, and no others have been solved.

This also explains why the standard remedy for the judged half is another model. If a predicate cannot recognise a hallucination, something that can be wrong must recognise it instead, and every such arrangement inherits the base-rate arithmetic. The literature on evaluating agents has been making a version of this point from the measurement side — that benchmark scores can be improved by changes that do not improve the system, and that cost and reliability have to be reported alongside capability[130] — and the failure-mode taxonomy work makes it from the diagnostic side.[127] The ladder makes it from the engineering side: when the verdict is judged, do not put the decision anywhere below rung five, because a judged verdict at rung two is a compensating transaction waiting to be written.

One more asymmetry deserves recording. Exactly one plate in the entire vocabulary is decided by a person, and it is recovery — a verdict delivered after the fact, about whether an incident is over. The control that agent operators actually depend on for irreversible actions, the approval gate, does not appear in the standard vocabulary at all. That is a gap in the language rather than in the practice, and it has a cost: the property that governs whether a gate helps is the finiteness of the reviewer's attention, and a term that does not exist does not get budgeted. The one study to model this directly finds what the ironies-of-automation literature would predict — that a more escalating oversight policy can be the less safe one, and that the safety-optimal escalation rate sits below escalating everything.[56,52] Empirical work on how developers actually oversee software agents finds the same pressure.[131,132,133]

11. Designing for the ratchet

NODE THE DESIGN  /  GUIDE WORD REVERSE

Six things follow from the shape, and none of them is a novel technique. They are all reorderings of things the field already does.

Classify actions by rung before designing any control for them. This is the move Magentic-UI makes explicit with its three-valued irreversibility tag,[105] and it is what the Replit response amounted to after the fact. Most tool catalogues are organised by subject matter — files, search, payments — and subject matter does not predict reversal cost. A delete with a thirty-day recycle bin is rung three; a delete without one is rung zero; they have the same name in the schema. Until the catalogue carries the rung, no gate can be sized correctly, because the size of a gate should be set by what it costs to be wrong and not by how important the tool sounds.

Buy rungs before buying accuracy. A staging environment, a dry-run mode, a recycle bin, a write-ahead log, an outbox, a two-phase send with a delay before delivery: each of these moves a class of action up the ladder, and each is a piece of ordinary deterministic engineering with no error rate. Moving an action from rung one to rung three is worth more than moving a classifier from 95 to 99 per cent recall, because the first change is exact and the second is judged and still subject to the base rate. The planning-only mode is the strongest form of this: it moves the entire run to rung five and pays for it in usefulness, honestly and visibly.

Escalate rather than block. A flag is still at rung five. Nothing has happened to the request except that a second judgement will now be made about it, so the first stage can be tuned far more liberally than a blocking layer could ever be. This is the structural difference between a wall and a cascade, and the lab's version and the published production version agree on both the direction and the rough size of the effect: roughly the same recall for an order of magnitude fewer false blocks, with the expensive stage running on a few per cent of traffic.[100]

Budget the human's attention as a scarce resource, because it is. Escalating everything is not the safe end of a spectrum with escalating nothing at the other end; both ends are bad, and the optimum sits strictly inside.[56] An approval gate that fires on every write trains the reviewer to approve, which is Bainbridge's point restated with a button.[52,53] The gate should fire on rung, and rung should be assigned by a predicate wherever one exists, with the judged path reserved for the genuinely ambiguous middle. The human-computer-interaction literature has been saying for years that high automation and high human control are not opposite ends of one slider, and that the interfaces which achieve both are the ones that make the system's state and its next action legible before it acts.[134,135]

Prefer constraints on reach over judgements about text. The two guardrails that are decided exactly are the two that operate on the action rather than the content, and they are the two with a theory of what they can enforce.[88,90] The same conclusion arrives from the security side: defences that control what data can flow where outperform defences that classify what the text says,[120,122] and the exposure condition that makes an agent attackable is a statement about capabilities rather than about prompts.[119] A capability an agent does not have cannot be misused by a jailbreak, a poisoned document or a bad day.

Make the run replayable, and treat that as a safety property rather than a convenience. Determinism is at rung five for a reason. Without it there is no bisection, no replay, no re-decision on the original evidence, and no honest post-incident account of what the system actually did. This is not free — batch-invariant serving costs throughput[57,59] — but the alternative is a system whose failures cannot be reproduced, which is a system whose failures cannot be closed out.

A closing observation about where the standards are going, because the regulatory apparatus is converging on the same variable from a different direction. ISO 21448 exists because a component began failing without malfunctioning.[39] ISO/PAS 8800 extends the machinery to AI outputs specifically.[40] The management-system and risk-guidance standards now widely referenced provide the process scaffolding.[136,137,138] The agentic security taxonomies published through 2025 and 2026 move excessive agency up their rankings and add categories for memory and inter-agent channels.[128,129] National security agencies issued joint guidance on agentic adoption in 2026.[139] The European framework, whose high-risk obligations were deferred by the 2026 Digital Omnibus, is built around human oversight by a person with “the necessary competence, training and authority”.[140,141] That phrase is doing the same work the ratchet does. Authority is the capacity to stop something before it happens, which is to say the capacity to hold a step at rung five.

The question in the title is not answered once for a system. It is answered separately for every action the system can take, and the answer is a design input rather than an outcome. What the twenty-two plates show is that the field's vocabulary is well developed at the two ends and thin in the middle: rich in mechanisms for undoing things that were never very hard to undo, rich in names for failures that cannot be undone at all, and sparse in exactly the region where an agent spends most of its time — steps that have committed something, that can be offset but not erased, and whose verdict nobody can compute. That region is where the engineering is. Every step taken there costs what it costs to take back, and the ratchet does not turn the other way.

Method. Twenty-two named practices and failure modes, taken from a working taxonomy of agent reliability, were each placed on a six-rung ladder of reversal cost and tagged with what settles whether they have acted. The coding rule was fixed before the plates were written: the rung is the reversal cost of the residue the plate leaves when the step it governs goes wrong. All tallies are recomputed from the coding table at build time.

Lab. Eight experiments, standard library only, seed 20260910, run at build time; every figure and every quantity in the prose is read from their output. E1 additionally uses a public corpus of 2,489,303 tokens to measure a real top-two gap distribution, and is explicit that an interpolated trigram model is not a transformer. Two experiments were rebuilt after their first versions were degenerate and the rebuilds are disclosed in the text: E1's first corpus measurement was dominated by exact ties that are an artefact of counting rather than a property of language, and E7's first position analysis asked whether reordering clauses saves an instruction, which a permutation cannot do — the invariance became the finding.

Figures. 13 figures and 22 gauges generated programmatically. Checked by an analytic pass for text overflowing the viewBox and for same-baseline collisions, then rasterised for visual review; the visual pass caught chip-label overruns and a nested-scaling error the analytic pass could not see.

Type. Commissioner (Kostas Bartsokas) for display and interface, PT Serif for body text, Atkinson Hyperlegible Mono for data and labels — the last drawn by the Braille Institute for character distinction, which seemed the right register for an article about failures that do not announce themselves. Commissioner offers a slant axis rather than a true italic, so emphasis is set in the serif.
