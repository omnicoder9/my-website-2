01Cheaper per what?

An agent programme collects a bill and a stopwatch, and almost every decision anyone makes about it is justified by one of the two. Cut the prompt. Route the easy ones to a smaller model. Turn on caching. Run the tools in parallel. Use the batch endpoint. Each of these is a real technique with a real mechanism, and each is usually presented as a percentage: thirty per cent off, two and a half times faster.

Off what, though, and faster at what. The percentage always has a denominator, and the denominators are not interchangeable. A token is not a call; a call is not a step; a step is not a run; a run is not a finished piece of work; a finished piece of work is not the thing the organisation was buying. Between each pair sits a conversion factor that the technique itself does not control, and several of those factors are large enough to reverse the ranking they were quoted to justify.

This article takes the standard vocabulary of agent cost and performance engineering — token reduction, caching, routing, batching, smaller models, streaming, parallel calls, speculative decoding, and the economics that sit on top of them — and reads every item in it as an answer to one question: how far does the saving travel before it stops being guaranteed?

The answer turns out to sort the field sharply, and not along the line people usually draw. It does not separate cheap techniques from expensive ones, or simple from sophisticated. It separates the techniques that change what the model computes from the ones that change when, where or how many times the same computation happens. Only the second kind can be shown to be free, and in the vocabulary below there are exactly 2 of them out of 28.1,2

Three things follow from that, and they are the article's claims. First, a per-token price is not a measurement of anything stable; it is a number a vendor chooses from a curve that the buyer cannot see. Second, cost and latency are not two readings of one quantity but two currencies joined by a single exchange rate — batch occupancy — and most of the engineering in this field is a way of avoiding having to set it. Third, the denominator that matters to whoever signs the invoice is the one almost nothing in the stack is instrumented for, and the gap between it and the token is wide enough that under a minute of a person's attention per failed run is enough to invert a five-fold difference in list price.

There is a lab at the end: eight deterministic experiments, standard library only, whose output is reproduced verbatim. Every number quoted in what follows comes from it or from a published price list.

02Where the price comes from

A transformer serves a request in two phases with almost opposite appetites.3,4 Prefill reads the whole prompt at once: every token can be processed in parallel, the arithmetic is dense, and the machine is compute-bound. Decode then emits one token at a time, and each step must move the entire weight matrix — and every waiting sequence's key-value cache — from memory to the arithmetic units in order to produce a single token per sequence. Decode is bandwidth-bound, and has been since the shape of the problem was first written down.5,6,7

Both phases have had a decade of engineering aimed at the same target: moving fewer bytes. Attention kernels were rewritten to be aware of the memory hierarchy rather than of the mathematics8,9; the key-value cache was narrowed by sharing heads and then by evicting or compressing what the model turns out not to attend to.10,11,12 None of that changes the shape of the problem. It changes the constants, and the constants are what the price list is made of.13,14

The consequence is the one fact everything else in this article rests on. In decode, the cost of reading the weights is paid once per step no matter how many sequences are waiting on it. Serving one sequence and serving four hundred take almost the same time, so the price of a token is divided by however many sequences happen to be in the batch.15,16

On the modelled node in experiment three, moving from a batch of one to the largest batch that fits divides the cost of an output token by 47.3 and multiplies the time to produce one by 8.96. Those are the same action. There is no setting at which both improve, and no amount of engineering that removes the trade-off, because it is a statement about memory bandwidth rather than about software.17,18

Two things follow that are not obvious from a price list. The first is that the batch is a shared resource whose size is decided by memory, and the memory left over for sequences is whatever the weights did not take. Longer prompts mean larger key-value caches, which means fewer sequences fit, which means the fixed cost of reading the weights is divided among fewer payers.10,16,19

Going from a thousand tokens of context to a hundred and twenty-eight thousand cuts the feasible batch from 3397 sequences to 26 and multiplies the real unit cost of an output token by 102. The buyer sees none of this: long-context requests are billed at the same published per-token rate as short ones on the current generation of models.20,21

The second consequence is that any latency promise is a price. If the service guarantees a per-token budget, that budget caps the batch, and the cap sets the unit cost. In the experiment, relaxing the per-token budget from 3 to 24 milliseconds divides the unit cost by 7.52. A vendor publishing one price per token is averaging over that curve on the buyer's behalf, using a batch composition the buyer does not control and cannot observe.22,23

This is why the published price list is itself an argument. On the current line-up, a cached input token costs a tenth of an uncached one, an output token costs five times an input token, a deferred batch request costs half of an interactive one, and a premium-latency request costs twice.1,20,24 Nothing about the text changes across those rates. Only its position does.

The span from the cheapest counter to the dearest is a factor of five hundred, and every step of it is a fact about scheduling rather than about language. An engineer who moves a token from the output counter to the cached-input counter has done something worth two orders of magnitude; an engineer who shortens the same token's text has done something worth a few per cent.

03A ladder for savings

The techniques in this field are usually sorted by mechanism — prompt-side, model-side, serving-side. That grouping predicts nothing useful, because two techniques with the same mechanism can have entirely different standing. Caching a prefix and summarising a transcript both reduce input tokens. One of them cannot change an answer and the other one routinely does.

The sorting used here is different. For each technique, ask at which denominator its benefit is still guaranteed to be non-negative, using only the preconditions the technique itself already requires. Nothing is credited for working well in practice; the question is what the mechanism can be made to promise.

The rule that keeps this from being arbitrary is stated once and applied identically to all thirty-three line items: the rung is the coarsest denomination at which the benefit is guaranteed non-negative, given only what the technique already assumes about the workload. A technique that needs a repeating prefix is judged on workloads with a repeating prefix; it is not penalised for failing on workloads it never claimed. What it is judged on is everything downstream of its own mechanism.

So prefix caching sits at the top. The provider's own documentation states that the response is identical to the one you would have received without it.1 Given a recurring prefix, nothing the buyer measures gets worse: the input bill falls, the time to first token falls, and the tokens are the same tokens. Summarising a transcript sits three rungs down, not because it works less often but because its mechanism is the deliberate loss of information, and a lost constraint buys more steps than the summary saved.25,26

A second axis runs across the ladder and is genuinely independent of it: which of the two currencies each technique spends. Most writing on this subject treats cost and latency as the same axis with different units. They are not, and the ladder makes the difference legible.

Of the 28 techniques, 13 improve both cost and latency, 7 trade one directly against the other, and the rest move one and leave the other alone. The 13 that improve both are not a bonus; they are almost all techniques that reduce the number of bytes that have to move, and in decode that quantity is both the time and the money.5,27

Each of the thirty-three plates below carries the same five fields — what it buys, what it charges, what the workload must be like, what breaks it, and how the loss shows up — and a gauge. The gauge is a receipt: six line items from the outcome at the top down to the token at the bottom, with the lines that no longer hold struck through, a subtotal rule under the last one that does, and two chips recording which way money and time move. A filled chip means the quantity improves; an open chip means it gets worse; a dashed chip means it does not move.

A technique guaranteed only as far as the run, which lowers cost and raises latency. The three lines above the subtotal are struck through: nothing about the mechanism promises that the success rate survives, so the two denominations that depend on it are not available, and neither is the strictly-dominant line at the top.

04The line items

Thirty-three entries: twenty-eight techniques and five denominators. Each is placed by the rule in the previous section and nothing else.

The tokens you never send

Everything here works on the prompt. The prompt is the part of an agent run that grows without anyone deciding it should.

L01

Prefix caching

D5 · strictly dominant
buysThe input bill and the time to first token for every prompt segment the provider has already encoded.
chargesA one-time write premium of 1.25x base input, and a clock: five minutes by default, an hour at twice the write price.
needsA byte-identical prefix that recurs inside the lifetime. Tools, then system, then messages, in that order.
breaksAny edit at or before the breakpoint. A timestamp, a request id, a language runtime that randomises JSON key order, a toggled tool, a changed effort setting.
fails asA bill that does not fall and an error that never arrives. The only symptom is two usage counters.

The output is identical to what you would have got without it. That is what puts it alone at the top of the ladder.1,16,20

L02

Server-side prefix sharing

D5 · strictly dominant
buysMemory, and the prefill of every concurrent request that starts with the same tokens.
chargesEngineering in the serving layer, and an eviction policy that someone has to own.
needsConcurrent requests that share a head. A system prompt, a few-shot block, a document everyone is asking about.
breaksPersonalisation at the front of the prompt. One user id in position zero and every request is its own tree.
fails asFragmentation: the pool is full of blocks nobody else can use.

Paging the KV cache is the same idea the operating system had about physical memory, arrived at for the same reason.16,28,29,30

L03

Context compaction

D2 · guaranteed per step
buysThe quadratic part of the prompt: a transcript that is summarised stops growing linearly in every later request.
chargesFidelity. Some fact that was retrievable becomes a paraphrase or disappears.
needsRedundancy in the transcript, and a compaction that keeps constraints rather than narrative.
breaksWhen the dropped span held the constraint, the identifier or the thing already tried.
fails asAn agent that redoes work it has already done, and pays for the summary as well.

The saving is certain at the step and unknown at the run: a compaction that loses a constraint buys more steps than it saved.25,26,31,32

L04

Tool-catalogue pruning

D1 · guaranteed per call
buysThe tokens a tool definition occupies on every single call for the whole life of the run.
chargesA discovery step, or a judgement made before the task is understood.
needsA catalogue substantially larger than any one task needs.
breaksWhen the pruned tool was the right one, or when the discovery step has to run on most tasks anyway.
fails asA confident call to a near neighbour of the tool that was removed.

Declaring a browser toolset costs about 6,600 input tokens before the conversation starts; the tool-use system prompt alone is 286 to 804 depending on model and tool choice.20,33,34,35

L05

Retrieval budget

D2 · guaranteed per step
buysInput tokens, in the largest single block most agents send.
chargesRecall, at exactly the rate the ranker is wrong.
needsA ranker whose ordering is better than the cut you are making.
breaksA flat relevance distribution, where passage ten is as useful as passage two.
fails asA confident answer assembled from an incomplete file, with no signal that anything is missing.

Retrieving less is also retrieving better: measured effective context falls short of advertised context on every model that has been tested.36,37,38,39,40

L06

Results that never enter context

D2 · guaranteed per step
buysThe tokens a large tool result would have occupied, on this call and on every later one.
chargesSight. The model manipulates a handle it cannot read.
needsResults with enough structure that code can traverse them without the model reading them.
breaksWhen the answer is in the part that was never read into the model's view.
fails asA correct program run over the wrong rows.

Reported effect on one internal workload: average context on a complex research task falling from 43,588 tokens to 27,297.28,31,33

The tokens you never generate

Output tokens are the dearest thing on the price list, at five times the input rate across the whole current line-up. These plates are about producing fewer of them.

L07

Output caps and schemas

D1 · guaranteed per call
buysOutput tokens, which cost five times what input tokens cost.
chargesTruncation, and whatever a constrained decoder does to the model's own distribution.
needsA task whose answer has a knowable size.
breaksWhen the cap lands mid-answer and the caller retries the whole call.
fails asA well-formed object with a field the model never got to think about.

The cap guarantees this call is cheaper. It guarantees nothing about the step, because a truncated answer is often re-asked.34,41

L08

Reasoning-effort control

D1 · guaranteed per call
buysThe largest controllable block of output tokens in a modern agent, and with it the largest block of latency.
chargesAccuracy on exactly the tasks that needed the thinking.
needsA task mix in which most items are easy, and some way of telling which.
breaksWhen difficulty is not observable before the work is done, which is the usual case.
fails asA short confident answer to a hard question.

Changing the effort setting also invalidates the message cache, so the two levers in this article that look most independent are coupled through the prompt hash.20,42,43,44

L09

Step budgets

D3 · guaranteed per run
buysThe tail of the run, which is where agent spend actually lives.
chargesRuns that would have finished on step twenty-one.
needsA budget set above the honest step distribution rather than above the median.
breaksHeavy tails. If step counts are lognormal the mean is not near the mode and the budget cuts real work.
fails asTruncation recorded as task failure, which then justifies a bigger model.

The only complete loop detector is the one that is blind: a step budget catches every pathology because it recognises none.45,46,47

L10

Exact response cache

D4 · outcome-neutral
buysThe entire call, on every request that is byte-identical to one already answered.
chargesStaleness, and the variance of a sample frozen into a fact.
needsRepeated identical requests, which is rarer in agents than in web serving because the transcript is in the key.
breaksAny per-request entropy at all.
fails asAn answer that was right last Tuesday.

The outputs are drawn from the same distribution as the call it replaces, which is why this sits one rung below prefix caching rather than three.29,30,48,49

L11

Semantic response cache

D0 · guaranteed nowhere
buysThe call, on every request judged similar enough to one already answered.
chargesCorrectness, at a rate nobody measures, in exchange for a hit rate everybody measures.
needsAn estimate of what a wrong answer costs. Without it the threshold is unfalsifiable.
breaksTwo intents that embed close and answer differently. Negation is the classic one.
fails asA fluent, confidently wrong answer that is fast and free.

Break-even false-hit rate is one over the cost of a wrong answer and does not depend on the hit rate at all.48,49,50,51

The machine you rent

Same task, different silicon. Every plate here changes what the model computes, which is why none of them reaches the top of the ladder.

L12

Model routing

D1 · guaranteed per call
buysThe gap between price tiers, on every request the router sends down.
chargesA classifier's errors, which are correlated with exactly the cases that needed the bigger model.
needsA signal available before the work is done that predicts whether the cheap model will succeed.
breaksWhen difficulty is only visible in the answer.
fails asA cheap wrong answer that nothing downstream distinguishes from an expensive right one.

Published routers report 40 to 85 per cent cost reduction at near-parity quality, always on a fixed query distribution and always with the router trained on it.52,53,54

L13

Cascade escalation

D0 · guaranteed nowhere
buysThe strong model's price on every request the verifier accepts.
chargesThe cheap call, unconditionally, plus a second round trip on everything that escalates.
needsA verifier, and a verifier is a second model or a scoring function with its own error rate.
breaksFalse acceptance. Every point of it costs a fixed amount of end-to-end accuracy.
fails asAn answer that passed a check nobody validated.

In any two-stage cascade the exchange rate between accuracy and money is exactly the strong model's own accuracy, independent of everything else in the design.54,55,56

L14

A distilled task model

D1 · guaranteed per call
buysBoth prices at once: a small model is cheaper per token and faster per token, because both are set by how many bytes have to move.
chargesA training pipeline, a data set, and a model that is now yours to maintain.
needsA task narrow enough and stable enough to be worth freezing.
breaksTask drift. The distribution the teacher was sampled on stops being the distribution you serve.
fails asQuiet degradation on the newest ten per cent of traffic.

Scaling laws written for training cost give the wrong model size once inference is in the objective: the optimum moves smaller and longer-trained.14,57,58

L15

Quantisation

D1 · guaranteed per call
buysBytes moved per token, which in decode is the same thing as time and the same thing as money.
chargesNumerical fidelity, unevenly: the damage concentrates in outlier channels.
needsA calibration set that looks like production.
breaksLong-tail inputs the calibration never saw.
fails asA model that benchmarks identically and behaves differently on the cases you built it for.

It also buys batch size: halving the bytes of the KV cache doubles how many sequences fit beside the weights.27,59,60,61

L16

Sparse activation

D1 · guaranteed per call
buysArithmetic per token, by routing each token through a fraction of the parameters.
chargesMemory, all of it. Every expert must be resident even though each token visits few.
needsA serving footprint that can hold the whole model.
breaksSmall deployments, where the memory the experts occupy is the binding constraint and the arithmetic never was.
fails asAn expensive machine running at low utilisation to serve a cheap-sounding parameter count.

This is the technique that made the current price list possible, and it is the one a buyer has least ability to choose.62,63,64,65

The clock you buy

Nothing in this group changes a token. They change when the token is produced, and the price list charges for that directly.

L17

Deferred batch execution

D4 · outcome-neutral
buysHalf the bill, on both input and output, at a published rate.
chargesThe clock. The answer arrives within a window, not within a second.
needsWork nobody is waiting for: evaluation, backfill, enrichment, overnight triage.
breaksAnything with a person at the other end, and anything whose result feeds the next step of the same run.
fails asNot at all. This is the cleanest trade on the list, which is why it is so rarely the one that gets built.

A 50 per cent discount is worth more than most of the token engineering in group one, and costs a scheduler.20,24

L18

A premium latency tier

D4 · outcome-neutral
buysTime, at a rate the vendor prints.
chargesMoney, at twice list on the models where it is offered.
needsA task where a second is worth more than the multiple.
breaksNothing. It is a price, not a technique.
fails asOn the invoice, visibly, which makes it the most honest line in this article.

Batch and fast mode together span a factor of four in price for the same tokens from the same model, decided entirely by when you want them.20

L19

Continuous batching

D4 · outcome-neutral
buysOccupancy. Requests join and leave the running batch between decode steps instead of waiting for the batch to drain.
chargesPer-request latency, which rises with the number of neighbours sharing the step.
needsA stream of arrivals, which is to say a server rather than a script.
breaksBursty, uneven traffic where the batch is empty when you need it and full when you do not.
fails asThroughput graphs that look excellent beside a tail latency nobody plots.

Iteration-level scheduling was reported at up to 36.9x the throughput of the batch-level scheduler it replaced, at the same latency target.15,16,22

L20

Chunked prefill and phase splitting

D4 · outcome-neutral
buysThe stall that a long prefill imposes on everyone already decoding, by slicing it or moving it to different hardware.
chargesScheduler complexity, and a small tax on the prefill itself.
needsA mixed workload: long prompts arriving while short generations are in flight.
breaksUniform traffic, where there is no interference to remove.
fails asA p99 that is mysteriously worse than the p50 by a factor nobody can attribute.

Prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bound; running them on the same device at the same time means one of them is always the wrong shape for the machine.66,67,68

L21

Background execution

D4 · outcome-neutral
buysThe person's wait, not the machine's. The run takes exactly as long and nobody watches it.
chargesSession runtime, which some platforms meter separately from tokens, and the engineering of resumption.
needsA task the requester can walk away from, and a way to hand back the result.
breaksAnything conversational, and anything whose failure needs a human in the loop within the run.
fails asA silent, expensive run that nobody checks until the bill.

Metered session runtime is the one place where an agent's idle time has a printed price.69,70,71

The work you overlap

Concurrency does not remove work. It removes waiting, and only the waiting that was not on the critical path to begin with.

L22

Streaming

D1 · guaranteed per call
buysTime to first token, and nothing else. The run ends at the same instant it would have.
chargesNothing measurable, which is why it is universal.
needsA reader, human or otherwise, who can start using a prefix.
breaksA consumer that parses the whole response before acting, which is most agent plumbing.
fails asInvisibly: a streamed response into a buffer is an unstreamed response with extra frames.

The oldest number in this article is the one that justifies it: the threshold above which a person stops feeling that a system is responding to them.70,71,72,73

L23

Parallel tool calls

D3 · guaranteed per run
buysRound trips. Calls with no dependency between them ride in one assistant turn.
chargesNothing on the bill. The same tokens are emitted either way.
needsIndependence, which is a property of the task's dependency graph and not of the framework.
breaksA chain. Where every call needs the previous result, the graph is a line and there is nothing to overlap.
fails asA wave that waits for its slowest member, so the measured speedup is well below the round-trip reduction.

The API counts a run of consecutive tool-use blocks as one position in its cache lookback, which is what stops a wide parallel turn from evicting the previous turn's cache entry.41,74,75

L24

Multi-agent fan-out

D0 · guaranteed nowhere
buysWall-clock on tasks that decompose into independent searches, and context, by giving each branch its own window.
chargesTokens, at roughly fifteen times a chat interaction, plus a coordination problem that has no analogue in the single-agent case.
needsGenuinely separable subtasks and a cheap way to reconcile their findings.
breaksShared state. Where the branches need each other's intermediate results, the orchestrator becomes the bottleneck it was meant to remove.
fails asDuplicated work, contradictory findings, and a bill that scales with the fan-out rather than the answer.

On one published internal evaluation token usage alone explained about 80 per cent of the performance variance, which is a statement about the metric as much as the architecture.76,77,78

L25

Speculative tool prefetch

D0 · guaranteed nowhere
buysThe latency of a call you correctly guessed the model was about to make.
chargesEvery call you guessed wrong, including its side effects.
needsRead-only tools with a predictable next step.
breaksAny tool that writes. A speculatively executed write is not speculative.
fails asA rate limit consumed by requests nobody asked for.

The hardware version of this idea has a rollback mechanism. The agent version usually does not.17,18,79

The work you do twice

Three plates that deliberately compute more than the answer needs, and differ entirely in what they get back for it.

L26

Speculative decoding

D4 · outcome-neutral
buysDecode latency, by checking several drafted tokens in one forward pass of the target model.
chargesArithmetic, strictly and always: the verification runs more positions than it accepts at every acceptance rate below certainty.
needsA drafter that agrees with the target often, and a decode phase with arithmetic to spare.
breaksA batch large enough to be compute-bound. Above that point the speculated positions are charged in full.
fails asIt does not fail on quality. That is the whole point of it.

The output distribution is provably the target model's own. Of everything in this article it is the only technique that adds computation and still cannot change an answer.2,80,81,82,83

L27

Repeated sampling

D0 · guaranteed nowhere
buysCoverage. The probability that at least one of k attempts is correct rises steeply for small k.
chargesk times the tokens, and a selection problem that is usually harder than the original task.
needsA verifier, a vote or a domain where answers can be checked. Without one, coverage is not accuracy.
breaksOpen-ended generation, where the best of k is only findable by the thing you were trying to avoid paying for.
fails asA benchmark number reported at pass@k and deployed at pass@1.

Uniform choice among k attempts gives exactly the single-attempt rate. The lift a selector must deliver over chance does not fall as k rises.42,43,84,85

L28

Retry on failure

D0 · guaranteed nowhere
buysSuccess rate, on the tasks whose failures are independent.
chargesCost and latency together. It is the only plate here that worsens both.
needsFailures that are actually independent, which means transient rather than structural.
breaksA never-solvable population. The retry curve saturates at the solvable fraction and keeps billing.
fails asA success rate that improves while cost per successful task gets worse.

Everything about whether retrying is a saving lives at one denomination -- cost per successful task -- and nowhere else.45,86,87

The unit you report

Not techniques but denominators: the five numbers an agent programme is actually asked for, read as claims about how far a measurement travels.

L29

Price per token

D0 · guaranteed nowhere
buysComparability, apparently.
chargesNothing, and it is worth what it costs.
needsA token that means the same thing on both sides of the comparison.
breaksImmediately. Tokenisers differ between vendors and between generations of one vendor; one current family is documented as producing about 30 per cent more tokens for the same text.
fails asA spreadsheet in which the cheaper model is the one that counts more finely.

A price per token is a price per unit of a vendor-defined measure. Nothing else on an invoice works this way.20,88,89,90,91

L30

Cost per call

D1 · guaranteed per call
buysA number an engineer can act on, at the granularity a trace already has.
chargesEverything above it. A call is not a step, a step is not a run.
needsOnly instrumentation, which is why it is the number everyone has.
breaksAs soon as two systems take different numbers of calls to do the same thing.
fails asOptimisation that moves work from one call into two.

This is the denomination most cost dashboards report, and it is one rung above the one that is meaningless.92,93

L31

Cost per run

D3 · guaranteed per run
buysThe first denomination that corresponds to something a user asked for.
chargesNothing about whether the run worked.
needsA definition of where a run starts and ends, which multi-agent systems make genuinely hard.
breaksWhen the failure rate differs between the things being compared, which is the only interesting case.
fails asA cheap system that is cheap because it gives up early.

Cost-controlled evaluation exists because accuracy-only leaderboards rewarded agents for spending without limit.45,94,95

L32

Cost per successful run

D4 · outcome-neutral
buysThe first honest comparison between two systems with different reliability.
chargesA definition of success that someone has to defend, and enough runs to estimate it.
needsAn acceptance test. Without one this number cannot be computed at all.
breaksHeterogeneous task difficulty. A headline pass rate and a repeated-trial pass rate imply very different distributions.
fails asConfident ratios computed from fifty runs with intervals nobody printed.

It is also the first denomination at which a cheaper model can lose to a dearer one, and usually does.45,86,94,96

L33

Cost per delivered outcome

D5 · strictly dominant
buysThe only denomination the buyer actually pays in: a resolved ticket, a merged change, an hour not spent.
chargesAttribution. Almost nothing in the stack is instrumented at this level.
needsA causal claim, and usually an experiment, about what would have happened otherwise.
breaksComplementary investment. The measured return of a general technology lags its deployment by years, and the intangible work is what fills the gap.
fails asTwo organisations reporting opposite returns from the same system, both correctly.

Every technique in this article is a claim about this number. Two of them can prove it.97,98,99,100,101

05What the ledger says

Two of twenty-eight are free. Prefix caching and server-side prefix sharing are the only techniques whose benefit is guaranteed all the way up, and they are the two whose mechanism is not doing again what has already been done. Everything else either changes the computation or moves it, and both of those spend something.1,16,28

The highest-scoring group is the one that changes nothing. All five members of the clock you buy sit at exactly D4, outcome-neutral, for a group mean of 4.00 — the highest in the article. Deferred batch execution, a premium latency tier, continuous batching, chunked prefill and background execution do not touch a token. They decide when the identical computation happens and who waits for it, and the price list charges for that directly, by a factor of four between the cheap end and the dear end.15,20,24,66

The lowest-scoring group is the one everybody reaches for first. The machine you rent — routing, cascades, distillation, quantisation, sparse activation — has a group mean of 0.80, the lowest here, with four of its five members guaranteed only at the call and one guaranteed nowhere. Every one of them substitutes a different computation, so none can promise the answer survives. These are also the techniques with the largest published headline percentages.52,54,59

The most populated rung is the one that promises least. 9 of 33 line items sit at D1: the call they touch is cheaper, and nothing above that is guaranteed. A further 7 sit at D0 and guarantee nothing at any denomination. Together that is 16 of 33, very nearly half the standard vocabulary, whose benefit is an empirical claim about a particular workload rather than a property of the mechanism.

Nothing sits in the middle. The distribution is bimodal: 14 of 28 techniques at D1 or below and 9 at D4 or above, with only 5 in between. There is no gentle gradient from usually safe to usually risky. Either the mechanism leaves the computation alone, in which case it is outcome-neutral or better, or it does not, in which case it stops being guaranteed at the call. The mean is 2.07 and almost nothing is near it.

The two currencies are not symmetrical. 19 of 28 techniques lower cost and 19 lower latency, but the trades run slightly the other way than the folklore suggests: 4 techniques buy latency with money — speculative decoding, the premium tier, multi-agent fan-out, prefetch — against 3 that buy money with latency. Both lists are short, and every entry on the first one exists because decode leaves arithmetic idle.

06Reading a number you were given

The ladder is a way of asking what a claimed saving is worth. The same question can be asked of a claimed price, and the answer is more uncomfortable, because the unit itself is not stable.

A token is a vendor-defined unit produced by a vendor-chosen subword algorithm.88,89 Tokenisers differ between vendors, and they differ between generations of the same vendor: the current documentation notes that one recent family's tokeniser produces roughly thirty per cent more tokens for the same text than the previous one.20 A price per million tokens is therefore a price per million units of a measure the seller defines and may redefine. The same text costs different amounts in different languages for the same reason, which has been measured and is not small.90,91

The deeper oddity is that the unit is an artefact of a compression scheme. Subword vocabularies are chosen to make a corpus short, which is a coding problem88,102; billing by the resulting symbol count means the buyer pays for how well the seller's encoder happens to fit the buyer's text.

Even holding the tokeniser fixed, the per-token price answers a question almost nobody is asking. What an agent programme wants to know is what a unit of finished work costs, and the path from one to the other runs through two multipliers the price list says nothing about: how many tokens a model needs to finish, and how often it finishes at all.

Experiment one models this directly. Three models are given the same task, with assistant output held equal per step so that only per-step productivity and terminal success differ. The list-price spread from the cheapest to the dearest is 5.00x. Per step it is 4.60x. Per run it is 3.92x. Per successful run it is 2.03x, and two of the three models have become indistinguishable: the middle model costs 1.033 times the cheapest one per finished task, on a list price twice as high.

The compression is not an artefact of the chosen parameters. It is structural. A model that costs more per token generally needs fewer steps and fails less often, and those two effects run against the price in the denominator. Which is to say that the per-token spread is an upper bound on the spread that matters, and usually a loose one.45,94

One denomination further and the ranking does not compress but reverses. To deliver an outcome you retry until a run succeeds, and every failed run costs some amount of a person's attention: triage, re-prompting, undoing a half-finished change. Add that term and the crossings are exact.

The dearest model overtakes the cheapest at $0.86 of human cost per failed run and overtakes the middle one at $4.06. At a fully loaded sixty dollars an hour those are 52 and 244 seconds of somebody's attention. A five-fold difference in list price is reversed by less than a minute of a person noticing that something went wrong.

This is the article's central practical point, and it does not depend on the particular numbers. Wherever a failure has a cost that is paid outside the token bill — and in an agent programme it nearly always does — the optimisation that looks obvious at the token is the wrong one at the outcome. Cost-controlled evaluation exists for exactly this reason, and remains rare for exactly the reason that it is hard to instrument.45,94,95 The agent benchmarks in common use were designed to measure whether a task was completed, not what completing it consumed, and the ones that report cost at all report it as a secondary column.86,103,104,105 The same is true one level down, where tool-use evaluations score whether the right function was called and not how many tokens the catalogue cost to carry.34,106,107

Averages are the other trap. Agent cost distributions are heavy-tailed: a small number of runs that thrash consume a large share of the bill, so the mean cost of a run is not the cost of a mean run, and a budget set from the mean is set from a number that describes almost no individual case.47,108,109

The same caution applies to the industry's most-quoted trend. The price of a fixed level of benchmark performance has fallen very fast: estimates range from nine-fold to nine-hundred-fold per year depending on the milestone, and a careful decomposition puts the frontier figure at five- to ten-fold per year with roughly three-fold of that attributable to algorithmic efficiency rather than hardware.110,111 That is a real and large deflation in the price of a capability. It is not a deflation in anyone's bill, because the unit of consumption moved at the same time: a task that used to be one call is now a loop, and one published measurement puts multi-agent systems at roughly fifteen times the tokens of a chat interaction.76,112

07Where the time actually goes

Latency in an agent is not the latency of a model call. It is the sum of a sequence of model calls, each of which waits for the tools of the previous one, and the standard advice — run the tools in parallel — addresses the smaller half of that.

Experiment six generates dependency graphs over twenty-four tool calls and schedules them in waves, where a wave is one assistant turn and the calls inside a wave overlap. At a low dependency rate the graph allows 3.63 waves instead of twenty-four calls, a round-trip reduction of 84.9 per cent. The realised speedup is nowhere near that.

The speedup is bounded below by 2.63x and above by 6.62x, and which end you get is decided by the length of the model turn. That is the opposite of the intuition: parallel tool calls pay more when the model is slow, because what a wave removes is round trips, and a round trip carries a model turn regardless of what the tools cost. When the tools are the expensive part, a wave is priced at its slowest member, and with a heavy-tailed latency distribution that is well above the average.74,75,109

The corollary is unwelcome. Where the model turn dominates the wall clock, the only remaining lever is fewer turns, and fewer turns is a planning problem rather than a concurrency problem. No scheduler fixes an agent that needs eleven steps to do what could be done in four.69,113

The other place agent latency hides is the queue. A fleet of agent workers is a multi-server queue, and multi-server queues have the property that the last few points of utilisation are extremely expensive.114,115,116

Between seventy and ninety per cent utilisation the modelled fleet shrinks by 22.2 per cent and the ninety-ninth-percentile queueing delay grows by 14.4x, from 11.6 to 167.3 seconds. The next five points buy a further 5.3 per cent of the fleet and cost another 2.26x on the tail.

Utilisation is also the wrong word for two different things. The hardware-side version — what fraction of the accelerator's peak arithmetic a workload actually achieves — is a training metric that transfers badly to decode, where the machine is memory-bound by construction and a low arithmetic utilisation is the correct operating point rather than a defect.117,118,119 The fleet-side version is the queueing quantity above. Reporting one and managing the other is a common and expensive confusion, and it is an old one: a server that is busy is not a server that is doing useful work, and neither is proportional to what it draws.120,121

Both curves are the same decision. This is why compute utilisation makes such a poor target: it is a measure of how close the system is to the point where its tail explodes, presented as a measure of efficiency.122,123,124 An agent platform asked to raise utilisation and lower p99 has been given one instruction twice, in opposite directions. The scaling argument that rescues parallel hardware — that the work grows to fill the machine — does not rescue a latency target, because the target is fixed while the work is not.74,125

Speculative decoding is the cleanest counter-example in the article, and worth dwelling on because of what it proves rather than what it does. It drafts several tokens with a small model and verifies them in a single forward pass of the large one, accepting a prefix of the draft under a rule that provably preserves the target model's output distribution.2,80

The expected number of accepted tokens per verification is bounded above by the draft length plus one, and reaches it only at perfect agreement. So the arithmetic per emitted token is strictly greater than one at every acceptance rate below certainty: at an acceptance rate of 0.80 the best draft length in the modelled range is 7, worth 3.08x on walltime and costing 2.01x the arithmetic. It is never free in FLOPs and it is always free in quality — the exact inverse of every technique in group three.

It looks free anyway, because decode is memory-bound and the extra positions ride along in a forward pass the machine was going to make. The experiment locates where that stops being true: at a draft length of fifteen and two thousand tokens of context, the arithmetic roof falls inside the feasible batch range, and past that point every speculated position is charged in full. The technique is free where the machine is idle and expensive where it is not, which is the general shape of everything in group five as well.

08What a saving has to be right about

Every technique on the ladder has a precondition, and the interesting failures are not failures of the mechanism but of the precondition. Three of them are worth stating precisely, because in each case the precondition is checkable and almost nobody checks it.

Caching assumes the prefix is stable. An agent loop resends its transcript every turn, so the billed input grows as the square of the turn count while the text the provider has never seen grows linearly.

At fifty turns, 96.42 per cent of every billed input token is a re-read of text already sent in the same run. Caching that prefix takes the input bill to 14.12 per cent of list, a 7.08-fold saving, and changes the output not at all. Putting one token that varies per request — a clock, a request id, a serialiser that randomises key order — at the head of the prompt takes it to 125 per cent, because every turn is now a fresh write at the write premium and no read ever hits. The spread between the best and the worst caching outcome on identical work is 8.85x, and the agent behaves the same way in both.1

The failure is silent by construction. There is no error; there are two usage counters that a dashboard has to be looking at. The documented lookback window makes this sharper: the API checks at most twenty positions back from a breakpoint, so a turn that adds many blocks can push the previous write out of the window. A run of consecutive tool-use blocks counts as one position, which is precisely what keeps a wide parallel turn from evicting its own cache — without that rule the window is exhausted at 12 parallel calls.1

A cascade assumes the verifier is good. Escalation schemes answer with a cheap model, check the answer, and pay for the expensive one only when the check fails.54,55 Experiment seven takes the arithmetic apart.

Accuracy and cost both fall linearly in the verifier's false-accept rate, and both derivatives are constant. Their ratio is exactly the strong model's own accuracy — 0.85 here — and it does not depend on the cheap model, the price ratio, or anything about the verifier except the rate itself. A cascade does not choose an exchange rate between accuracy and money; it chooses a position on a line whose slope was fixed before the design started. With a perfect verifier the cascade beats always-using-the-big-model on accuracy, at 58.0 per cent of the bill, because the cheap model is right on some items the expensive one misses. It falls back to parity at a false-accept rate of 0.288, and past that it is strictly worse than paying full price.

A semantic cache assumes you know what a wrong answer costs. The net saving from a response cache is the hit rate times one minus the false-hit rate times the price of a wrong answer, so the break-even false-hit rate is one over that price — and it does not involve the hit rate at all.

This is the invariance worth carrying away. The hit rate decides how much you save; it has no bearing on whether saving is a good idea. A cache that hits ninety per cent of the time and a cache that hits ten per cent of the time break even at the same false-hit rate. If a wrong answer costs fifty times a call, the cache must be right on more than ninety-eight per cent of its hits; going from a cache that is wrong half a per cent of the time to one wrong one and a half per cent removes two-thirds of the entire saving. And the hit rate itself is mostly a property of the demand rather than the cache: a thousand entries return 71.4 per cent of requests under one Zipf exponent and 48.6 per cent under another.29,49,50

The common structure in all three is that the precondition is about the workload, not the code, and the workload is the thing that changes without a deploy. A caching strategy validated in March is invalidated in April by a product decision to personalise the system prompt. A router trained on last quarter's traffic is a router for last quarter's traffic.126,127,128

09The unit the buyer pays in

At the top of the ladder is the only denomination that appears in a budget: the cost of a delivered outcome. Everything in this article is ultimately a claim about that number, and almost nothing in the stack is instrumented for it.

The gap is not an oversight. A token is counted by the vendor, a call by the client library, a run by the orchestrator; a delivered outcome is counted by whatever system the work was for, and the join between the two is a business decision rather than a technical one. Telemetry conventions for generative systems have standardised the left-hand side of that join and can do nothing about the right.92,93

What evidence exists about the right-hand side is mixed in a way that is itself informative. A field study of 5,172 customer-support agents found a 15 per cent average increase in issues resolved per hour, concentrated almost entirely in less experienced workers, with small declines in quality among the most experienced.97 A randomised writing experiment found large time savings and a quality increase.98 A field experiment with consultants found gains inside a jagged capability frontier and losses outside it.101 A randomised study of experienced developers working in their own repositories found that participants believed they were faster and were measurably slower.99 An early controlled study of code completion found a very large reduction in time-to-complete on a single well-specified task.129

Those are not contradictory results. They are the same result at different denominations. Time-on-task falls; quality moves in either direction depending on whether the work was inside the system's competence; the aggregate depends on the mix and on complementary investment that takes years to make. The productivity of a general technology shows up late, after the intangible reorganisation that lets it be used, which is the shape economists have been describing since long before this particular technology.100,130,131,132 And where a task cannot be automated at all, its relative price rises as everything around it gets cheaper, so the residual human step in an agent workflow becomes a larger share of the total precisely because the rest worked.133

Three practical consequences follow for anyone setting a budget.

The marginal cost of a wrong answer is the parameter that matters, and it is usually unmeasured. It sets the break-even of every cache, the acceptable false-accept rate of every cascade, the value of every retry, and the crossover between model tiers. Experiment eight shows it setting a cache's break-even directly; experiment one shows it reversing a five-fold price ranking. An organisation that cannot state it to within an order of magnitude cannot evaluate any of the techniques in this article, and will default to the denominator that is easiest to read.51,134

Falling unit prices do not produce falling bills. The price of a fixed capability has fallen by something between one and three orders of magnitude per year depending on how it is measured, and total spending has risen, because cheaper inference makes agentic patterns affordable and agentic patterns consume far more.76,110,111 This is the oldest observation in resource economics and it arrived here on schedule.112 Nor is the deflation mostly hardware. Per-transistor scaling has not delivered an order of magnitude a year since power stopped scaling with area, so most of the fall has come from serving software and from model design — which also means it is path-dependent on what today's accelerators happen to be good at.135,136,137

The deflation is an argument for waiting, and the option has a price. If a capability costs an order of magnitude less in a year, then building an expensive workaround today is buying an asset that depreciates at ninety per cent a year. That does not mean wait; it means that the engineering worth doing is the engineering whose value does not depend on current prices — instrumentation, acceptance tests, the definition of a successful run — and the engineering worth deferring is the elaborate substitution of a cheap model for an expensive one.138,139

There is one more denominator that belongs on the ladder and is not on it, because almost nobody is billed in it: energy. Inference energy per request varies by orders of magnitude across task types and model sizes, and the measurements that exist are hard to reconcile with the price list because the price list is a commercial instrument and the energy is a physical one.140,141,142 Every batching result in this article is also an energy result: a token produced at a batch of four hundred moved the same weights as a token produced at a batch of one, and divided the cost of moving them four hundred ways.

There is a last consequence, and it is less comfortable than the others. The list price is a schedule of trades a vendor is willing to make, and it is legible: half price for a day's delay, twice price for speed, a tenth for a cache hit, five times for an output token rather than an input one.20,24 For most agent programmes the largest available savings are sitting in that schedule, unclaimed, while effort goes into prompt compression worth a few per cent. The reason is not ignorance. It is that claiming them requires knowing which of your work can wait, which is a question about the business, and compressing a prompt is a question about the prompt.143,144

The last five points, 90% to 95%, buy 5.3% more of the fleet
and cost another 2.26x on p99.
Utilisation and tail latency are the same number read from opposite
ends; an agent platform is usually asked to maximise both.

E5  SPECULATIVE DECODING IS A PURE LATENCY PURCHASE
==========================================================================
Leviathan et al.'s expected accepted tokens per verification is
  E[a] = (1 - alpha^(gamma+1)) / (1 - alpha),
and the walltime improvement with a draft/target cost ratio c is
  S = (1 - alpha^(gamma+1)) / ((1 - alpha)(gamma*c + 1)).
The target model still runs gamma+1 positions through one forward
pass per verification, so the ARITHMETIC per emitted token is
  F = (gamma + 1 + gamma*c) / E[a].

Draft/target cost ratio c = 0.05.

At alpha=0.80 the best draft length in this range is gamma=7, worth
3.0823x on walltime and costing 2.0067x the arithmetic per output token.

F = 1 requires E[a] = gamma + 1 + gamma*c, and E[a] <= gamma+1 for
every alpha < 1, so F > 1 ALWAYS.  Speculative decoding cannot be
arithmetic-neutral at any acceptance rate below certainty: it is a
way of spending compute to buy time, and it is the only technique in
this article's vocabulary that leaves the output distribution exactly
unchanged while doing so.

It is free only while decode is memory-bound.  On E3's node a
verification pushes gamma+1 positions through ONE forward pass, so
the arithmetic per step is (gamma+1)*2*P*B while the memory traffic
is unchanged.  The roof is where (gamma+1)*2*P*B/FLOPS = (W+B*KV*L)/BW:
   gamma    context    B at roof B_max (memory)
       0       2048        never           1698
       0       8192        never            424
       5       2048           64           1698
       5       8192          878            424
      15       2048           20           1698
      15       8192           29            424
At gamma=0 (ordinary decode) the arithmetic roof is out of reach at
every batch that fits.  At gamma=15 and 2048 tokens of context it is
inside the feasible range, and past that point every speculated
position is charged in full.  The technique is free where the
machine is idle and expensive where it is not.

inside a wave run concurrently.  Tool latencies are lognormal with
median 2.00 s and sigma 1.00, so a wave waits for its slowest member.

E7  A CASCADE IS ONLY AS GOOD AS ITS ESCALATOR
==========================================================================
Cheap model correct on 0.62 of tasks, expensive on 0.85.
Cost ratio cheap:expensive = 0.20 (list output prices, Haiku 4.5 vs Opus 5).
A verifier accepts the cheap answer.  beta = it rejects a CORRECT
cheap answer; phi = it accepts an INCORRECT one.

  accuracy   = p_c(1-beta) + [p_c*beta + (1-p_c)(1-phi)] * p_e
  escalation = p_c*beta + (1-p_c)(1-phi)
  cost       = r + escalation      (in units of one expensive call)

    beta      phi   accuracy   escalation       cost vs always-big
    0.00     0.00     0.9430       0.3800     0.5800        58.0%
    0.05     0.05     0.9222       0.3920     0.5920        59.2%
    0.10     0.10     0.9014       0.4040     0.6040        60.4%
    0.20     0.10     0.8921       0.4660     0.6660        66.6%
    0.10     0.25     0.8530       0.3470     0.5470        54.7%
    0.10     0.50     0.7722       0.2520     0.4520        45.2%
    0.30     0.00     0.9151       0.5660     0.7660        76.6%

A PERFECT verifier gives accuracy 0.9430 (above always-big's 0.85,
because the cheap model is right on some items the big one misses)
at 58.0% of the always-big bill.

Now hold beta = 0 and raise phi (false accepts):
     phi   accuracy       cost  accuracy lost
    0.00     0.9430     0.5800         0.0000
    0.10     0.9107     0.5420         0.0323
    0.20     0.8784     0.5040         0.0646
    0.30     0.8461     0.4660         0.0969
    0.40     0.8138     0.4280         0.1292
    0.50     0.7815     0.3900         0.1615
    0.70     0.7169     0.3140         0.2261
    1.00     0.6200     0.2000         0.3230

Both derivatives are constant: d(accuracy)/d(phi) = -(1-p_c)*p_e =
-0.3230 and d(cost)/d(phi) = -(1-p_c) = -0.3800.  Ten points of false
acceptance therefore cost 3.23 points of end-to-end accuracy and
save 3.80 points of the bill, everywhere on the curve.
Their ratio is exactly p_e = 0.85: in ANY two-stage cascade the price
of a point of accuracy, paid in money, is the strong model's own
accuracy -- independent of the cheap model, the price ratio and the
verifier.  Only WHERE you sit on the line is negotiable.

The cascade matches always-big accuracy (0.85) at phi = 0.2879, where
it costs 47.1% of an always-big bill.  Above that false-accept rate
the cascade is strictly worse than paying full price.

E8  WHAT A SEMANTIC CACHE HAS TO BE RIGHT ABOUT
==========================================================================
A response cache saves one call on a hit.  If a hit is WRONG -- two
different intents collapsed onto one entry -- it costs whatever a
wrong answer costs, in units of one call: K.
  net saving per request = h * (1 - phi*K)
so the break-even false-hit rate is phi* = 1/K, INDEPENDENT of the
hit rate h.  The hit rate decides how much you save; it has nothing
to do with whether saving is a good idea.

         K max tolerable phi
         2       50.0000%
         5       20.0000%
        10       10.0000%
        25        4.0000%
        50        2.0000%
       100        1.0000%
      1000        0.1000%

Hit rate under a Zipf(s) intent distribution with M entries cached
(exact, from the distribution -- no sampling):
       s        M     hit rate
     0.8      100       25.58%
     0.8     1000       48.64%
     0.8     5000       72.41%
     1.0      100       49.49%
     1.0     1000       71.42%
     1.0     5000       86.77%
     1.2      100       73.51%
     1.2     1000       88.45%
     1.2     5000       95.50%

The same 1000 entries return 71.42% of requests at s=1.0 and 48.64% at
s=0.8: the shape of the demand decides the saving, not the size of
the cache.

Net calls saved per request at K=50 (h=0.7142):
       phi    net saved/req   share of max
    0.000%           0.7142         100.0%
    0.200%           0.6428          90.0%
    0.500%           0.5357          75.0%
    1.000%           0.3571          50.0%
    1.500%           0.1786          25.0%
    2.000%           0.0000           0.0%
    3.000%          -0.3571         -50.0%
Going from a 0.5%-wrong cache to a 1.5%-wrong one removes 66.7%
of the entire saving, and at phi = 2.0% the cache is exactly worth
nothing however often it hits.

With K = 1 -- a wrong answer costs one wasted call and nothing else
-- phi* = 100%: every cache is worth having.  Everything about
whether to cache sits in the estimate of K, which is a claim about
the business and not about the cache.

11What would have to change

The ladder's shape is not a law. It is a description of what today's interfaces make promisable, and three specific changes would move things up it.

An acceptance test attached to the run. The reason so much of the vocabulary is stranded at D1 is that nothing downstream of a call is observable to the thing making the call. A technique that could demonstrate it had not moved the success rate would be at D4 by definition. That requires a per-run verdict, which requires an acceptance test, which is the piece of infrastructure agent programmes most reliably do not build.45,86,94

Prices that expose the curve. The published rate is one point chosen off a batch-occupancy curve. Tiers already exist at the extremes — deferred batch at half, premium latency at double — and each is used precisely because it makes a trade explicit.20,24 A finer-grained schedule of latency-for-price would let buyers sit where their workload belongs rather than where the average does, and would make the second half of this article unnecessary.

A unit that survives a model change. As long as the billing unit is a vendor-defined token, cross-generation comparison is not possible without recounting, and a thirty per cent change in tokenisation is a thirty per cent change in price that appears nowhere on the price list.20,90 Any denominator further up the ladder — a call, a run, a resolved ticket — would be stable across that change.

Until then the honest position is the one the ladder describes. Two techniques out of twenty-eight can be shown to be free. Seven more are provably neutral about the answer and spend a different currency instead, and every one of those seven is worth reaching for before anything in group three, because their preconditions are about scheduling rather than about the model's behaviour. The rest are bets on a workload: often good bets, sometimes excellent ones, but bets, and the only way to know is to measure at a denomination coarse enough to include the failures.

The question the article started with has a short answer. Cheaper per what: per the thing that is actually being bought, which is almost never a token, and which nothing in the stack will count for you.
