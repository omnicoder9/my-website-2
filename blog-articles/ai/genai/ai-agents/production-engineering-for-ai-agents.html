1The discipline and its assumption

Production engineering is not a collection of tools. It is a discipline with a single object: to make change safe. Every technique in it — version control, reproducible builds, immutable images, declarative infrastructure, staged rollout, rollback, disaster recovery — exists to answer one question under pressure. Something is wrong; what changed, and can it be put back?

The discipline earned its methods the hard way. Jim Gray's 1985 survey of why computers stop found that most outages traced not to hardware but to software and to operations — to people changing things 1. The response, over four decades, was to make change into an artefact: something with a name, a content hash, a provenance record and a predecessor. Continuous delivery made the pipeline the only path to production 2. The twelve-factor formulation insisted that configuration live outside the build and that processes be disposable 3. Containers made the runtime a shippable object 4, and the OCI image specification made that object addressable by the digest of its own content 5. Google's cluster managers made the desired state declarative so that the difference between what you asked for and what is running became a computable quantity 6 7. Blue-green and canary deployment made the transition itself a controlled variable 8 9. Supply-chain work closed the loop by binding the artefact to the inputs that produced it, in a statement somebody signs 10 11 12. The research programme that measures all this found, repeatedly, that the organisations which deploy most often are also the ones that break production least, because small reversible changes are cheaper to reason about than large ones 13.

Every one of those methods rests on an assumption so basic that it is rarely stated. The thing you deploy is the thing that decides what the system does. Pin the artefact and you have pinned the behaviour. Roll the artefact back and you have rolled the behaviour back. The assumption is not quite true of any system — databases hold state, caches hold staleness, humans hold credentials — but it is true enough of a stateless web service that the whole apparatus works, and the exceptions are handled as exceptions.

An agent breaks the assumption in a way that is not an exception. Consider what actually determines the output of a single agent turn in production. The application image, certainly. But also: which snapshot of the model the vendor's alias resolved to this morning; what the retrieval index contains, which is what last night's ingestion put there; what the tool servers said their tools do, which their operators changed on Tuesday; what the tenant's configuration record says; whether the prompt cache was warm; how many other people's requests happened to be in the same forward-pass batch. None of these is in the image. Several are not in your organisation. One of them is not anywhere: no copy of it exists that anybody could name, restore or diff.

The usual response is to treat this as a monitoring problem — if you cannot control it, at least observe it — or as an evaluation problem, to be solved by more offline scoring. Both help. Neither touches the structure. The structural fact is that the production apparatus is defined over a unit of change, and the agent's behaviour is defined over a set of determinants that is much larger than that unit and only partly overlaps it. Deployment, rollback, staging, disaster recovery and multi-tenancy are all operations on the unit. They are exact, correct, well-tooled operations on a minority of what matters.

So the organising question of this article is not what an agent deployment looks like. It is: what moves when you deploy, and what moves when you do not? For every determinant of production behaviour, there is a place where the copy that governs the next request is actually held, and a party whose action changes it. Name that place and you can say, without arguing about architecture, exactly which of your existing practices reach it. Most of them reach one place. This article is an attempt to map the rest.

2Custody, and how to read it

The device is a ladder of six rungs. Each rung names a place where the authoritative copy of a determinant can live — not a severity, not an importance, and not a layer of the stack. K5 is inside the image digest. K4 is in your deployed configuration, outside the digest but inside your change process. K3 is in your own durable state, written by the system as it runs. K2 arrives with the request. K1 is held in somebody else's control plane and changes on their calendar. K0 is nowhere: no copy exists to pin.

The coding rule has to be stated precisely or the ladder becomes an opinion survey. The rung is where the copy that governs a live request is held, given the ordinary documented deployment of the technology — not the strictest configuration a determined team could reach. A model identifier written as a vendor alias sits at K1 because the alias-to-snapshot mapping is the vendor's to change 14. The same plate written as a dated snapshot sits at K4. The ordinary case is what is coded; the achievable case is recorded separately, as movability, and it turns out to be the most actionable column in the ledger.

A second rule covers derived determinants. Some things in the list are not values but verdicts — an evaluation score, a canary decision. For those, the rung is the outermost custody of anything the verdict depends on, because a verdict is only as pinned as its least pinned input. That is why the evaluation gate sits at K1 rather than at the rung of your eval set: the number moves when the judge moves, and the judge is not yours.

The orthogonal axis is notice: how you find out the determinant changed. Scheduled means you caused it and know what you did. Announced means somebody tells you in advance, with a date. Observed means you learn from the behaviour, afterwards, if you are looking. The axis matters because the entire incident-response vocabulary — correlate against deploys, check the change log, roll back the most recent change — presumes the scheduled case.

Every plate carries the same gauge: a chain anchored at the artefact, running outward through the six positions, sealed at the rung where the governing copy lives and drawn hairline beyond. A short chain means the determinant is close to something your pipeline touches. A long chain means it is not. The chain is always drawn at full width so that a glance across the article compares like with like, and the notice mark sits in the corner: filled for scheduled, half-filled for announced, open for observed.

Thirty-one determinants are coded, in six groups: the artefact you build, the platform it lands on, the pipeline that promotes it, the inference path, the context assembled for each call, and the tenancy, secrets and deadlines around all of it. The list is not exhaustive and does not try to be. It is meant to be complete over the vocabulary a production engineer already uses — if a practice has a name (canary, rollback, quota, rotation, retirement), there is a plate for what it governs.

The matrix is worth pausing on before the plates, because it contains the article's least surprising and most consequential fact. The scheduled column is exactly the set of plates at K5 and K4, with nothing else in it, and nothing at K5 or K4 outside it. That is very close to a tautology — a change you make is a change to something you hold — and the tautology is the point. Change management is coextensive with custody. It is not a practice that could be extended, with more discipline, to cover the other nineteen determinants; it is a practice whose definition excludes them.

3The artefact you build

Start where the discipline works, because it does work, and because the contrast is the argument. Four plates sit here, and three of them are at K5.

B01

Application image

K5 · scheduled
decidesthe agent loop itself: how a step is planned, how a tool result is parsed, how context is compacted when it overflows
governing copythe layers named by the OCI manifest digest
changes whena build is pushed and the workload's image reference is changed
pinned bythe sha256 digest in the image manifest 5
fails asa mutable tag resolving to different layers on two nodes, so two replicas run different agents under one version string

This is the only plate on which every classical deployment guarantee holds unmodified, and it is the reason the rest of the article is needed.

B02

Dependency closure

K5 · scheduled
decidesthe semantics of everything the loop calls: HTTP client timeouts, JSON parsing strictness, tokenizer behaviour
governing copythe resolved lockfile baked into the image
changes whenthe lock is regenerated and a new image is built
pinned bya lockfile with per-package hashes, and the digest that contains it
fails asa floating version range resolved at build time, which silently moves the closure to the registry's calendar rather than yours

Resolution is the moment custody is taken. A range that is resolved at build time is K5; the same range resolved at container start is K1.

B03

Agent framework revision

K5 · scheduled
decidesretry policy, tool dispatch order, how many steps are attempted before the loop gives up, what a truncated context does
governing copythe framework package inside the dependency closure
changes whenthe pinned framework version is raised and rebuilt
pinned bythe lockfile entry and the image digest
fails asa minor release that changes loop semantics without changing any API you call, so no compiler and no type checker sees it

The framework owns more of the observable behaviour than the application code does, and it is versioned like a utility library.

B04

Self-hosted model weights

K4 · scheduled · movable
decideseverything the model decides, for teams that serve their own
governing copyan object-store path or registry reference resolved when the pod starts
changes whenthe reference is repointed, or the object behind it is replaced
pinned bya digest, if the reference carries one; a bucket path if it does not
fails asa pod restarting onto a different checkpoint than its neighbours, with no deployment event to correlate against

Kubernetes image volumes, added in v1.35, let weights ship as an OCI artefact and raise this plate to K5 15.

The application image is the one determinant over which every classical guarantee holds without modification. The digest names the bytes; two replicas with the same digest run the same code; the previous digest is retained and can be restored in seconds; a signed attestation can bind the digest to the commit and the builder 16 17. If agent behaviour were a function of the image, this article would be four paragraphs long.

The dependency closure is more interesting than it looks, because it is where custody is taken. A version range is not a pin; it is a standing instruction to somebody else's registry. Resolved at build time and written into a lockfile that the image contains, the closure is K5. Resolved when the container starts — an unpinned install in an entrypoint script, a plugin downloaded on boot — the same declaration puts the closure at K1, on the registry's calendar rather than yours. Nothing in the source distinguishes the two cases. The moment of resolution does.

The agent framework deserves its own plate because of how much of the observable behaviour it owns. The loop that decides when to call a tool, how many steps to attempt, what to do with a malformed tool result, and how to compact a context that has overflowed is library code, versioned like a utility. A minor release that changes the retry policy or the compaction heuristic changes what the agent does on precisely the inputs that were already marginal, and it does so without changing any signature that a type checker or a test double would notice. This is Hyrum's observation about implicit dependencies 18 applied to a loop whose observable behaviour is mostly emergent. Semantic versioning promises that the API is stable 19; nobody promised the agent was.

Self-hosted weights are the plate that has moved most recently. The conventional pattern — a bucket path or a registry reference, resolved when the pod starts — is K4: your reference, your change process, but not covered by the image digest, and silently repointable. Kubernetes v1.35 shipped image volumes, which let a model ship as an OCI artefact mounted into the pod 15 20, and that single change moves the weights to K5, under the same digest discipline as the code. It is the clearest example in the whole matrix of a determinant being pulled up a rung by engineering rather than by exhortation.

And then the group ends, and with it the comfortable part. The natural next question is how much of the behaviour these four plates actually fix. The answer is a multiplication rather than a fraction: take the determinants that vary independently of the digest during the fortnight a build is deployed, count the distinct values each takes, and multiply.

Ninety thousand seven hundred and twenty distinct behavioural configurations can be reached under one release tag, and the image digest pins none of them. Two thousand staging runs, drawn uniformly — which is far more favourable than real pre-production traffic, which concentrates — reach 2.18 per cent of the space. Half of it would take sixty-two thousand eight hundred and eighty-two. Nothing in that calculation is a criticism of staging. It is a statement about what a version string is naming.

4The platform it lands on

The platform group contains the determinants that the infrastructure decides on your behalf. Only one of the five is at K4.

P01

Node and accelerator assignment

K1 · observed
decidesthe arithmetic: which silicon, which driver, which kernel library version executes the forward pass
governing copythe cloud provider's placement decision at scheduling time
changes whenthe provider rotates hardware, patches a driver, or your pod is rescheduled
pinned byan instance-type string, which names a class and not a device
fails astwo replicas of one digest returning different logits, discovered as an unexplained shift in an offline metric

You can pin an instance family. You cannot pin the die.

P02

Replica count in force

K3 · observed
decideshow much concurrency exists, and therefore queueing delay, batch sizes upstream, and the rate at which you spend quota
governing copythe controller's current desired-replicas number
changes whenthe autoscaler reacts to a metric, on its own loop, without a deploy
pinned bynothing pins it; the policy that produces it is K4
fails asan autoscaler steady at a comfortable metric while the work queue grows, because the metric it reads is not the resource that is exhausted

The scaling policy is in your manifest. The number that governs the next request is not.

P03

Execution ceiling

K1 · announced
decidesthe longest task the deployment can complete at all, and therefore which problems the agent is allowed to be good at
governing copythe platform's hard maximum, inside which your own setting lives
changes whenthe platform publishes a new limit
pinned bythe documented maximum for the runtime you chose
fails asa ceiling that truncates the hardest runs and leaves the mean untouched, so aggregate metrics look healthy

Cloud Run allows a request up to 60 minutes 21; AgentCore allows 15 minutes synchronous, 60 minutes streaming and 8 hours asynchronous 22.

P04

Ingress route and traffic split

K4 · scheduled
decideswhich build a given request reaches, and in what proportion
governing copythe route object in your cluster or the revision weights in your service
changes whenyou apply a new route or shift a weight
pinned bythe manifest under version control
fails asa split expressed in requests while the thing you are comparing is measured in completed tasks

Gateway API's InferencePool makes the model server pool a first-class backend with its own endpoint picker 23.

P05

Durable execution history

K3 · observed
decideswhat a resumed run does after a crash, a deploy or a worker restart
governing copythe event history the workflow engine replays
changes whenthe run makes progress
pinned bya run identifier, which names the history but does not fix it
fails asa replay against new code that no longer produces the same decisions, so the resumed run diverges from the one that was interrupted

Durable execution makes the run survive the deploy, which also means the run survives the rollback 24.

Node and accelerator assignment is at K1 for a reason that is easy to miss: you can pin an instance family, and you cannot pin a die. A rescheduled pod may land on a different revision of the same accelerator, with a different driver and a different kernel library, and the forward pass is floating-point arithmetic whose reduction order depends on all three. The effect is small and the consequence is not, because the failure mode is a shift in an offline metric with no deployment event to correlate against — the exact shape of incident that the change-log reflex cannot resolve.

The replica count is at K3, which is the plate most likely to be argued with. The scaling policy is in your manifest and is plainly K4. The number that governs the next request is the output of a controller running its own loop against a metric 25, and no deploy is involved. The distinction is not pedantic: it is the difference between a quantity you can diff against yesterday and a quantity you can only observe.

It also sets up the failure that makes agent workloads distinctive. A turn is mostly waiting. In the lab's model — nine steps, six seconds each, sixty milliseconds of local work per step — a pod spends 1.11 per cent of a turn's wall clock on its own CPU. The offered concurrency at twelve turns per second is 648 turns in flight, which at forty slots per pod needs seventeen pods. At seventeen pods, CPU utilisation reads 21.2 per cent against a 70 per cent target, so the horizontal autoscaler's own formula asks for six.

Six pods is 240 slots against 648 turns in flight: a load factor of 2.70, at which no latency exists because the queue does not reach a steady state. The autoscaler is not malfunctioning. It is reading the resource the work does not consume, and the queue is invisible in that signal, so the controller is stable while the system is not. The fix is old and well understood — scale on concurrency or on queue depth, which is what event-driven autoscaling exists for 26 — but it is a fix that has to be chosen, and the default is CPU. Queueing theory has said the same thing since Erlang: what saturates is the server, and the server here is somebody else's fleet 27 28. Under heavy traffic the waiting time is governed by utilisation and variability rather than by the mean 29, and agent step latencies are highly variable.

The execution ceiling is at K1 because the maximum is the platform's, and your own timeout setting lives inside it. Cloud Run allows a request up to sixty minutes, with five minutes as the default 21. AgentCore Runtime documents fifteen minutes for a synchronous request, sixty for a streaming connection and eight hours for an asynchronous job, along with a hundred-megabyte payload and a one-gigabyte image 22, and it terminates a session after fifteen minutes idle or eight hours of lifetime 30. Serverless platforms were designed for short, stateless, event-driven functions 31, and the workloads that shaped their limits were dominated by very short invocations 32. An agent turn is neither short nor stateless, which is why agent-specific runtimes have grown session semantics that ordinary function platforms do not have.

The lab's three workload classes make the consequence concrete. A five-minute ceiling is invisible to a short support turn, removes 9.26 per cent of coding turns, and removes 68.80 per cent of research turns. What it removes is never a random sample: the coding turns it cuts average 44.6 steps against a class mean of 14, and they carry 29.5 per cent of all the step work the class performs. A ceiling is a filter on task difficulty that leaves the mean latency looking healthy, and its effect on a quality metric is indistinguishable from the model having become worse at hard problems.

The ingress route is the group's one K4 plate, and it is the plate the ecosystem has most recently rebuilt. Gateway API's Inference Extension makes a pool of model servers a first-class backend with its own endpoint picker, so that routing can be aware of cache state and queue depth rather than treating a model server as a generic HTTP endpoint 33 23. The same period produced the sharpest available illustration of a K1 lifecycle plate: the Kubernetes project announced in November 2025 that Ingress NGINX would be retired, and the Steering and Security Response Committees followed in January 2026 with a statement that about half of cloud-native environments depended on it and that none of the alternatives was a drop-in replacement 34 35 36. The cadence underneath that is itself a K1 determinant: the project maintains release branches for the three most recent minor versions and patches a release for roughly a year 37, so the floor under every cluster moves on a schedule nobody deploying an agent chose.

Durable execution closes the group with a genuine paradox. Recording every step in a replayable history is the right answer to crashes, worker restarts and long-running human-in-the-loop waits 24, and it is what makes an eight-hour agent session survivable at all. It also means the run survives your deploy — and therefore survives your rollback. A history recorded under one version, replayed against another, is a determinant at K3 that no artefact operation can reach.

5The pipeline that promotes it

The pipeline group is where the mismatch becomes uncomfortable, because these are the plates that are supposed to be the control. Three of the five are at K5.

C01

Unit and integration verdict

K5 · scheduled
decideswhether the artefact is allowed to be built at all
governing copya deterministic function of the artefact and the test code, both inside the digest
changes whenthe code or the tests change
pinned bythe commit and the digest together
fails asa green suite that exercises only the part of the system the suite can reach, which is the part that was already under control

The strongest gate in the pipeline can only see one rung.

C02

Evaluation gate verdict

K1 · observed · movable
decideswhether a candidate is good enough to promote
governing copya score computed from your eval set, a model you do not hold, and often a judge you do not hold either
changes whenany of its three inputs changes, including the two you do not control
pinned bythe eval set's version; the model and judge are pinned only if you pin them
fails asa score that moves because the judge moved, read as a change in the candidate

Pinning a dated judge snapshot raises this plate to K4; pinning the candidate as well is what makes the comparison a comparison.

C03

Canary decision

K2 · observed
decideswhether the new build is promoted or withdrawn
governing copya comparison over live traffic, whose composition arrives from outside
changes whentraffic changes, which it does continuously
pinned bythe analysis configuration; never the traffic
fails asa window long enough for the error-rate signal and far too short for the quality signal, so quality regressions are promoted by default

See the lab: the same window that resolves a transport regression admits quality regressions of up to seventeen points.

C04

Rollback target

K5 · scheduled
decideswhat production runs after the undo
governing copythe previous revision's digest, retained by the platform
changes whena revision is retired out of the retained history
pinned bythe digest, and the platform's revision-history limit
fails asan undo that restores the code and leaves everything the bad build wrote, routed to, or taught the store

Rollback is exact over K5 and K4 and has no purchase at all below them.

C05

Build provenance

K5 · scheduled
decideswhat the artefact is allowed to claim about its own inputs
governing copya signed attestation naming the builder, the source and the materials
changes whena new build produces a new statement
pinned bythe subject digest inside the signed statement 10 11
fails asprovenance that covers the image and says nothing about the prompt, the tool catalogue or the model, which is most of what decided the output

The supply-chain apparatus is complete and correct over exactly the rung that was never the problem.

The unit and integration verdict is a deterministic function of the artefact and the test code, both inside the digest. That is exactly what makes it strong and exactly what limits it. A test suite can only exercise what it can hold constant, so it holds the model constant by not calling it, holds the tools constant by mocking them, and holds retrieval constant by fixing a corpus. Each of those decisions is correct, and each of them removes a K1 or K3 determinant from the thing being tested. The strongest gate in the pipeline can see one rung. This is the production-readiness gap that the machine-learning literature named a decade ago — that the code is a small box in a much larger diagram 38, that the tests which matter are tests of data and models rather than of functions 39, and that the practices differ from conventional software engineering precisely in what cannot be held fixed 40 41.

The evaluation gate is the derived-rung rule's clearest case. Its score depends on your eval set, which is yours; on a model, which is not; and very often on a judge model, which is also not. Pin nothing and the number moves for three independent reasons, at least two of which arrive without notice. The agent benchmark literature has been explicit that cost, reproducibility and standardisation are as much of the problem as capability 42, and that reported failures concentrate in specification and coordination rather than in the base model 43. Benchmarks such as SWE-bench, tau-bench and OSWorld are useful precisely because they fix the environment 44 45 46; the fixing is the contribution. A gate that does not fix its inputs is measuring a moving object with a moving ruler.

The canary decision is at K2 because what it compares is live traffic, whose composition arrives from outside and changes continuously. Progressive delivery is one of the most reliable techniques in the discipline 9 47, and it works because the signal it watches — error rate, latency, saturation — is observed on every request. Agent quality is not.

Hold the statistics constant and vary only the observation rate. At five per cent of two hundred requests per second, a transport-error regression from 0.50 to 0.75 per cent is resolvable in 20.5 minutes at ninety-five per cent confidence and eighty per cent power. The same rigour applied to a four-point drop in task success — one completed task per twenty-five model calls, thirty per cent of them eligible for an automatic verdict, a judge that is ninety per cent accurate in both directions — needs 5.78 hours, a factor of 16.9. Nobody bakes a canary for six hours. So the practical question is what the shorter window can resolve: inside the 20.5 minutes that settles the error-rate question there are 147 judged tasks per arm, and the smallest true quality regression they can detect is 17.1 points. Anything shallower is promoted, on schedule, with a green canary. This is not a failure of the canary; it is the consequence of running a task-rate signal through a request-rate window.

The rollback target is at K5, and the plate's honesty is in the fifth field. The undo is exact over the artefact and has no purchase below it. Consider a build that writes bad records into the retrieval store — a summarisation change, a broken extraction, an agent writing back conclusions drawn under a prompt that was wrong. The lab models a store of fifty thousand records, twelve writes per minute, eight retrieved per step.

An hour between the bad deploy and the rollback leaves 720 records, and 10.96 per cent of later retrievals touch at least one of them. The code is restored in seconds. With no targeted cleanup, the poisoned share falls below one per cent only after thirty days, as the store grows around it. Four hours undetected gives 37.79 per cent. The number that matters operationally is not the detection delay but the ratio between the two clocks: the artefact's rollback takes seconds and the residue's takes weeks, and only one of them appears on the incident timeline.

Build provenance completes the group and completes the irony. The supply-chain apparatus — signed statements, verifiable builders, hermetic inputs, graduated levels of assurance 16 10 12 — is rigorous, well-specified and increasingly automatic. It covers the image. It says nothing about the prompt, the tool catalogue, the index or the weights, which is to say it covers the rung that was never the problem. That is not an argument against provenance. It is an argument for extending the subject of the attestation to the whole bundle that determines behaviour, which is a solvable problem and mostly an unsolved one.

M06

Batch composition

K0 · observed · movable
decidesthe floating-point reduction order in the forward pass, and therefore which token wins a near-tie

fails asa reproduction attempt that fails for a reason no log can carry, ending in the belief that models are simply nondeterministic

Batch-invariant kernels make this reproducible on serving you control 50 51; nothing makes it reproducible on serving you do not.

The model identifier is the single highest-leverage line of configuration in an agent deployment, and the difference between its two forms is invisible in review. An alias is a subscription: the provider repoints it and every request after that moment is served by different weights. A dated snapshot is a pin. Providers document both and describe the lifecycle in four states — active, legacy, deprecated, retired — with the last one returning an error 14. Writing the snapshot moves the plate from K1 to K4 and converts a silent change into a migration with a published date. It is the cheapest rung change available anywhere in the matrix.

The weights themselves stay at K1 whatever you write, because the pin names the snapshot without preserving it. What the pin buys is notice, and notice is generous by the standards of most dependencies: Anthropic commits to at least sixty days before retirement 14; OpenAI publishes at least six months for generally available models, three for specialised variants, and as little as about two weeks for previews 48. Sixty days is a long time in a change log and a short time in a migration that requires re-evaluating an agent whose behaviour is not specified anywhere, which is the practical reason model retirement is an operations problem rather than a dependency-bump problem. The measured trend in how long a task an agent can complete has been moving quickly enough 52 that the replacement is usually better and differently wrong.

Decoding parameters are at K4 and are the group's only scheduled plate. They are also the most commonly misunderstood, because temperature zero is widely believed to buy reproducibility. It buys the greedy path through a distribution that is itself not stable.

Which brings the ladder to its floor. Production inference servers batch requests dynamically to keep the accelerator busy — continuous batching 53, paged key-value caches 54, speculative decoding 55 — and the composition of the batch your request joins is determined by other people's traffic. Thinking Machines Lab's analysis in September 2025 made the mechanism precise: common kernels for matrix multiplication, normalisation and attention are not batch-invariant, so the reduction order for your tokens depends on the batch size, tiny floating-point differences propagate, and a near-tie in the argmax flips 50. The batch existed for microseconds and no log records it. A seed does not reach it. There is no copy of it to pin, which is what K0 means. Batch-invariant kernels fix this on serving you control, at a performance cost, and subsequent work has explored recovering the performance while keeping the guarantee 51; none of it is available to you as a caller of somebody else's endpoint.

Between those two sit the two plates that decide whether the request is served at all. Which upstream actually answered is at K3: the gateway's live state — breaker positions, health checks, retry budgets — not the policy that produced it. A fallback that succeeds is a silent second deployment, shipping itself at the moment you are least able to inspect it, and producing a population of responses from a model nobody evaluated. A fallback that fails is an outage you can see. The first is worse.

Rate limits and quota are at K1 and are the one determinant in the matrix that other people can exhaust on your behalf. Limits are enforced per organisation across several axes at once — requests and tokens, per minute and per day — and whichever is hit first governs; issuing more keys does not enlarge the pool 49. Tiers are unlocked by cumulative spend rather than by need 56. Providers also ration the rate of increase: OpenAI's guidance is that beyond a million input tokens per minute, traffic should grow by no more than fifty per cent every fifteen minutes 49. That is a sentence with a disaster-recovery consequence hiding in it, and it is taken up in the tenancy section. The transport-level machinery for all this is ancient and adequate — 429 with a retry hint 57 58, exponential backoff with jitter so that clients do not resynchronise 59 — but a refusal at step seven of a twelve-step plan is not a failed request, it is a failed task, and the two are counted in different places.

This group holds the determinants that most people would call "the agent" if asked to point at it. None is at K5.

X05

Prompt cache state

K1 · observed · movable
decidesthe latency and the price of the call, and therefore how much context the design can afford to carry
governing copythe provider's ephemeral cache, with a five-minute lifetime by default and an hour at twice the write price 61

pinned bynothing; the cache is keyed on a prefix you can only hope to reproduce
fails asa prefix reordered for readability, quietly turning every request into a cache miss and multiplying the bill

Caching makes the order of the prompt an operational variable, which is the sort of coupling that outlives whoever introduced it.

The system prompt is the plate where the industry's own behaviour is the evidence. Prompts migrated out of source code and into registries and config stores for good operational reasons: they change more often than code, they are edited by people who do not deploy, and a prompt change should not require a rebuild. Each of those reasons is a description of leaving K5. The result is a determinant with more influence over output than any function in the repository, edited without review, versioned by whatever the registry happens to offer, and outside the rollback. In June 2026 OpenAI deprecated vendor-hosted reusable prompt objects, scheduling the API for shutdown, and the published migration advice is to move the prompt content into application code 48. Read as a custody move, that is K1 to K5 in one instruction, and it is the right direction.

The tool catalogue is a prompt written by people who do not know they are writing one. Every connected server publishes its own tool names, descriptions and schemas, and the model chooses among them on the strength of that wording. The operator of a server can rename a parameter, reword a description or add a tool without any change on your side, and the effect lands on tool-selection behaviour across every tenant simultaneously. The protocol has been moving toward making the catalogue cacheable and explicitly versioned, with list results carrying a time to live and a cache scope 60, which is precisely the machinery required to pin a catalogue snapshot and move the plate to K4. Whether teams use it is a different question. The security literature has treated the catalogue mainly as an injection surface 62 63 64 65 66; it is also, more mundanely, an unversioned dependency on somebody else's prose.

Retrieval contents and agent memory are both at K3, and they are the determinants that make an agent's behaviour a function of its own history. What the agent believes is usually more decisive than what it was instructed to do, and what it believes is whatever ingestion and its own write-backs put in the store. Memory in particular has a K3 determinant with a K5 review process: the code that writes memories is reviewed, and the memories are not. The practical consequence is that a prompt change is evaluated against a population of stored context that predates it, which is a confound nobody controls for because it does not look like a variable.

The prompt cache is at K1 and is the group's quiet one. Providers cache the processed prefix of a request and charge a fraction to read it back — with a five-minute lifetime by default, an hour available at twice the write price, and a small number of explicit breakpoints 61. That turns the order of the prompt into an operational variable. Move a stable block after a volatile one for readability and every request becomes a cache miss; the behaviour is unchanged and the bill is not. It is a coupling between presentation and cost that has no analogue in ordinary service design, and it reliably outlives whoever introduced it.

Observability is the discipline's answer to all four of these, and it has matured: OpenTelemetry's generative-AI semantic conventions give a shared vocabulary for recording the model, the parameters, the tool calls and the token counts of a span 67. Recording is necessary and is not custody. A trace tells you which prompt revision served a request; it does not let you put the old one back, and it does not exist for the batch.

Kubernetes retired Ingress NGINX in March 2026 after warning that about half of cloud-native environments depended on it 34 35; OpenAI scheduled three platform products for shutdown on one date 48.

Tenant configuration is at K2 and is, read plainly, a deployment made by somebody outside your change process. A support engineer enabling a tool for one customer has altered what the agent can do, for that customer, with no artefact, no review and no rollback. Configuration error has been the leading cause of user-visible failure in large systems for as long as anyone has measured it 70 71, and two of the most consequential outages of the last two years were configuration rather than code: a feature file that grew beyond the size its consumer expected 72, and a content update that its own vendor's root-cause analysis traced to a parameter-count mismatch between a validator and a consumer 73. Neither would have been caught by a test of the artefact, because neither was in the artefact. The canonical demonstration is older still: Knight Capital's 2012 loss came from deploying new code to seven of eight servers and reusing a repurposed feature flag, with the regulator's order noting the absence of a second review and of controls to halt the resulting order flow 74.

Credentials are at K4 and scheduled, which makes them look safe. The lab shows why they are not. Rotation is scheduled against a calendar; runs are not. With the coding-turn distribution — mean 126 seconds, median 86 — 3.50 per cent of runs cross an hourly rotation boundary, and to lose fewer than one run in ten thousand the old credential must stay valid for 728 seconds, 5.8 times the mean run and 8.5 times the median.

That number is a property of the tail, not of the average, which is why overlap windows sized from a mean produce a steady trickle of authorisation failures that look like permission bugs. The tension sharpens as sessions get longer: an eight-hour AgentCore session 22 crosses an hourly boundary eight times, and no overlap shorter than the session helps it. Short-lived workload identity is the right direction for everything else — it is the core of zero-trust practice 75, the reason SPIFFE issues frequently-rotated identity documents 76, and the standing advice on cryptoperiods 77 — and it is in direct tension with work that runs for hours. Delegation rather than possession is the structural answer: a run that holds an exchangeable token rather than a long-lived secret can be re-issued mid-flight 78 79. The secrets that leak are usually the ones nobody rotated at all, which remains the larger problem by volume 80.

The isolation boundary is at K1 because the platform provides it and the platform's model is the platform's to change. The strong end of the market is explicit about both halves of the bargain: AgentCore gives every session a dedicated microVM with its own CPU, memory and filesystem, terminates it at the end of the session and sanitises memory 68 — lightweight virtualisation of the kind that made this affordable in the first place 81 82 — while stating plainly that it does not enforce the mapping from session to user, which is yours to get right 69. Compute isolation is the easy half. The hard half is the store, because the memory, the index and the workflow history are shared by construction, and the guidance for multi-tenant clusters has always been that the boundary must be reasoned about per resource rather than per platform 83 84.

Quota allocation is at K4 and is the one lever in this group you fully control. The lab makes the choice concrete: one organisation budget of two million tokens per minute, twelve thousand tokens per request, twenty-five tenants, aggregate demand at eighty per cent of the ceiling, and one tenant that is forty per cent of demand and bursts to five times its rate one minute in twenty.

Under a pooled budget the smallest tenant is refused on 4.70 per cent of its requests, against 0.18 per cent on the same arrival stream with the neighbour's burst removed: 96.3 per cent of its refusals are caused by a tenant it shares a key with and has never heard of. Strict proportional sub-quotas remove that coupling and make things worse for exactly the tenant they were meant to protect — 11.68 per cent refused — because an allocation sized to a small tenant's mean is smaller than its own variance, and 24.8 per cent of the budget goes unused. Sub-quotas with borrowing of the unclaimed remainder get the smallest tenant to 0.43 per cent while keeping the largest tenant's overflow off everyone else. The lesson generalises past quota: isolation sized at the mean is not isolation, and the small participant is the one it fails.

The same arithmetic decides how much to share in the first place.

Pooling independent tenants saves capacity because peaks do not coincide, and the saving is bounded: with a coefficient of variation of 0.6 and capacity sized at the ninety-ninth percentile, the ceiling is 58.3 per cent and eighty per cent of it is reached at twenty-six tenants. Going from twenty-five shared tenants to two hundred buys 7.5 further points of capacity and multiplies by eight the number of customers that one bad prompt, one poisoned index or one mis-set flag reaches. After the knee, the capacity argument for sharing has stopped paying and only the blast radius keeps growing.

Disaster recovery is where the K1 plates of this group and the last one meet, and where the classical plan quietly stops working. The plan assumes that capacity is a property of infrastructure: provision a second region, keep it warm, fail over, accept a defined recovery time. October 2025 supplied the reminder that the assumption about regions still needs testing — a latent race condition in one region's internal DNS automation emptied the record for a database endpoint, and the resulting cascade took new instance launches and load-balancer health with it for most of a day 85 86. Every serious team's response was to look again at cross-region failover.

For an agent, the binding constraint on the secondary is not compute. It is a quota, held at K1, which was provisioned for the traffic the secondary was carrying rather than the traffic it is about to carry. Providers also ration the rate at which traffic may grow: the published guidance beyond a million input tokens per minute is to increase by no more than fifty per cent every fifteen minutes 49. Both halves of that are reasonable from the provider's side, and default quotas do get raised on the provider's own schedule 87. Neither is under your control on the day.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. A secondary provisioned at a tenth of demand, ramping at the documented rate, needs ninety minutes to carry the load and sheds the equivalent of 58.8 minutes of complete outage on the way — while the failover itself took seconds and every dashboard says the service is up. At a twentieth of demand it is 83.1 minutes. The recovery time objective in the runbook is measuring the switch; the number that customers experience is the ramp. The only fixes are to hold real reserved capacity on the secondary, to degrade deliberately to a cheaper model or a shorter step budget so that the same quota covers more tasks, or to write down honestly that the recovery objective is ninety minutes. Testing the failover without testing the quota tests the part that was never going to fail.

Two clock plates end the group. A model retirement date is a deadline that arrives whatever else is happening, and the failure mode is an inventory that missed a model string in a scheduled job nobody owns. Upstream component lifecycle is the same thing one layer down, and 2026 supplied both varieties at once: a piece of cluster infrastructure that about half of cloud-native environments depended on was retired with a year of warning and no drop-in replacement 35, and a provider scheduled three platform products — a hosted evaluation product, a visual agent builder and a reusable-prompt API — for shutdown on a single date 48. The plates that end this article are the plates you cannot move, and the only available practice is an inventory and a calendar.

Six plates sit at K5, and three of those six are the pipeline's own machinery — the test verdict, the rollback target, the provenance statement. Strip those and the artefact itself accounts for three determinants of production behaviour out of thirty-one. Twelve sit at K1 or K0, held on somebody else's calendar or on nobody's. The distribution is not a scandal; it is what it means to build on a platform. What is worth noticing is which practices are attached to which rungs.

The scheduled column contains exactly the twelve plates at K5 and K4 and nothing else. Every determinant at K3 or below is learned about after the fact or on somebody else's notice. That near-tautology is the ledger's central result, because the entire incident vocabulary — correlate against deploys, read the change log, roll back the most recent change — is defined over the scheduled set. It is not that teams are undisciplined about the other nineteen. It is that discipline, as the word is used in this field, is a relation between you and things you hold.

Nine of the thirty-one are movable by ordinary engineering. Six of them are cheap: the model identifier to K4 by writing a dated snapshot instead of an alias, the system prompt to K5 by putting it in the repository, the tool catalogue to K4 by pinning a revision, the evaluation gate to K4 by pinning the judge, self-hosted weights to K5 by shipping them as an image volume, and the isolation boundary by running your own sandbox. The remaining three — the weights behind the API, the batch your request joins, and the cache that holds its prefix — move only if you serve the model yourself, which is a capital decision rather than an engineering one. The first six cost an afternoon each and are, on this evidence, the highest-return work available anywhere in the matrix: each one converts a determinant you can only watch into one you can deploy, review, canary and roll back, and brings the whole existing apparatus with it at no further cost.

The seven-stage lifecycle in the brief — develop, test, evaluate, deploy, observe, improve, retire — can be read against the same ladder.

Only two stages write anything below K4, and both write it as a side effect rather than as an act of deployment: observing records what happened, improving edits the store. The three stages that carry a formal gate — test, deploy, retire — cannot act below their own depth. That is the structural reason a pipeline can be green while an agent drifts, and it is why "the deploy was clean" and "the behaviour changed" are not contradictory statements about an agent.

One limitation should be stated plainly. The coding is mine, the rule is stated and applied uniformly, and a reader who disagrees about a plate will mostly disagree about what counts as the ordinary documented deployment rather than about the rule. The replica-count plate is the most arguable; so is treating the execution ceiling as K1 when your own setting sits inside it. Moving either one changes the counts by one and changes nothing about the shape.

11What follows

The practical consequence is not a new methodology. It is a change in what the existing methodology is applied to.

If deployment is the operation that makes change safe, and if the unit it operates on covers six of thirty-one determinants, then the useful move is to enlarge the unit. That is what pinning a dated model snapshot does, what putting the prompt in the repository does, what pinning a tool-catalogue revision does, what shipping weights as an OCI artefact does. Each converts a determinant from something you observe into something you deploy, and each brings with it the whole apparatus — review, digest, provenance, canary, rollback — for free, because the apparatus was never the scarce thing. Custody was.

The second move is to make the artefact's attestation say what the artefact actually is. A provenance statement whose subject is an image and whose materials are source repositories is true and incomplete; the bundle that decides behaviour includes the prompt revision, the catalogue snapshot, the model identifier, the index generation and the decoding parameters. Nothing in the attestation formats prevents that 10 11; it is a matter of naming the right subject. A release that names its full context bundle is a release you can actually roll back, and one you can actually compare. The documentation practice that grew up around models — a card stating what was released, what it was evaluated on and where it should not be used 88 — is the same instinct one level up, and the bundle is the thing that now needs the card.

The third move is to stop expecting the artefact's operations to reach state. Rollback restores code; it does not unwrite. Everything at K3 needs its own inverse, designed deliberately: a generation stamp on the index so that records written by a withdrawn build can be excluded in one operation, a memory store that records which prompt revision wrote each entry, a workflow history that knows which version produced it. These are ordinary data-engineering measures 89, and they are the only things that make the rollback of an agent mean what the word means elsewhere. Compensation rather than reversal, and idempotent writes so that compensation can itself be retried, is the well-worn pattern 90 91.

Fourth, size the safety mechanisms to the signal they actually receive. A canary window set by the error-rate signal admits quality regressions of seventeen points; if quality is what you are protecting, the honest options are a longer bake, a much larger canary share, a paired comparison on identical inputs rather than a population comparison, or an explicit acceptance that the quality gate is offline and the canary is a smoke test. Any of those is defensible. Believing the canary covers quality is not.

Fifth, treat the determinants you cannot move as a schedule rather than as a risk register. Retirement dates, upstream support windows and platform ceilings are the only part of the K1 group that arrives with notice, and notice is only useful to an organisation that maintains an inventory of which model strings, which catalogue endpoints and which platform products it depends on, and reviews it against published lifecycle pages on a cadence. That is unglamorous work. It is also the entire remedy for the plates at the bottom of the ladder, and the alternative is discovering the dependency on the day it stops resolving 48 35.

Sixth, choose the isolation boundary and the sharing factor on purpose. The capacity argument for pooling tenants is real and bounded, and it stops paying around the point where the blast radius starts to matter. Sub-quotas with borrowing dominate both pooling and strict allocation in the model above, and the same shape — a floor per participant, a shared surplus — applies to rate limits, to concurrency slots and to human attention during an incident.

None of this is specific to agents in the way that the excitement around agents suggests. It is specific to systems whose behaviour is determined by things their operators do not hold, which describes a large and growing share of production software. Regulatory frameworks are converging on the same view from a different direction, asking for logging, human oversight and post-market monitoring of systems whose behaviour the deployer does not fully specify 92 93 94 95 96, and the industry's own measurements keep finding that the constraint is organisational capability rather than tooling 97. Reliability engineering has always insisted that you cannot manage what you have not defined 98 99, that the way to learn how a system fails is to break it deliberately 100, that tail behaviour is where distributed systems actually live 101, and that rapid change is survivable only when its blast radius is bounded 102 103. All of that still holds. The question this article has tried to answer is narrower and prior to it: before you can manage the change, you have to know whose change it was. The ledger says that for most of what decides what an agent does, it was not yours.

Which is worth saying without alarm. Depending on things you do not control is not a defect of this architecture; it is the trade that made the architecture possible, and the same trade underwrites every managed database and every cloud region. What is new is only that the dependency now reaches the behaviour rather than the availability. The response is the same one the discipline has always made: name the thing, find out who holds it, and move what you can into the artefact. Everything after that is a calendar.
