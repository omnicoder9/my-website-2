01The question

An agent run fails at four in the morning. A customer says the assistant booked the wrong room, or refunded twice, or spent nine minutes doing something a human would have done in twenty seconds. You open the trace.

What you find there was decided months ago. Whether there is a span at the point where the agent chose that tool was decided when someone wrapped, or did not wrap, that call site. Whether the span carries the prompt was decided when someone set a content-capture flag that is off by default 1. Whether this particular run survived at all was decided by a sampler at the root of the trace, before anything interesting had happened 2. Whether the dashboard's success rate means what you now need it to mean was decided when somebody wrote down what success was. None of these decisions was made with your question in mind, because nobody had your question yet.

This is not a failure of diligence. It is the structure of the problem. Telemetry is emitted at write time and read at read time, and the gap between them is where all the difficulty lives. The observability literature has a name for the good case — being able to ask questions you did not anticipate 3 — and the whole engineering discipline consists of arranging, in advance, for that to be possible later. Every field you add, every span you place, every retention window you set, every sampling rule you write is a bet about which questions will matter.

The vocabulary of AI agent observability is usually presented as a list: telemetry, tracing, monitoring, platforms; prompts, responses, tool calls, token usage, latency, errors; success rate, failure rate, hallucination rate. Presented that way it looks like a checklist, and it invites the reading that these are alternative things to instrument, more or less of which you might do depending on budget. That reading is wrong in a way that costs money at four in the morning. The items on that list are not interchangeable, and they do not differ mainly in cost. They differ in how much you had to know in advance for them to work.

That is the axis this article is organised around. Twenty-six items from the standard vocabulary are placed on a six-rung ladder according to the deepest commitment each one requires before the run it describes. The claim the ladder makes is falsifiable and slightly uncomfortable: the signals that answer the questions incidents actually raise sit systematically deeper than the signals that are on by default, and the deepest rung of all — the one where a measurement's meaning has to be fixed before the thing it measures happens — is where almost every monitor lives.

02Where the word comes from

Observability is a borrowed word, and the loan has terms.

Rudolf Kálmán introduced it in 1960 as a structural property of a dynamical system: a system is observable if its internal state can be determined, over a finite interval, from its outputs 4. The definition is exact, it is a property of the system rather than of the instrument, and it comes paired with a dual notion, controllability, in a way that makes the whole thing a statement about what the system's own structure permits 5. If a state is unobservable, no cleverness at the output recovers it. You add a sensor or you redesign the plant. Luenberger later gave the constructive side: an observer is a model you run alongside the system, driven by the same inputs, whose own state converges on the real one 6. Every control textbook since states the rank test that decides the question 7.

Software borrowed the word around 2016 and kept about half of it. What carried over was the aspiration — reconstruct the internals from the outputs — and the insistence that this is about the unanticipated case rather than the dashboard you already built 3 8. What did not carry over was the exactness. There is no rank test for a microservice. There is also no equivalent of Luenberger's observer, because software systems do not have a compact state model you can run in parallel; the nearest thing is a trace, which is a record rather than a model.

The record-keeping tradition is older than the word. Pinpoint, in 2002, tagged requests as they moved through a J2EE application and used the resulting paths to localise faults statistically 9. Magpie extracted per-request resource consumption by stitching events from several layers of one machine 10. X-Trace propagated trace metadata in band, across network layers and administrative boundaries, on the argument that a fault visible only at the seam between two layers is invisible to either one alone 11. Dapper, published by Google in 2010, is the design most current systems descend from: annotate requests with a trace identifier, propagate it, sample aggressively, keep the overhead low enough that it can be on everywhere 2. The sampling was not an afterthought. Dapper's authors treated it as the central engineering constraint, and the subsequent literature has largely been about undoing the damage it does: The Mystery Machine inferred causal ordering from logs rather than propagated context 12; Pivot Tracing let an operator install new queries into a running system rather than choosing the fields in advance 13; Canopy separated capture from the many different views built on it 14; Sifter and its relatives biased sampling toward unusual traces rather than uniformly 15 16; Hindsight kept trace data briefly at the edge and retrieved it only when something downstream flagged the request as interesting 17.

The design space itself has been mapped. A workflow-centric account of tracing sets out the choices a tracing system must make — what to sample, when to decide, what causality to record — and argues that most deployed systems make them implicitly 18. Related work showed how much can be recovered without propagated context at all, by inference over ordinary logs 19 20. And a study of production failures in distributed data systems found that most catastrophic outcomes came from error-handling code that was itself never exercised — a finding about where instrumentation is thinnest that transfers almost unchanged to agent frameworks 21.

Read as a sequence, that is a twenty-year argument with a single decision: the keep-or-drop rule that has to fire before you know whether the request matters. Every one of those papers is an attempt to move that decision later.

A parallel tradition worked inside the process rather than across the network. DTrace made it ordinary to instrument a production system safely and dynamically, without a rebuild, and established the expectation that instrumentation should be something you turn on during an incident rather than something you shipped six months ago 22; eBPF carried the same idea into Linux and made kernel-level observation programmable 23. The distinction matters for the argument here, because dynamic instrumentation is the one technique that genuinely lowers a rung: it converts "somebody had to place a probe" into "somebody can place a probe now". No equivalent exists for the inside of a model call.

The open-source backends arrived from the same lineage. Zipkin came out of Twitter as a Dapper implementation 24; Jaeger came out of Uber and became a CNCF project, and its principal author's book remains the fullest practitioner account of what a trace can and cannot support 25 26. What both made clear at scale is that collecting traces and analysing them are different problems: surveys of automated trace analysis find the field long on collection and short on inference 27, and the microservice benchmarks built to study the question showed how quickly a request graph outgrows what a person can read 28. An agent run is smaller than a thousand-service request graph and harder to read, because the interesting edges are semantic rather than structural.

Meanwhile the vocabulary consolidated. OpenTracing gave a portable API, OpenCensus gave an API with an implementation, and in 2019 the two merged into OpenTelemetry under the Cloud Native Computing Foundation 29. The W3C made propagation a standard rather than a vendor choice: traceparent and tracestate became a Recommendation in February 2020 30, with a second level adding guidance on identifier generation and a randomness flag, still a Candidate Recommendation Draft at the time of writing 31. OTLP settled the transport question 32. What remained unsettled — and remains unsettled now — is what the fields are called and what they mean.

The popular framing of the period, that observability consists of three pillars called metrics, logs and traces, has aged badly and was criticised early for describing storage formats rather than capabilities 8 3. The criticism is exactly the one this article is built on. A metric, a log line and a span are not three views of the same underlying thing. They are three different bets about what you would want to know, made at three different points in the pipeline, and they fail differently. A metric fails by having been keyed by the wrong dimensions. A log fails by having been retained for the wrong window. A span fails by not existing where you needed it. Calling them pillars implies they hold up the same roof.

The infrastructure beneath each has its own well-documented economics. Time series databases are built for bounded key spaces and compress ruthlessly on that assumption 33 34; event stores of the Scuba kind are built for the opposite case, where you want to slice by anything and accept the cost 35. Quantile sketches exist because you cannot keep every value and still answer percentile questions cheaply 36 37. None of this is specific to AI. All of it becomes acute the moment the most interesting field in your telemetry is a paragraph of English that has never been seen before and will never be seen again.

03Why an agent run is not a request

The classical tracing model assumes a request: something with a beginning, an end, a deterministic-enough code path, a bounded set of downstream calls, and a result that is right or wrong in a way a status code can express. An agent run violates every clause.

It is not deterministic. The same prompt does not produce the same output, and the reasons are structural rather than incidental — sampling temperature is the obvious one, but even greedy decoding is not reproducible across batch sizes, because floating-point reduction order depends on how requests happened to be batched together on the server 38. That single fact undermines the most natural debugging move available in ordinary software. You cannot re-run it and watch.

Its most important field is unbounded free text. The prompt is the input, and the prompt for step twelve contains the entire history of the run so far. Nothing in the metrics tradition can index that. A time series database wants attributes whose value sets are small and stable 39 33; a prompt is a value that occurs exactly once in the lifetime of the system.

Its control flow is a loop with a data-dependent trip count. A run may make three model calls or thirty, decided by the model itself as it goes. The span tree is therefore not a fixed shape you can reason about statically, and the number of steps is a variable that turns out to explain more about a run's cost and latency than anything about the model does.

Its errors are mostly not errors. A tool that returns a polite failure in prose with a 200 status, a retrieval that returns plausible but irrelevant documents, a model that answers confidently from nothing — none of these raise. Work on hallucination has made the mechanism reasonably clear, including the argument that standard evaluation practice actively rewards confident guessing over acknowledged uncertainty 40 41, and the empirical result that a model's stated reasoning is not a reliable report of the process that produced its answer 42 43. That last finding matters enormously here, because the obvious way to record why an agent did something is to ask it, and the obvious way is unsound.

Its failures are frequently social rather than local. The largest annotated study of multi-agent failures had to build its taxonomy by reading traces, because the failures were things like an agent misunderstanding its own specification or two agents disagreeing about the state of the world — categories no status field carries 44. Related work on agent benchmarking has made the parallel point that headline numbers routinely fail to separate capability from harness, budget and scaffolding 45.

Its latency behaviour is also unlike a single service call. A run is a sequence of dependent waits, and the classical result about variability at scale — that a service composed of many components will be dominated by the slowest of them — applies with a twist, since an agent's components run in sequence rather than in parallel 46. E7 below measures what that does to the percentiles.

And its horizon is long. A workflow may involve a queue, a retry an hour later, a human approval the next morning, and a second agent. The trace, if there is one, has to survive all of that, and the decision to keep it was taken at the beginning.

The pattern itself has a short and well-documented lineage. ReAct established the interleaving of reasoning and acting that nearly every agent loop still follows 47, and Toolformer established that tool invocation could be learned rather than scripted 48. What both bequeathed to observability is a control flow in which the model decides the next span.

The benchmark literature is worth reading as an observability document rather than a capability one, because each benchmark had to decide what it could see. SWE-bench can score a run because a test suite either passes or does not 49. GAIA can score a run because the answer is a short string with a known value 50. WebArena and OSWorld need execution-based checks against the state of an environment, because there is no answer string to compare 51 52. τ-bench needs a simulated user and a database state comparison, and reports pass^k rather than a single pass rate precisely because one run does not characterise an agent 53. Every one of those is an F5 commitment made by the benchmark's authors before any model saw the task, and the reason production monitoring is harder than benchmarking is that production has no author.

The context economics are also unlike anything in a request-shaped system. Tool catalogues are large: Anthropic's own measurements put a five-server setup at 58 tools and roughly 55,000 tokens of definitions before a single user message, with about 134,000 tokens of definitions observed internally before optimisation, and report that deferring tool definitions until they are needed cut that to about 8,700 tokens while lifting internal evaluation accuracy substantially 54. Those definitions are part of the input at every step, which is why the tool catalogue is a telemetry problem and not only a prompt-design one. The same body of work reports that the most common failures are wrong tool selection and wrong parameters — exactly the two things a span records only if somebody enabled argument capture. And long contexts do not behave uniformly: retrieval accuracy varies with position in the window 55, and effective context falls short of advertised context on every model that has been carefully measured 56. A trace that records only that 180,000 tokens were sent is not recording the thing that determined the outcome.

Finally, the highest-value telemetry an agent produces is partly written by people who are not your users. Tool outputs and retrieved documents are attacker-reachable, and indirect prompt injection turns them into instructions 57. The defensive literature is active — AgentDojo gave the field a benchmark for measuring it 58, and design-level defences that keep untrusted content out of the control path have shown large reductions in attack success at a real cost in capability 59 — and the industry risk taxonomies now treat excessive agency and hidden context as first-order categories 60 61. For observability this cuts two ways. Recording tool outputs is how you find out you were attacked. Recording tool outputs is also how the attack payload gets into your logging system, your dashboards, and eventually your incident review.

The conventions have caught up with the shape, if not yet with the semantics. The GenAI semantic conventions now model an agent run as a span tree rather than as a sequence of independent model calls: an invoke_agent span at the root, chat spans for each model call, execute_tool spans for each tool invocation, with create_agent, invoke_workflow, plan and retrieval operations also defined 1. Version 1.41.0 of the core conventions added streaming metrics — time to first chunk and time per output chunk — precisely because one duration was concealing two different waits, and added invoke_workflow as an operation in its own right 62 1. Model Context Protocol conventions live in the same vocabulary, so a tool call issued over MCP is describable in the same terms as the agent that issued it 63 1.

The project has published its own explainer on how a model call should be described, which is the best short statement of the intended data model 64.

Two things about the current state are worth stating plainly, because a great deal of secondary writing gets them wrong. First, none of the gen_ai attributes are stable. In June 2026 the core semantic-conventions repository deprecated every gen_ai, OpenAI-specific and MCP definition it held and moved them to a dedicated repository, where they continue to be marked Development 65 1; the core conventions themselves are at 1.44.0 66. Second, message content is opt-in. A conforming, fully instrumented agent emits a beautiful tree in which the prompts and the tool arguments are absent by default, for good reasons involving personal data 1 67. The tree is cheap. The two fields that say what actually happened are the two that somebody has to turn on.

04The ladder

Six rungs, in the order a telemetry pipeline commits to them.

F0 is the record that exists as a by-product of running the system at all. F1 is a retention decision: the bytes were produced anyway, and the only question was how long to keep them. F2 is a probe: somebody had to decide that this point in the code emits anything. F3 is a field list: the probe had to be told which attributes to carry, and anything not named is simply absent. F4 is a keep rule: which runs survive had to be settled before it was known which ones would matter. F5 is a definition: the meaning of the measurement — the rubric, the judge, the threshold, the bucket boundaries, what counts as success — had to be fixed before the run it measures, and applying a new definition later does not produce the same number.

The rungs nest. A field list is useless without a probe; a keep rule operates on things a probe emitted; a definition applied at emission presupposes everything below it. So a signal's rung is the deepest commitment it requires, and it inherits the rest.

Two coding decisions keep this from being arbitrary. The rung is assigned by what the signal needs in order to answer the question its own name implies, not by the best answer obtainable with unlimited effort. Token usage sits at F2 because a span carrying the provider's usage block answers "what did this step consume"; the fact that the invoice exists whatever you do is an F0 observation about a different question. And where a family splits — tool calls are cheap, tool call arguments are not — the plate is coded at the level the name implies and the split is recorded in the note.

The ordering of F3 and F4 is the one place the ladder takes a position that could be argued the other way. A missing dimension can often be recovered by re-parsing a payload that was kept; a run that was never kept cannot be recovered by anything. That asymmetry is why selection sits deeper than fields. F5 sits deepest because even a complete raw record does not reproduce a judgement whose definition has since changed: you can re-score, but you cannot compare the new score to the old series.

Running orthogonally to the ladder is a second question, which the gauge records in its corner: if the commitment was missed, what recovers the answer?

Three classes. Lost means nothing does: an unpropagated trace context, an untimed duration, a prompt that was never captured. Derived means the answer survives in some other record — the provider's invoice, the tool's own database rows, the framework's checkpoint store — and can be reconstructed by joining to it, which usually means asking a system you do not own for data keyed by identifiers that are not yours. Replay means the step can be re-run from what was kept, which is sound only if the input was fully recorded and the world has not moved; given the batch-dependence result above, it is weaker than it sounds 38.

One feature of the completed matrix is worth flagging before the plates, because it is the sort of result that looks like an error. The F0 rung is empty. Not one of the twenty-six items in the standard vocabulary is free. That does not mean nothing lives at F0 — three things do, and they are systematically underused. The provider's billing record exists whether or not you instrument anything. The tools the agent called have their own systems of record, with their own logs and their own row-level history. And the user received the output, which is the single most reliable copy of what the system actually said. In an incident, these three are often available when the telemetry is not, and they are usually consulted last.

05The record of a step

The first nine plates cover what can be recorded about a single step: what went in, what came out, what it cost, and what broke.

The pattern across them is a split. The metadata — that a tool was called, how long it took, how many tokens it used, whether it raised — sits at F2, needing only a probe. The content — what the prompt said, what the tool returned — sits at F3, needing a probe that was told to carry it. And exactly one item, the one practitioners most want, sits at F5.

The T06 result deserves its own paragraph because it is the plate most likely to be disputed. "Agent decisions" is on every vocabulary list, and platforms advertise decision tracing. What they record is the chosen action, and often the model's own account of why it chose it. Neither is a record of the decision. The chosen action is one branch of a choice; the alternatives available at that moment can be recorded — the conventions define a tool-definitions attribute for exactly this, opt-in — but the ranking over them is not something the run computes in any form a probe can read out. And the model's stated reason is a further generation, produced after the fact, which the faithfulness literature shows can be systematically unrelated to the process that actually produced the answer 42 43. To have a record of a decision you must decide beforehand what counts as one and emit a judgement about it at the time. That is an F5 commitment wearing an F2 name.

The GenAI conventions stop at the tool boundary; ordinary service instrumentation resumes on the other side.

Three of the four sit at F2, because propagation and span placement are instrumentation. The fourth, workflow tracing, sits at F4, and the reason is the twenty-year argument described earlier. A workflow whose interesting part happens six hours after its root span began has had its keep-or-drop decision made by a head sampler that knew nothing 2 68. Tail sampling in the collector moves the decision to the end of the trace, at the cost of buffering 69; the research line from weighted sampling through Sifter to Hindsight moves it further still 15 16 17. For agent workloads the case for moving it is stronger than for HTTP traffic, because the volumes are lower, the per-run value is higher, and the interesting runs are a small and identifiable minority.

R01 is the only plate in the whole vocabulary that is destroyed by a component doing nothing at all. A service that drops the traceparent header emits no error, degrades no metric, and breaks every trace that passes through it 30. The experiment in E3 below quantifies what that looks like when read at the level of runs rather than spans.

07The standing questions

Monitors are different in kind from the signals beneath them. A signal records what happened. A monitor asserts that a particular quantity, computed a particular way, is the thing to watch.

Five of the seven sit at F5. This is the ledger's strongest result and it is worth being careful about why, because "success rate is at the top of the ladder" sounds like a complaint about rigour and is not.

Two distinct mechanisms put a monitor at F5. The first is definitional. Success, failure and hallucination are not properties a run emits; they are verdicts someone applies. Applying a verdict requires a rubric, and for anything beyond a schema check that rubric is either a human or a model acting as judge 70 71. A judge has its own error rate, its own position and verbosity biases 72, its own version, and its own drift. Change the judge and the trend line moves without the system moving. That is why the definition has to precede the measurement: not for tidiness, but because a comparison across time is only meaningful if the instrument was the same at both ends.

The second mechanism is aggregation. A latency monitor or a token counter is a pre-aggregated series, and pre-aggregation is a definition applied at emission: bucket boundaries, dimension list, aggregation temporality, all fixed in advance 73. This is not a flaw. It is what makes metrics cheap enough to keep forever, and the exponential histogram exists precisely to give a bounded relative error at a bounded cost 73 36. But a percentile on a dashboard is a reconstruction, accurate to a bound somebody chose, and it can only be sliced by dimensions somebody named. E8 below measures both halves of that: how much resolution costs, and what no resolution returns.

The other tradition a monitor inherits from is machine-learning operations, which has been saying for a decade that the model is the small part of the system and the surrounding machinery is where the debt accumulates 74 75. Production-readiness rubrics for ML systems put monitoring requirements on the same footing as test requirements 76; surveys of deployment case studies find monitoring and quality assurance among the most consistently underestimated stages 77; and interview studies of practitioners describe an experiment-then-observe loop in which the hardest part is knowing whether a change helped 78. The distinctive addition that agents make is that the criterion itself is now usually a model, and work on aligning LLM-based evaluators with human judgement finds that the criteria have to be developed iteratively against real outputs rather than specified up front 79 — which is an uncomfortable finding for a rung that requires the definition to be fixed in advance, and one worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over.

It is worth being precise about what a monitor inherits from the operational tradition it grew out of. The four golden signals — latency, traffic, errors, saturation — were proposed as a minimal set for a request-serving system 80, with service-level objectives as the mechanism that turns them into decisions 81, and the resource-oriented methods that predate them were built for the same shape 82. Three of the four transfer to agents without difficulty. Errors does not, for the reason given in T09: an agent's characteristic failure is a well-formed answer that is wrong. The gap between "the four golden signals are all green" and "the system is doing the wrong thing" is not a monitoring gap that better dashboards close. It is the reason this family sits at F5.

Detection is a second, separable question, and it is one where the statistics are unforgiving. Deciding that a success rate has moved is a hypothesis test on a proportion, and the sample sizes involved are larger than intuition suggests: a sequential probability ratio test is the right instrument for noticing a shift with the fewest observations 83, and cumulative-sum schemes remain the standard way to make a small persistent change visible against noise 84. What both require is, again, foreknowledge — the baseline rate and the size of the shift worth alerting on have to be stated in advance. Reporting the interval rather than the point estimate is the minimum discipline here; exact binomial intervals are conservative but honest 85, and the Wilson interval is the usual practical compromise 86 87.

For hallucination specifically, there is a family of cheaper monitors that avoid some of the judge's problems by sampling the model against itself and checking for consistency rather than for truth 88. They are still an F5 commitment — consistency thresholds are a definition — but they are a definition you can hold fixed more easily than a judging model you will want to upgrade.

Cost and tool errors sit lower, at F3, and the reason is instructive. Both are arithmetic over attributes rather than judgements. Cost needs the served model and the cache split on the span, plus a price table by date, and then it is division 1. Tool errors need a tool name and an error type on the same span. Neither requires anyone to have decided in advance what counts as bad — only what counts as a field.

The commercial landscape moved decisively during 2026 in a direction that supports the reading. ClickHouse acquired Langfuse in January, on the stated grounds that LLM observability is fundamentally a data problem — that is, a retention and query problem, F1 90. One under-used piece of the standard belongs here. It is an F3 decision — you have to name the keys in advance — but it is the cheapest way to make later slicing possible.

The convergence is real and it is on the vocabulary, not on the product. Which means the interesting differences between these platforms are increasingly about where each one puts the expensive decision: on your retention budget, on your framework choice, on your evaluator definitions, or on your ingestion rules.

There is one more thing the platform layer has to carry, and it is not a feature. Prompts and tool payloads are the highest-value telemetry in the system and they are also, routinely, personal data. Data minimisation and storage limitation are not optional 67 105; the EU AI Act adds record-keeping duties for high-risk systems and a minimum log-retention period for deployers 106 107; management-system and risk-framework standards both expect the retention policy to be a decision rather than an accident 108 109. The practical answer the ecosystem has converged on is redaction in the collector, before the payload reaches the backend 110 111 — which is itself an F3 decision, made in advance, about which fields survive.

09The lab

Eight experiments, standard library only, seed 20260911, each one deterministic and re-runnable. They exist to put numbers on claims that are otherwise made by assertion. Two were rebuilt during the build and the rebuilds are disclosed at the end of the section.

E1 · What a sampling rate does to a rare failure mode

Take 200,000 runs a day with a 0.30% failure rate and the common default of 1% head sampling. The simulated day retained 1,957 traces, 8 of which had failed. Estimating the failure rate from that sample gives 0.41% with a Wilson 95% interval of 0.21% to 0.80% 86 87 — the ends differ by a factor of 3.9, which is not a measurement.

The more useful number is not about rates at all. A failure mode that occurs twenty times a day leaves no trace whatsoever, on 81.79% of days, at 1% sampling. Not a degraded trace: nothing. Whereas keeping every error plus 1% of everything else costs 1.33% of the volume and retains 100% of the failures. To retain a hundred failures a day by head sampling alone you would need to keep 15.15% of all traffic.

The point is not that head sampling is wrong. It is that a head sampler is a prediction, made at the root, about which runs will be interesting — and for agent workloads, where volumes are low and per-run value is high, it is a prediction you rarely need to make 69 17.

E2 · What a metric can be keyed by

Seven bounded attributes — service, environment, provider, model, operation, tool name, error type, at 3, 3, 4, 6, 9, 40 and 4 values — give a ceiling of 311,040 possible time series. Over 200,000 simulated runs with Zipf-distributed tool and model popularity 112, 48,383 distinct combinations actually occurred, 15.56% of the ceiling. That is the shape a time series database is built for: bounded above, saturating, compressible 33 34.

Now key by something per-run. Sessions produced 28,572 series and prompts produced 200,000 — one per run, by construction, because a prompt containing the whole conversation so far is unique. A per-run key overtakes the entire bounded cross-product after 37 hours of traffic and never stops growing. Adding user identity at 50,000 users multiplies the ceiling to 15.55 billion.

This is the cleanest statement of why prompts cannot be a metric dimension and why event stores rather than metric stores are where agent telemetry ends up 35 39.

E3 · One broken hop, one broken run

Model a run as a root span with a chain of twelve hops below it — handoffs between agents, an async queue, a background worker — where the link above each hop is dropped with probability p. At p = 2%, 78.54% of runs assemble completely, against an analytic 78.47%, so 21.46% of runs are damaged. Meanwhile only 11.13% of individual spans are orphaned. The same defect reads 1.93 times larger at the level anyone cares about than at the level a span-based health check would see.

At a 2% per-hop loss rate, half of all runs are damaged by depth 34 — well inside the range a multi-agent system reaches.

Trace context is the one signal with no partial-credit failure mode. It works or the run is in pieces, and the component responsible reports nothing at all 30 11.

E4 · What the token counter counts

An agent that re-sends its transcript at every step pays for it every time. Simulating a 24-step run with a 2,400-token system prompt and tool definitions resent at each call, the run bills 302,931 prompt tokens summed over spans, while the entire conversation it ever produced is 23,667 tokens. The counter is correct and the natural reading of it is wrong by a factor of 12.80.

The step-24 call sends 9.48 times as many prompt tokens as step 1. The last six steps of twenty-four carry 41.51% of all prompt tokens. Doubling the horizon from 24 to 48 steps multiplies prompt tokens by 3.69, because the total grows with the square of the step count. Generated output, over the whole run, is 1.40% of the prompt tokens.

Three consequences follow for monitoring. A mean of tokens per run is the wrong summary of a quadratic long-tailed quantity. A sum of input tokens across spans is not the size of the conversation, and labelling a dashboard as though it were invites exactly that misreading. And cache-read tokens, which the conventions added as separate attributes for good reason, are the difference between the counter being an accounting artefact and being a cost 62 1.

E5 · What a retention policy can answer

Six things about a step are recordable: the system prompt with tool definitions, the message history, the model identifier with decoding parameters, the tool call arguments, the tool results, and the retrieved documents. That is 64 possible retention policies. Score each against five questions an incident actually raises — which tool ran with what arguments; why that tool and not another; whether the answer was supported by the evidence; whether the same input would do the same thing today; and whether the tool returned the wrong thing.

Of 64 policies, 25 answer none of the five. The median answers one. Exactly one policy answers all five, and it is the one that keeps every field. The distribution across questions is lopsided in a way that matters: the cheap question, which tool ran with what arguments, is answerable by 32 of 64 policies, while each of the three questions an incident actually turns on is answerable by only 8.

Searching all 720 orderings of the six fields for the one that buys questions fastest gives arguments, then results, then the system prompt, then the message history, then model parameters, then retrieved documents, with an area under the unlock curve of 17 against 7 for the worst ordering. Even the best ordering answers only two of five with half the fields kept. There is no cheap subset. The questions people ask in incidents are conjunctive, and conjunctions are expensive.

E6 · What the bytes cost

Using E4's run and four characters per token, three capture policies: every span's full input and output, at 1,199.8 KiB per run; the conversation stored once plus span metadata, at 94.4 KiB; and span metadata alone — names, identifiers, durations, counts — at 2.0 KiB. At 200,000 runs a day those are 245.73, 19.34 and 0.41 GB per day, which in a 10 TB budget buy 41, 517 and 24,655 days of retention.

The middle policy is the interesting one. Capturing each span's full input stores the transcript 12.98 times over, because the transcript is precisely what each call sends. Storing the conversation once and referring to it from the spans costs a thirteenth of the bytes and loses nothing an incident needs. The cheapest policy is 606 times cheaper than the most expensive and, by E5, answers none of the five questions.

E7 · A percentile is a property of the thing you measured

Give every model and tool call a lognormal latency whose p95 is exactly 1.000 seconds, and let runs make a mean of 10.5 such calls in sequence. Run latency comes out at p50 3.54 s, p95 6.60 s, p99 8.35 s.

Adding up the per-call p95s predicts 10.50 s. The actual run p95 is 6.60 s, 62.8% of the prediction, because independent waits average out rather than compounding. The run's own tail is tighter than the calls': run p99/p95 is 1.27, against 1.78 for a single call.

Two operational consequences. The slowest single call carries a mean of 28.69% of run latency, so the longest bar in the waterfall explains under a third of the wait — and in the slowest 1% of runs it still carries only 35.25%. And step count explains 44.83% of the variance in run latency, with per-call variation accounting for the rest; the slowest 1% of runs average 14.33 steps against 10.50 overall. The lever is the loop, not the call.

E8 · What a histogram gives back

Take the same run-latency distribution and recover its p99 from an OpenTelemetry exponential histogram at five resolutions 73. At scale 0, base 2, the estimate is 6.00 s against a true 7.70 s, an error of 22.06% inside a worst-case bound of 33.33%. At scale 3, base 1.0905, the error is 0.40% inside a bound of 4.33%. At scale 8 it is 0.085%. The realised error is not monotone in scale — scale 5 came out slightly worse than scale 3 — while the bound is; a useful reminder that the bound is the promise and the realised error is luck.

Resolution, though, is the recoverable part of the loss. What no scale returns is which run produced any given value, the p99 of a subset the series was not keyed by, or the correlation between latency and any attribute nobody named. One day of raw values is 1.6 MB against 2.6 kB for the histogram, a factor of 625. That factor is what you are paying for the answers you can no longer get.

Rebuilds and withdrawals

Two experiments were rebuilt mid-build and the earlier versions are not reported. E3's first version counted orphaned spans by summing the subtree below every break, which double-counts spans lying below more than one break; the correct count is the spans below the first break, and fixing it moved the headline gap from 1.87 to 1.93. E7 was rebuilt entirely. Its first version compared run latency against an invented ten-second budget, found that 0.22% of runs breached it, and would have supported a much more dramatic story about tail amplification than the data justify — sequential independent calls concentrate rather than amplify. The rebuilt version reports the concentration, which is the honest and more useful finding.

One further caveat applies to E1, E3, E7 and E8: their inputs are stylised distributions chosen to be plausible, not measurements of any real system. What they establish is the shape of the relationship — how the run-level reading diverges from the span-level one — and not the specific percentage any particular deployment will see.

10What the ledger says

Twenty-six plates, a mean rung of 3.19, and six of the twenty-six unrecoverable if their commitment was missed.

Nothing is free. The F0 rung is empty. Every item in the standard vocabulary of agent observability requires at least a probe placed before the run. The records that genuinely cost nothing — the provider's invoice, the tools' own databases, the output the user received — are not on anyone's list and are consulted last in practice.

The valuable fields are the deep ones. Prompts and responses sit at F3, one rung deeper than the metadata around them, because content capture is a separate opt-in decision 1. Agent decisions sit at F5. The three items that most directly answer "why did it do that" are the three that a default installation does not give you.

Monitors are a different layer from signals. Five of the seven monitors sit at F5 against a modal rung of F2 for the step-level signals. That gap is not about difficulty of implementation; it is about when the meaning has to be fixed. A signal can be added on the day. A monitor whose definition changes is a monitor whose history is worthless, which is why "we'll define success later" is not a deferral but a decision to have no trend line.

Loss concentrates in the assembly layer. Of the six unrecoverable plates, four are about structure rather than content: trace context, workflow correlation, duration, and the decision itself. Content, mostly, is derivable from somewhere — the tool's own records, the framework's checkpoint, the invoice. Structure is not, because it exists only in the act of recording.

Recovery usually means asking someone else. Nineteen of the twenty-six are classed derived rather than lost, which sounds reassuring until you notice what derivation involves: joining to a system you do not own, keyed by identifiers that are not yours, within its retention window rather than yours. "Derived" is a statement about physics, not about your Tuesday.

Three honest weaknesses in the ledger. First, the rung assignments describe conventional practice and default configurations, not what is achievable — a team that emits its own candidate-action records could move T06 down two rungs, at the cost of building something nobody ships. Second, the F3/F4 ordering is a judgement about recoverability that a reader who cares more about cost than about recoverability would reverse. Third, twenty-six items is the vocabulary as it is usually taught, and a vocabulary is not a system; a real deployment has signals that appear on no list, and those are often the ones that resolve the incident.

11Who reads it, and when

Everything above is about what a system can record. The reason it matters is that somebody has to read it, usually in a hurry, usually with an incomplete idea of what went wrong.

The resilience engineering literature is unusually clear-eyed about this. Complex systems, on Richard Cook's account, run in a degraded mode as a normal condition, fail in ways that require several contributing causes at once, and are defended by practitioners whose ongoing improvisation is what keeps them working 113. Post-incident accounts systematically overestimate how obvious the cause was, because the investigator knows the outcome and the operator did not 114. The people doing the work under time pressure are adapting to a situation nobody designed for, and what limits them is not intelligence but the set of hypotheses they can cheaply test 115. The SNAFUcatchers study of real software incidents found that most of the effort in a hard incident goes not into fixing anything but into building a shared, good-enough model of what the system is currently doing 116.

Read that way, a telemetry pipeline is a hypothesis-testing budget, allocated in advance. Every rung on the ladder is a class of hypothesis that either can or cannot be tested at four in the morning:

Whether the agent saw the document — testable if content capture was on, not otherwise. Whether the tool returned something different from what the model acted on — testable if both the arguments and the results were kept. Whether this run is representative or an outlier — testable only if the population it came from was retained, which the sampler decided. Whether this is new — testable only if the comparison quantity was defined the same way last month. Whether it was the model or the harness — testable only if the harness emitted anything at all, which is why the agent-benchmarking critique about not separating capability from scaffolding is an observability finding as much as a methodological one 45.

Two practical consequences follow, and they cut against instincts that feel responsible.

The first is that adding a signal after an incident is often the wrong lesson. The classic post-incident action item — "add logging around X" — pays off only if the next incident is in the same place, and the whole thrust of Cook's argument is that it will not be 113 117. The generalisable version of that action item is almost always a rung, not a field: move the sampling decision later; capture the conversation once rather than per span; give the run a stable correlation key. Those changes make whole classes of hypothesis testable rather than one.

The second is that the analysis has to survive the discovery that the system did something reasonable. Systems-theoretic accident models make the point that accidents in software-intensive systems typically arise from inadequate control and unsafe interactions between components that each behaved as specified 118, and multi-agent failure taxonomies find exactly this pattern: specification problems and inter-agent misalignment dominate, with verification failures a distant third 44. Telemetry organised only around component health will keep reporting that every component was healthy. Telemetry organised around a run — its goal, its steps, its constraints and what it did to the world — can at least pose the question.

There is a version of blamelessness that is really an evidence policy. If the record is thin enough, every incident review resolves to a story about judgement, because a story about judgement is the only kind the evidence can support 117 114. Deciding in advance to keep the transcript is, among other things, a decision about what the organisation will be able to argue about afterwards.

12Designing the bet

If the ladder is right, then the practical question is not "what should we instrument" but "which commitments do we want to have already made, and which do we want to keep open".

Some things are worth deciding early because deciding them late is impossible. Trace context propagation across every boundary you control is the clearest case: it is cheap, it is standardised, and its absence is unrecoverable and silent 30 32. A run boundary — an invoke_agent span, or whatever your framework calls it — is the second, because without a root there is no denominator and no per-run rate can be computed at all 1. A stable correlation identifier that survives queues and human approvals is the third.

Some things are worth deciding early because the alternative is a number that cannot be compared with itself. What counts as success. Which evaluator, at which version, with which rubric. What the failure taxonomy is, knowing it will be wrong and will need versioning 44. These are the F5 commitments, and the discipline they require is version control over definitions, not just over code.

And some things are worth keeping open, deliberately. The dominant argument for tail-based sampling in agent systems is not cost; it is that a decision made at the end of a run is made with information a decision made at the beginning does not have 69 17. The dominant argument for storing the conversation once and referring to it, rather than capturing each span's full input, is not only the factor of thirteen in E6 — it is that a stored transcript can answer questions you have not thought of, and a pre-aggregated counter cannot. The dominant argument for keeping raw payloads within a short window, even if the metrics are kept for years, is that E5's questions are conjunctive and the conjunction is only available while the payloads are.

Against all of that stands a real constraint that is not merely bureaucratic. The most useful telemetry an agent produces is text a person typed, and the obligations that attach to it are the ordinary ones: collect what you need, keep it as long as you need it and no longer 67 105. High-risk systems under the AI Act carry record-keeping duties and a minimum log-retention period for deployers 106 107, and management-system standards expect these choices to be documented decisions 108 109. The resolution the ecosystem has converged on — redact in the collector, before the backend, with the rules under review — is itself a bet made in advance about which fields matter 110 111. There is no configuration that avoids the bet. There is only the choice of which bet to make and how legible it is to the person who will have to live with it.

Which returns to the four in the morning. The question you will be asked is not "was the system observable"; it is "what happened to this run". The honest answer is determined by decisions taken long before, by people who did not know the question. The best any of us can do is make those decisions knowing that is what they are — and to notice, when choosing between a cheap signal and an expensive one, that what makes the expensive one expensive is usually not the bytes. It is that you had to know, in advance, that you would want it.
