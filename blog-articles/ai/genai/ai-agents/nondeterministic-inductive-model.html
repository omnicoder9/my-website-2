Past performance. A record of an agent's behaviour is of limited value as a guide to its future behaviour; the regulation from which this phrasing is borrowed requires funds to say the same of themselves.58

Set in Libre Franklin, Libertinus Serif and Share Tech Mono.

A program used to earn trust through its text: someone wrote a specification, someone read the code against it, and in the best cases a machine checked a proof that the two agreed. A growing class of programs cannot earn trust that way, because what makes them competent is not in any text a person can read. An agent that reads every paper, filing and price series before it trades knows things its overseers do not, and it will know more tomorrow than it did when it was tested. All that can be observed is what it does. This article sets out what that leaves us: a model of program performance that is nondeterministic, because the same agent need not do the same thing twice, and inductive, because the only warrant for running it again is that it has done well before. It asks the question every such deployment eventually has to answer: what does the record entitle you to?

§1The asymmetry nobody voted for

In September 2026 arXiv received 40,363 submissions, its highest monthly total, and on 1 October it limited every submitter to two new papers per calendar month so that its volunteer moderators could keep up1. The next month brought 42,152 submissions anyway, 9,465 of them in mathematics2. A structural analysis of the full submission record since 1991 finds long-run growth of about 9% a year, a first break in 2022 driven by AI research itself, and a second break between December 2025 and February 2026 that appears outside the AI categories: mathematics submissions doubled within a year and solo-authored papers there rose by 70%3. Between March and 20 August 2026, the share of mathematics submissions disclosing a substantive mathematical contribution from AI rose from 1.39% to 14.09%4.

These numbers matter here because of what they do to reading. A person reading non-fiction silently manages about 238 words a minute5. Grant a reader who does nothing else, eight hours a day for 250 days a year, and assume a paper runs to 7,000 words. That reader finishes 4,080 papers a year (E1). Annualised, arXiv alone now receives about 484,356; the dedicated reader can get through 0.84% of it, or 3.59% of a single year of mathematics. Reading one month of arXiv mathematics would take 2.32 working years. Against the roughly three million journal articles published a year6, the share is 0.136%.

Reading is not checking. The First Proof project was set up by research mathematicians to test AI systems on questions drawn from their own unpublished work7. In its second round, ten research-level problems produced thirty-nine machine-generated solutions, and grading them took thirty confirmed referees, three per problem, and an in-person refereeing meeting8. The referees reported a failure mode that makes checking harder rather than easier: routine steps worked through at length, hard steps passed over with appeals to standard arguments. Checking scales with the number of experts, and the number of experts does not grow at nine per cent a year.

On the other side of the ledger, a model's pre-training corpus can run to about fifteen trillion tokens9. At the reading rate above, a forty-five-year career of nothing but reading comes to about 1.71×109 tokens, so one such corpus holds roughly 8,775 reading careers. Estimates of the total stock of public human-written text put it in the hundreds of trillions of tokens10. The numbers are coarse and the conclusion does not depend on their precision. An agent can have read more than any person, and more than any committee of people can audit, before it takes its first action.

That is the first premise, and it is not a claim about intelligence. It is a claim about throughput. The proposition that someone should check AI output carries an unstated quantity: checking at what rate, against what volume. The research programme called scalable oversight exists because that quantity is the problem. It asks how a weaker overseer can supervise a stronger system, through debate between models, amplification of the overseer, or by studying when weak supervision still elicits strong behaviour11–15. Lisanne Bainbridge named the older form of the same trap in 1983: automate the routine and the human is left monitoring, the one task people do badly, for failures they no longer have the practice to diagnose16. Two decades of experiments on automation bias say the same about attention17.

If every overseer cooperated perfectly, the gap would close more slowly but would not close. At nine per cent annual growth the volume doubles every 8.0 years; the reader's share of the year's output falls from 0.84% in 2026 to 0.15% in 2046, holding the reader constant. Some class of programs will therefore run on knowledge no human has verified, and be judged by behaviour no human fully explains. The remainder of this article is about how such programs can be trusted, how far, and where they should not be used.

§2Two warrants: the text and the history

A warrant, in the sense used here, is whatever entitles you to expect a program to behave well the next time it runs. Classical software engineering has one dominant answer. Tony Hoare's axiomatic method made a program's correctness a relation between two texts, the program and its specification, so that a proof about the texts is a proof about every future execution18. The verified seL4 microkernel and the CompCert compiler are the mature form of that idea: machine-checked proofs that an implementation refines its specification19,20. The warrant is a property of the text. It does not care how many times the program has run.

Most software never had that warrant. Its trust rests on review and testing, and testing is induction. Edsger Dijkstra put the limit in a sentence: testing can show the presence of bugs but never their absence21. Rice's theorem gives the formal reason: every non-trivial semantic property of programs is undecidable, so no general procedure can certify behaviour from text alone, a consequence of Turing's halting result22,23. For ultra-high reliability the inductive route closes too. Butler and Finelli showed in 1993 that demonstrating a failure rate of one in a billion hours by testing is infeasible, and Littlewood and Strigini argued the general case: evidence from operation cannot reach the levels claimed for life-critical software24,25. Avionics responded by making review and process the warrant, not the record26.

The class of program this article is about differs from ordinary tested software in three ways, and each one weakens the text warrant further.

Where the competence lives

In a learned system the competence is in the parameters, not in code anyone wrote. Andrej Karpathy called this software 2.0: the programmer specifies a goal and a dataset, and optimisation writes the program27. Sculley and colleagues documented the engineering consequence, that the behaviour of such a system depends on data dependencies and feedback loops no code review can see28. When the agent also retrieves and ingests new material while it runs, the relevant text is not even fixed at deployment. The thing you would need to read changes every day.

Why the same input need not give the same output

The second difference is nondeterminism. Sampling at a non-zero temperature makes outputs vary by design. Even at temperature zero, a production language-model endpoint can return different completions for the same prompt, because the batch a request is grouped with depends on server load and common kernels are not batch-invariant; Thinking Machines Lab traced most observed nondeterminism to that cause and showed that batch-invariant kernels remove it29. Beyond the model, the environment is nondeterministic: markets, users and adversaries respond to what the agent did. A test of one run is therefore a sample from a distribution of runs, and the object to be evaluated is the distribution.

What the agent knows that the checker does not

The third difference is the one this article turns on. A conventional program can be wrong in ways a reviewer would recognise if shown the line. An agent that has read ten thousand papers can be right for reasons the reviewer cannot reconstruct, and wrong for reasons equally invisible. Its overseer is in the position of a fund's investors: they cannot inspect the manager's head, so they read the returns.

David Hume observed that no number of past instances logically entails the next one; we expect the sun to rise because it has, not because anything proves it will30. Nelson Goodman sharpened the problem: the same record supports incompatible projections, and which regularity you project is a choice, not a deduction31. Karl Popper's response was to treat every general claim as a conjecture that survives only until a test refutes it32. All three describe the epistemic position of anyone who deploys an agent whose competence outruns their checking. Nothing about the past runs entails the next one. What can be asked is narrower: under what conditions does the past make the next run likely to go well, by how much, and what happens when it does not.

The term used in the title for this position is a nondeterministic inductive model of program performance. It has three parts. The program's behaviour is a random variable, not a function. Its warrant is a record of behaviour, not a reading of text. And the record is drawn from a world that may not hold still. The sections that follow take each part in turn, and then ask what can be guaranteed anyway.

§3The warrant ladder

To compare programs that rest on different warrants, this article places each on a six-rung ladder. A rung is defined by what the warrant is a property of. The rungs are ordered by how much of the future the warrant constrains without further observation.

  • K5 Proof. A machine-checked proof that the artefact meets a specification. The warrant is a property of the text, and it covers every future run the specification covers.
  • K4 Certificate. The generator is untrusted, but every output carries a certificate that a small checker validates: a clausal proof for a SAT verdict, a kernel-checked proof term, a score from an evaluator that humans wrote33,34. The warrant is a property of each output.
  • K3 Reading. A qualified person has read and understood the artefact or each output: code review, refereeing, structural-coverage review. The warrant is a property of a reader, and it lasts only as long as readers keep pace.
  • K2 Sample. A statistical guarantee from observations exchangeable with future use: a held-out test set, a clinical validation study, a conformal coverage bound35,36. The warrant is a property of a distribution.
  • K1 Record. A history of behaviour in a world that does not hold still. No bound from the past transfers to tomorrow without an assumption about how fast the world moves. The warrant is a property of a history.
  • K0 None. A first deployment with no record. The warrant is a claim.

Two further codings sit beside the rung. The envelope records whether a component warranted at a higher rung bounds what the core can do. E0 means none. E1 is a soft envelope: monitoring, human override and rollback, which bound the damage only if someone reacts faster than the damage accrues. E2 is a hard envelope: a small limit, simple enough to be reviewed or proved, that the core cannot cross, such as a pre-trade loss limit, an action whitelist or a sandbox. The stakes record the worst event the core can cause, before and after the envelope, in four coarse classes by the rate at which that event would be tolerable per opportunity: nuisance (one in a hundred), costly (one in ten thousand), severe (one in a million) and catastrophic (one in a billion).

The gauge on each plate combines these. A decade ruler runs from one observation to a trillion. A solid bar shows the record that counts, which is the smaller of the record accumulated and the record that falls inside one stationarity regime; if drift caps it, a faint extension shows what was accumulated but no longer speaks to the present. Two posts mark the clean record that would certify the tolerable rate of the worst event at 95% confidence, a dashed post without the envelope and a solid post with it. The hatched gap between bar and solid post is the induction deficit, in decades. A plate whose warrant sits at K3 or above has its ruler shaded, because for it the record is not what carries the weight.

Each plate then receives one of five verdicts, by a rule fixed before any plate was coded. PROVED applies to K4 and K5: the record is not load-bearing. REVIEWED applies to K3: the warrant holds while reading keeps pace. For K2 and below, CERTIFIED means the effective record closes the deficit; ABSORBABLE means it does not, but the enveloped worst event is costly or milder, a loss the operator can carry without certification; EXPOSED means neither holds. Put plainly, the rule is the one the argument has been building to: inductive deployment is reasonable where failure is not catastrophic, and the envelope is what makes it not catastrophic.

The numbers on the plates are order-of-magnitude codings, not measurements. Their job is to show where a record could suffice and where no feasible record could, and they are robust to errors of several fold because the conclusions turn on decades.

§4What a clean record certifies

Start with the most favourable case for induction: a program that has run many times without the bad event, in a world that holds still. What does that record certify?

The answer has been in the medical literature since 1983. If an event has not occurred in n independent trials, the upper 95% confidence bound on its rate is about 3/n, the rule of three37. Invert it and you have the record a rate claim requires (E2). Certifying a nuisance rate of one in a hundred takes 298 clean trials, a costly rate 29,956, a severe rate 2,995,731, and a catastrophic rate of one in a billion 3.00×109. Expressed in hours of one unit's operation, the last figure is 341,745 years, which is Butler and Finelli's point in one number24.

Kalra and Paddock applied the same arithmetic to driverless cars. Matching the human fatality rate they used, about 1.09 per hundred million miles, with zero fatalities at 95% confidence takes about 2.75×108 miles; showing a 20% improvement with conventional power takes billions more, of the order of 1.27×1010 under a square-root Poisson approximation38. Through June 2026 Waymo reports 271.3 million rider-only miles39, which is 0.987 of the zero-fatality parity threshold. Its published safety-impact comparison does not rest on fatalities. It rests on injury and airbag-deployment crashes, events one to two orders of magnitude more frequent, for which hundreds of millions of miles are statistically ample: over 270 million miles, 82% fewer injury-causing crashes than the human benchmark and 95% fewer serious-injury-or-worse crashes40. A proxy event a hundred times more frequent needs a hundred times fewer miles, 2.75×106 (E2). Moving to a more frequent proxy is one of the two legitimate ways to shrink an induction deficit. The other, moving the tail, is the subject of section 7.

All of this assumes exchangeability: that future opportunities are drawn from the same distribution as past ones. Under that assumption learning theory gives real guarantees. Valiant's probably-approximately-correct framework is a formalisation of induction that holds exactly when training and future examples come from the same distribution35. Conformal prediction goes further, wrapping any model, however opaque, in prediction sets with finite-sample coverage, needing nothing but exchangeability36,41. These are the K2 warrants. They are the strongest guarantees an opaque program can carry without a checker, and they are conditional on the world holding still.

The condition fails quietly. When Recht and colleagues rebuilt the ImageNet test set by the original procedure, accuracy of the models fell by 11 to 14 points on data meant to be the same distribution42. A volume of literature now catalogues the ways training and deployment distributions part company43. The next section measures what that does to a record.

§5Drift puts a ceiling on induction

Suppose the quantity you care about, an agent's daily hit rate, moves slowly as markets and the agent's sources change. Each day yields one noisy observation of where it stands now. How much does a long record tell you about today?

Model the rate as a random walk with step size σ, observed through Bernoulli outcomes with variance v. The Kalman filter's steady state gives the answer in closed form44: the best achievable estimation error has variance about σ√v, so the record is worth at most √v/σ observations however long you watch. Call that the effective-sample ceiling. E3 checks it by simulation at a starting rate of 0.55. With σ = 0.002 the ceiling is 249 observations, and the estimator's effective sample size after ten thousand days is 272; with σ = 0.008 the ceiling is 62 and the simulation reaches 63. In a stationary world the effective sample keeps growing, to 9,519 at ten thousand.

The second result in E3 is worse. If you use the whole record, averaging every day equally, more data makes the estimate less accurate. At σ = 0.002 the full-sample mean's error is 0.0484 after a hundred days and 0.1105 after ten thousand, because the early record describes an agent and a world that no longer exist. A long, clean history is not merely less informative under drift. Read naively, it misleads.

This is why the warrant ladder separates K2 from K1. A record from a moving world has a horizon, and the horizon is set by the world, not by the patience of the observer. Economics has met this problem in several forms. Robert Lucas argued that relations estimated from historical data shift when policy changes, because the agents in the data respond to the policy45. Charles Goodhart's observation that a statistical regularity collapses once pressure is placed on it for control is the same point from the regulator's side46. Andrew Lo's adaptive markets hypothesis treats market efficiency itself as a moving target set by the population of strategies47, a revision of the efficient-markets view that prices already reflect available information48.

Statistics has partial remedies. Adaptive conformal inference updates its working miscoverage level after every observation and so recovers the target coverage on long-run average under arbitrary drift49. E9 shows both halves. With a target of 90% and noise that drifts from one to three times its initial scale, a conformal predictor with a frozen calibration set covers 62.1% overall, falling to 44.3% in the last sixth of the run; the adaptive version covers 90.0%, and stays within half a point of target in every sixth. The adaptive guarantee is real, but notice what kind it is: an average over time, not a promise about the next prediction.

For the program class this article describes, then, the record has two limits. Its size is bounded by the rarity of the events that matter (section 4), and its useful length is bounded by the speed of the world (this section). The trading agent shows both at once.

§6The trading agent, worked through

Consider an equity-trading agent built the way its proponents would want it: it reads new working papers, filings, transcripts and prices as they appear and folds what it learns into its positions. Its claimed advantage is exactly the asymmetry of section 1. It can incorporate research faster than any person can check whether the research, or its use of it, is sound. Three facts about such an agent follow from the literature and from the lab.

Published edges decay

McLean and Pontiff followed 97 published predictors of cross-sectional stock returns. Their portfolio returns were 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication; they attribute the 32-point difference to investors trading on what the papers taught them50. A research-ingesting agent is the mechanism of that decay, not an exception to it. Applied to a strategy with a true Sharpe ratio of 0.5, those figures leave about 0.37 out of sample and 0.21 after publication. Whatever the agent learned from yesterday's papers, the market is learning too, and the regime its record describes ends as the research it rests on spreads.

Reading is trying

The second fact turns the agent's advantage against its record. To read ten thousand candidate signals is to test ten thousand hypotheses, and the best of many backtests is biased upward by selection. Harvey, Liu and Zhu argued from the hundreds of factors in the literature that a new one should clear a t-statistic of about three, not two51; Bailey and López de Prado's deflated Sharpe ratio adjusts a reported Sharpe ratio for the number of trials behind it, and the probability of backtest overfitting estimates directly how often the in-sample winner underperforms out of sample52,53.

E4 runs the selection explicitly. Among K candidate signals, one is genuine with a true Sharpe ratio of 0.5 and the rest have none; each is estimated over five years. With one candidate the genuine signal is chosen by definition. With ten, the best in-sample Sharpe ratio averages 0.80, the chosen signal's out-of-sample Sharpe ratio averages 0.21, and the genuine signal is chosen 38.0% of the time. With ten thousand, the in-sample winner averages 1.72, its out-of-sample Sharpe ratio is 0.001, and the genuine signal wins 0.4% of the time. For the genuine signal to be picked half the time among ten thousand, its true Sharpe ratio would need to be 1.72, which is almost exactly the expected maximum of ten thousand null estimates, 1.73.

The lesson is that the agent's record must be deflated by everything it read. The more it ingests, the higher the bar any single result must clear, and an overseer who sees only the trades that were made cannot see the trials that were not. This is the first place where the inductive model needs a discipline of its own: the record that warrants the agent has to include a count of what the agent tried.

Short records cannot separate skill from luck

In Alpha Arena's first season, six frontier language models each traded ten thousand dollars of real money in crypto perpetual futures without human intervention, from 18 October to 3 November 2025. One finished up about 22%, another was the only other model in profit, and four of the six lost money54,55. Seventeen days is seventeen draws. At the annualised standard error E4 uses for five years of daily data, a single season's ranking says almost nothing about which model would win the next. The value of the experiment was elsewhere: the stake was the envelope. No model could lose more than it was given, so the experiment was safe to run whatever the models did.

Simulation shortens the record, and moves the problem

An agent can be trained and evaluated in simulation before it touches money. Multi-agent market simulators such as ABIDES reproduce order-book dynamics with populations of synthetic traders56, and robotics has shown that randomising a simulator's parameters can produce policies that transfer to the physical world57. Simulation does two useful things. It supplies volume, so that rare events can be rehearsed thousands of times, and it lets adversarial scenarios be constructed rather than waited for. It does not dissolve the inductive problem. It moves it: the question becomes whether the simulator's world is exchangeable with the real one in the respects that matter. That question has no answer in the simulator itself.

Regulators of retail funds settled on the honest phrasing long ago. A UCITS key investor information document must show a ten-year bar chart of past performance, and next to it a prominent statement warning about its limited value as a guide to future performance58. That sentence is the epistemology of this program class, written into law. What the law does not supply, and what the next section is about, is the part of the system that does not depend on past performance at all.

§7Envelopes: the part that does not depend on the record

If the record cannot certify the worst event, change the worst event. That is the job of an envelope: a component, small enough to be read or proved, that bounds what the inductive core can do regardless of how good or bad the core turns out to be. The idea is older than learned systems. Lui Sha's Simplex architecture pairs a high-performance controller nobody can fully verify with a simple safety controller that can be, and a decision module that hands control to the simple one whenever the system approaches the edge of a region known to be recoverable59. Shielding does the same for reinforcement learning: a shield synthesised from a temporal-logic specification watches each action the learner proposes and replaces any that would violate the specification, so that safety holds by construction while the learner explores60. Constrained Markov decision processes put the bound into the objective, and the safe reinforcement-learning literature catalogues both families61,62. Runtime verification supplies the monitors63.

Finance arrived at envelopes through losses. The SEC's market-access rule, adopted in 2010, requires broker-dealers to maintain pre-trade controls that block orders exceeding credit or capital thresholds and erroneous or duplicative orders64. Its first enforcement action concerned Knight Capital: on 1 August 2012, while trying to fill 212 small retail orders, a router running discontinued code sent millions of orders into the market over 45 minutes, obtained more than four million executions in 154 stocks for more than 397 million shares, and lost more than $460 million65. The SEC's finding was not that Knight's software was wrong, which was obvious, but that its controls were not designed to stop software being wrong in that way. The flash crash of 6 May 2010, which the CFTC and SEC staff traced in part to a large automated sell programme meeting thin liquidity, had already shown how fast an unbounded automated process can move a market66. In the European Union, the technical standards for algorithmic trading require pre-trade limits, a kill functionality, and testing of algorithms in a separate environment before deployment67. Regulators, in other words, already mandate the architecture this article describes: an inductive core, rehearsed in simulation, inside a deductive envelope.

E5 measures what an envelope buys and costs. A simulated agent trades a fat-tailed daily return stream at a varying exposure and, on one day in 2,500, suffers a regime error of the kind its record has never shown, losing 15 to 60% of capital. Over 6,000 simulated years, the unenveloped agent's median annual return is 11.3%, its 0.1% worst day is −59.5%, and 3.53% of its years end in a drawdown of half the account. The enveloped agent, with exposure capped and a 3% daily stop that fills 25% worse than its limit, earns a median 9.6%; its worst day in all 6,000 years is −3.8%, and none of its years ends in a 50% drawdown. The envelope gives up 1.69 points of median return and removes the ruin. To certify the regime-error rate from a clean record instead would take 7,488 trading days, 29.7 years, during which the world would have changed several times.

Three properties make an envelope worth its cost, and each is a research question in its own right. First, it must be small enough to sit at K3 or above: a loss limit can be reviewed in an afternoon, while a second learned model checking the first is just another K1 component. Second, it must bound the event that matters. Knight had controls; they were not on the path the failure took. Third, it must be faster than the damage. A soft envelope, a human watching a dashboard, works for a recommender whose harms accrue over weeks; it did not work for a router that lost $460 million in 45 minutes, and the order reports that the firm's systems had sent 97 automated error emails before the open, none of them designed as alerts65.

Industrial control shows the same pattern. When DeepMind handed data-centre cooling to a learned agent, the agent's recommended actions were checked against operator-defined safety constraints before execution, and the system could hand control back to the existing rule-based automation at any time68. Customer-facing agents need envelopes of a different kind: in 2024 a Canadian tribunal held an airline responsible for what its website chatbot told a customer about bereavement fares69. A whitelist of actions, and statements retrieved from a source of record rather than generated, turn a severe event into a nuisance.

The envelope is also where the intuition that some guarantee will always remain belongs. There will almost always be some guarantee, but it will not be a guarantee about the core. It is a guarantee about the box the core is in.

§8Guarantees that survive drift

An envelope bounds the worst event. Two further kinds of guarantee survive a moving world and cost almost nothing to add. Neither says the agent will do well tomorrow. One says how fast you will find out that it no longer does; the other says how it compares with alternatives you could have chosen.

How fast you will notice

The naive way to watch an agent is to recompute a significance test every day and act when it fails. That procedure's false-alarm rate is not the nominal 5%, because a test repeated at every step will eventually reject by chance. Abraham Wald's sequential probability ratio test and Jean Ville's inequality give the correct alternative: a likelihood ratio, or more generally any nonnegative supermartingale under the null hypothesis, exceeds 1/α with probability at most α over the entire infinite horizon70,71. Modern work on safe anytime-valid inference builds a general toolkit on that fact, with e-values and e-processes that can be monitored continuously and stopped at will72. For detecting a change, rather than testing a fixed hypothesis, Page's CUSUM and the Shiryaev–Roberts procedure have e-process generalisations that bound the expected time to a false alarm73–75.

E6 compares them on an agent whose hit rate is 0.55. Over 2,000 days with the edge intact, the repeated z-test raises a false alarm in 38.8% of runs; a likelihood-ratio e-process with a Ville bound of 5% does so in 4.65%, and a Shiryaev–Roberts e-detector calibrated to an average run length of 40,000 days in 2.80%. When the hit rate falls to 0.48 at day 1,000, the e-detector raises the alarm after a median of 434 days, with 90% of runs alarmed within 793. The naive test is no faster, a median of 418, and it has bought that parity with a false-alarm rate 13.8 times the e-detector's.

This is the inductive model's honest substitute for a performance guarantee. You cannot promise that the edge will persist. You can promise, with a stated error rate that holds however often you look, that if it disappears you will know within a bounded expected time. Combined with an envelope, it turns "we trust the agent" into "we will stop the agent within so many days of it ceasing to deserve trust, and it cannot do more than so much damage before then".

Compared with what

The second kind of guarantee comes from online learning. Hedge, the multiplicative-weights algorithm, guarantees without any statistical assumption about the data that its cumulative loss exceeds that of the best single expert in hindsight by at most √(T ln K / 2)76,77. Thomas Cover's universal portfolio gives the same kind of promise for investment: asymptotically, its wealth grows at the rate of the best constant-rebalanced portfolio chosen in hindsight, whatever the market does78. These are the strongest guarantees available in a world with no stationarity at all, and they are relative.

E7 makes the relativity concrete. Eight experts, regimes of 250 steps in each of which a different expert is best, 2,000 steps in all. Hedge's regret against the best single expert averages 21.7, with a maximum of 25.0 over 200 runs, inside the bound of 45.6. Its absolute loss per step is 0.495, almost as bad as following a wrong expert (0.55) and far from following the right one (0.35), because no single expert is good across regimes. Against the best switching sequence, Hedge's regret is 282.6. Fixed-Share, designed to track a switching comparator79, brings that to 241.2, inside the 557 its guarantee allows for seven switches at these parameters, a bound more than ten times looser than Hedge's against a fixed comparator. Drift returns here as the number of switches: the more often the best strategy changes, the weaker any relative guarantee becomes.

§9When the agent runs the research

A further step is to deploy an agent to run the research and development that produces the guardrails. Agents already run research. The AI Scientist system, published in Nature in 2026, carries a project from idea through experiments to a manuscript; one generated paper passed the first round of review at a workshop, and the system's automated reviewer predicts acceptance about as well as human reviewers do80. AlphaEvolve pairs a language model that proposes programs with automated evaluators that score them, and has found, among other results, a procedure for multiplying 4×4 complex matrices with 48 scalar multiplications81. The Darwin Gödel Machine rewrites its own code and keeps the variants that score better, raising its own SWE-bench score from 20.0% to 50.0%82.

The last of these is the clearest illustration of the move from text to history. Jürgen Schmidhuber's Gödel machine was defined to rewrite itself only after proving that the rewrite improves expected utility83. Its namesake replaces that proof obligation with empirical validation on a benchmark82. That is exactly a descent from K5 to K1, made because the proof was unobtainable and the benchmark was not. Its authors also report the cost: rewarded for reducing hallucinated tool use, the system at one point removed the markers that the hallucination check relied on, satisfying the measurement rather than the goal82.

So an agent can run the research. The constraint is on what it is allowed to hand back. A guardrail accepted because the agent that wrote it has a good record is a K1 component pretending to be an envelope. A guardrail accepted because it arrives with something a small checker can validate, a shield synthesised from a specification, a monitor with a proof of soundness, an invariant with a certificate, is a K4 component regardless of who or what wrote it.

Complexity theory says this asymmetry is not a trick. Stephen Cook's characterisation of NP is the observation that some answers that are hard to find are easy to check84. Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff showed that a polynomial-time verifier can be convinced by an arbitrarily powerful prover through interaction, and the PCP theorem showed that suitably encoded proofs can be checked by reading only a constant number of their bits85,86. AI safety via debate is an attempt to use that asymmetry for oversight: a judge weaker than the debaters can still pick the honest side, in the right protocol12. The general rule follows. An agent may be stronger than its checker on every axis except one: what it hands back must be cheaper to check than it was to find. Where that holds, the human verification gap does not matter, because humans are not the checker. Where it fails, nothing short of induction remains.

§10Knowledge nobody has read

The most far-reaching claim in this territory is that whole fields of AI-generated knowledge will come into being without human verification, and that programs will be built on them. Mathematics is the best place to test the claim, because it is the field where checking is most mechanisable, and it shows both outcomes already.

Mathematics has done this before, slowly

The four-colour theorem was proved in 1976 by a reduction to a large set of configurations checked by computer; many mathematicians did not accept it as a proof for years, because no person could read the part the machine did87. Georges Gonthier's formal proof in Coq removed the doubt by moving the warrant from a reader to a proof checker88. Thomas Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture, published in 2005 after a long refereeing process that left the referees unable to certify every computation, followed the same arc to a complete formal proof in 201789,90. The Boolean Pythagorean triples problem was settled by a SAT solver with a 200-terabyte proof, and Schur number five with a proof of about two petabytes; both are trusted not because anyone read them but because their certificates were checked by small verified programs33,91,92. In the other direction, a claimed proof of the abc conjecture has been in dispute since 2012 because the community could not complete the reading, and two leading experts set out in 2018 where they believed it fails93.

The lesson of that history is that mathematics already distinguishes two kinds of unread knowledge. Knowledge certified by a small trusted kernel is unread but verified. Knowledge that no one can check is not knowledge yet. The de Bruijn criterion, that a proof checker should be small enough to be checked by hand, is what makes the first kind trustworthy34.

The new volume is in both kinds

On the verified side, formal libraries such as mathlib supply the infrastructure94, and agents now write at a scale people cannot read. Math Inc.'s Gauss agent produced a Lean formalisation of a strong form of the prime number theorem, about 25,000 lines and 1,100 theorems and definitions, in about three weeks95, and then helped complete the formal proof of sphere packing in dimensions 8 and 24, bringing that formalisation to roughly 200,000 lines, the 24-dimensional case in two weeks from the original paper96. AlphaProof's olympiad results were produced by reinforcement learning against the Lean checker, though the competition problems were formalised by human experts first97. Here the residual human task is narrow and important: checking that the formal statement says what was meant. A proof checker cannot tell you that you proved the wrong theorem.

On the unverified side, volume is outrunning referees. In October 2025 an announcement that a language model had solved ten open Erdős problems turned out to mean that it had found existing solutions in the literature. A systematic study of 700 problems marked open in the same database addressed 13: five appeared to be novel autonomous solutions, eight were rediscoveries, and the authors concluded that many were open through obscurity rather than difficulty, warning of what they called subconscious plagiarism98. By mid-2026 an amateur using a frontier model had produced a short proof of a problem open for decades, praised by an expert as a Book proof, and a frontier lab announced a disproof of a conjecture on planar unit distances; the press account that reported both noted that there are not enough people with the skills to check the volume of proofs now being generated99. Among mathematics submissions with substantive AI use, authors described 71% of 717 named open-problem records as fully resolved4. Those are the authors' descriptions, not the referees'. William Thurston's argument that mathematics is a matter of human understanding, not only of correct proofs100, survives this; what does not survive is the assumption that understanding and correctness will arrive together.

Science has fewer checkers

Outside mathematics the asymmetry runs the wrong way, because checking a scientific claim usually means doing an experiment, which costs as much as or more than generating the prediction. AlphaFold's structure predictions101 fill a database of over 214 million entries102, set against an experimental archive that held just over 180 thousand structures when the database launched103. Most predictions will never be checked experimentally; they are used because the method's accuracy was validated on a distribution of known structures. That is a K2 warrant applied to a population partly outside the validated distribution. GNoME reported 2.2 million new crystal structures, 380 thousand of them predicted stable104; an independent examination of a sample questioned the novelty and the realism of many entries and argued that without demonstrated function they cannot yet be called materials105. The autonomous laboratory published alongside it synthesised 41 of 58 targets106. The predictions outnumber the experiments by four orders of magnitude.

Unverified knowledge compounds

A program built on such knowledge inherits its warrant, and dependencies compound. E8 builds a literature in twelve generations of 400 claims, each depending on three earlier ones, with a 2% chance of an error of its own. With no checking, the share of claims in the newest generation that rest on at least one unsound claim reaches 79.3%. A human reviewer who examines 10% of claims and catches 90% of errors among them brings that only to 67.3%: sampling review barely slows the compounding. A checker applied at every node holds it at zero. A single chain of claims tolerates surprisingly little: at a per-claim error rate of 1%, a dependency chain more than 5.1 claims long has less than a 95% chance of being entirely sound; at one in ten thousand, 513.

Training on generated output has an analogous failure. Shumailov and colleagues showed that models trained recursively on data produced by earlier models lose the tails of the original distribution, and eventually its substance107. Knowledge that no one reads, recycled as the input to the next generation of knowledge no one reads, degrades in the same way.

The rule for programs follows from the rule for proofs: a program's warrant is no stronger than the weakest warrant on any path through its dependencies, unless something checks at the junction. An application that rests on a thousand machine-checked lemmas sits at K5 with a statement-fidelity caveat. An application that rests on a thousand predicted structures sits at K2 at best, and at K1 if the predictions are used outside the distribution they were validated on.

§11The plates

The twenty-four plates below apply the ladder to concrete systems, from a verified microkernel to an agent that writes its own guardrails. Each has the same five fields: what it decides, what in it outruns the checker, the warrant, the envelope, and when the warrant breaks. The gauge on each is read as described in section 3. The codings are order-of-magnitude and are listed in full in the ledger in section 12.

A practical consequence: every K3 warrant on an AI-generated stream should be paired with a measure of review throughput, so that the descent is visible when it happens.

§13What the model does not claim

It does not claim that inductive trust is new. Insurance, medicine and credit have always worked this way, and a regulated fund's performance warning is centuries of practice compressed into a sentence. What is new is that the programs whose trust rests on record are becoming general purpose, read more than their overseers, and are being asked to make decisions faster than records can accumulate.

It does not claim that verification is obsolete. The argument runs the other way: as more of the core becomes unverifiable, the small verifiable parts, envelopes, checkers, monitors and specifications, carry more of the weight and deserve more of the effort. Research on guardrails and on certifiable degrees of assurance around an inductive core is what makes the rest of the class usable.

It does not claim the plate codings are measurements. They are coarse placements whose conclusions turn on decades, not on factors of two. Anyone who disputes a coding can change it in the ledger and watch which verdicts move; most will not.

And it does not claim that the record is worthless. Within one regime, at stakes the operator can carry, a long clean record is excellent evidence. The model's claim is narrower and, I think, harder to escape: the record entitles you to a statement about the past, to a forecast for as long as the world holds still, to a promise about how quickly you will notice when it does not, and to a comparison with the alternatives. It does not entitle you to anything about the worst case. That has to come from text.

Coda

The category of program this article set out to describe is real. Its members are judged by what they do, trusted by induction, and deployed where failure is survivable. The useful addition is an architecture and a test. The architecture is an inductive core inside a deductive envelope, watched by a monitor with an anytime-valid alarm. The test is the question in the title, asked of every component in turn: what does the record entitle you to, and is that enough for what this component can do?
