Who checks the work?

the thesis, and why it is a question about supply

Fifteen directions are currently described as the frontier of generative AI. They are usually presented as a capability list: windows get longer, memory persists, models hear and speak, agents act, robots move, worlds are simulated, systems improve themselves, and eventually the whole apparatus turns around and does science. Read that way, the list looks additive and largely independent — fifteen research programmes that happen to be running at the same time, each of which will arrive when its own engineering is finished. The capability curve underneath them is not in dispute: it has been described by scaling relations for years, it has held across several orders of magnitude, and it is largely the same set of models powering all fifteen.[1,2,3,4]

This article reads the same fifteen a different way. Every one of them extends what a generative model can attempt. Not one of them extends, by itself, what can be checked. A longer context lets a model answer questions about a book; it does not supply a way of knowing whether the answer is right. An agent can take three hundred actions; nothing about taking them tells you which one was wrong. A world model can produce a minute of coherent video; coherence is not correctness. In each case the direction moves the attempt rate and leaves the verification rate where it was, and the gap between the two is where the field's actual difficulty now lives.

The thesis is therefore a question about supply. What supplies the ground truth for this direction, and what does one unit of it cost? The answer turns out to sort the fifteen more cleanly than any grouping by modality or architecture. Directions whose verdicts are cheap, exact and machine-obtainable have moved fast and continue to move: formal mathematics, program synthesis, anything whose output is executable. Directions whose verdicts require a person, a physical trial or a replication have moved slowly regardless of how much capability was pointed at them, and the published record shows them failing in a characteristic way — excellent scores against the check that exists, collapse against the check that matters.

This is not a claim that capability does not matter, and it is not the familiar observation that benchmarks are imperfect. It is a structural claim with an arithmetic behind it. When a generator is paired with a verifier, the returns to scaling the generator are bounded by properties of the verifier, and those bounds are computable. Sampling more candidates raises the chance that a good one exists, but the value of that coverage is capped by how well the selector can recognise it; a false-accept rate of five per cent puts a ceiling on answer accuracy that no amount of additional sampling lifts.[5,6] Checking an agent's work more often reduces cost but cannot lengthen its reliable horizon, because the expected number of undetected errors depends on detection recall and the number of steps, and not at all on the checking interval. Raising the rate at which a research pipeline proposes hypotheses does not change the fraction of accepted findings that are true; it changes how many false ones are published.[7] Each of these is derived and measured in the lab below.

Three consequences follow, and they organise the argument. First, the sequence in which the fifteen directions become reliable is predicted better by the cost of their ground truth than by the difficulty of their modelling. Second, the standard response to a missing verifier — build a model that judges — inherits the generator's blind spots, and the size of that inheritance can be quantified. Third, the last three directions on the list, in which the system produces new knowledge, are the ones where the verification deficit is largest and where the consequences of running the loop open are not confined to the system: they enter a shared corpus.

A note on what this article is not arguing. Nothing here says that a direction without a cheap verifier is unimportant or doomed. Robotics has the most authoritative verifier in the taxonomy — physics — and its problem is throughput, not validity. Native audio has no ground truth for the property it exists to deliver, and is nonetheless obviously useful. What the frame buys is an explanation of rate: why some of these fifteen have compounded over three years and others have produced impressive demonstrations that do not accumulate. The organising claim is that you can read the difference off the verification column.

Three kinds of check

what counts as verification, and what a verdict costs

To make the question answerable rather than rhetorical, three things need fixing: what counts as a check, how the checks available to each direction are enumerated, and what a verdict costs.

Three classes of check. A check is exact when a machine can decide it and the decision is the property of interest: a proof kernel accepting a term, a test suite passing, a string matching a planted needle, a virtual machine reaching a specified final state. Exactness here is not a claim about importance but about decidability — the verdict is reproducible, cheap, and cannot be argued with. A check is a proxy when it is automatic and fallible: a reward model, a language model acting as a judge, an aggregate video-quality metric, a discriminator. Proxies scale like exact checks and fail like human ones, which is what makes them attractive and what makes them dangerous. A check is human when a person's judgement is irreducibly in the loop: a listening panel, an expert adjudicating a reasoning chain, peer review, a replication.

The boundary that matters is not exact-versus-approximate but closed-versus-open. An exact check is closed: its verdict is defined by a procedure, and optimising against it is optimising against the thing itself. A proxy is open: it has a distribution of errors, and any process that searches hard enough against it will find the errors. This is Goodhart's observation in its measured form — reward-model overoptimisation follows a lawful curve in which the true objective rises, peaks and falls as the proxy continues to improve.[8,9,10]

It is worth being explicit about which verifier the field has actually been using, because it is easy to forget that there is one. Modern instruction following was built by putting a person in the loop and training a model to predict their preferences — a proxy check, deliberately constructed, and one that worked well enough to define a product category.[11,12] Its successors substituted a model for the person, which is the same move again one level up.[13] The scalable-oversight programme exists precisely because that ladder runs out: at some point the supervisor is evaluating work it could not have produced, and the question of what grounds the verdict has to be answered again.[14,15]

Enumerating the check set. Each of the fifteen plates below names the checks actually used for that direction in the published literature — between five and six of them — and classifies each one. The gauge on every plate is a bar showing how those named checks divide across the three classes. This is a count over a stated list, not an estimate: the reader can disagree with a classification, and the disagreement is about a specific named item rather than about a number that arrived from nowhere. The classification rule is the one above, applied to the check as it is used in practice rather than as it could be used in principle. A multiple-choice answer key is exact even when the question it answers is a poor proxy for the capability; that distinction is carried by the plate's silent failure field, not smuggled into the bar.

Costing a verdict. Each plate also declares an order-of-magnitude cost, in seconds, for obtaining one verdict on one unit of that direction's output, together with the basis for the figure. Where a published measurement exists it is used: the long-horizon desktop benchmark reports a median of about 1.6 hours of skilled human operation per task, which is where the authoring cost for that plate comes from.[16] Where none exists, the figure is a declared order of magnitude and is marked as such. These are decades, not measurements, and the ledger treats them accordingly — the claim being tested is whether the ordering by cost predicts the ordering by progress, and an ordering is robust to a factor of three in any single entry.

One clarification about amortisation, because it is the most common objection to this kind of accounting. Many checks are expensive to author and cheap to run: a benchmark task takes hours to write and milliseconds to grade. The cost declared on each plate is the cost of a marginal verdict on a new unit of output, which is the quantity that governs whether verification can keep up with generation. Where authoring cost dominates — as it does for execution-graded desktop workflows — the plate says so, because that authoring cost is exactly what caps the number of distinct things that can ever be checked.

A final piece of scaffolding. Throughout, the word verifier is used in the loose engineering sense of anything that produces a verdict, not in the complexity-theoretic sense. The formal literature is nonetheless the right background intuition: the asymmetry between finding and checking is the oldest useful fact in the area, program checkers formalise the idea that a result can be validated without trusting the process that produced it, and the limits are equally old — no interesting semantic property of a program is decidable in general.[17,18,19]

03

The lab

nine deterministic experiments behind every number here

Nine experiments were run to fix the quantitative claims. All are deterministic, seeded, and use only the Python standard library; the output below is reproduced verbatim from the build and every number quoted in the prose, the plates and the ledger comes from it. They are deliberately small. None of them is a model experiment, and none is meant to be: each isolates a piece of arithmetic that governs a direction, so that the arithmetic can be inspected rather than asserted.

L1 measures what a noisy verifier does to best-of-k selection. L2 separates coverage from answer accuracy under a checker with a false-accept rate. L3 is pure arithmetic on a reference transformer configuration: cache bytes, attention share, and the length at which the quadratic term takes over. L4 computes top-1 retrieval precision as a store grows, and the ceiling imposed by superseded copies. L5 costs a duplex audio turn in tokens and milliseconds. L6 derives the optimal verification interval on an agent trajectory and then shows what that interval cannot buy. L7 runs iterated self-training with and without a real-data anchor. L8 prices a panel of correlated model judges. L9 applies the screening identity to an automated discovery pipeline.

The framing has a specific target. A large fraction of the last three years' progress in reasoning came from spending more compute at inference — intermediate reasoning steps, many samples with a vote, search over branches, and explicit trades of test-time compute against parameter count.[20,21,22,23] Every one of those methods contains a selection step, and the selection step is a verifier. Where the verifier is an interpreter running a test, this is the pass@k regime and it works.[24,25] Where the verifier is a vote or a learned scorer, the arithmetic below applies.

Two results are worth flagging before the plates, because they recur. L2's ceiling is closed-form: as the number of samples grows, the correct candidates grow like kp and the false accepts like kα(1−p), so the accuracy of the answer actually returned converges to a constant that depends on the checker and not on k. At a 5% false-accept rate that constant is 0.4070 for the problem population used, against coverage of 0.9614 at k=1024 — a 55.6-point gap that no additional sampling closes. And L6's near-invariance is sharp in the regime that matters: while blocks are short enough that the per-block error probability stays small, the expected number of undetected errors over N steps is Np(1−r), which contains no checking interval at all. These two facts are the article's spine.

The first of them is visible in L1 as well, in a form that is easier to feel. With an exact verifier, 1,024 samples extract 3.24 standard deviations of quality. With a verifier whose observation is corrupted by unit-variance noise — a correlation of 0.707 with true quality, which is generous for a learned judge — the same 1,024 samples extract 2.30, and the whole exercise is worth about what 64 samples would have been worth under an exact check. Sixteen-fold more compute, bought back by the checker.

04

Input surfaces

D01–D05 · longer, older, richer, faster input

The first five directions all do the same structural thing: they widen the channel into the model. More tokens, older tokens, tokens that were pixels, tokens that were sound, tokens arriving against a clock. None of them changes what the model does with what it receives, and — this is the part that gets lost — none of them supplies any new means of telling whether what comes back is right.

They are nonetheless the easiest group to verify, for a reason worth stating plainly: in four of the five cases the referent is in the room. The document is in the window, the image is on the screen, the transcript exists. Ground truth is not merely obtainable, it has been handed over with the question. That the checks still fail to bind — that models answer visual questions without using the image, and retrieve stale facts as current — is the most direct evidence available for the article's thesis, because here the excuse of expense is unavailable.

The engineering that made this group possible deserves naming, because it is genuinely hard and entirely orthogonal to the question here. Windows grew through relative and rotary position encodings, extrapolation schemes, IO-aware attention kernels, sequence-parallel attention and sub-quadratic sequence models whose expressive limits are themselves now characterised;[26,27,28,29,30,31,32,33] serving grew through paged caches, attention sinks and speculative decoding.[34,35] Retrieval was the alternative answer and remains the cheaper one.[36] Every item on that list raises what a model can be handed. None of them is a verifier.

Read together, the first five plates make a point that none of them makes alone. The two directions with the cleanest exact checks — long context and multimodal reasoning — are also the two where the published failures are most embarrassing, and that is not a coincidence. A cheap exact check gets used a great deal, which means it gets optimised against, which means it stops measuring what it was built to measure. Planted-needle retrieval is a perfect example: it is exact, it costs nothing, models score near the top of it, and the property everyone actually wants — that a model uses a long input as well as a short one — degrades well before the advertised limit.[41,42] The multimodal case is starker still, because the audit is trivial to run and rarely was: strip the image and see whether the score moves.[57]

The other three plates in the group show the opposite failure. Audio, real-time interaction and persistent memory have expensive or absent ground truth for the exact property they exist to deliver — how it sounded, whether the interruption was apt, whether the recalled fact is still true — and each has settled on an exact check for an adjacent property instead. Word error rate measures the lexical content that the text bottleneck already handled. Turn-taking latency measures timing, which is one-sided: a model that never interrupts is scored well by it. Recall@k measures whether a fact was found, not whether it was superseded, and L4 shows that the second question has an answer the retriever cannot influence — with the current fact and four stale copies in the store, at most 20% of top hits are current no matter how good the embedding is.

The substitution is rational in each case and the aggregate effect is not. Where the exact check is adjacent rather than central, capability accrues to the adjacent property. That is the mechanism by which a field can improve steadily on everything it measures while the thing it wanted stays where it was.

05

Action surfaces

D06–D09 · when the model changes something

The second group crosses a line. In the first five directions the model emitted an answer that a person then read. In these four it emits actions, and the actions change something: a repository, a filesystem, a database, a physical object. Two things follow immediately. The model's own output becomes its next input, so errors compound rather than merely occur. And the world now holds a record of what happened, which means an exact check exists — if someone writes it.

That conditional is the whole story of this group. Software engineering became the first domain in which agents did useful work, not because code is intellectually easier than the alternatives but because a repository has a test suite: the verdict is exact, costs a second, and can be run ten thousand times a night.[76,77] Desktop work followed, at the price of writing an execution-based grader for every task by hand. Robotics has the most authoritative verdict of all and cannot run it more than once at a time.

The benchmark landscape for this group is unusually informative if read as a list of verification strategies rather than a list of tasks. Repository issues are graded by the project's own tests; web tasks by scripted end states; assistant tasks by reference answers with a deterministic scorer; tool-and-user tasks by comparing the final database against a target; and consequential office work by a mixture of checkpoints and a simulated colleague.[76,78,79,80,81,82] Each design is a decision about what can be decided by machine, and the difficulty of that decision, not the difficulty of the task, is what sets how fast the corresponding capability improves.

The four action plates line up almost exactly along verification cost, and the ordering of progress follows. Repository-level software work has an exact verdict that costs a second and was already written by someone else, and it is where agents first became useful. Desktop work has an exact verdict that costs hours to author, and the consequence is visible in the numbers: on the original 369-task suite, where each task carried a single pass/fail script and runs averaged about 30 steps, scores climbed from 12.2% at publication against a 72.4% human baseline, closely enough that a harder successor had to be built. On the long-horizon successor, where 108 tasks carry an average of 27.25 weighted checkpoints and runs average 318 tool calls, the strongest configuration finishes 20.6% of tasks while scoring 54.8% on partial credit.[16,90]

It is tempting to read that as a capability gap and leave it there. The arithmetic says something more specific. L6 models a trajectory as N steps each failing independently with probability p, checked every k steps at a cost of c step-equivalents, with detection recall r. Two results fall out. The first is a cost optimum: at p=0.02 and c=1 the measured minimum sits at k=7 with a cost of 394.9 step-equivalents against 612.2 for checking every step and 129,045.8 for never checking, and the small-p approximation k*=√(c/p) gives 7.07 — the same square-root structure as the classical checkpoint-interval result for long-running computations.[110,111]

The second result is the one that matters. While the blocks are short enough that pk is small — which is the whole practically relevant range — the expected number of undetected errors is Np(1−r), and that expression contains no k at all. Across k = 1 to 30, total cost falls by 35% while the probability that a 300-step run ends clean moves only from 0.5425 to 0.4492 at r=0.9. Checking thirty times more often is worth nine points. Raising detection recall from 0.90 to 0.99 at a fixed interval of seven is worth forty-one, moving 0.5241 to 0.9370. The interval is a cost decision; correctness is a recall decision, and the two knobs are not interchangeable. Holding 95% cleanliness caps the trajectory at 25.6 steps at r=0.9 and 256.5 at r=0.99; running the 318 tool calls of a average long-horizon desktop task at that standard requires recall of 0.992. That is a demanding number for an execution-based grader and an implausible one for a model judge.

Robotics and embodied AI sit at the other end and fail differently. The verdict there is authoritative and unbatchable, so the field's response has been to distribute evaluation across sites rather than make it cheaper.[98,99] The characteristic failure follows directly: policies reporting above 90% on standard suites have been measured collapsing toward zero under perturbations of object position, instruction or layout. Detecting that requires running the perturbed trial, and running the perturbed trial costs another trial. When verdicts are expensive, the set of things anyone checks shrinks to the set of things that were checked last time, which is precisely the condition under which memorisation is reported as competence.

06

Manufactured worlds

D10–D11 · generating the thing truth is about

The third group inverts the relationship. Rather than seeking ground truth, these two directions manufacture the thing that ground truth is normally about: a world model predicts futures that have not happened, and generative simulation produces training data that nobody collected. Both are responses to scarcity — of real experience in the first case, of human-generated text in the second[112] — and both are strictly correct as strategies. Imagined experience is how model-based control has always worked.[109,113,114]

What they cannot manufacture is the verdict. A generated world is checkable against recorded video, which measures pixels; against physics probes, which measure the handful of laws someone wrote probes for; and against human preference, which measures plausibility. None of these is the property being claimed. The one exact test — does a policy trained in here work out there — is available and is the most expensive verdict in the taxonomy, because obtaining it means training the policy and deploying it.

Generative simulation carries a second failure that is fully characterised and worth stating precisely, because it is often described loosely. L7 runs the minimal version: fit a Gaussian by maximum likelihood on n samples, generate the next generation's data from the fit, repeat. 07

Imported and internal verifiers

D12–D13 · the exception, and its counterpart

The fourth group is the exception that establishes the rule, and its counterpart. Neuro-symbolic systems are the only direction in the fifteen where verification is genuinely solved: a proof kernel's verdict is exact, costs microseconds, is reproducible on any machine, and cannot be negotiated with. Self-improving systems are the direction that most depends on a verifier and most often has to settle for a proxy. Placing them together is deliberate: they are the same architecture — propose, check, keep — running against verifiers that differ by nine orders of magnitude in cost and by a categorical difference in kind.

The contrast between these two plates is the cleanest evidence in the article. Both are propose-and-check architectures. In one, the check is a proof kernel: exact, microsecond-cheap, reproducible, and immune to persuasion. In the other, the check is a benchmark score: exact per test, proxy as a measure of the capability, and optimised against by construction. The first has produced results that hold up under any amount of scrutiny — olympiad-level formal mathematics, verified algorithmic improvements.[129,130,132] The second has produced large, real, narrow gains: 20.0% to 50.0% on SWE-bench, 14.2% to 30.7% on Polyglot.[131]

The theoretical version of self-improvement demanded a proof that a modification improves expected utility before adopting it.[134] The working version replaced the proof with a benchmark run, and said so. That substitution is the single most consequential move in the whole taxonomy, because it is what makes the loop practical and what makes its limit a property of the harness. Where the harness is exact and the objective is narrow, the loop compounds. Where the harness is a proxy, reward-model overoptimisation applies, and it is lawful rather than anecdotal: the true objective rises, peaks and declines while the proxy keeps improving.[9,10]

The standard defence is a panel — several judges, on the theory that their errors will cancel. L8 prices it. Sixteen judges each missing a defect 20% of the time, with errors generated by a one-factor Gaussian copula, miss jointly 0.00032 of the time when independent, 0.00965 at correlation 0.6, and 0.07415 at correlation 0.9 — worth 5.00, 2.88 and 1.62 independent judges respectively. Model judges trained on overlapping data, in the same paradigm, sharing failure modes, sit at the wrong end of that range, and the literature already reports the specific correlation you would least want: evaluators favour their own generations.[66,67]

There is also a limit to how far the exception generalises. The formal plate's silent failure is the formalisation gap, and it is not a minor caveat. The kernel certifies the statement it was handed. In the olympiad-level result, the competition problems were formalised into Lean by human experts before the system began, which means the step from an informal question to a formal one — the step where meaning is fixed — sat outside the verified region.[130] That step is exactly the one that has no calculus, and it is the reason the exception has stayed an exception: the two-thirds of the AAAI presidential panel's respondents who judged that human-like reasoning would need to be substantially symbolic were not describing an available architecture.[137]

Producing new truth

D14–D15 · output that leaves the system

The last two directions produce claims about the world, and release them. This is a different kind of output from everything above it. An agent's mistake is contained by the system it touched; a wrong finding enters a shared corpus that other researchers and other models will condition on, and the verdict that would catch it — replication — is the slowest and most expensive in the taxonomy. It was already the binding constraint before machines started proposing.[7,138,139]

The last group is where the argument's stakes are highest, and where the numbers are least forgiving. An automated research pipeline is a screening process, and screening processes obey a single identity. With a prior π that a proposed hypothesis is true, significance α and power 1−β, a positive result is true with probability (1−β)π / [(1−β)π + α(1−π)]. At π=0.01, α=0.05 and 80% power, that is 0.1391. This is not a fact about machines; it is the reason a substantial fraction of published findings in several fields do not replicate.[7,138,139]

What automation changes is throughput, and throughput does not appear in the identity. L9 makes the consequence concrete by holding human confirmation capacity fixed at 20 claims a day and raising the proposal rate. At 20 proposals a day, everything that screens positive gets confirmed and the pipeline's truth rate is 1.00. At 2,000, the confirmed share falls to 17.4% and the truth rate to 0.289. At 20,000 it is 1.7% and 0.154, and at 200,000 it is 0.17% and 0.141 — converging, as it must, on the unassisted positive predictive value. The pipeline does not get less accurate. The corpus does, because a rising fraction of it never met a verifier.

Two escapes exist and both are costly. Tightening the screen works: holding the posterior at a coin flip with a 1% prior requires α ≤ 0.00808, a 6.2-fold tightening, which cuts yield proportionally and returns the throughput advantage. Raising the prior works better: at α=0.05 and 80% power, a 0.5 posterior needs proposals that are right 5.9% of the time before testing, and a 0.9 posterior needs 36%. That is a real and underrated argument for machine-generated hypotheses — a controlled study of over a hundred NLP researchers found language-model ideas rated as more novel than human ones, though less feasible — but it relocates the problem rather than dissolving it, because judging a prior is itself a verification task.[142,143]

The AI-scientist plate closes the loop by automating the interface to the scientific record itself. The reported result is genuine and carefully qualified by its authors: a fully generated manuscript passed the first round of peer review at a workshop of a top machine-learning conference, with reviewer scores of 6, 7 and 6 placing it in the top 45% of submissions to a venue with a 70% acceptance rate, and the system's automated reviewer predicts acceptance decisions about as well as human reviewers do.[146] Every stage of that chain is a proxy, and all of them are predicting the same quantity — acceptance — which is a proxy for interest and novelty rather than for truth. The authors name the risk directly: overwhelmed review systems and added noise in the literature. It is the one place in the taxonomy where the cost of an unverified output is not paid by the operator.

The ordering is the article's claim in one column. The top of the table — formal derivation at microseconds, needle retrieval and answer keys at tenths of a second, end-state assertions at seconds — is a list of the places where generative AI has compounded rather than merely improved. The bottom — replication at expert-months, policy transfer at training runs, listening panels and peer review — is a list of the places where three years of capability growth have produced demonstrations that do not accumulate into reliability.

Two features of the table are worth reading carefully, because they cut against the simple story. First, cheap does not mean safe. The three directions with the cheapest verdicts include two whose published failures are the most striking in the taxonomy: long context and multimodal reasoning. Cheapness causes heavy use, heavy use causes optimisation, and optimisation against an adjacent property produces exactly the pattern observed — near- perfect needle retrieval alongside degradation on aggregation, near-expert benchmark scores alongside failures at counting circles. The variable that protects a direction is not the cost of its check but whether the check is closed — whether optimising against it is optimising against the thing itself. Formal derivation is cheap and closed. Answer keys are cheap and open.

Second, expensive does not mean stalled. Robotics has the most authoritative verifier in the table and its constraint is throughput, not validity; every trial that is run means what it says. That is a materially better position than the one occupied by the directions whose verdicts are cheap and proxy-based, where a great many verdicts are obtained and a diminishing fraction of them mean anything. The distinction between an expensive true signal and a cheap approximate one is the distinction between a slow field and a drifting one.

Aggregated across all fifteen plates, the named check sets divide as 41 exact, 22 proxy and 22 human across 85 named checks (48% / 26% / 26%). That aggregate is not a measurement of the field; it is a measurement of what fifteen literatures have settled on, which is a different and more useful thing. Roughly half the checks in current use are exact, and the ones that are exact cluster in the places where the output happens to be executable, machine-readable or multiple-choice. Almost none of them are exact about the property the direction was proposed to deliver.

10

What would change the answer

four ways the argument fails

An argument organised around a single variable owes the reader an account of what would falsify it. Four things would.

A general-purpose verifier that is cheap and closed. The entire argument rests on the claim that most interesting properties admit no decidable test, which is a theorem about programs rather than a contingent engineering fact.[19] But the theorem constrains generality, not usefulness. Interactive proof systems and program checkers already show that verification can be enormously cheaper than production for whole classes of problem.[17,18] If a practical analogue emerged for natural-language claims — a system that certifies rather than judges, with a soundness guarantee rather than an accuracy figure — the cost column collapses and the ordering with it. Nothing in the current literature looks like that. Prover-verifier training and debate are attempts in the direction, and both currently produce legibility improvements rather than guarantees.[15,148,149]

Weak supervision that reliably elicits strong capability. If a weaker verifier can consistently draw out correct behaviour from a stronger generator, then verification cost stops scaling with capability and the deficit stops widening. This is an active research programme with real early results and honest limits: weak-to-strong generalisation recovers a substantial fraction of the gap on some tasks and does not close it.[150,151] A robust version would change the shape of the ledger's bottom half.

Verifier-free improvement that survives contact. Several plates would look different if capability gains could be shown to accrue without an external signal. The current evidence points the other way: models asked to correct their own reasoning without external feedback do not reliably improve and often degrade;[136] the best-documented reasoning gains come from settings with verifiable rewards;[152,153] and one prominent account of hallucination attributes it directly to evaluations that reward confident guessing over acknowledged uncertainty.[154] A demonstration of durable self-improvement against no external check would be a genuine refutation.

There is a measurement version of the same question, and it is instructive that the benchmarks designed to resist optimisation are the ones that have held. A suite built on core-knowledge priors, with every task validated by independent non-expert humans, stayed hard for years; its interactive successor withholds language instructions entirely and scores efficiency against human action baselines, and frontier systems sat below one per cent at release. A separate proposal grounds a definition of general capability in a battery of cognitive domains and reports near-zero scores on long-term memory storage while other domains saturate.[155,156,157] The shared design principle is that the check is authored to stay closed under the optimisation that will be applied to it. That is a discipline, not a technology, and it is available to every direction in the ledger.

Progress ordered against the cost column. The ledger makes a prediction. If, over the next two years, the fastest reliability gains appear in the directions with the most expensive verdicts — if robotics generalisation or automated discovery outruns formal reasoning and executable code — then the variable this article selected is not the operative one. That is a real risk. Verification cost is not the only thing that differs across these fifteen; data availability, commercial pull and the sheer difficulty of the underlying modelling differ too, and any of them could dominate. The argument for this variable is that it explains the observed pattern with one parameter and predicts the failure modes rather than merely labelling them.

It is also worth stating what the frame does not claim. It does not say that the deficit is permanent, or that the directions with expensive verdicts are less valuable. It says that they are on a slower clock, and that the clock is set by something other than the capability of the generator. The last three years supply an unusually clean natural experiment for that: model capability rose sharply and roughly uniformly across every one of the fifteen directions, because it is largely the same models underneath. Reliability did not. Where it rose, an exact and closed check was already in place.

Which returns the question to where it started. The interesting thing about the frontier is no longer what a model can attempt — that list is long and getting longer, and every item on it is real. It is what happens to an attempt after it is made: who reads it, what they compare it against, how much that comparison costs, and whether anything in the loop is capable of saying no. Fifteen directions, one shared bottleneck. Who checks the work?
