1 What control is, and where it sits in physical AI

A control system makes a physical quantity, such as a joint angle, a wheel speed, a contact force or a drone's attitude, behave as desired despite disturbances and imperfect knowledge of the system. Its central idea is feedback: measure what the system is actually doing, compare it with what it should be doing, and use the difference to decide what to do next [1]. Feedback makes a system insensitive to many errors in its components and to disturbances that nobody modelled, which is why it is found in every engineered machine that moves, from steam engines and aircraft autopilots to industrial robots and the motor drives inside a humanoid [1,2]. The same idea carries a cost: a feedback loop can become unstable if it reacts too strongly or too late, and much of control theory is about getting the benefits of feedback without that risk [1,3].

In a physical AI system, control is the layer that touches the world. A typical robot stack is hierarchical (Figure 1). At the top, task planning and reasoning decide what to do, for example "open the drawer, then pick up the cup"; these topics are covered in the companion article on task planning and, for language-driven systems, in the article on foundation models for physical AI. Motion planning decides how to move in space without collisions, producing paths and trajectories (see the motion planning article). Control decides, many times per second, which torques, forces or velocities to command so that the body actually follows those trajectories, makes and regulates contact, and rejects disturbances [4,5]. The layers run at very different rates: a task planner may act once per second or less, a whole-body controller or model predictive controller at 50–1000 Hz, and motor current loops at several kilohertz [4,6]. The boundaries blur: model predictive control is planning repeated in a feedback loop, and modern learned policies often replace several layers at once, but they still hand their outputs to conventional low-level controllers [7,8].

Control theory is usually divided into "classical" and "modern" schools. Classical control, developed between the 1920s and 1950s, describes single-input single-output systems by transfer functions and designs controllers such as PID with frequency-domain tools (Nyquist and Bode plots, gain and phase margins) and the root locus [10–13]. Modern control, from about 1960, describes multi-input multi-output systems by state-space differential equations and introduced controllability and observability, state feedback and observers, optimal control (LQR and LQG), and later robust, adaptive and nonlinear control and model predictive control [2,14–16]. In the 2010s and 2020s, machine learning added data-driven models, learned policies and learned components inside classical architectures [17–19]. This article covers all three, emphasising what each offers a robot and how they fit together.

The article belongs to the Planning, Reasoning and Control part of the series. It assumes the rigid-body dynamics and system identification treated in the physical modeling article and the Kalman filters and observers of the sensor fusion and state estimation article, and it complements the reinforcement learning article (which covers safe RL and the LQR from the learning side) and the world models article (which covers sampling-based MPC with learned models). Each section introduces concepts and history, the main algorithms and technologies, and a short runnable Python program whose real output is shown.

2 A short history of feedback control

2.1 Regulators, governors and stability

Feedback devices are ancient: Ktesibios' water clock in Hellenistic Alexandria regulated the level of a tank with a float valve, and Cornelis Drebbel's incubator in the early seventeenth century regulated temperature [20]. The centrifugal governor, adapted by James Watt in the 1780s to regulate the speed of steam engines, became the emblem of automatic control; engineers soon noticed that governors sometimes "hunted", oscillating instead of settling. In 1868 James Clerk Maxwell analysed governors with linearized differential equations and showed that stability depends on the roots of the characteristic equation, the first mathematical theory of a feedback system [21,22]. Routh and Hurwitz then gave algebraic stability tests, and in 1892 Aleksandr Lyapunov published a general theory of the stability of motion, including what are now called Lyapunov functions, which became the foundation of nonlinear control nearly a century later [23,24].

2.2 The classical era: ships, amplifiers and servomechanisms

In 1922 Nicolas Minorsky analysed the automatic steering of ships, observing how helmsmen act on the heading error, its integral and its rate of change, and formulated what we now call three-term or PID control [22,25]. At Bell Telephone Laboratories, Harold Black invented the negative-feedback amplifier (published 1934), which traded gain for insensitivity to the drift of vacuum tubes [26]; Harry Nyquist's 1932 "Regeneration theory" gave the graphical stability criterion that bears his name [10]; and Hendrik Bode's 1945 book on feedback amplifier design introduced the gain and phase plots, margins and fundamental limits that still structure loop design [11]. Ziegler and Nichols published their empirical PID tuning rules in 1942 [27], wartime work on gun directors and radar created servomechanism theory, and Walter Evans introduced the root locus in 1948 [12]. Norbert Wiener's Cybernetics (1948) presented feedback as a principle shared by machines and animals [28].

2.3 The modern era: state space, optimality and robustness

Around 1960 the field changed character. Bellman's dynamic programming and Pontryagin's maximum principle gave general theories of optimal control [29,30]. Rudolf Kalman introduced the state-space description of linear systems, the concepts of controllability and observability, the linear quadratic regulator and, with Bucy, the Kalman filter [14,15,31,32]; Luenberger introduced state observers [33]. The Apollo guidance computer and aerospace applications made these methods practical [16,34]. By the late 1970s it was clear that optimal designs could be fragile: Doyle showed that linear-quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) controllers can have arbitrarily small stability margins [35], and an adaptive-control flight test ended in the 1967 loss of the X-15-3 [36]. Robust control (H∞ and μ-synthesis) [37–39] and rigorous adaptive control [40,41] followed. Model predictive control spread through the process industries from the late 1970s [42,43], and Lyapunov-based nonlinear methods matured in the 1980s and 1990s [24,44,45].

2.4 Robots and learning

Robot control developed alongside: resolved-rate control [46], force feedback and compliance [47–49], hybrid position/force control [50], impedance control [51], computed torque and the recursive Newton–Euler algorithm that made it real-time [52,53], adaptive manipulator control [54] and the operational space formulation [55]. Learning entered control early, through adaptive control, iterative learning control [56], neural network identification and control [57] and reinforcement learning on the cart-pole [58,59]. Since the mid-2010s, trajectory optimization and MPC have run in real time on humanoids and quadrupeds [6,9], while deep reinforcement learning in simulation has produced locomotion and flight controllers that rival or exceed hand-designed ones [7,60–62]. 3 PID control

3.1 The three terms

The proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller computes its command from the error e(t) = r(t) − y(t) between the reference r and the measured output y:

u(t) = Kp e(t) + Ki ∫0t e(τ) dτ + Kd de(t)/dt

The proportional term reacts to the present error; on its own it usually leaves a steady-state error when a constant load (gravity, friction, a pushing hand) must be resisted, because some error is needed to produce the holding force. The integral term accumulates past error and keeps growing until the error is zero, removing that offset. The derivative term reacts to the predicted future error and adds damping [1,71]. PID is by far the most widely used control algorithm: surveys of industrial practice report that the large majority of control loops are PID or PI loops [72,73]. In robots, PID loops regulate motor currents, joint velocities and positions, and they remain the interface that most higher-level controllers and learned policies command [7,74].

3.2 Practical PID

The textbook equation needs four modifications before it works on hardware [71,75]. First, pure differentiation amplifies measurement noise, so the derivative is low-pass filtered (a filter time constant of Td/N with N typically 5–20). Second, differentiating the error produces a "derivative kick" whenever the setpoint jumps, so the derivative is usually taken of the measurement only. Third, actuators saturate: when the motor is already at full voltage the integrator keeps accumulating error it cannot act on, then overshoots badly once the error changes sign. This integrator windup is prevented by anti-windup schemes such as conditional integration or back-calculation, which bleeds the integrator by the difference between the saturated and unsaturated commands [71,76]. Fourth, controllers run in discrete time, so the integral and derivative are discretized and the sampling period must be short compared with the closed-loop time constants [77]. Feedforward terms, such as gravity compensation or the torque needed to follow a planned acceleration, are added outside the PID terms so that feedback only has to correct what the model got wrong [5,74].

3.3 Tuning

Ziegler and Nichols' 1942 method raises a proportional gain until the loop oscillates steadily at the "ultimate" gain Ku with period Pu, then sets Kp = 0.6Ku, Ti = Pu/2, Td = Pu/8 [27]. The rules aim for quarter-amplitude decay, which gives aggressive tuning with large overshoot and small robustness margins; Åström and Hägglund showed how a relay experiment can find the same oscillation safely and developed tuning rules with explicit margin specifications, the basis of the auto-tuners in commercial controllers [71,78]. Model-based tuning chooses gains from an identified model to meet bandwidth and margin targets, and frequency-domain analysis checks the result: the gain margin is how much the loop gain can grow before instability and the phase margin how much extra lag, for example from a time delay, can be tolerated [1,13]. Time delays and unstable dynamics impose limits that no tuning can overcome [3].

4 State-space models and feedback

4.1 State, controllability and observability

Modern control describes a system by a state vector x that summarizes everything about the past needed to predict the future, an input u and an output y. For a linear time-invariant system,

ẋ = A x + B u, y = C x + D u,

with discrete-time counterpart xk+1 = A xk + B uk [1,14]. Robots are nonlinear, but linearizing their dynamics about an operating point (an upright balance pose, a hover, a nominal trajectory) yields such a model, valid nearby; the rigid-body dynamics that are linearized are derived in the physical modeling article [5,74]. Kalman introduced two structural properties [14]. A system is controllable if the input can steer the state from any initial value to any final value; for linear systems this holds if and only if the controllability matrix [B, AB, …, An−1B] has full rank n. It is observable if the state can be reconstructed from the output history, which holds if and only if [C; CA; …; CAn−1] has full rank. These tests answer design questions directly: whether an actuator suffices to stabilize a mechanism, and whether a sensor suffices to estimate it.

4.2 State feedback and pole placement

With the full state available, the control law u = −Kx gives closed-loop dynamics ẋ = (A − BK)x. If the system is controllable, the eigenvalues ("poles") of A − BK can be placed anywhere the designer chooses (with complex values in conjugate pairs) [79]; for a single input, Ackermann's formula gives the gain explicitly [80]. Pole locations set decay rates and damping, but choosing them is not obvious for many states and inputs, and fast poles demand large inputs and amplify noise; the linear quadratic regulator of Section 6 replaces pole choice with a cost trade-off [1,81].

4.3 Observers and the separation principle

Usually only some states are measured. A Luenberger observer runs a copy of the model corrected by the output error, x̂̇ = A x̂ + B u + L(y − C x̂), so that the estimation error obeys ė = (A − LC)e; if the system is observable, L can place the observer poles freely, by duality with state feedback [33,82]. The Kalman filter is the observer whose gain is optimal for Gaussian process and measurement noise, and its extended, unscented and invariant variants are the workhorses of robot state estimation; they are treated in detail in the sensor fusion article [31,32]. For linear systems, the separation principle states that the closed-loop poles of observer-based feedback u = −K x̂ are the union of the controller and observer poles, so the two can be designed independently; LQR combined with a Kalman filter is the LQG controller [1,81]. Separation guarantees stability of the nominal design, not robustness: Doyle's 1978 counterexample showed that LQG loops can have arbitrarily small margins, and loop transfer recovery and robust control were developed in response [35,83].

5 Linear and nonlinear control

5.1 When linear control is enough, and when it is not

Linear controllers are designed on a linearized model, so they are guaranteed to work only near the operating point. Many robot tasks stay close enough: a joint servo, a hovering drone or a balancing robot rarely leaves a small neighbourhood of its nominal state, and linear design with adequate margins is often the right choice [1,13]. Others do not. Robot arm dynamics contain configuration-dependent inertia, Coriolis and gravity terms; actuators saturate; friction and contact switch discontinuously; and swinging, jumping, walking and flying aggressively take the system far from any single equilibrium [74,84]. Nonlinear control theory provides tools for these cases, built on Lyapunov's idea: if a scalar "energy-like" function V(x) is positive away from the goal and decreases along every closed-loop trajectory, the goal is stable, and the region where this holds is a certified region of attraction [23,24,45].

5.2 Gain scheduling and feedback linearization

Gain scheduling designs linear controllers at several operating points and interpolates between them according to a scheduling variable such as speed or altitude; it is the traditional method for flight control and remains common because each local design can be checked with linear tools [85]. Feedback linearization instead cancels the nonlinearity: if the dynamics are ẋ = f(x) + g(x)u, a control u = g(x)−1(v − f(x)) turns the system into a linear one driven by a new input v, which is then controlled linearly [24,44]. For a fully actuated robot arm with dynamics M(q)q̈ + C(q,q̇)q̇ + g(q) = τ, this is the computed torque or inverse dynamics controller,

τ = M(q)[q̈d + Kd(q̇d − q̇) + Kp(qd − q)] + C(q,q̇)q̇ + g(q),

which makes every joint error behave like an independent, linearly damped spring [5,74]. Its cost is model dependence: errors in M, C and g appear as disturbances. The recursive Newton–Euler algorithm made the inverse dynamics computable in real time [52], and experiments in the 1980s showed that model-based feedforward and computed torque track markedly better than independent joint PD at speed [53]. A weaker but more robust result is that PD control plus gravity compensation stabilizes any rigid manipulator at a setpoint, because the closed loop is passive; this underlies most compliant robot controllers [74,86].

5.3 Lyapunov design, energy shaping and underactuated systems

Many robots are underactuated: they have fewer actuators than degrees of freedom, as with a cart-pole, an acrobot, a quadrotor, or any walking robot in flight or on one foot. Their dynamics cannot be cancelled and must be exploited [84,87]. Energy-based control is one classical approach: Åström and Furuta swung up a pendulum by regulating its total energy to that of the upright equilibrium, then switched to a linear balancing controller near the top [88], and Spong treated the acrobot similarly [87]. More generally, a control Lyapunov function is one that some admissible input can always decrease; Artstein and Sontag showed that its existence implies stabilizability and gave a universal formula for a stabilizing feedback [89,90]. Backstepping builds such functions recursively for systems in cascade form [91]. Sliding-mode control drives the state onto a surface on which the error decays, using a switching term large enough to dominate bounded uncertainty; it is robust but can chatter, so the switch is usually smoothed in a boundary layer [45,65].

Legged locomotion is a showcase for nonlinear and hybrid control. Walking alternates continuous swing phases with impacts, so it is a hybrid dynamical system [92]. Zero-moment-point (ZMP) methods keep the centre of pressure inside the support polygon, and Kajita and colleagues generated walking patterns by preview control of a linear inverted pendulum model [93,94]; hybrid zero dynamics designs virtual constraints whose restricted dynamics yield stable periodic gaits with provable properties [92]. Verification tools such as sums-of-squares programming can certify regions of attraction of nonlinear controllers, and LQR-trees combine many locally verified LQR controllers into a feedback policy that covers a large region [95].

6 Optimal control

6.1 Problem and principles

Optimal control chooses the input trajectory that minimizes a cost, typically

J = ℓf(x(T)) + ∫0T ℓ(x(t), u(t)) dt subject to ẋ = f(x, u), constraints on x and u,

and so turns design goals (reach the target, use little energy, avoid limits) into a mathematical problem [96,97]. Two principles underlie the theory. Bellman's principle of optimality states that the tail of an optimal trajectory is optimal for the remaining problem, which leads to dynamic programming and, in continuous time, the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman partial differential equation for the optimal cost-to-go V(x,t); its minimizer gives an optimal feedback law [29,97]. Pontryagin's maximum principle gives necessary conditions for an optimal trajectory through a costate (adjoint) variable, yielding a two-point boundary value problem; it explains bang-bang solutions of minimum-time problems with bounded inputs [30,96]. Dynamic programming suffers from the curse of dimensionality, which is one motivation for the approximate methods of reinforcement learning (see the reinforcement learning article) [17,98]. Optimal feedback control has also been proposed as a theory of human motor coordination [99].

6.2 The linear quadratic regulator

For linear dynamics and quadratic cost Σ xkTQxk + ukTRuk, the optimal cost-to-go is quadratic, V(x) = xTPx, and the optimal control is linear state feedback u = −Kx with

K = (R + BTPB)−1BTPA, P = Q + ATPA − ATPB(R + BTPB)−1BTPA,

the discrete algebraic Riccati equation [15,81]. The weights Q and R express the trade-off between error and effort more intuitively than pole locations, and the continuous-time LQR with full state feedback has guaranteed robustness: a gain margin from one-half to infinity and at least 60° of phase margin in each input channel [81,83]. Kalman also asked the inverse question, which feedback laws are optimal for some quadratic cost, an early form of inverse optimal control [100]. The LQR is the benchmark problem for learning-based control, where sample-complexity results compare model-based and model-free methods (see the reinforcement learning article) [101,102].

6.3 Trajectory optimization

For nonlinear systems, optimal control is usually solved numerically along a single trajectory [103]. Indirect methods solve Pontryagin's conditions; direct methods discretize the problem into a finite nonlinear program. Direct collocation represents states and inputs at knot points and imposes the dynamics as constraints [104]; multiple shooting integrates the dynamics over short intervals and enforces continuity between them [105]. Differential dynamic programming (DDP), introduced by Mayne in 1966, instead iterates a backward pass that builds a local quadratic model of the cost-to-go along the current trajectory and a forward pass that applies the resulting improved feedback law [63,106]. Its Gauss–Newton variant, the iterative LQR (iLQR), drops second derivatives of the dynamics [107], and box-constrained DDP handles input limits [108]. These methods produce, at no extra cost, time-varying LQR gains that stabilize the system around the optimal trajectory. Fast implementations made online trajectory optimization of humanoids feasible [109], and contact-implicit formulations even let the optimizer choose when contacts are made and broken [110]. Libraries such as Crocoddyl, Drake and CasADi provide these solvers for robots [111–113].

7 Model predictive control

7.1 Receding-horizon control

Model predictive control (MPC) solves a finite-horizon optimal control problem at every sampling instant, starting from the current measured or estimated state, applies only the first input of the optimal sequence, and repeats at the next sample [114,115]:

minu0..N−1 Σk=0N−1 ℓ(xk, uk) + Vf(xN) s.t. xk+1 = f(xk, uk), x0 = x̂(t), xk ∈ 𝒳, uk ∈ 𝒰, xN ∈ 𝒳f

Re-solving from the measured state turns open-loop optimization into feedback. MPC's distinctive strength is that it handles constraints explicitly: actuator limits, joint limits, friction cones, obstacle and safety-margin constraints are written into the problem rather than enforced by clipping or tuning, and the controller anticipates them, slowing down before it reaches a limit rather than hitting it [114,116]. It also handles multivariable coupling and preview of future references naturally.

MPC originated in industry. Richalet and colleagues reported "model predictive heuristic control" in 1978 [42], and Shell's dynamic matrix control followed; by the 2000s thousands of MPC applications were running in refineries and chemical plants, where slow dynamics left time for optimization [43,117]. The theory caught up later: with a terminal cost Vf that is a control Lyapunov function and a terminal constraint set 𝒳f that is invariant under a local controller, the optimal cost decreases along closed-loop trajectories and MPC is stabilizing and recursively feasible [66,114]. A common choice, used in the example below, is the LQR cost-to-go xTPx as terminal cost. Robust variants handle bounded disturbances, for example tube MPC, which plans a nominal trajectory with tightened constraints and keeps the true state in a tube around it with an ancillary feedback law [118].

7.2 Solving fast enough

For linear models, quadratic costs and polyhedral constraints, each MPC step is a convex quadratic program (QP). Explicit MPC precomputes the solution as a piecewise-affine function of the state, so that online evaluation is a table lookup, practical for small systems [119]. Online solvers include active-set methods with warm starting such as qpOASES [120], interior-point methods, and first-order splitting methods such as the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) [121] used by OSQP, which factorizes one matrix and then iterates cheap projections [68]. Nonlinear MPC solves a nonlinear program, typically by sequential quadratic programming with multiple shooting; the real-time iteration scheme performs a single SQP step per sample, warm-started from the previous solution, so that the solution improves continually while the system moves [122]. Code-generation frameworks such as acados and modelling tools such as CasADi produce solvers that run at kilohertz rates on embedded processors [69,113]. Sampling-based MPC such as model predictive path integral control trades optimality guarantees for the ability to use arbitrary, even learned or non-differentiable models; it is discussed in the world models article [123,124].

7.3 MPC in robots

MPC has become a standard tool for agile robots. In automated driving it is used for path tracking with tyre and actuator limits [125]. In legged locomotion, Wieber's linear MPC of the centre of mass made humanoid walking robust to strong pushes [126]; the MIT Cheetah 3 computes ground reaction forces with a convex MPC over a simplified single-rigid-body model [9]; and whole-body nonlinear MPC through contacts has run on quadrupeds [127]. The Atlas humanoid at the DARPA Robotics Challenge combined trajectory optimization, a time-varying LQR around planned centre-of-mass motion and a QP-based whole-body controller [6]. In drone racing, a comparison of MPC with RL on the same quadrotor found that RL policies could exploit the platform's limits more fully, mainly because RL can optimize the task-level objective directly rather than tracking a precomputed reference [62]. MPC remains attractive where an explicit model and hard constraints are needed and trustworthy.

8 Adaptive and robust control

Every controller is designed with a model that is wrong. Payloads change the inertia of an arm, batteries drain, joints wear, friction varies with temperature, and contact surfaces differ. There are two classical responses: robust control designs a fixed controller that works for every system in a specified uncertainty set, while adaptive control adjusts the controller online as it learns about the system [128,129].

8.1 Robust control

Classical gain and phase margins are a simple form of robustness. Zames' small-gain theorem showed that a feedback interconnection of stable systems is stable if the product of their gains is less than one, which allows uncertainty to be represented as an unknown but norm-bounded system in the loop [130]. Zames then posed the problem of minimizing the worst-case (H∞) gain from disturbances to errors [37], and Doyle, Glover, Khargonekar and Francis solved it with two Riccati equations, making H∞ synthesis as practical as LQG [38]. The structured singular value μ handles uncertainty with known structure, such as independent uncertain parameters [39]. Fundamental limits apply whatever the method: Bode's sensitivity integral implies that pushing sensitivity down at some frequencies raises it at others, and unstable poles and time delays make the trade-off worse [3,11]. In robot motion control, disturbance observers estimate the lumped effect of model error and external forces from the difference between commanded and observed motion and cancel it, a simple and widely used robust technique [131]. Sliding-mode control (Section 5) and tube MPC (Section 7) are robust nonlinear methods.

8.2 Adaptive control

Adaptive control began with aircraft autopilots in the 1950s. Model reference adaptive control (MRAC) adjusts controller parameters so that the closed loop follows a reference model; the original "MIT rule" adjusted them by gradient descent on the squared tracking error [132], which could become unstable, and Parks redesigned the adaptation law from a Lyapunov function so that stability was guaranteed [133]. Åström and Wittenmark's self-tuning regulator combined online least-squares identification with a control design based on the current estimates [64]. The fatal crash of the X-15-3 in 1967, which flew with an adaptive flight control system, and the 1980s discovery that adaptive laws could be destabilized by unmodelled high-frequency dynamics and small disturbances, led to robust modifications such as parameter projection, dead zones and σ-modification [36,40,41]. L1 adaptive control decouples fast adaptation from robustness with a low-pass filtered control law [134].

For robot manipulators, Slotine and Li exploited the fact that the dynamics are linear in the inertial parameters, M(q)q̈ + C(q,q̇)q̇ + g(q) = Y(q,q̇,q̈)a, to design a controller τ = Y â − KDs with adaptation law â̇ = −ΓYTs, where s is a sliding variable combining position and velocity error; a Lyapunov argument proves that tracking errors converge to zero for any bounded parameter error [54]. Physically consistent parameter identification and adaptive control remain active topics (see the system identification section of the physical modeling article) [135]. Learned adaptation has revived the idea: rapid motor adaptation trains a network to infer environment parameters from recent history, and Neural-Fly adapts the last layer of a learned aerodynamic model online with a provably stable adaptation law [136,137].

9 Impedance and force control

9.1 Why position control fails at contact

Position control is designed to reject disturbances, and to a stiff position controller a contact force is just another disturbance. When a stiff robot touches a stiff environment, small position errors (a misplaced part, a calibration error of a millimetre) become very large forces, and the impact itself can bounce or damage the robot, the part or a person [138,139]. Manipulation, assembly, polishing, walking and any physical interaction with people therefore need controllers that regulate force or the relationship between force and motion. Force sensing is covered in the sensors article [140].

9.2 Approaches

Early work used force feedback to correct manipulator motion [47] and active stiffness control, which makes the end effector behave like a programmable spring in Cartesian space [49]. Mason formalized compliant tasks by natural and artificial constraints: in each direction of a task frame, the environment constrains either position or force, and the controller should control the other [48]. Raibert and Craig's hybrid position/force control implemented this with a selection matrix that assigns each task-space direction to a position or a force loop [50].

Hogan's impedance control (1985) took a different view: since the robot cannot control force and motion independently in the same direction, it should control the dynamic relationship between them, making the end effector behave like a chosen mass-spring-damper, Mdẍ̃ + Ddx̃̇ + Kdx̃ = Fext, with x̃ the deviation from a virtual equilibrium trajectory [51]. On a torque-controlled robot, the simplest impedance controller is task-space PD plus gravity compensation, which needs no force sensor. Admittance control is the dual implementation for stiff, position-controlled industrial robots: a force/torque sensor measures the interaction force, a virtual mass-spring-damper turns it into a reference motion, and the existing position loop tracks that reference [139]. Impedance control suits stiff environments and low-friction, backdrivable or torque-sensing hardware; admittance control suits soft environments and high-gain position-controlled hardware. Khatib's operational space formulation provided the task-space dynamics needed to control end-effector motion and force consistently, and extended them to redundant robots with null-space control of secondary tasks [55].

Contact stability is subtle. Colgate and Hogan showed that a robot is guaranteed to remain stable when coupled to any passive environment only if its own port impedance is passive [141], which explains why delays, sensor-actuator non-collocation and high force-feedback gains cause chatter at contact. Hardware has evolved to help: series elastic actuators place a spring between motor and load and control force through its deflection [142]; the DLR and KUKA lightweight arms measure joint torques and use passivity-based impedance control [143]; and quasi-direct-drive "proprioceptive" actuators in legged robots are transparent enough to sense and control contact forces through motor current alone [144]. Torque-controlled robots also detect collisions from the residual between expected and measured torques [145], and collaborative robots are designed against biomechanical force and pressure limits for contact with people [146].

In whole-body control of humanoids and quadrupeds, contact forces are decision variables: a QP at each control cycle computes joint torques and contact forces that realize prioritized task-space accelerations while respecting friction cones and torque limits [6,147,148]. Learning has also entered here: variable impedance can be learned by reinforcement learning [149], and an impedance controller in end-effector space has been shown to be a better action space for learned contact-rich manipulation than joint torques or positions [150].

10 Learning-based control

Learning enters control in several distinct places: it can learn a model that a conventional controller then uses, tune or adapt a controller's parameters, learn feedforward signals from repetition, learn the controller itself, or learn certificates of stability and safety [17–19]. Reinforcement learning, which learns policies from rewards, is covered in depth in the reinforcement learning article and imitation learning in the imitation learning article; this section concentrates on learning inside a control-theoretic framework.

10.1 Learning models for control

The most direct route is to learn or improve the dynamics model. Narendra and Parthasarathy proposed neural networks for the identification and control of nonlinear dynamical systems in 1990 [57]; locally weighted regression and Gaussian processes became popular for learning robot inverse dynamics, because they learn quickly and fit online [151,152]. Learned models combine naturally with MPC. Learning-based MPC separates a nominal model, used for safety guarantees, from a learned model used for performance [153]; Gaussian-process MPC propagates the learned model's uncertainty into tightened constraints [154] and has been used to race autonomous cars at the limits of tyre friction [155]. Learned residual dynamics added to a nominal model enabled close-to-ground drone landing with stability guarantees and agile flight in strong winds [137,156]. Koopman-operator methods lift nonlinear dynamics into a higher-dimensional space where they are approximately linear, so that linear MPC can be applied [157–159], and data-enabled predictive control skips the model altogether, predicting directly from a matrix of past input-output data using Willems' fundamental lemma [160,161]. PILCO-style policy search with probabilistic models and differentiable MPC layers inside neural networks connect these ideas to reinforcement learning [162,163].

10.2 Learning from repetition and tuning controllers from data

Iterative learning control (ILC), introduced by Arimoto, Kawamura and Miyazaki in 1984, applies to systems that repeat the same task: after each trial, the feedforward signal is corrected with the tracking error of the previous trial, uj+1(t) = Q[uj(t) + L ej(t + d)], so that repeating errors are learned away [56,164]. The learning filter L and lead d determine convergence, and a low-pass Q filter trades perfect tracking for robustness to noise and non-repeating effects [164]. ILC is used in robots, printers, wafer stages and other repetitive machines, and has given quadrotors precise tracking of aggressive trajectories after a few trials [165]. Controller parameters can also be tuned from data directly: safe Bayesian optimization tunes the gains of a quadrotor controller with a Gaussian-process model of performance while never trying gains predicted to be unsafe [166].

10.3 Learning controllers and certificates

When the controller itself is learned, control theory supplies tools to check it. Learned policies can be verified or trained together with Lyapunov functions, for example by expanding a region of attraction certified by a Gaussian-process model during safe model-based RL [167], or by training a neural network Lyapunov function alongside the controller [168]. For the LQR, sample-complexity analyses quantify how much data model-based and model-free methods need to reach a given suboptimality, and show the benefit of robust synthesis with estimated error bounds [101,102]. Deep RL policies for legged robots and drones are now trained in simulation with domain randomization and adaptation (see the sim-to-real section of the reinforcement learning article) [7,60,61]; learned controllers have also stabilized tokamak plasmas [169].

11 Hybrid systems: learned policies with conventional controllers

The most capable physical AI systems today are neither purely learned nor purely model-based. Learned components bring perception, generality and the ability to optimize hard-to-model objectives; conventional controllers bring stability, constraint satisfaction, interpretability and decades of engineering practice. They are combined in a few recurring patterns (Table 2).

11.1 Patterns of combination

Learned policies on top of servo loops. Almost every learned robot policy outputs references for conventional low-level controllers rather than raw motor currents. The ANYmal locomotion policies output joint position targets tracked by joint PD loops at a higher rate, with a learned actuator network modelling the drives in simulation [7,60]; manipulation policies learned from demonstrations output action chunks of end-effector or joint targets, executed by position or impedance controllers [170,171]; and vision-language-action models generate continuous actions at tens of hertz for low-level controllers to track (see the foundation models article) [8,172]. Choosing the action space, for example impedance targets rather than torques, is itself a control design decision that strongly affects learning [150].

Residual learning. A conventional controller provides a reasonable baseline and a learned component adds a correction: residual reinforcement learning adds a learned policy output to a hand-designed controller for contact-rich assembly [173,174], and residual model learning corrects a nominal dynamics model inside a model-based controller [155,156]. The baseline makes learning faster and safer, because the system behaves reasonably from the start.

Learning and optimization in a hierarchy. Learned modules can choose what an optimal controller should do, and the controller ensures it is done dynamically consistently. In legged locomotion, RLOC uses an RL policy to plan footholds that model-based control executes [175], while deep tracking control trains a policy to track the trajectories of a model-based optimizer, combining the optimizer's planning with the learned policy's robustness to slips and terrain [70]. Learned value functions can serve as terminal costs for short-horizon MPC [124], and higher still, language-model planners select skills that conventional or learned controllers execute (see the task planning article) [176].

Safety filters and supervisors. A safety layer monitors the learned policy and overrides it only when necessary. The Simplex architecture runs a high-performance but unverified controller alongside a simple verified one and a decision module that switches to the verified controller when the state approaches the boundary of a region it can recover from [177,178]. Control barrier functions minimally modify a nominal action in a QP so that a safe set stays forward-invariant [67,179]; predictive safety filters use MPC to check that a safe backup trajectory always exists [180]; and Hamilton–Jacobi reachability computes the set of states from which safety can be maintained under worst-case disturbances [181–183]. These methods are surveyed in a unified view by Hsu and colleagues and discussed from the learning side in the safe RL section of the reinforcement learning article [19,184].

12 Practice, tools and open problems

12.1 Implementation issues

Most control failures on real robots come from issues that are absent from textbook models [1,77]. Sampling and delay: a discrete controller and its sensors, filters and communication add latency, which costs phase margin and limits achievable bandwidth, so loop rates are chosen several times faster than the desired closed-loop bandwidth [3,77]. Saturation: actuators and amplifiers have limits, so anti-windup, reference shaping or explicit constraints (MPC) are needed [76,116]. Friction, backlash and compliance in gears and belts introduce nonlinearities and resonances that limit gains, especially in force control [139,185]. Noise: derivative and high-gain terms amplify sensor noise, so estimation and filtering are designed together with control [1]. Cascades: robot drives are usually nested loops, with a fast current loop inside a velocity loop inside a position loop, each tuned several times faster than the one outside it [4,74]. Hardware such as torque-transparent actuators and high-rate force sensing changes which controllers are feasible at all [144,186].

12.2 Tools

Control design and analysis libraries include the Python Control Systems Library [187]; optimization frameworks such as CasADi, acados and OSQP generate fast solvers for MPC [68,69,113]; rigid-body dynamics libraries such as Pinocchio compute dynamics and derivatives for optimal control [188]; Crocoddyl and Drake provide trajectory optimization and model-based design and verification for robots [111,112]; and MuJoCo was designed for model-based control as well as simulation [189]. On the robot, ros_control (ros2_control in ROS 2) standardizes the interface between controllers and hardware [190].

12.4 Open problems

  • Guarantees for learned controllers. Certificates for neural policies operating from high-dimensional perception, under distribution shift, remain limited to small systems or conservative filters [19,168,184].
  • Contact-rich control. Optimizing through making and breaking contact reliably and in real time is still difficult, for both model-based and learned controllers [110,111,139].
  • Adaptation without excitation. Robots must adapt to new payloads, terrains and wear quickly and safely, often without the rich excitation that classical identification assumes [129,136,137].
  • Interfaces between layers. How foundation-model planners, learned policies and conventional controllers should exchange goals, constraints and uncertainty is largely an engineering art [8,18,176].
  • Benchmarks and comparison. Careful comparisons of model-based and learned control on the same hardware are rare and highly informative [17,62].

Conclusion

Control is where physical AI meets physics. Its classical core, feedback with enough gain to reject disturbances but not so much that it destabilizes, still runs in every motor loop, usually as PID. State-space methods made multivariable systems, observers and optimal control tractable; nonlinear and Lyapunov methods handle large motions and underactuation; trajectory optimization and model predictive control plan dynamically feasible motions and respect constraints in real time; robust and adaptive control deal with model error; and impedance and force control make contact safe and useful. Learning now supplies models, feedforward, tuning and entire policies, and the most effective systems combine learned components with conventional controllers that keep them stable, constrained and safe. The examples in this article show the recurring lessons in miniature: integral action needs anti-windup; observability decides what a sensor can tell you; linear designs fail far from their operating point; optimal trajectories need feedback; constraints should be predicted, not clipped; adaptation can track without identifying; compliance beats stiffness at contact; repetition can be learned; and a verified fallback lets a learned policy be used where it is good and overruled where it is not.
