1 What reactive behavior is, and where it sits in physical AI

A robot acts on predictions: a map of where things are, a model of how its body moves, a plan for what to do next. In the physical world those predictions are always somewhat wrong. A person steps into the path, a gust pushes a drone sideways, a cup turns out to be heavier and more slippery than it looked, a door that should be open is closed, a sensor fails. Reactive behavior is the part of a robot's intelligence that responds to such events directly from current sensor readings, quickly enough to matter, without waiting for a deliberative process to rebuild its world model and think again [1,2]. The term covers a spectrum. At one end are reflexes: fixed, fast mappings from a stimulus to a response, such as stopping when a bumper closes or squeezing harder when a grasped object starts to slip. In the middle are feedback controllers that continuously cancel disturbances and short-horizon planners that re-solve a local problem many times per second. At the other end is replanning: revising a plan when an observation shows that its assumptions no longer hold [1,3,4].

What unites these behaviors is a time budget. A walking robot that is pushed has a few hundred milliseconds to place a foot before it falls; an arm that touches a person must react within milliseconds to keep the contact force low; a car at highway speed covers a metre every 30–40 ms [5–7]. Deliberation, whether symbolic task planning, global motion planning or inference in a large vision-language-action model, usually takes tens of milliseconds to seconds, so it cannot be the only source of behavior. Biology solved the same problem with a hierarchy of loops: spinal reflexes respond within tens of milliseconds, brainstem and cerebellar circuits coordinate posture and balance, and the cortex plans [8–10]. Robots are built in the same layered way (Figure 1). Reactive layers run close to the sensors and actuators at high rates and act on local information; deliberative layers run more slowly over richer models; and the interfaces between them decide which layer is in charge at each moment [11–13].

The user-facing topics of this article map onto those layers. Reflexive responses to sensor readings (Section 3) are the basic stimulus–response mechanisms and the ways of arbitrating between them. Real-time collision avoidance (Section 4) covers both avoiding contact before it happens and detecting and reacting to it when it does. Disturbance rejection (Section 5) concerns forces the robot cannot avoid, from load torques on a joint to pushes on a humanoid. Grasp correction and slip detection (Section 6) applies the same ideas at the fingertips. Replanning after unexpected events (Section 7) is reactivity at the level of plans, and safe stopping and recovery (Section 8) is what a robot does when nothing else works. Section 9 discusses how these pieces are combined with deliberation and with learned policies, and Section 10 covers practice, evaluation and open problems.

2 A short history: from reflex arcs to safety filters

2.1 Reflexes in animals and the first reactive machines

The physiologist Charles Sherrington's 1906 lectures on The Integrative Action of the Nervous System established the reflex arc, a chain from receptor through the spinal cord to muscle, as a unit of analysis, and showed how reflexes are combined, inhibited and ordered by priority to produce coordinated behavior [8]. Later work showed that much "automatic" behavior is not a fixed reflex but a fast, adaptive feedback response: people standing on a moving platform recover balance with characteristic ankle or hip strategies depending on the support surface [10], and people holding an object adjust grip force to its friction within a fraction of a second of contact [16,17]. Both observations later shaped robot balance and grasp controllers.

The first reactive robots were built soon after the first electronic computers. W. Grey Walter's "tortoises" of the late 1940s used two vacuum-tube "neurons", a light sensor and a bump contact to seek moderate light, avoid obstacles and return to a recharging hutch; Walter emphasized how rich behavior emerged from very simple circuits interacting with the environment [18]. Valentino Braitenberg's thought experiment Vehicles (1984) made the same point with imaginary vehicles whose sensors are wired directly to their wheels: crossing or uncrossing two wires turns a light-avoiding vehicle into a light-attacking one, behaviour an observer is tempted to describe as fear or aggression [19].

2.2 The behavior-based revolution

Mainstream robotics in the 1970s followed the sense–model–plan–act cycle of SRI's Shakey, which built a symbolic world model and planned before every action and therefore moved slowly and brittlely [20]. In 1986 Rodney Brooks proposed the subsumption architecture: a robot built from layers of simple, concurrently running behaviors, each connecting sensors to actuators, with higher layers able to suppress the inputs and inhibit the outputs of lower ones [13]. Brooks argued that "the world is its own best model" and that intelligence could be built incrementally from situated, embodied behavior rather than from central representations [21,22]. In parallel, Agre and Chapman's Pengi played a video game with routines driven by the immediate situation [23], Arkin's motor schemas blended behaviors by summing their output vectors [24], Maes studied action selection among competing goals [25], and Rosenblatt's DAMN let behaviors vote over candidate commands [26]. Arkin's book Behavior-Based Robotics (1998) summarized the decade [1].

Purely reactive systems proved hard to scale to tasks that need memory and foresight, and by the late 1980s hybrid architectures had appeared that put a reactive layer under a deliberative one. Firby's reactive action packages and Georgeff and Lansky's procedural reasoning system executed plans by selecting context-appropriate methods at run time [27,28], and the three-layer architectures of the 1990s, such as 3T and ATLANTIS, separated a fast reactive controller, a sequencer and a slow deliberator [11,12]. This layering remains the norm: modern behavior trees, which generalize subsumption and sequential composition, are its sequencing layer [29,30], and the reactive layer is now usually a set of controllers and filters rather than hand-wired behaviors.

2.3 Fast feedback for contact, balance and motion

A second line of work came from control. Khatib's 1986 potential-field method computed avoidance forces for manipulators in real time from distances to obstacles [31], and Borenstein and Koren's vector field histogram and Fox, Burgard and Thrun's dynamic window approach made reactive navigation practical on mobile robots [32,33]. Raibert's hopping and running machines of the 1980s balanced through simple feedback laws for foot placement, body attitude and hopping height [34]. In motion control, Ohnishi's disturbance observer (1987) estimated and cancelled disturbance torques in servo drives [35,36]. Stentz's D* (1994) let robots replan efficiently as their maps changed, and its descendants were used on planetary rovers and autonomous cars [37–40]. In tactile manipulation, Howe and Cutkosky detected slip from skin vibrations and Tremblay and Cutkosky detected incipient slip before it became gross sliding [41,42].

2.4 Safety, sharing space and learning

From the 2000s, robots began to share space with people, and reactive safety became a design discipline. Lightweight torque-controlled arms such as the DLR-III used model-based observers to detect collisions without extra sensors and react within milliseconds [6,43], crash tests quantified what contact forces and speeds were tolerable [44], and standards defined collaborative operation, speed and separation monitoring and power and force limiting [45,46]. Legged robots gained principled push recovery through the capture point and capturability [5,47]. Control barrier functions gave a general way to filter any nominal command into a provably safe one [48,49]. Since about 2019, learned locomotion policies have shown remarkable robustness to pushes, slips and terrain, learned in simulation and executed reactively on hardware [50–52], and modern robot foundation models are often paired with fast low-level controllers and safety filters that handle what the slower model cannot [53–55]. 3 Reflexive responses to sensor readings

3.1 Stimulus, response and latency

A reflex in a robot is a mapping from a sensed condition to an action that runs without consulting a world model or a plan. Examples are everywhere in deployed systems: a mobile base stops when its bumper or a safety laser scanner's protective field is triggered; a robot vacuum backs off and turns when a cliff sensor sees no floor; a drone climbs when its downward range sensor reads too short a distance; a gripper closes when its fingertip proximity sensors detect an object between the fingers; a joint switches to compliant mode when its torque exceeds a limit [58–60]. Reflexes have three attractive properties. They are fast, because the computation is trivial and can run in firmware or on a safety controller close to the sensor. They are robust, because they depend on one measurement rather than on a chain of perception and estimation. And they are easy to verify, because their input–output behavior can be stated and tested exhaustively [1,61].

The most important number for a reflex is its latency: the time from the physical event to the start of the response. It is the sum of sensing time (exposure, integration or scan period), transport and processing time, the period of the control loop that consumes the measurement, and the actuator's response time. During that interval the robot keeps moving at its current velocity, so a reflex's useful range must exceed the distance travelled during the latency plus the braking distance. Real-time systems engineering is largely about making these delays small and, just as important, bounded: a loop that usually runs in 1 ms but occasionally takes 50 ms has an effective latency of 50 ms [62,63]. Event cameras, which report brightness changes asynchronously with microsecond resolution, show how far latency can be cut: a quadrotor using them detected and dodged objects thrown at it with a total perception latency of a few milliseconds [57,64].

3.2 Combining behaviors: arbitration and fusion

A robot has many reflexes and many goals, and they often conflict: seeking a goal says "go forward", avoiding an obstacle says "turn left". The behavior-based literature distinguishes two families of coordination mechanisms [1,13,24,26]:

  • Arbitration (competitive coordination) selects one behavior at a time. In subsumption, a fixed priority network lets one layer suppress or inhibit another; in state-based arbitration, the active behavior depends on a discrete mode; in winner-take-all schemes, behaviors bid by activation level [13,25]. Arbitration keeps each behavior's output intact, which is valuable for safety reflexes whose response must not be diluted, but switching can cause dithering when two behaviors alternate.
  • Fusion (cooperative coordination) blends the outputs. Motor schemas sum weighted vectors, much like Khatib's potential fields; voting schemes such as DAMN let behaviors rate candidate commands and pick the best compromise; fuzzy controllers blend rule outputs [24,26,31]. Fusion produces smooth motion but inherits the classic weakness of potential fields: vectors can cancel and leave the robot stuck in a local minimum, as analysed in the motion planning article [65,66].

Modern systems use both. Safety reflexes such as protective stops are arbitrated at the highest priority, often in separate certified hardware, while performance behaviors are fused, frequently as cost terms or constraints in an optimization. Hierarchical quadratic programs, for example, solve several objectives in strict priority order, so that collision avoidance and joint limits always win over reaching while lower-priority tasks are still satisfied as far as possible [67,68]. Riemannian motion policies and geometric fabrics fuse reactive behaviors such as reaching, obstacle avoidance and joint-limit avoidance by weighting each by a state-dependent metric that expresses in which directions it cares, giving smooth combination with stability guarantees [69–71].

Not all reflexes are of the stimulus–response type. Animals generate rhythmic movements such as walking and swimming with central pattern generators, neural oscillators that run without sensory input but are entrained and reset by it, for example lengthening a stance phase or triggering a stumble-correction response when a foot hits an obstacle [72]. Robot locomotion controllers built on oscillators use sensory feedback in the same way, as phase resets and reflex-like modulations superimposed on a rhythm [72].

4 Real-time collision avoidance and collision reaction

Collision handling has two halves. Avoidance keeps the robot from touching what it should not touch, using distance information from range sensors, depth cameras or a world model; it overlaps with motion planning, and the global methods (configuration space, sampling and optimization planners) and the classical local methods (potential fields, vector field histograms, the dynamic window approach, velocity obstacles and reciprocal avoidance) are treated in the collision avoidance and dynamic environments sections of the motion planning article [31–33,73,74]. Reaction deals with contacts that happen anyway, because something was not seen, moved unexpectedly or was deliberately touched; it is the domain of collision detection, isolation and reaction strategies [15]. This section focuses on what both halves look like at the reactive layer.

4.1 Reactive avoidance for manipulators and mobile robots

For manipulators working near people, the reactive layer must compute distances between the moving body and obstacles every control cycle. Flacco and colleagues computed robot-to-obstacle distances directly in the depth image of a camera, avoiding a full 3D reconstruction, and used them to generate repulsive velocities in real time [75]. Elastic bands and elastic strips deform a planned path or a task-consistent trajectory continuously under repulsive forces, so the plan "gives way" around moving obstacles while staying connected to the goal [76,77]. Dynamical-system approaches encode a motion as a vector field and modulate it near obstacles; Khansari-Zadeh and Billard showed that a suitable modulation keeps the robot out of convex obstacles while still converging to the target [78,79]. Riemannian motion policies and geometric fabrics, introduced above, are used for reactive reaching among obstacles on industrial and research arms [70,71], and Kappler and colleagues demonstrated a full system in which real-time perception feeds reactive motion generation that adapts to moving objects and people [80]. Any of these must hand its output to a trajectory generator that respects velocity, acceleration and jerk limits; Kröger and Wahl's online trajectory generation computes such a trajectory from an arbitrary current state within one control cycle, which makes "instantaneous" reactions to sensor events smooth and executable [56].

Mobile robots and vehicles use the same ideas with more emphasis on dynamics. A vehicle cannot stop instantly, so a reactive layer must avoid not only colliding states but inevitable collision states, from which every future trajectory collides [81,82]. Automotive autonomous emergency braking (AEB) is a mass-produced reflex of exactly this kind: radar or camera measurements of the time to collision trigger full braking when the driver does not react. Crash data from the United States showed that vehicles with forward collision warning plus AEB had about half the rate of front-to-rear crashes of vehicles without it [83]. Formal versions of the same idea define a safe longitudinal and lateral distance from the response times and braking capabilities of the vehicles involved and command a "proper response" whenever it is violated [7].

4.2 Safety filters: barrier functions and speed and separation monitoring

A clean way to make any controller reactive to obstacles is to wrap it in a safety filter. A control barrier function (CBF) h(x) is positive in the safe set (for example, squared distance minus squared clearance), and the filter solves, at each time step, a small quadratic program that changes the nominal command unom as little as possible subject to ḣ(x,u) ≥ −α h(x). The constraint lets the robot approach the boundary of the safe set only ever more slowly, so the set is forward invariant: a robot that starts safe stays safe, provided the constraint remains feasible under the actuator limits [48,49]. Singletary and colleagues compared CBFs with artificial potential fields for obstacle avoidance and showed that potential fields can be viewed as a special, more conservative case [84]. Predictive safety filters and Hamilton–Jacobi reachability generalize the idea to longer horizons and worst-case disturbances, and a unified view of these filters, with their use around learned policies, is given by Hsu and colleagues [55,85,86]; the safe RL and hybrid control sections of the series discuss them as supervisors for learned controllers.

In industrial human–robot collaboration, the same idea appears as speed and separation monitoring (SSM). ISO/TS 15066 describes four collaborative operating modes: safety-rated monitored stop, hand guiding, SSM and power and force limiting [45]. In SSM the robot's speed is limited so that it can always come to a stop before a person who is walking toward it at a standard approach speed can reach it; the protective separation distance adds up the distance the person covers during the robot's reaction and stopping time, the distance the robot covers during its reaction time, the robot's stopping distance, an intrusion distance and measurement uncertainties [45,87]. Section 8 computes it. SSM assigns responsibility clearly (the robot must be stationary at contact) but is conservative, which is why reactive avoidance methods that steer around people are an active research topic [88,89].

4.4 Collision detection, isolation and reaction

When contact happens, a robot must notice it, find where it is and respond. Haddadin, De Luca and Albu-Schäffer organized this into a collision event pipeline: pre-collision (avoidance), detection, isolation (which link), identification (the external force), classification (intended or accidental) and reaction [15]. Detection can use force/torque or tactile skins, but the most widely used method needs no extra sensor. The generalized momentum observer, introduced by De Luca and colleagues for actuator fault detection and applied to collisions on the DLR lightweight arms, compares the robot's generalized momentum p = M(q) q̇ with what the commanded torques and the model predict [43,90]. Its residual

r = KO ( p(t) − ∫0t ( τ + CT(q,q̇) q̇ − g(q) + r ) ds − p(0) )

obeys ṙ = KO(τext − r), so it is a first-order low-pass filtered estimate of the external joint torque. It needs only joint positions, velocities (no accelerations) and commanded torques. Because a contact on link i produces torques only at joints 1 to i, the last nonzero component of r isolates the link [15,43].

Reaction strategies range from simply stopping, through switching to a zero-gravity torque mode in which the robot "floats" away when pushed, to reflex strategies that command a torque proportional to the residual so that the robot actively moves in the direction of the external force, and admittance-based retraction [6,43]. Crash tests with the DLR arm showed why the reaction matters: the peak force of an unconstrained impact is governed mainly by the robot's speed and effective mass and is over before any controller can respond, but if a body part is clamped between the robot and an obstacle, a robot that keeps pushing produces dangerous forces, which a fast reaction prevents [44]. This is why ISO/TS 15066 limits both transient and quasi-static contact forces for power and force limited robots [45].

5 Disturbance rejection and push recovery

Some forces cannot be avoided: payloads change, joints have friction, wind gusts hit drones, people bump into robots, and terrain gives way under feet. Disturbance rejection is the ability to keep doing the task despite them. Feedback is the basic mechanism, and the general theory of loop gain, robustness and adaptation is covered in the PID and adaptive and robust control sections of the control article [92]. This section concentrates on the reactive techniques that estimate a disturbance as it happens and cancel it, and on the special case of legged robots, where rejecting a large disturbance may require a discrete action such as taking a step.

5.1 Feedback, internal models and disturbance observers

A feedback controller rejects a disturbance only after it has caused an error, and how well it does so depends on its gain at the disturbance's frequencies. The internal model principle of Francis and Wonham states that for a controller to reject a persistent class of disturbances asymptotically, it must contain a model of the process that generates them: an integrator for constant loads, an oscillator at the right frequency for periodic vibration [93]. This is why integral action removes steady load errors and why repetitive or resonant controllers are used against periodic disturbances.

A complementary approach estimates the disturbance explicitly and subtracts it. The disturbance observer (DOB), introduced by Ohnishi in the 1980s for motion control, compares the torque that was commanded with the torque that a nominal model says was needed to produce the measured motion; the difference, low-pass filtered by a Q-filter, is the estimated "lumped" disturbance, which includes external loads, friction and model errors, and is fed back to cancel them [35,36]. Inside the filter's bandwidth, the DOB makes the plant behave like the nominal model, so the outer controller can be designed for an ideal system. The bandwidth is the key trade-off: a faster Q-filter rejects faster disturbances, but it passes more sensor noise into the torque command and, with real sampling, delays and model errors, eventually destabilizes the loop [36,94]. Han's active disturbance rejection control (ADRC) generalizes the idea with an extended state observer that treats everything the simple model does not explain as one "total disturbance" state to estimate and cancel [95]. The momentum observer of Section 4 is the same idea for multi-joint robots: it estimates external torques that a controller can then either compensate or react to [15,43].

Disturbance estimation is also central in aerial robots, which are constantly pushed by wind. Incremental nonlinear dynamic inversion uses measured angular acceleration and the last actuator command to compute an increment that cancels whatever caused the difference between the desired and actual acceleration, which makes quadrotor attitude control remarkably insensitive to unmodelled aerodynamic moments [96]. Learned approaches estimate a low-dimensional representation of the current wind condition online and feed it to the controller, as in Neural-Fly [97]. At the extreme, Mueller and D'Andrea showed that a quadrocopter that loses one, two or even three propellers can still be controlled, by letting it spin about a vertical axis and controlling the direction of that axis [98]: reactive recovery can mean switching to a completely different way of flying.

5.3 Balance and push recovery in legged robots

A standing or walking robot is rejecting a disturbance all the time: gravity tries to topple it, and every push changes its momentum. The simplest useful model is the linear inverted pendulum, in which the centre of mass (CoM) moves at constant height z0 and is accelerated away from the centre of pressure (CoP) p: ẍ = ω2(x − p), with ω = √(g/z0) [99]. The CoP must lie inside the support polygon of the feet, which is the zero-moment-point condition used in classical humanoid walking [100,101]. The pendulum's divergent motion is captured by the instantaneous capture point ξ = x + ẋ/ω, introduced by Pratt and colleagues and, in biomechanics, by Hof as the "extrapolated centre of mass" [5,102]. It obeys ξ̇ = ω(ξ − p): the capture point runs away from the CoP exponentially, so the robot can come to rest without stepping only if it can place its CoP on (or beyond) the capture point. If the capture point leaves the foot, the robot must step, and stepping onto the capture point brings it to rest.

This gives a hierarchy of push-recovery strategies that mirrors human balance [10,103]: the ankle strategy shifts the CoP within the foot; the hip strategy rotates the upper body to change angular momentum and briefly move the centroidal moment pivot beyond the foot; and the stepping strategy moves the support polygon. Koolen and colleagues formalized this as N-step capturability, the set of states from which the robot can come to rest in N steps or fewer given its step-length and step-time limits [47], and Englsberger and colleagues extended the capture point to the three-dimensional divergent component of motion used in many humanoid walking controllers [104]. Model predictive controllers for walking and running generalize these analytic results to whole-body dynamics and changing contacts [105,106]. Learned locomotion policies, trained in simulation with random pushes, recover from kicks and slips without explicit capture-point computations, but their behavior can be interpreted in the same terms [50–52].

Fast detection of the push, from joint torques, IMU data or the momentum observer, is therefore as important as the stepping strategy itself [5,103].

6 Grasp correction and slip detection

6.1 Why grasps fail, and how people keep them

A grasp holds an object by friction: each fingertip can apply a tangential force no larger than the friction coefficient μ times its normal force, so the contact forces must lie inside friction cones, and grasp planners choose contacts whose cones together can resist the expected loads [107,108]. In practice the friction coefficient is unknown and varies with material, moisture and contamination; the object's mass and centre of mass are uncertain; and the load changes as the arm accelerates, the object is tilted or something is poured into it. A grasp planned with a fixed force will therefore either crush delicate objects or drop slippery ones unless it is corrected during execution [109,110].

People solve this with a tactile reflex. Johansson and Westling showed that when lifting objects, people set their grip force to a small safety margin above the slip limit for the friction they feel at first contact, and change grip and load forces in parallel as the load changes; when a small local slip occurs, tactile afferents in the fingertip trigger an automatic grip-force increase with a latency of roughly 70 ms, much faster than voluntary reaction [9,16,17]. Two lessons carry over to robots. First, the most useful signal is incipient slip, the partial slip at the edge of a contact that precedes gross sliding, rather than sliding itself. Second, the right control variable is the ratio of grip force to load force, adapted to the estimated friction.

6.2 Detecting slip

Contact mechanics explains why incipient slip is observable. When a tangential force is applied to an elastic contact, the outer annulus of the contact area slips while a central region still sticks; as the tangential force approaches the friction limit, the stuck region shrinks to nothing and gross sliding begins [111,112]. Robot slip detectors exploit several physical signatures [109,110,113]:

  • Vibration. Sliding produces high-frequency vibrations; Howe and Cutkosky detected them with accelerometers in a soft fingertip skin, and many later sensors use piezoelectric or high-rate pressure signals [41,114].
  • Localized micro-slip. Tremblay and Cutkosky's sensor had small nibs on its surface whose individual slips signalled incipient slip, which was used to estimate friction during manipulation [42].
  • Shear-field deformation. Vision-based tactile sensors image the deformation of an elastomer; the GelSight family tracks printed markers, and the inhomogeneity of their displacement field reveals partial slip before gross sliding [115–117]. Optical sensors such as the TacTip have been used with classifiers to detect slip in tens of milliseconds [118].
  • Force ratio and learned predictors. With force/torque or multimodal sensors such as the BioTac, slip can be predicted from the tangential-to-normal force ratio or by classifiers trained on labelled slip events, which also distinguish rotational from translational slip [119,120].

Section 7 of the sensors article describes these transducers; the remainder of this section concerns what a robot does with their signals.

6.3 Grasp correction

Reactions to slip range in scope. The simplest increases grip force, either in steps when slip is detected or continuously to keep a margin from the friction limit; Veiga and colleagues stabilized novel objects by predicting slip with learned classifiers and controlling each finger independently [120,121]. When more force is not an option, because the object is fragile or the gripper is at its limit, the robot can change the grasp: Hsiao and colleagues used fingertip pressure and proximity sensors on a PR2 gripper to adjust the hand position and re-grasp when contacts did not match expectations [122], learned models predict whether a grasp will succeed from vision and touch and choose a corrective regrasp [123,124], and tactile regrasp methods simulate how the tactile image would change under a proposed adjustment [125]. At a larger time scale, closed-loop visual grasping re-estimates the grasp pose at camera rate and tracks moving or displaced objects, as in Morrison and colleagues' generative grasping network [126]. Slow corrections are delegated upward: a dropped object is an event for the task-level executive, handled by the retry and replanning machinery in the task planning article.

7 Replanning after unexpected events

Reflexes and feedback keep a robot safe and on track in the short term, but some surprises invalidate the plan itself: a corridor is blocked, an object is not where the map said, a grasp fails, a person asks for something else. Then the robot must replan. Replanning raises three questions: when to replan, how to replan fast enough, and how to stay safe while the new plan is being computed.

7.1 When to replan

Robots replan either periodically or on events. Receding-horizon methods replan at a fixed rate whatever happens; model predictive control is the canonical example, re-solving a finite-horizon optimal control problem every few milliseconds from the latest state estimate so that feedback and replanning become the same thing [127–129]. Event-triggered replanning instead runs when a monitor detects a significant discrepancy: a new obstacle on the current path, a prediction error above a threshold, a failed precondition [130,131]. Event triggering saves computation and keeps plans stable, which matters when people or other robots act on them, but it requires monitors that detect the events reliably; execution monitoring and fault detection are discussed in the task planning and sensor fusion articles [132,133]. In practice systems mix both: a local planner or MPC runs continuously, and a global replanner is triggered when the local layer reports that it is stuck.

7.2 How to replan fast: incremental and anytime search

When the world changes only a little, most of the previous search is still valid. Incremental search algorithms reuse it. Stentz's D* repaired a backward search from the goal as a robot discovered obstacles [37]; Lifelong Planning A* (LPA*) formalized incremental heuristic search with the notions of g-values and one-step look-ahead rhs-values, re-expanding only vertices whose values became inconsistent [134]; and D* Lite applied LPA* to a moving robot, searching from the goal so that the robot's changing position only affects the heuristic [38]. Anytime algorithms such as ARA* return a suboptimal plan quickly and improve it while time remains, and Anytime D* combines both properties [135,136]. These algorithms have been used in the field: the Mars Exploration Rovers ran Field D* on board for global path planning, and the Carnegie Mellon vehicle Boss, winner of the 2007 DARPA Urban Challenge, used Anytime D* to plan in parking lots and to replan when roads were blocked [39,40,137]. Sampling-based planners have their own replanning variants: replanning RRTs reuse the parts of a tree that remain valid, and RRTX maintains an asymptotically optimal tree that is rewired as obstacles appear and disappear [138,139].

7.4 Staying safe while replanning

Replanning takes time, and the robot is moving while it happens. A robot that waits for a new plan before acting may reach an inevitable collision state in the meantime, so safe replanning schemes always keep a fallback. Petti and Fraichard's partial motion planning returns the best partial trajectory found within a fixed time budget, constrained to avoid inevitable collision states [81,82]; Bekris and Kavraki's greedy-but-safe replanning commits only to trajectory segments that end in a state from which a known safe contingency, such as braking, exists [140]. Contingency MPC for automated vehicles optimizes a nominal trajectory jointly with a contingency trajectory that shares its first part and stays feasible if a predicted hazard materializes [141]. The same principle underlies predictive safety filters: the robot always holds a verified backup plan, and the reactive layer executes it if replanning does not deliver in time [55,85]. At the task level, replanning interacts with repair and with plan stability, as discussed in the task planning article [142,143].

8 Safe stopping and recovery behavior

Every reactive layer eventually meets a situation it cannot handle: a sensor fails, a filter becomes infeasible, a fault is detected in a motor or the software, or the situation is simply outside anything the system was designed for. The last line of defence is to bring the robot into a safe state and, when possible, to recover from it and continue. Both steps are less obvious than they sound.

8.1 What a safe state is

For an industrial arm the safe state is usually standstill with brakes engaged. For many other robots "stop" is not safe: a quadrotor that cuts its motors falls, a walking robot that freezes may topple, a car that brakes hard in a fast lane may be hit from behind, and an arm that is holding a heavy part or is in contact with a person may need to keep some torque on. Safety engineering therefore distinguishes fail-safe systems, which have a passive safe state they can always fall back to, from fail-operational systems, which must keep working, possibly with degraded performance, long enough to reach a safe state [61,144]. Driving automation formalizes this as the minimal risk condition, a stable stopped state that an automated vehicle reaches when a trip cannot be completed, for example by pulling over [145,146]. Drones land or hover; legged robots crouch, sit or fall in a controlled way. Fujiwara and colleagues' UKEMI controller made a humanoid fall backward in a controlled way that reduced impact, Ha and Liu planned sequences of contacts that minimize damage during a fall, and learned classifiers predict a fall early enough to trigger such responses [147–149].

8.2 Stopping functions in machinery standards

Industrial robot safety is built on machinery standards that define how stopping must work [46,150]. IEC 60204-1 defines three stop categories: category 0, stopping by immediate removal of power to the actuators (an uncontrolled stop, with brakes); category 1, a controlled stop with power available to the actuators to achieve the stop and then removed; and category 2, a controlled stop with power left available [150]. Emergency stops, initiated by people through red mushroom buttons, must use category 0 or 1, and resetting an emergency stop must not by itself restart the machine [151]. Robot standards add the protective stop, initiated automatically by safeguards such as light curtains, laser scanners or the collaborative-operation functions of ISO/TS 15066 [45,46]. How reliable a safety function must be is expressed as a performance level (PL a to e) under ISO 13849-1 or a safety integrity level under IEC 61508, which constrain architecture (redundancy, diagnostics) and component failure rates [152,153]. ISO 13855 sets minimum distances for safeguards from the approach speed of the human body and the system's overall stopping time, and analogous requirements exist for driverless industrial trucks and personal-care robots [59,154,155].

The choice of category is a trade-off. A category 0 stop does not depend on the drives or the control software working, which makes it robust to the failures that may have triggered it, but brakes are usually weaker than motors and engage only after a delay, and the robot may leave its path while coasting. Category 1 and 2 stops are faster and keep the robot on its path but rely on the drive electronics. Many modern robots therefore use a category 1 stop monitored by a safety controller that removes power if deceleration is not as expected [46,152]. Safety functions are typically implemented in certified safety controllers and drive functions such as safe torque off, separate from the main robot software, and are triggered by watchdogs that detect when a heartbeat from a computer or network link is missing [62,152].

8.4 Recovery behaviors

After a safe stop, a robot should, where appropriate, recover without human help. Recovery behaviors are short, pre-designed or learned routines that try to restore the preconditions for normal operation. Navigation stacks include recoveries such as clearing the local costmap, rotating in place to re-observe the surroundings, backing up and waiting, invoked by a behavior tree when the planner or controller fails [156,157]. Legged robots learn to stand up after a fall: the ANYmal policies of Hwangbo and colleagues and the recovery controller of Lee and colleagues were trained in simulation to get up from arbitrary fallen configurations [50,158]. In reinforcement learning, Recovery RL learns a separate recovery policy that takes over when a learned safety critic predicts that the task policy is about to enter a dangerous region [159], and "leave no trace" learns a reset policy alongside the task policy and aborts episodes early when the reset policy predicts that it could no longer return to the start [160]. Some recoveries need people, and a good reactive system knows when to ask: it reports what failed, what it tried and what it needs, as discussed for task-level failures in the task planning article.

The safety of the whole cycle (detect, stop, recover, resume) is a system property. Runtime safety monitors such as SMOF check a small set of safety rules independently of the main controller and trigger interventions when a rule is about to be violated [161]; system-theoretic safety analysis treats accidents as failures of control over unsafe interactions rather than only as component failures, which suits complex robots whose hazards arise from interactions among correctly working parts [162]. For learned and AI components, standards for the safety of the intended functionality and for AI in road vehicles add requirements for analysing performance limitations and unknown situations, which is where reactive monitoring and safe stopping become part of the safety case [144,146,163].

9 Combining reflexes, deliberation and learning

9.1 Layers, rates and authority

The architectural question raised by the behavior-based debate of the 1980s, how to combine fast reactions with slow reasoning, has a broadly agreed answer: layers that run at different rates over different representations, with clear rules about which layer has authority over which outputs [1,11,12]. Three rules recur in successful systems.

  • Safety has the last word and the shortest path. Protective stops, collision reactions and safety filters sit between every other layer and the actuators, run at the highest rate, and can override everything above them; the most critical are implemented in separate, certified hardware [15,55,152]. The Simplex architecture states the principle for controllers in general: an unverified high-performance controller runs only while a simple verified controller can still recover the system [164,165].
  • Reactive layers handle what they can and report what they cannot. A local planner that is blocked, a filter that is infeasible, a grasp that keeps slipping or a fall that has happened is an event for the layer above, which may replan, choose another skill or ask for help. Behavior trees make this upward flow explicit: conditions are re-checked at every tick, and failure of a child propagates to fallback nodes that try alternatives (see the behavior trees section of the task planning article) [29,30].
  • Priorities are explicit. When several reactive objectives compete, strict priorities (hierarchical QPs, null-space projection) or metric-weighted fusion (RMPs, geometric fabrics) state precisely what yields to what [67,68,70,71]. Hand-tuned weighted sums without priorities are a frequent source of surprising behavior near constraint boundaries.

Model predictive control blurs the line between layers, because it is a planner that runs fast enough to be a reflex: legged robots replan footstep forces at hundreds of hertz and quadrotors replan trajectories at the rate of their state estimator, so a push or a gust is handled by the same optimization that produces normal motion [106,129,166]. Even then, a simpler reflex and a safety layer remain underneath, because solvers can fail to converge and models can be wrong [14,85].

9.2 Learned reactive behavior

Learning has changed what the reactive layer can do. Reinforcement-learned locomotion policies run at tens to hundreds of hertz on proprioception and, in later versions, on terrain perception; trained with randomized pushes, friction and terrain in simulation, they recover from kicks, slips and stumbles with reflex-like responses that nobody programmed explicitly [50–52]. A learned policy beat human champions at first-person-view drone racing by reacting to its state estimate at high rate [167]. Learned visuomotor manipulation policies close the loop through vision: diffusion policies and action-chunking transformers predict short sequences of actions and re-plan at each chunk boundary or blend overlapping chunks, which trades reactivity against temporal consistency [168,169]. The imitation learning and sim-to-real articles describe how such policies are trained.

Robot foundation models add a further layer. Large vision-language-action models are too slow to be reflexes, so recent systems split them: a slower vision-language "System 2" interprets instructions and the scene, and a faster action model, "System 1", produces continuous motor commands at tens of hertz, as in the hierarchical Hi Robot and the dual-system GR00T N1 [53,54,170]. Below these, conventional controllers and safety layers still execute and guard the commands (see the foundation models article). Learned reactive behavior has a characteristic weakness: it is reliable inside its training distribution and can fail silently outside it. Practical systems therefore pair learned policies with out-of-distribution and anomaly detectors that trigger fallbacks, with residual designs in which learning only corrects a conventional controller, and with safety filters that bound what the learned policy can command [55,171–173].

10 Practice, evaluation and open problems

10.1 Implementation

Reactive behavior is as much a software and hardware engineering problem as an algorithmic one. Loops must run at fixed rates with bounded jitter, which usually means real-time operating systems or real-time kernels, lock-free communication between threads and careful separation of fast loops from slow perception [62]. ROS 2 provides real-time-capable communication with quality-of-service settings such as deadlines and liveliness, and ros_control and its ROS 2 successor provide a standard structure for hardware interfaces and controllers that run in the real-time loop [63,174]. Sensors should be time-stamped at acquisition so that reactions use the true age of each measurement, and every reactive behavior should have a defined response to missing or stale data, typically a stop or a degraded mode [61,62]. Online trajectory generation turns abrupt reactive decisions into smooth, limit-respecting motions [56].

10.2 Evaluating reactive behavior

Reactive behaviors are evaluated by how they handle the unexpected, so evaluation must create the unexpected deliberately. Useful practices include randomized and adversarial scenario testing in simulation, as in the examples of this article, where conclusions about latency, arbitration or safety filters were drawn from hundreds of random episodes rather than one demonstration; fault injection, in which sensors are blocked, delayed or corrupted and actuators weakened; physical perturbation tests such as standardized pushes and impacts; and long-duration field trials that measure false-alarm rates as well as missed detections [44,61,144]. Metrics should include response latency (with its worst case, not only its mean), minimum separation and peak and quasi-static contact forces, recovery success rate and time, and the cost of conservatism: how often and for how long the reactive layer stopped or slowed the robot unnecessarily [45,87,88]. 10.3 Open problems

  • Guarantees under real limits. Safety filters and reachability analyses are only as good as their models and their feasibility under actuator limits; scaling them to high-dimensional robots, contact and perception uncertainty remains open [55,173,175].
  • Reacting to people without freezing. Standards-compliant stopping is safe but conservative, and avoidance that predicts and negotiates with people, rather than merely stopping for them, is not yet certifiable [45,88,89].
  • Learned reflexes you can trust. Learned policies react impressively within their training distribution; detecting when they are outside it, and handing over gracefully, is a central problem for deploying foundation-model-driven robots [55,173,176].
  • Touch at scale. Slip detection and grasp correction depend on tactile sensors that are still costly, fragile or hard to integrate, and on models that generalize across objects and contact conditions [109,110,113].
  • Recovery and restart. Much less is known about recovering well than about stopping safely; general recovery skills, and the decision of when to ask a human for help, remain largely hand-engineered [132,159,160].

Conclusion

Reactive behavior is how a robot copes with being wrong. Reflexes map sensor readings directly to actions within milliseconds; arbitration and fusion combine them, each with its own failure mode. Real-time avoidance keeps the robot clear of obstacles and people, safety filters make that clearance a guarantee where the actuators allow it, and momentum observers detect and react to the contacts that happen anyway. Disturbance observers and integral action cancel forces the robot cannot avoid, and capture-point reasoning tells a legged robot where to step when a push is too large to absorb in place. Grip–load coupling with incipient-slip sensing holds unknown objects gently and securely. Incremental and receding-horizon planners revise plans when the world changes, with a safe fallback while they compute, and safe stopping and recovery are the last line of defence. The examples in this article show recurring lessons in small form: latency costs collisions and pushes; fusion gets stuck where arbitration dithers; a filter's guarantee ends where its feasibility ends; reactions cannot remove impact peaks but can prevent clamping; observer bandwidth is limited by noise; sensing incipient slip beats reacting to sliding; reusing search beats starting over; and restart logic matters as much as stop logic.
