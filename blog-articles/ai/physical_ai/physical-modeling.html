1 Physical models, world models and physical AI

The previous article in this series described how a robot measures the world [1]. Measurement alone does not tell a robot what will happen when it pushes a box, swings a leg or pours a liquid. For that it needs a model: a description, explicit or learned, of how the state of the world evolves in response to actions. The psychologist Kenneth Craik proposed in 1943 that an organism carrying "a small-scale model" of external reality in its head could try out alternatives and react to situations before they arise [2]. Eighty years later the same idea, under the name world model, sits at the centre of machine learning for control: Ha and Schmidhuber trained agents inside the "dream" of a learned generative model of their environment [3], and companies building physical AI now describe large video-prediction networks as "world foundation models" for robots and vehicles [4].

Robotics has always relied on world models of a particular kind: the equations of classical mechanics. Model predictive control repeatedly solves an optimal-control problem over a predicted future using a dynamic model of the plant [5]; computed-torque control cancels a manipulator's modeled inertia, Coriolis and gravity terms [6,7]; and physics simulators let engineers and reinforcement-learning agents rehearse millions of trials safely [8,9]. Physical models in robotics span a spectrum, summarized in Figure 1:

  • White-box (first-principles) models are derived from Newton's laws or their Lagrangian and Hamiltonian reformulations, with parameters such as masses and lengths taken from CAD or measurement [10,11].
  • Grey-box models keep the physical structure but estimate uncertain parameters (inertia, friction, stiffness) from data, which is the classical task of system identification [12,13].
  • Black-box models learn the dynamics directly from observations with function approximators such as neural networks or Gaussian processes, without assuming the form of the equations [14,15].

This article follows that spectrum. It starts with the mechanics that every physical model inherits, then works through kinematics, multibody dynamics, contact, and continuum materials, before turning to identification, simulation and learned world models. Throughout, small runnable programs show the core algorithms and verify them against closed-form answers.

2 Newtonian mechanics: forces, torque, momentum and energy

2.1 The laws of motion

Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687) set out three laws: a body remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted on by a force; the change of motion is proportional to the applied force; and to every action there is an equal and opposite reaction [17]. In modern notation the second law for a particle of constant mass is

F = dp/dt = m a,    p = m v

where p is linear momentum. For rotation about a point, the moment of a force (torque) is τ = r × F, angular momentum is L = r × p, and the rotational analogue of the second law is τ = dL/dt [10]. For a rigid body these combine into the Newton–Euler equations: the total external force equals mass times the acceleration of the centre of mass, and the total external torque about the centre of mass equals the rate of change of angular momentum, I ω̇ + ω × (I ω), where I is the inertia tensor [10,11]. The gyroscopic term ω × (I ω) is why a spinning body can change its rotation axis with no torque applied, as Section 4 shows.

2.2 Work, energy and conservation laws

The work done by a force along a path changes kinetic energy T = ½ m v²; for conservative forces it can be written as the negative change of a potential energy V, so the total mechanical energy E = T + V is conserved [10]. Momentum and energy conservation are not independent postulates: Emmy Noether proved in 1918 that every continuous symmetry of a system's action corresponds to a conserved quantity, so invariance under time translation gives energy conservation, invariance under spatial translation gives momentum conservation, and invariance under rotation gives angular-momentum conservation [39]. These invariants are useful in practice. They provide sanity checks for simulators, constraints for estimators, and, as discussed below, inductive biases for learned models.

2.3 Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics

For systems with many interacting parts and constraints, writing free-body diagrams becomes unwieldy. Lagrange's Mécanique analytique (1788) instead describes a system by generalized coordinates q and the Lagrangian ℒ(q, q̇) = T − V; the equations of motion follow from the Euler–Lagrange equations [10,18]:

d/dt (∂ℒ/∂q̇) − ∂ℒ/∂q = τ

where τ collects generalized non-conservative forces such as motor torques. Constraint forces that do no work drop out automatically. Hamilton's 1835 reformulation uses coordinates and conjugate momenta (q, p) and the Hamiltonian H = T + V, giving first-order equations q̇ = ∂H/∂p, ṗ = −∂H/∂q [19]. Hamiltonian flows preserve phase-space volume and the symplectic structure, a property that numerical integrators can be designed to respect [40]. Applied to a robot arm, the Lagrangian approach produces the familiar manipulator equation of Section 4 [6].

2.4 Numerical integration: turning laws into predictions

Except in special cases, the equations of motion must be integrated numerically, and the integrator is part of the model [40]. Explicit Euler, xk+1 = xk + h f(xk), is simple but systematically injects energy into oscillatory systems. Symplectic integrators such as symplectic (semi-implicit) Euler and the Störmer–Verlet or leapfrog scheme, popularized in molecular dynamics by Verlet in 1967, exactly preserve a nearby "shadow" Hamiltonian, so their energy error stays bounded over very long times instead of drifting [40,41]. Higher-order methods such as classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) are far more accurate per step but are not symplectic, so their energy error grows slowly over very long horizons [40]. 2.5 Physics as an inductive bias for learning

The same structure can be built into learned models. Hamiltonian neural networks parameterize H(q, p) with a neural network and obtain dynamics from Hamilton's equations, so the learned model conserves a learned energy by construction [43]. Deep Lagrangian networks learn the mass matrix and potential of a robot so that predicted torques obey the Euler–Lagrange equations, which improved generalization to unseen trajectories in their experiments on robot arms [44]. Section 7 returns to this theme with grey-box identification.

3 Kinematics and robot motion constraints

Kinematics describes motion without reference to the forces that cause it: the geometry relating joint variables to the positions, orientations and velocities of a robot's links [7,45]. Joint positions are measured directly by encoders, whose operation was described in the previous article [1]; kinematics turns those readings into the pose of the hand, foot or tool.

3.1 Configuration space and rigid-body motions

A robot's configuration is a complete specification of the position of every point on it, and the number of independent coordinates needed is its degrees of freedom (DOF). A free rigid body in space has six (three translational, three rotational); a typical industrial arm has six joints so that it can place its end effector at an arbitrary position and orientation within its workspace [45]. Rigid-body poses form the special Euclidean group SE(3) and orientations the rotation group SO(3), which are not vector spaces: rotations do not commute and any three-parameter representation (such as Euler angles) has singularities, which is why robot software uses rotation matrices, unit quaternions or exponential coordinates [45,46].

3.2 Forward kinematics

Forward kinematics computes the end-effector pose from joint values by chaining the transforms of each link. Denavit and Hartenberg's 1955 convention describes each joint-to-joint transform with four parameters and remains widespread in industry [22]. Brockett's product-of-exponentials formulation expresses the same map as a product of matrix exponentials of joint twists, T(q) = e[S1]q1 ⋯ e[Sn]qn M, which avoids link-frame conventions and connects kinematics to Lie group theory [45–47].

3.3 Velocity kinematics, singularities and inverse kinematics

Differentiating forward kinematics gives the Jacobian J(q), which maps joint velocities to end-effector velocities, ẋ = J(q) q̇, and by the principle of virtual work maps end-effector wrenches to joint torques, τ = JTF [7,45]. Whitney's resolved-motion rate control (1969) commanded joint rates through the inverse Jacobian to move a hand along a desired Cartesian path [48]. Where J loses rank, at a singularity, some end-effector directions cannot be achieved and the required joint velocities become unbounded. Yoshikawa's manipulability measure w = √det(J JT) quantifies the distance from singularity [49].

Inverse kinematics (IK) solves for joint values that achieve a desired pose. Closed-form solutions exist for many industrial arm geometries, but the general six-revolute-joint arm can have up to 16 solutions and requires solving a high-degree polynomial [50]. Numerical IK iterates Jacobian-based updates. The damped least-squares (Levenberg–Marquardt) update introduced to robotics by Wampler and by Nakamura and Hanafusa, Δq = JT(J JT + λ²I)−1e, trades a small tracking error near singularities for bounded joint velocities [51,52]. When the robot has more joints than task dimensions it is redundant, and joint motions in the null space of J move the arm without moving the end effector, which can be used for secondary goals such as avoiding joint limits or obstacles [7].

3.4 Holonomic and nonholonomic constraints

Joints and closed kinematic loops impose holonomic constraints, equations among the configuration variables that reduce the dimension of the configuration space. A rolling wheel instead imposes a nonholonomic constraint on velocities that cannot be integrated into a constraint on positions: a car cannot move sideways instantaneously, yet by parallel parking it can reach any position and heading [53,54]. For a unicycle-like robot with heading θ, the no-slip constraint is ẋ sin θ − ẏ cos θ = 0. Nonholonomic systems are controllable but cannot be stabilized to a point by smooth time-invariant state feedback, which is why their motion planning and control form a distinct field [53]. Practical motion constraints also include joint position, velocity, acceleration and torque limits, and collision avoidance with the environment, all of which planners must respect [7,54].

4 Rigid-body and multibody dynamics

4.1 The single rigid body

A rigid body's mass distribution enters its dynamics only through ten numbers: the mass, the three coordinates of the centre of mass, and the six independent entries of the symmetric 3×3 inertia tensor [11,13]. In the principal-axis frame the inertia tensor is diagonal, and with no external torque Euler's rotation equations read I1ω̇1 = (I2 − I3)ω2ω3 and cyclic permutations [10]. Their solutions show that rotation about the axes of largest and smallest moment of inertia is stable while rotation about the intermediate axis is unstable, the "tennis racket" or Dzhanibekov effect [55]. A rigid-body model that gets this wrong will mispredict tumbling objects, satellites and thrown tools.

4.2 The manipulator equation

For a chain of rigid links connected by joints, applying Lagrange's equations with the joint angles as generalized coordinates yields the equation of motion used throughout robotics [6,7]:

M(q) q̈ + C(q, q̇) q̇ + g(q) = τ + J(q)T Fext

Here M(q) is the symmetric positive-definite joint-space mass matrix, C(q, q̇)q̇ collects Coriolis and centrifugal terms, g(q) is the gravity torque, τ the joint torques and Fext an external wrench mapped through the contact Jacobian [7]. Two computational problems arise. Inverse dynamics computes τ for a desired motion and is used in feedforward and computed-torque control; forward dynamics computes q̈ given τ and is what a simulator integrates [11].

4.3 Recursive algorithms

Deriving M and C symbolically and evaluating them is costly for long chains. In 1980 Luh, Walker and Paul published the recursive Newton–Euler algorithm (RNEA), which computes inverse dynamics in time linear in the number of links: an outward pass propagates velocities and accelerations from the base to the tip, and an inward pass accumulates forces and torques from the tip back to the base [25]. Hollerbach showed that a recursive Lagrangian formulation also achieves linear cost and compared the complexity of the formulations [56]. Walker and Orin then showed how to use inverse dynamics to build forward dynamics, for example by computing the mass matrix one column at a time and solving the resulting linear system, and introduced the composite-rigid-body algorithm for M [57]. Featherstone's articulated-body algorithm (1983) solves forward dynamics directly in O(n) time [26], and his spatial-vector notation, which packs angular and linear quantities into 6-D vectors, is now the standard language of rigid-body dynamics libraries [11]. Modern implementations such as Pinocchio also provide analytical derivatives of these algorithms for optimization and learning [58].

4.4 Floating bases, closed chains and constraints

Legged robots, humanoids and free-flying manipulators have a floating base: six unactuated coordinates describing the pose of the torso are added to the joint coordinates, and the robot can only move its base through contact forces with the environment [7,11]. Closed kinematic loops (parallel robots, four-bar linkages, a humanoid with both feet on the ground) are handled by adding constraint equations with Lagrange multipliers, giving differential-algebraic equations. Integrated naively, the constraint residual drifts; Baumgarte's 1972 stabilization adds feedback terms on the constraint violation to keep it bounded [60]. Engines such as MuJoCo, Drake and Pinocchio-based solvers formulate joints either in reduced (joint) coordinates, which satisfy the constraints exactly, or in maximal coordinates with explicit constraints [8,58,61].

5 Contact mechanics, friction and collision

Almost everything useful a robot does (walking, grasping, pushing, assembling) happens through contact, and contact is the least well-modeled part of robot physics [62,63]. Contact is unilateral (bodies can push but not pull), friction is set-valued at zero velocity, and impacts change velocities almost instantaneously. These features make the dynamics nonsmooth.

5.1 Elastic contact: Hertz

Heinrich Hertz's 1882 analysis of two elastic bodies with curved surfaces pressed together was the first solution in contact mechanics [20]. For a sphere of radius R on a flat, the contact radius grows as the cube root of the load and the normal force grows with indentation δ as F = (4/3) E* R1/2 δ3/2, where E* is an effective modulus combining both materials' Young's moduli and Poisson's ratios [64]. Hertzian and related compliant models are the basis of penalty or soft contact in simulation, where a small interpenetration generates a restoring force; Hunt and Crossley added a damping term proportional to δnδ̇ so that the energy lost in impact matches a coefficient of restitution [65].

5.2 Friction

Amontons (1699) and Coulomb (1785) found that sliding friction is roughly proportional to the normal load and independent of the apparent contact area [66]. In the Coulomb model, a contact can transmit a tangential force ft only within the friction cone ‖ft‖ ≤ μ fn. While sticking, any force inside the cone is possible; while sliding, the force lies on the cone boundary and opposes the slip velocity [62,63]. Real friction is richer: the static coefficient exceeds the kinetic one, friction falls with increasing speed at low velocity (the Stribeck effect), and there is viscous friction, pre-sliding displacement and frictional memory [67]. Dynamic models such as the LuGre model represent these effects with an internal bristle-deflection state and are widely used for friction compensation in servo control [67,68].

5.3 Impacts and the complementarity formulation

When rigid bodies collide, velocities change in an instant through impulsive forces. Newton's impact law sets the post-impact normal separating velocity to −e times the approach velocity, with coefficient of restitution e between 0 (perfectly plastic) and 1 (perfectly elastic); Poisson's and Stronge's energetic definitions differ when friction acts during the impact [69]. With Coulomb friction, rigid-body dynamics can even have no solution or many, as Painlevé's paradox of a sliding rod shows [63].

The modern solution is to formulate contact at the velocity or impulse level over a time step. Moreau's sweeping process treated unilateral contact and dry friction as measure differential inclusions [70]. Stewart and Trinkle (1996) and Anitescu and Potra (1997) wrote each time step as a linear complementarity problem (LCP): for each contact, the normal impulse and the separation velocity are both non-negative and their product is zero (bodies either touch and push, or separate with no force), and a polyhedral approximation of the friction cone yields similar conditions for the tangential impulse [29,30]. Anitescu and Potra proved the resulting LCP always has a solution, and these time-stepping schemes do not need to detect individual impact events [30,63]. Many engines solve such problems approximately by projected Gauss–Seidel iterations over contacts, clamping each impulse to its cone. MuJoCo instead relaxes the complementarity conditions into a convex optimization problem, which has a unique solution and is analytically invertible at the cost of a small, tunable softness [71].

5.4 Collision detection

Before contact forces can be computed, a simulator or planner must find which bodies touch and where. The usual pipeline has a broad phase, which prunes pairs whose bounding volumes do not overlap using sweep-and-prune or bounding-volume hierarchies, and a narrow phase, which computes distances or penetration depths and contact points between candidate pairs [72]. Hierarchies of oriented bounding boxes (OBBTree) made interference detection between large polygonal models fast [73]. For convex shapes, the Gilbert–Johnson–Keerthi (GJK) algorithm computes the distance between two bodies by finding the point of their Minkowski difference closest to the origin; it needs only each shape's support function (the farthest point in a given direction), so the same code handles polytopes, spheres, capsules and their sweeps [28,72]. Libraries such as FCL package these algorithms for motion planning [74]. The example implements 2-D GJK and checks it against brute force.

5.5 How good are contact models?

Contact models are approximations, and their parameters vary with surface condition. A dataset of more than a million planar pushes found that a nominally identical push repeated 2000 times produced a structured, non-Gaussian spread of outcomes, and that the coefficient of friction was not constant but varied with position on the surface, direction, speed and time, which bounds the accuracy any deterministic Coulomb model can achieve [75]. Comparisons of physics engines likewise found that the results of a simulation depend on the engine and that each engine performed best on the kind of system it was designed for [76]. Trajectory optimization through contact, such as the method of Posa, Cantu and Tedrake that treats contact forces as decision variables with complementarity constraints, lets planners choose when and where to make contact rather than scripting it [77].

6 Deformable objects, fluids and material properties

Cloth, cables, food, tissue, soft grippers, soil and liquids do not behave as rigid bodies. Their state is a continuous field (displacement, velocity, pressure) rather than a handful of coordinates, and their behaviour depends on material properties [78,79]. Soft robots built from elastomers make deformation a design feature rather than a nuisance, and modeling it is one of the main open problems of the field [80].

6.1 Material properties and continuum mechanics

Robert Hooke's 1678 law, "ut tensio, sic vis" (as the extension, so the force), is the origin of linear elasticity [81]. In continuum form, stress (force per area) is proportional to strain (relative deformation). An isotropic linear-elastic material is characterized by two constants, usually Young's modulus E, the ratio of uniaxial stress to strain, and Poisson's ratio ν, the ratio of lateral contraction to axial extension [78]. Large deformations require nonlinear (hyperelastic) constitutive laws; materials that dissipate energy are viscoelastic; and materials that deform permanently beyond a yield stress are plastic [82]. Density, stiffness, damping, yield behaviour and friction are the material parameters a robot must know, or estimate, to predict how an object will respond to manipulation.

6.2 The finite element method

The finite element method (FEM) discretizes a continuum into elements and approximates the displacement field by piecewise polynomials, turning the governing partial differential equations into a system M ü + K u = f with stiffness matrix K and mass matrix M [82,83]. Its mathematical roots include Courant's 1943 use of piecewise-linear functions in a variational method [21]; its engineering form came from aircraft structural analysis by Turner, Clough, Martin and Topp in 1956 [84], and Clough coined the name "finite element method" in 1960 [85]. In computer graphics, Terzopoulos and colleagues' 1987 elastically deformable models brought continuum elasticity to animation [86], and frameworks such as SOFA apply FEM to interactive surgical simulation and soft-robot control [87].

6.3 Real-time deformables: mass-springs and position-based dynamics

FEM is accurate but can be expensive and, with explicit integration, requires small time steps for stiff materials. Baraff and Witkin's implicit integration of cloth made large time steps stable for interactive simulation [88]. Position-based dynamics (PBD), introduced by Müller and colleagues in 2007, goes further: it predicts positions ignoring internal forces, then iteratively projects them onto constraints (for example, fixed distances between neighbouring particles) and derives velocities from the change in position [32]. PBD is unconditionally stable, easy to control and widely used in games and robot-learning simulators, but its effective stiffness depends on the time step and iteration count; extended PBD (XPBD) adds a compliance parameter so that stiffness becomes a physical material property independent of the solver settings [89]. 6.4 Particle and hybrid methods

Large deformation, fracture and topology change are awkward for meshes. Smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH), introduced independently by Lucy and by Gingold and Monaghan in 1977 for astrophysics, represents a continuum by particles that carry mass and interpolate fields with smoothing kernels [90–92]. The material point method (MPM) of Sulsky, Chen and Schreyer combines Lagrangian particles that carry history-dependent material state with a background Eulerian grid on which the equations of motion are solved [93], and became well known when Stomakhin and colleagues used it to simulate snow for animation [94]. Differentiable MPM implementations, such as those written in the DiffTaichi language, let gradients flow through such simulations for control and design optimization [95].

6.5 Fluids

Newtonian fluids obey the Navier–Stokes equations, a statement of momentum conservation for a continuum with viscous stress proportional to strain rate, together with mass conservation, which for an incompressible fluid becomes ∇·u = 0 [79]:

ρ (∂u/∂t + (u·∇)u) = −∇p + μ∇²u + f,    ∇·u = 0

Harlow and Welch's marker-and-cell method of 1965 introduced the staggered grid still used for free-surface flows [96], and Chorin's projection method (1968) splits each time step into an update ignoring pressure followed by a projection onto divergence-free velocity fields, obtained by solving a Poisson equation for the pressure [97]. Stam's "stable fluids" (1999) combined this projection with semi-Lagrangian advection, which traces each grid point backward along the flow and interpolates, giving a solver that is stable for any time step and therefore suitable for interactive use [31,98].

Robotics applications such as pouring, scooping and swimming commonly use SPH, grid or MPM solvers, or learned surrogates of them [92,99].

6.6 Learned simulators of continua

Because high-fidelity continuum simulation is expensive, learned surrogates have become an active area. Graph network simulators represent particles or mesh nodes as graph vertices and learn message-passing updates; Sanchez-Gonzalez and colleagues showed one architecture simulating fluids, rigid solids and deformable materials [99], and MeshGraphNets extended the approach to mesh-based cloth, structural and aerodynamic simulations [100]. Fourier neural operators learn mappings between function spaces and solve families of partial differential equations orders of magnitude faster than conventional solvers in the authors' benchmarks [101]. Physics-informed neural networks instead embed the governing equations in the training loss, enabling both forward simulation and inference of unknown parameters from sparse data [102].

7 System identification: learning dynamics from observations

A model is only as good as its parameters. System identification is the construction of dynamic models from measured input and output data [12]. Zadeh used the term "identification" for this problem in 1956 [23], and Åström and Eykhoff's 1971 survey established it as a field of control engineering [24]. Its foundation is much older: least squares, published by Legendre in 1805 and claimed by Gauss, who said he had used it since 1795 [103].

7.1 The identification loop

Ljung describes identification as a loop: design an experiment, collect data, choose a model structure, fit the model by minimizing a criterion such as the prediction error, and validate it on fresh data; if validation fails, revise the structure or experiment [12]. For linear systems, classical structures include ARX and state-space models. Ho and Kalman's 1966 realization algorithm constructs a state-space model from impulse-response data [104], and subspace methods such as N4SID estimate state-space models directly from input-output data using numerical linear algebra [105].

7.2 Identifying robot dynamics

Rigid-body dynamics has a convenient property: although nonlinear in the joint variables, the manipulator equation is linear in the inertial parameters of the links, τ = Y(q, q̇, q̈) θ, where Y is a known regressor matrix and θ stacks each link's ten inertial parameters (and, if desired, friction coefficients) [13,27]. Atkeson, An and Hollerbach (1986) and Khosla and Kanade (1985) exploited this to estimate link and load parameters by least squares from measured joint torques and motions [13,27]. Not all parameters affect the dynamics independently: Gautier and Khalil showed how to compute the minimal set of identifiable base parameters [106]. Identification quality depends on how well the experiment excites the dynamics; Swevers and colleagues designed periodic excitation trajectories, parameterized as finite Fourier series, that are optimized for the accuracy of the estimated parameters [107]. Least-squares estimates may also be physically impossible (negative masses or inertia tensors that violate the triangle inequality); Wensing, Kim and Slotine enforce physical consistency with linear matrix inequalities [108].

7.3 Discovering equations: sparse regression

When the structure of the equations is unknown, one can search for it. Schmidt and Lipson's symbolic regression recovered conservation laws and equations of motion from measured data of mechanical systems such as pendulums [110]. 7.4 Black-box and hybrid learned dynamics

Narendra and Parthasarathy showed in 1990 that neural networks could identify and control nonlinear dynamical systems [14]. Gaussian-process models add calibrated uncertainty: PILCO propagated model uncertainty through long-horizon predictions to learn control policies from a few trials on real hardware [15]. Neural ordinary differential equations parameterize the continuous-time vector field with a network and integrate it with an ODE solver, which handles irregularly sampled data naturally [113]. In practice the most successful robot models are often hybrids. Hwangbo and colleagues trained an "actuator network" from measured data to model the series-elastic actuators of the ANYmal quadruped, combined it with a rigid-body simulator, and trained locomotion policies that transferred to the real robot [16].

8 Simulators, differentiable physics and sim-to-real transfer

8.1 Physics engines

A physics engine packages the models of Sections 2 to 6 (rigid-body dynamics, constraint solvers, collision detection, contact and sometimes deformables) behind a common interface. MuJoCo, introduced by Todorov, Erez and Tassa in 2012, was designed for model-based control: it uses generalized coordinates, a convex soft-contact model and fast evaluation of dynamics and its derivatives [8,71]. Drake emphasizes rigorous modeling and optimization-based planning and verification [61], and Pinocchio provides rigid-body algorithms and their analytical derivatives for optimal control [58]. Erez, Tassa and Todorov compared Bullet, Havok, MuJoCo, ODE and PhysX on speed and accuracy and found that each engine performed best on the type of system it was designed for [76].

8.2 Massively parallel and differentiable simulation

Two trends have changed how simulators are used in physical AI. The first is GPU parallelism. Isaac Gym ran both physics and policy training on the GPU, with thousands of environments in parallel [9], and Rudin and colleagues used it to train a quadruped locomotion policy in minutes of wall-clock time [114]. The second is differentiability. If the simulator provides gradients of future states with respect to actions, initial conditions or physical parameters, then control, design and system identification can use gradient-based optimization [115,116]. De Avila Belbute-Peres and colleagues differentiated through an LCP-based rigid-body solver so that physical parameters could be learned end to end with neural networks [115]; Degrave and colleagues built a differentiable engine for robot control [116]; DiffTaichi provided a language for writing differentiable simulators, including MPM [95]; and Brax implemented rigid-body simulation in JAX for accelerator hardware [117]. Contact makes such gradients delicate, because the true dynamics are nonsmooth, and smoothing the contact model trades physical fidelity for informative gradients, as the creeping block of Section 5 illustrates [63,71].

8.3 The reality gap

Policies trained in simulation often fail on hardware because the simulator's model differs from reality in unmodeled dynamics, parameters, latency and sensing: the reality gap. Three complementary strategies address it [36,118]:

  • Better models. Identify the robot's parameters (Section 7) and learn residual components such as actuator dynamics, as in ANYmal's actuator network [16].
  • Domain randomization. Train across a distribution of simulator parameters so that the real world looks like one more sample. Tobin and colleagues randomized rendering to transfer object detectors trained only in simulation [35]; Peng and colleagues randomized masses, friction, damping and latency to transfer a pushing policy to a real arm [36]; and OpenAI's automatic domain randomization enabled a robot hand to solve a Rubik's cube [119].
  • Closing the loop. Adapt the randomization distribution itself using real-world rollouts, as in the SimOpt approach of Chebotar and colleagues, which is a form of system identification over simulator parameters [118].

9 Learned world models and intuitive physics

9.1 World models in reinforcement learning

In reinforcement learning, a model predicts the next state and reward given the current state and action; algorithms that learn and plan with such a model are called model-based. Sutton's Dyna architecture (1991) interleaved learning from real experience with planning from simulated experience generated by a learned model [120]. Ha and Schmidhuber's "World Models" (2018) compressed images with a variational autoencoder, predicted the future in that latent space with a recurrent network, and trained a compact controller largely inside the learned model [3]. PlaNet learned a recurrent state-space model with deterministic and stochastic components and planned in latent space directly from pixels [37]. The Dreamer line of agents learns behaviours by imagining trajectories in such a latent model; DreamerV3 used one configuration across more than 150 tasks and was the first algorithm to collect diamonds in Minecraft from scratch without human data or curricula [38]. DayDreamer applied the approach to physical robots, learning quadruped walking from scratch in about an hour of real-world interaction without a simulator [121]. MuZero showed a different notion of a model: it learns only what is needed to predict rewards, values and policies for planning, not the full observation, and achieved superhuman play in Go, chess, shogi and Atari [122].

9.2 Intuitive physics in humans and machines

People predict physical events quickly without solving equations. Infants a few months old already expect objects to persist when hidden and to move continuously and solidly [123,124]. Yet adults' explicit physical intuitions can be systematically wrong: many students in McCloskey and colleagues' study predicted that a ball leaving a curved tube would continue on a curved path [125]. Battaglia, Hamrick and Tenenbaum proposed that people understand physical scenes by running approximate, probabilistic simulations, an "intuitive physics engine", and showed that a noisy Newtonian simulator predicted human judgments about the stability of block towers [33]. Lake and colleagues argued that such intuitive physics, together with intuitive psychology, is a core ingredient of human-like learning that machines lack [126].

Machine-learning research has pursued learned physical reasoning in parallel. Interaction networks treat a scene as objects and relations and learn to predict their dynamics, a precursor of the graph network simulators of Section 6 [99,127]. Benchmarks borrowed from developmental psychology test whether models are "surprised" by physically impossible events: IntPhys measures this for object permanence, shape constancy and spatio-temporal continuity [128], and Piloto and colleagues showed that a model with object-centred representations learned such expectations from visual data more readily than models without that structure [129].

9.3 Video world models and world foundation models

Generative video models trained on very large datasets now produce realistic clips of physical scenes, and their developers have framed them as simulators of the world [130]. Genie learned an interactive environment model, with a latent action space, from unlabeled Internet gameplay videos [131], and NVIDIA's Cosmos platform provides pretrained "world foundation models" intended to be fine-tuned as simulators for physical AI applications such as robotics and autonomous driving [4]. A different line, advocated by LeCun, argues that world models should predict in an abstract representation space rather than in pixels, so they need not model unpredictable details [132]; V-JEPA 2 learned such a representation from over a million hours of video and, after action-conditioned post-training on a small amount of robot data, was used for zero-shot planning of pick-and-place tasks [133].

Whether these models understand physics in the sense of Sections 2 to 6 is an open question. Kang and colleagues found that video generation models trained on simulated physical scenes generalized well within their training distribution but failed to extrapolate to out-of-distribution scenarios, behaving more like case-based retrieval than like learners of general physical laws [134]. The Physics-IQ benchmark found that the physical understanding of current video models was severely limited and largely unrelated to how visually realistic their output looked [135]. These results echo the pendulum example of Section 7: flexible learned models fit their training data, but explicit physical structure is what makes predictions extrapolate.

Conclusion

Physical modeling connects three centuries of mechanics with the newest learned world models. Newton's laws, Lagrange's and Hamilton's reformulations and Noether's conservation theorems provide structure; recursive algorithms make multibody dynamics fast enough for real-time control; complementarity and convex formulations make contact tractable; FEM, PBD, MPM and projection methods handle deformable materials and fluids; and system identification ties every model to measured reality. Learned models extend this toolkit to phenomena that are too complex to write down, but the examples in this article show the same trade-off repeatedly: structure buys extrapolation and interpretability, data buys flexibility. The most capable physical AI systems are likely to be those that combine both, using physics where it is known and learning where it is not.
