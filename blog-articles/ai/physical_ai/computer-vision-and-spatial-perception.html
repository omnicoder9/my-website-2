1 Spatial perception in physical AI

A robot that grasps a mug, follows a person down a corridor or plans a route across a warehouse must answer a sequence of questions about its surroundings: what is there, where exactly is it in three dimensions, how is it oriented, where am I relative to it, and how do the things around me relate to each other and to my task? Computer vision supplies most of those answers. It is the discipline concerned with recovering a description of the world from images, a problem that is ill-posed because a single image is a two-dimensional projection of a three-dimensional scene and therefore admits infinitely many interpretations [1,2]. Vision systems resolve this ambiguity with geometry (multiple views, known motion, active depth sensing) and with prior knowledge, which in modern systems is mostly learned from data [1,3].

The first article in this series described the transducers that produce visual data: RGB and stereo cameras, structured-light and time-of-flight depth cameras, lidar, and the inertial sensors that are fused with them (see Sensors §3, §4 and §6). This article picks up where that one stopped: given calibrated images, depth maps and point clouds, how does a physical AI system build the representations it needs to act? In robotics texts this is the province of perception, which sits between sensing and planning in the classical sense–plan–act architecture [4,5]. The same representations are increasingly consumed by learned policies; vision-language-action models such as RT-2, for example, map camera images and language instructions directly to robot actions, but they still inherit the strengths and failure modes of the visual features they are built on [6].

Three themes recur throughout. First, geometry and learning are complementary: projective geometry tells us exactly how 3-D points map to pixels [2], while learned models supply the priors needed to recognize objects and fill in what geometry alone cannot observe. Second, robots need metric, not just semantic, answers: a bounding box is useful for a photo-tagging service, but a manipulator needs a six-degree-of-freedom pose in its own coordinate frame, with an uncertainty estimate [5,7]. Third, perception is temporal: a robot sees a continuous stream, so tracking, filtering and mapping over time are as important as single-frame accuracy [8,9].

2 A short history of computer vision

2.1 From blocks worlds to computational theory

Computer vision began as a robotics problem. Lawrence Roberts' 1963 MIT dissertation recovered the 3-D structure of polyhedral "blocks world" scenes from a single photograph by extracting edges and matching them to known shape models, establishing the idea of model-based recognition [10]. In 1966 Seymour Papert proposed the MIT "summer vision project", which aimed to have students build a significant part of a visual system, including figure–ground separation and object naming, in a single summer [11]; the problem proved far harder than anticipated. In neuroscience, Hubel and Wiesel's recordings from the cat visual cortex revealed "simple" and "complex" cells tuned to oriented edges at particular positions, a hierarchical organization that later inspired convolutional networks [12,13]. David Marr's Vision (1982) framed vision as an information-processing problem to be analyzed at the levels of computational theory, representation and algorithm, and implementation, and proposed a progression from the image through a "primal sketch" and a viewer-centred "2½-D sketch" to object-centred 3-D models [14].

2.2 Geometry, features and statistical learning

Through the 1980s and 1990s the field built a rigorous geometric foundation. Longuet-Higgins showed in 1981 that the relative pose of two cameras can be recovered linearly from eight point correspondences [15], and the theory of multiple-view geometry was consolidated in the textbook by Hartley and Zisserman [2]. Robust estimation with RANSAC (1981) made it possible to fit such models in the presence of mismatched points [16]. Recognition, meanwhile, moved to local invariant features and statistical classifiers: Harris and Shi–Tomasi corners [17,18], Lowe's scale-invariant feature transform (SIFT) [19], histograms of oriented gradients (HOG) for pedestrian detection [20], and the boosted cascade of Viola and Jones, which made real-time face detection practical in 2001–2004 [21].

2.3 The deep learning era

Fukushima's neocognitron (1980) introduced a hierarchy of convolution-like feature extractors and pooling layers [13]; LeCun and colleagues trained such networks end to end with backpropagation to read handwritten ZIP codes in 1989 and generalized the approach as LeNet [22,23]. The decisive shift came when large labelled datasets met GPU training. ImageNet organized millions of images under the WordNet hierarchy [24], and its annual Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC) became the field's benchmark [25]. In 2012, the AlexNet convolutional network achieved a top-5 test error of 15.3% compared with 26.2% for the second-best entry [26], and within three years residual networks with up to 152 layers reached 3.57% top-5 error [27], below the roughly 5.1% estimated for a trained human annotator in the ILSVRC analysis [25]. Since 2020, transformer architectures [28] adapted to images as sequences of patches (the Vision Transformer, ViT) [29], and models trained on web-scale image–text pairs (CLIP) [30] or with self-supervision (DINOv2) [31], have provided general-purpose visual features that transfer to robotics with little or no task-specific labelling.

3 Image classification and object detection

3.1 Concepts

Image classification assigns one or more labels to a whole image. Object detection additionally localizes every instance of each class of interest, usually as an axis-aligned bounding box with a confidence score. For a robot, classification answers "is there a mug in view?"; detection answers "where are the mugs?", which is the first step towards picking one up. Detection is evaluated by matching predicted to ground-truth boxes using intersection over union (IoU), the area of overlap divided by the area of union, and summarizing the precision–recall curve as average precision (AP) per class and its mean (mAP) over classes. The PASCAL VOC challenge popularized AP at an IoU threshold of 0.5 [49]; the COCO benchmark, with 328,000 images and 2.5 million labelled instances, averages AP over IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 to reward precise localization [50].

3.2 Classical pipelines: features plus classifiers

Before deep learning, a detector was a hand-designed feature extractor followed by a trained classifier applied over a sliding window at multiple scales. Viola and Jones computed simple rectangle (Haar-like) features in constant time using an integral image, selected a small set of them with AdaBoost, and arranged the resulting classifiers in a cascade that rejects most background windows after a few evaluations; their detector processed 384×288 images at about 15 frames per second on a 700 MHz Pentium III [21]. Dalal and Triggs showed that histograms of gradient orientations, computed in small cells and contrast-normalized over overlapping blocks, combined with a linear support vector machine (SVM) [51], made a markedly better human detector [20]. Felzenszwalb and colleagues extended HOG to deformable part models, in which a root filter and several part filters are allowed to shift relative to each other at a learned cost [52].

3.3 Convolutional networks and transformers for classification

A convolutional neural network (CNN) stacks layers of learned filters, each followed by a nonlinearity and, periodically, spatial pooling, so that early layers respond to edges and textures and deeper layers to object parts and whole objects [3,23]. Weight sharing makes the representation translation-equivariant and drastically reduces the number of parameters compared with a fully connected network. AlexNet (eight learned layers, ReLU activations, dropout and GPU training) established the recipe [26]; VGG networks showed the benefit of depth using stacks of 3×3 convolutions [54]; and residual networks (ResNets) made networks with over a hundred layers trainable by learning residual functions with identity shortcut connections [27]. The Vision Transformer splits an image into 16×16-pixel patches, embeds each as a token and processes the sequence with self-attention; it matches or exceeds CNNs when pre-trained on sufficiently large datasets [29]. Contrastive image–text pre-training (CLIP) on 400 million image–caption pairs yields classifiers that can be specified in natural language at test time, a property that is especially valuable for robots that meet objects outside any fixed label set [30].

3.4 Deep object detectors

Modern detectors fall into a few families (Table 2):

  • Two-stage, region-based. R-CNN classified about two thousand bottom-up region proposals per image with CNN features and improved VOC 2012 mAP by more than 30% relative to the previous best result [55]. Fast R-CNN shared computation by pooling features for each region from a single convolutional feature map [56], and Faster R-CNN replaced external proposals with a learned region proposal network (RPN) that predicts objectness and box offsets relative to a set of reference "anchor" boxes, reaching about 5 frames per second with a VGG-16 backbone [41]. Feature pyramid networks (FPN) add a top-down pathway so that objects of all sizes are detected from semantically strong features [57].
  • One-stage, dense. YOLO ("you only look once") frames detection as a single regression from the image to a grid of boxes and class probabilities, running at 45 frames per second (155 for a smaller variant) [42]; SSD predicts boxes from multiple feature maps at different scales [58]. One-stage detectors face an extreme imbalance between a few object locations and many background locations; RetinaNet's focal loss, FL(pt) = −(1 − pt)γ log pt, down-weights easy negatives so that dense detectors can match two-stage accuracy [59].
  • Set prediction with transformers. DETR treats detection as direct prediction of a set of boxes, using a transformer encoder–decoder and a bipartite (Hungarian) matching loss between predictions and ground truth, which removes hand-designed anchors and non-maximum suppression [60,61].
  • Open-vocabulary detection. Detectors built on image–text models, such as OWL-ViT and Grounding DINO, accept free-text queries ("the blue screwdriver") and can detect categories never seen in their box-level training data [62,63]. For robots operating in homes and warehouses with long-tailed object sets, this is often the deciding capability.

4 Semantic, instance and panoptic segmentation

4.1 Concepts

Segmentation labels images at the level of pixels rather than boxes. Semantic segmentation assigns every pixel a class ("floor", "table", "cup") but does not distinguish between two cups. Instance segmentation produces a separate mask for each object instance, typically only for countable "things". Panoptic segmentation unifies the two: every pixel receives a class label and, for things, an instance identity, while amorphous "stuff" such as road, sky or floor receives a class only [65]. Robots use these outputs in different ways: semantic masks give traversable terrain and free surfaces, instance masks isolate a single object for grasping, and projecting masks into a depth image or point cloud yields per-object 3-D geometry [1,43].

Semantic segmentation is usually scored by per-class IoU and its mean over classes (mIoU), computed from a pixel confusion matrix; panoptic quality (PQ) multiplies a segmentation-quality term (mean IoU of matched segments) by a recognition-quality term (an F1 score over segments) [65]. Benchmarks such as Cityscapes, with 5,000 finely annotated urban images, drove much of the progress for mobile robots and vehicles [66].

4.2 Classical segmentation

Classical methods group pixels by similarity without semantic labels. Clustering in colour space with k-means [67,68] is the simplest. Watershed segmentation treats the gradient magnitude as a topographic surface and floods it from minima [69]. Graph-based methods represent pixels as nodes and similarities as edge weights: normalized cuts partition the graph by minimizing a cut cost normalized by the association of each part [70]; Felzenszwalb and Huttenlocher's greedy algorithm merges regions in nearly linear time using an adaptive internal-difference criterion [71]; and interactive graph cuts, including GrabCut, compute a globally optimal foreground–background labelling from user seeds via min-cut/max-flow [72,73]. These methods remain useful on robots as fast, training-free pre-processing, and the same graph formulations reappear as post-processing for learned models [74].

4.3 Deep segmentation

Fully convolutional networks (FCNs) replaced the fully connected layers of classification networks with convolutions and upsampling, producing dense per-pixel predictions trained end to end [75]. U-Net added a symmetric decoder with skip connections from encoder to decoder, enabling precise boundaries from few training images [76]. DeepLab introduced atrous (dilated) convolution to enlarge the receptive field without losing resolution, atrous spatial pyramid pooling for multi-scale context, and a fully connected conditional random field to sharpen boundaries [74]. For instances, Mask R-CNN adds a small mask-prediction branch to Faster R-CNN and replaces quantized region pooling with bilinear "RoIAlign" so that masks align with pixels [43]. Mask2Former uses a transformer decoder with masked attention to handle semantic, instance and panoptic segmentation with a single architecture [77].

The Segment Anything Model (SAM) changed how robots use segmentation. Trained on a dataset of 1.1 billion masks over 11 million images, it returns masks for objects indicated by points, boxes or masks, including object types it was never explicitly labelled with [47]; SAM 2 extends this to video with a streaming memory [78]. Combining an open-vocabulary detector with SAM gives language-queryable instance masks of arbitrary objects, a pattern widely used in recent open-vocabulary robot mapping systems [79].

5 3D reconstruction and point-cloud processing

5.1 Geometry of multiple views

A calibrated pinhole camera maps a 3-D point X to pixel coordinates via x ≃ K[R | t]X, where K holds the intrinsic parameters and (R, t) the camera pose (calibration is covered in Sensors §3.2–3.3) [2,80]. Two views of the same scene are related by epipolar geometry: corresponding normalized image points x1 and x2 satisfy x2T E x1 = 0, where the essential matrix E = [t]×R encodes the relative rotation and the direction of translation [2,15]. Because only the direction of translation is recoverable, a reconstruction from images alone has an unknown global scale.

x2T E x1 = 0,    E = [t]× R,    F = K2−T E K1−1

E can be estimated linearly from eight correspondences [15], with coordinate normalization making the linear algorithm numerically competitive with iterative methods [81], or from the minimal five correspondences using Nistér's algorithm [82]. Since feature matches always contain outliers, these solvers are wrapped in RANSAC, which repeatedly fits a model to a random minimal sample and keeps the hypothesis with the largest consensus set [16]. Given the pose, each correspondence is triangulated to a 3-D point.

5.2 Structure from motion and multi-view stereo

Structure from motion (SfM) extends two-view geometry to hundreds or thousands of images by incrementally registering new cameras and triangulating new points, then refining all cameras and points jointly with bundle adjustment, a sparse nonlinear least-squares minimization of reprojection error [83]. Photo Tourism demonstrated SfM on unordered internet photo collections [84], and COLMAP is a widely used open-source incremental SfM system [85]. Multi-view stereo (MVS) then computes dense geometry given the camera poses; the patch-based PMVS algorithm, for example, expands and filters a set of small oriented patches [86]. Dense two-view stereo, the special case used on stereo cameras, is described in Sensors §3.5 [87,88].

5.3 Depth fusion and surface reconstruction

Robots more often reconstruct from a moving depth camera. Curless and Levoy's volumetric method stores in each voxel a truncated signed distance function (TSDF) to the nearest surface and a weight, averaging new range images into the grid [89]. KinectFusion combined TSDF fusion with ICP-based camera tracking against the model to build dense surfaces in real time with a handheld Kinect [34,40]. Surfaces are extracted from such volumes with marching cubes [90]; from unorganized points they can be reconstructed by estimating oriented tangent planes and a signed distance function [91] or by solving a Poisson equation for an indicator function whose gradient matches the oriented normals [92].

5.4 Learned scene representations

Neural radiance fields (NeRF) represent a scene as a multilayer perceptron that maps a 3-D position and viewing direction to volume density and emitted colour, optimized so that volume-rendered images match the input photographs [45]. 3-D Gaussian splatting instead optimizes a set of anisotropic 3-D Gaussians that can be rasterized at real-time (30 frames per second or more) rates at 1080p resolution [46]. Both produce photorealistic novel views and are used for robot simulation and mapping. DUSt3R regresses dense "pointmaps" directly from pairs of uncalibrated images, recovering geometry and camera parameters without classical SfM [93]. Learned monocular depth networks complete the picture when only one camera is available (Sensors §3.5) [94].

5.5 Point-cloud processing

A point cloud is an unordered set of 3-D points, possibly with colour, intensity and normals, produced by lidar, depth cameras or stereo (Sensors §4). Libraries such as the Point Cloud Library (PCL) and Open3D implement the standard operations [95,96]:

  • Spatial indexing. k-d trees answer nearest-neighbour and radius queries efficiently and underpin almost every other step [97].
  • Downsampling and filtering. A voxel grid replaces all points in each voxel by their centroid, giving uniform density; statistical filters remove isolated outliers [95].
  • Normal estimation. The normal at a point is the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue of the covariance of its neighbours, i.e. the normal of the least-squares tangent plane [91].
  • Model fitting. RANSAC fits planes, cylinders and spheres in the presence of clutter, for example to remove a floor or tabletop [16].
  • Clustering and segmentation. Euclidean clustering grows regions of points closer than a distance threshold, isolating objects that rest on a removed support plane [98].
  • Registration. ICP aligns two clouds by alternating nearest-neighbour correspondence and closed-form rigid alignment; the point-to-plane error introduced by Chen and Medioni converges in fewer iterations on smooth surfaces [34,35,99]. Global registration uses local descriptors such as fast point feature histograms (FPFH) to obtain an initial alignment [100]. ICP is implemented in Sensors §4.3.
  • Learning on points. PointNet processes raw points with a shared per-point network and a symmetric max-pooling function, making it invariant to point order [44]; PointNet++ applies it hierarchically to local neighbourhoods [101]. For lidar object detection, VoxelNet and PointPillars convert points into voxel or vertical-column ("pillar") features that a 2-D or 3-D convolutional detector can consume [102,103].

6 Object pose and orientation estimation

6.1 Concepts and representations

The 6-DoF pose of a rigid object is the rotation R ∈ SO(3) and translation t ∈ ℝ3 that map points from the object's model frame into the camera (or robot) frame. A manipulator needs it to place a gripper relative to the object, and an assembly robot needs it to millimetre precision [4]. Rotations have several parameterizations, each with trade-offs: rotation matrices (nine numbers with orthonormality constraints), Euler angles (three numbers, but with singularities known as gimbal lock), axis–angle vectors, and unit quaternions (four numbers with a double cover, q and −q representing the same rotation) [4,104]. For neural networks the choice matters: Zhou and colleagues proved that every representation of 3-D rotations in four or fewer real dimensions is discontinuous, and showed that a continuous six-dimensional representation (two columns of the rotation matrix, orthonormalized) trains more accurately [105].

Pose estimation is instance-level when a CAD model or scan of the specific object is available, and category-level when the system must estimate the pose and size of previously unseen members of a class, for instance any mug; the normalized object coordinate space (NOCS) introduced a shared canonical frame for the latter [106]. Accuracy is commonly evaluated with the average distance between model points transformed by the estimated and true poses, and with the visible surface discrepancy and related metrics of the BOP benchmark, which standardized datasets and evaluation for 6-D pose estimation [107,108].

6.2 Geometric solvers

When 2-D image points are matched to known 3-D model points, the pose follows from the perspective-n-point (PnP) problem. Three correspondences give up to four solutions [109]; EPnP solves the general case in time linear in the number of points by expressing the model points as weighted sums of four virtual control points [110]. As with relative pose, PnP is run inside RANSAC to reject wrong matches and refined by Levenberg–Marquardt minimization of reprojection error [16,111]. When 3-D points are observed directly by a depth sensor, the rigid transformation minimizing squared point distances has a closed-form solution via the singular value decomposition (Kabsch, Umeyama) or unit quaternions (Horn) [104,112,113]; Umeyama's version also guards against returning a reflection. These closed forms are the inner step of ICP, which refines a coarse pose against a depth image [34].

Fiducial markers make the correspondence problem trivial: AprilTag and ArUco markers encode an identifier in a binary pattern whose four corners give a PnP solution, and they are widely used to calibrate cameras to robots and to track objects in experiments [114,115].

6.3 Learned pose estimation

Classical template matching, exemplified by LINEMOD's gradient-orientation and surface-normal templates, handled texture-less industrial objects and contributed a widely used benchmark of 15 objects [108]. Learning-based methods now dominate:

  • Direct regression. PoseCNN segments each object, estimates its 3-D translation by localizing its centre and predicting its depth, and regresses rotation as a quaternion; it also introduced the YCB-Video dataset [116].
  • Keypoints plus PnP. DOPE predicts the projected corners of each object's 3-D bounding box and solves PnP, and was trained purely on synthetic data that mixed domain-randomized and photorealistic images, yet was accurate enough for real robotic grasping [117]. PVNet predicts, for every pixel, a vector pointing towards each keypoint and recovers keypoints by RANSAC voting, which makes it robust to occlusion [118].
  • RGB-D fusion. DenseFusion fuses colour and geometric features per pixel and refines the pose iteratively [119].
  • Novel objects. FoundationPose estimates and tracks the pose of objects unseen during training, given either a CAD model or a few reference images, without fine-tuning [48].

Training data is the main practical constraint. Domain randomization, which renders synthetic training images with randomized textures, lighting and camera positions so that the real world appears to the network as just another variation, was shown to enable object localization to about 1.5 cm using simulated images alone [120], and it is now a standard tool for pose networks [117].

7 Human detection, skeleton tracking and gesture recognition

7.1 Why robots need to perceive people

Robots that share space with people must detect them for safety, track them to predict where they are going, estimate their body pose to hand over objects or imitate demonstrations, and recognize gestures and actions as a channel of communication [121]. Collaborative industrial robots, for example, may use person detection and distance measurement to implement the speed-and-separation monitoring mode described in ISO/TS 15066, in which the robot slows or stops as a person approaches [122].

7.2 Person detection

Pedestrian detection was a driving problem for the HOG descriptor [20] and for deformable part models, which represent a person as a root filter plus flexible parts [52]. Modern systems use the general-purpose detectors of Section 3 with "person" as one class, often coupled to an instance segmenter [41,43], and fuse detections with depth or lidar to obtain 3-D positions.

7.3 Body pose estimation

Human pose estimation localizes anatomical keypoints (joints such as shoulders, elbows and wrists) and connects them into a skeleton. Its intellectual roots are the pictorial structures of Fischler and Elschlager, which model an object as parts connected by spring-like constraints [123], made efficient for human bodies by Felzenszwalb and Huttenlocher [124]. The Microsoft Kinect brought real-time skeletal tracking to consumer devices: its algorithm classifies every pixel of a depth image into body parts with a randomized decision forest and proposes 3-D joint positions from the part distributions, running at about 200 frames per second on the console's hardware [39,125].

With deep learning, DeepPose regressed joint coordinates directly with a CNN [126]; convolutional pose machines instead predicted a heatmap per joint and refined them over several stages [127]. Two strategies emerged for multiple people. Top-down methods detect each person and then estimate a single pose inside each box; HRNet, which maintains high-resolution feature maps throughout the network, is a strong example [128], as is the transformer-based ViTPose [129]. Bottom-up methods detect all keypoints in the image and then group them; OpenPose groups keypoints using part affinity fields, 2-D vector fields that encode the location and orientation of limbs, so its runtime is nearly independent of the number of people [130].

For 3-D, datasets such as Human3.6M, with 3.6 million 3-D human poses captured with motion capture, made learning-based 3-D pose estimation possible [131]. Parametric body models go beyond skeletons to full body shape: SMPL represents a body with a template mesh of 6,890 vertices deformed by shape parameters and by the rotations of 23 joints plus a root [132], and HMR regresses SMPL parameters from a single image [133]. For on-device use, MediaPipe Hands predicts 21 3-D hand landmarks and BlazePose 33 body keypoints in real time on mobile phones [134,135].

7.4 Multi-person tracking

Tracking associates detections over time so that each person keeps a consistent identity. The SORT tracker showed that a simple design, a constant-velocity Kalman filter per track [8] and Hungarian assignment of detections to predicted tracks using IoU [61], with a good detector, gives competitive accuracy while running at 260 Hz [136]. DeepSORT adds a learned appearance descriptor to the association cost, reducing identity switches by 45% [137], and ByteTrack also associates low-confidence detections, recovering people who are partly occluded [138]. Tracking accuracy is measured with the CLEAR MOT metrics (MOTA and MOTP) [139] and the more balanced HOTA metric [140].

7.5 Gesture and action recognition

Gestures are deliberate, communicative movements of the hands, arms or body; their recognition has been studied with hidden Markov models, finite-state machines, neural networks and template matching [141]. An early influential system recognized sentence-level American Sign Language from video in real time with hidden Markov models [142]. Once a pose estimator provides skeletons, gesture recognition becomes classification of multivariate time series. Dynamic time warping (DTW), originally developed for spoken-word recognition, aligns two sequences by finding the monotonic warping path of minimum cumulative distance, making comparison invariant to differences in speed [143]. Deep models treat the skeleton sequence as a spatio-temporal graph; ST-GCN applies graph convolutions over joints and time [144] and was evaluated on datasets such as NTU RGB+D, which contains more than 56,000 skeleton sequences of 60 action classes [145].

8 Visual odometry and SLAM

8.1 Concepts

Visual odometry (VO) estimates a camera's ego-motion incrementally from its image stream. Simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) estimates the robot's trajectory and a map of the environment jointly, and, crucially, recognizes when the robot returns to a previously mapped place (loop closure) so that accumulated drift can be corrected [9,146]. VO is therefore a building block of visual SLAM: it is accurate locally but drifts without bound, whereas a SLAM system with loop closures produces a globally consistent map that the robot can later re-localize in [9,147]. Lidar and depth cameras support the same formulations (lidar odometry and mapping is introduced in Sensors §4.3), and fusing an IMU makes the motion scale observable for a monocular camera and bridges fast motion (Sensors §6.3) [148–152].

8.2 History

Moravec's Stanford Cart (1980) navigated by detecting distinctive image points and triangulating them with a sliding camera, an early instance of what would later be called visual odometry [32,147]. The probabilistic formulation of SLAM grew from work by Smith, Self and Cheeseman, who represented the robot and landmark positions in a single state vector with a full covariance matrix, the "stochastic map" [33]; the structure of the problem and the acronym SLAM were presented in the mid-1990s [146]. Nistér, Naroditsky and Bergen coined the term "visual odometry" in 2004 for a real-time system that estimated camera motion from tracked features [36], and VO was used operationally on NASA's Mars Exploration Rovers, where it corrected wheel slip on steep and sandy terrain [153]. Davison's MonoSLAM demonstrated real-time SLAM with a single camera using an extended Kalman filter [37]; Klein and Murray's PTAM split tracking and mapping into parallel threads, using keyframes and bundle adjustment rather than filtering [38]. ORB-SLAM consolidated the keyframe approach with ORB features for tracking, mapping, relocalization and loop closing [154,155], and was extended to stereo and RGB-D cameras and then to visual-inertial and multi-map operation in ORB-SLAM2 and ORB-SLAM3 [156,157].

8.3 Visual odometry algorithms

Feature-based (indirect) VO detects keypoints such as Harris or Shi–Tomasi corners [17,18], FAST corners [158] or ORB features, an oriented FAST detector with a rotation-aware binary descriptor about two orders of magnitude faster to compute than SIFT [19,155]. Points are matched by descriptor or tracked with the Lucas–Kanade optical-flow method [159]; motion is then estimated from 2-D/2-D correspondences with the five-point algorithm, from 3-D/2-D correspondences with PnP, or from 3-D/3-D correspondences with closed-form alignment, always inside RANSAC [16,82,110,147]. A sliding window of recent frames is refined by local bundle adjustment [83]. Direct methods skip keypoints and minimize photometric error over image intensities: LSD-SLAM tracks semi-dense high-gradient regions [160], and direct sparse odometry (DSO) jointly optimizes poses, inverse depths and a photometric camera model over a window of keyframes [161]. Learned systems such as DROID-SLAM replace hand-designed components with a recurrent network that iteratively updates optical flow and is coupled to a differentiable dense bundle-adjustment layer [162].

8.4 SLAM back ends: filters, smoothing and pose graphs

Early SLAM systems used the extended Kalman filter (EKF) over the joint robot–landmark state, which scales quadratically with the number of landmarks [33,146]. FastSLAM factored the posterior with a particle filter over trajectories and independent small filters per landmark [163]. The dominant modern formulation is smoothing as nonlinear least squares over a factor graph: variables are robot poses (and possibly landmarks), and factors encode odometry, observations and loop closures as probabilistic constraints [164,165]. Lu and Milios formulated globally consistent scan alignment as such a network of pose constraints in 1997 [166]. Sparse solvers such as g2o optimize these graphs efficiently [167], and iSAM2 updates the solution incrementally as new measurements arrive using a data structure called the Bayes tree [168]. For a pose graph, the maximum a posteriori estimate is

X* = argminX Σ(i,j) ‖ log( Zij−1 Xi−1 Xj ) ‖2Ωij

where Xi are poses, Zij is the measured relative pose between nodes i and j, and Ωij its information matrix [164,165].

Loop closure requires recognizing a previously seen place despite changes in viewpoint and lighting. Bag-of-binary-words methods such as DBoW2 quantize binary descriptors into a vocabulary tree and retrieve candidate images in milliseconds [169]; learned global descriptors such as NetVLAD improve robustness to appearance change [170]. Candidates are verified geometrically before being added to the graph, because a single false loop closure can corrupt the whole map [9].

8.5 Maps and evaluation

The map a SLAM system builds depends on its use: sparse landmark maps for localization [154], dense surfaces for interaction [40], and occupancy grids for navigation. Occupancy grids, introduced by Moravec and Elfes for sonar, divide space into cells that store the probability of being occupied, updated with a Bayesian log-odds rule as range measurements arrive [5,171,172]; OctoMap extends them to 3-D with octrees [173]. Systems are evaluated on benchmarks such as KITTI for driving [174] and the TUM RGB-D benchmark, which defined the absolute trajectory error (ATE) and relative pose error (RPE) metrics used above [175].

9 Scene understanding and spatial relationships

9.1 From objects to scenes

Detecting objects and reconstructing geometry is not yet understanding a scene. A robot asked to "put the apple in the bowl next to the sink" must know which surfaces support which objects, what is inside or on top of what, which objects are reachable or movable, and how phrases such as "next to" or "left of" map onto metric geometry. Research on holistic scene understanding showed early that reasoning jointly about objects, surfaces and camera viewpoint improves each of them; Hoiem, Efros and Hebert, for example, used the relation between object size, position in the image and the horizon to improve detection [176]. With depth sensing, Silberman and colleagues inferred physical support relations ("the cup is supported by the table") from RGB-D images in the NYU Depth V2 dataset of 1,449 densely labelled indoor scenes [177]. Large annotated 3-D scans such as ScanNet support learning of 3-D semantic segmentation and object detection in indoor environments [178].

The notion of affordances, introduced by the psychologist James Gibson to describe the possibilities for action that the environment offers an animal [179], is central for robots: what matters about a mug is that its handle is graspable and its interior can contain liquid. Affordance detection networks predict such functional regions pixel-wise [180].

9.2 Scene graphs

A scene graph represents a scene as nodes (objects with attributes) connected by edges (relationships such as "on", "holding" or "left of"). Johnson and colleagues introduced scene graphs for image retrieval [181]; the Visual Genome dataset annotated more than 100,000 images with dense objects, attributes and pairwise relationships [182]; and scene-graph generation methods predict such graphs directly from images [183]. For robots, scene graphs have been lifted into 3-D and organized hierarchically. Armeni and colleagues proposed a four-layer 3-D scene graph spanning buildings, rooms, objects and cameras [184]; 3-D dynamic scene graphs added places, structures and moving agents such as people on top of a metric-semantic mesh [185]; Hydra builds such graphs incrementally in real time [186]; and ConceptGraphs builds open-vocabulary 3-D scene graphs by fusing outputs of 2-D foundation models across views so that objects and relations can be queried in natural language [79].

9.3 Computing spatial relationships

Spatial predicates can be computed from 3-D object estimates in a gravity-aligned frame, which a robot obtains from its SLAM pose and IMU. Topological relations such as "on" and "inside" depend on contact and containment; proximity relations such as "near" depend on distances; and projective relations such as "left of" depend on a reference frame, which may be the viewer's, the robot's or the object's own [177,185]. 9.4 Bird's-eye-view and occupancy representations

Mobile robots and vehicles often reason in a bird's-eye-view (BEV) grid, where spatial relations to drivable space and other agents are simple to express. Lift-Splat-Shoot lifts each camera's image features into 3-D using a predicted per-pixel depth distribution and "splats" them onto a BEV grid [187]; BEVFormer queries multi-camera features with spatio-temporal transformers [188]; and BEVFusion merges camera and lidar features in a shared BEV space (Sensors §9) [189]. These representations are evaluated on driving datasets such as nuScenes [190].

9.5 Language, foundation models and spatial reasoning

Vision-language models connect perception to instructions. VLMaps fuses pretrained visual-language features into a 3-D reconstruction so that a robot can localize landmarks such as "the sofa" or execute spatial goals such as "between the TV and the sofa" [191]. SayCan combines a large language model's knowledge of which steps are useful with learned value functions that estimate which skills are feasible in the current scene [192]. PaLM-E injects continuous visual and state observations into a 562-billion-parameter language model for embodied reasoning [193], and RT-2 fine-tunes vision-language models to output robot actions directly [6]. Spatial reasoning remains a weakness of models trained mostly on 2-D image–text data; SpatialVLM addressed it by generating two billion spatial question–answer pairs from 10 million real images with 3-D lifting, improving quantitative distance and size estimates [194]. Explicit 3-D scene graphs and metric maps therefore remain valuable as grounding and verification for language-driven robot behaviour [79,186].

10 Engineering vision for robots that act

Vision models that perform well on benchmarks can still fail on robots. Several practical concerns apply across every capability in this article.

  • Calibration and frames. Every metric output (a pose, a point, a relation) is only as good as the camera intrinsics and the camera-to-robot extrinsics; hand–eye and multi-sensor calibration should be treated as part of the perception system (Sensors §9.1) [80,195].
  • Latency and rate. Control loops need perception results with bounded delay. Detection, segmentation and pose networks must be sized for the onboard computer, and fast trackers such as SORT or VO front ends can bridge the gaps between slower recognition updates [42,136].
  • Uncertainty. Downstream planners need to know when perception is unreliable. Kendall and Gal distinguish aleatoric uncertainty (noise inherent in the observation) from epistemic uncertainty (lack of model knowledge) and show how deep vision models can estimate both [7]; probabilistic estimators such as Kalman filters and factor graphs propagate covariance explicitly [5,164].
  • Robustness and distribution shift. Accuracy degrades under blur, noise, weather and digital corruptions that humans barely notice, as the ImageNet-C benchmark showed [196]. Robots should be tested in their target lighting and clutter, and complementary sensors whose failure modes do not coincide should be fused [197].
  • Data and simulation. Labelled data for robot-specific tasks (6-D poses, contact relations) is expensive. Synthetic data with domain randomization [117,120], self-supervised foundation features [31] and promptable models [47,48] reduce the need for manual labels.
  • Closing the loop. A robot can act to perceive better: move to resolve occlusion, push objects apart to segment them, or re-observe a place to close a loop. Perception for physical AI is therefore best evaluated by task success, not only by per-frame metrics [4,9].

Conclusion

Computer vision has moved from the hand-built geometric models of the 1960s, through decades of projective geometry, invariant features and probabilistic estimation, to learned foundation models that can recognize, segment and localize objects they were never explicitly trained on. For physical AI, these advances matter only when their outputs are metric, temporally consistent and calibrated to the robot's body: a detection becomes a 6-DoF pose, a stream of poses becomes a map, and a map annotated with objects and relations becomes a world model that a planner or a language-conditioned policy can act on. The classical algorithms shown in this article, from RANSAC and PnP to Kalman tracking, pose-graph optimization and occupancy grids, remain the scaffolding into which learned components are inserted, and understanding both is what allows engineers to build robots that see reliably enough to act.
