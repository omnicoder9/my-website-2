1 Why physical AI needs generative and predictive models

A discriminative or regression model maps an input to a single output: an image to a label, a state to an action. A generative model instead learns a probability distribution p(x), or a conditional distribution p(x | c), from which new samples can be drawn [1,2]. A predictive model is a conditional generative model whose output lies in the future: the next state of a system given its present state and an action, the next frames of a video, or the trajectories other agents will follow [2,3]. In physical AI, the robots, vehicles and other embodied systems that sense and act in the real world, the two ideas meet constantly, because the quantities a robot cares about are rarely single-valued.

Three properties of the physical world make distributions, rather than point estimates, the natural object to learn.

  • Multimodality. There is usually more than one good way to act. A demonstrator may pass an obstacle on the left or on the right, grasp a mug by the handle or the rim. A regressor trained with squared error predicts the average of these choices, which can be an action nobody demonstrated and that fails, such as driving into the obstacle. Mixture density networks, energy-based policies, discretized action transformers and finally diffusion policies were all motivated by this problem [4–7].
  • Stochasticity and partial observability. The same observed state can lead to different futures because of unobserved contact details, friction, or the intentions of other agents. A useful predictive model must therefore represent a spread of outcomes, and its samples should look like real outcomes rather than an implausible blur of them [8–10].
  • Data scarcity at the tails. Robots fail on rare situations: unusual clutter, lighting, or a car that cuts in sharply. Generative models of scenes and scenarios let engineers create such situations deliberately, at scale, instead of waiting for them to occur [11–13].

Figure 1 shows where generative and predictive models enter a robot learning system. They can act as the policy itself, generating actions or whole trajectories conditioned on observations; as the world model, predicting states, images or other agents' motion; and as the data engine, producing environments, augmented demonstrations and safety-critical test cases. Since 2020 one family of techniques, denoising diffusion and its close relative flow matching, has become the common machinery behind all three roles [14–17].

2 A short history: from Boltzmann machines to diffusion policies

2.1 Learning distributions with neural networks (1985–2013)

Generative modelling with neural networks began with the Boltzmann machine, an energy-based model whose units are sampled stochastically and whose learning rule matches the statistics of the model to those of the data [21]. The Helmholtz machine introduced the pairing of a generative network with a recognition network that infers latent causes, the ancestor of today's encoders and decoders [22]. For control and robotics, an influential idea of the 1990s was the mixture density network, which lets a neural network output the parameters of a Gaussian mixture and so represent multi-valued mappings such as inverse kinematics, where several joint configurations reach the same hand position [4]. Energy-based learning provided a general framework in which a model assigns low energy to compatible input–output pairs and inference means minimizing energy [23].

2.2 The deep generative model boom (2013–2019)

Between 2013 and 2016 four families of deep generative models appeared in quick succession. Variational autoencoders (VAEs) made latent-variable models trainable by backpropagation through a reparameterized sample [24,25]. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) trained a generator against a discriminator and produced the first sharp synthetic images, at the price of unstable training and "mode collapse", in which the generator covers only part of the data distribution [26,27]. Autoregressive models factorized a distribution into a product of conditionals and generated one element at a time [28]; with discrete latent codes and later the transformer, autoregression became the basis of token-based image, video and action models [29,30]. Normalizing flows built exact, invertible density models from chains of simple transformations [31–33], and neural ordinary differential equations generalized them to continuous time [34]. In robotics these models were used for video prediction [8,35,36], for planning with visual foresight [37,38], for latent world models [39,40] and, through GANs, for making simulated images look real [41,42].

2.3 Score matching and diffusion (2005–2021)

A separate line of work studied how to fit unnormalized densities. Score matching learns the gradient of the log-density, the score, without computing a normalizing constant [43], and Vincent showed that training a denoising autoencoder is equivalent to score matching on noise-perturbed data [44]. Langevin dynamics turns a score into a sampler [45]. In 2015 Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues proposed diffusion probabilistic models, which destroy data with a gradual noising process and learn to reverse it [46]. Song and Ermon trained score networks at many noise levels and sampled by annealed Langevin dynamics [47], and in 2020 Ho, Jain and Abbeel's denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM) reached image quality competitive with GANs using a remarkably simple noise-prediction loss [14]. Song and colleagues then unified both views as stochastic differential equations whose time reversal, a classical result of Anderson [48], generates data; the same framework yields a deterministic "probability-flow" ordinary differential equation (ODE) [15]. Within two years improvements in noise schedules, samplers, guidance and architectures made diffusion the dominant method for image generation [49–55].

2.4 Flow matching and the move into robotics (2022–2026)

In 2022 three groups independently proposed training continuous normalizing flows by simple regression onto the velocity of a prescribed path between noise and data: flow matching [16], rectified flow [56] and stochastic interpolants [57]. Flow matching keeps the scalability of diffusion training, includes diffusion as a special case, and makes straight sampling paths, and hence fast sampling, easy to encourage [58,59]; it was adopted for large image generators [60].

Robotics adopted these tools rapidly. Diffuser (2022) generated entire state–action trajectories with a diffusion model and planned by guiding the sampler [18]; Decision Diffuser conditioned generation on returns and constraints [61]; and diffusion was used to imitate human behaviour in games [62] and as the policy class in offline reinforcement learning [63]. Diffusion Policy (2023) showed that denoising short chunks of future actions conditioned on images gave large gains over earlier behaviour-cloning methods on real manipulation tasks [7], and it was quickly extended to 3D inputs, goal conditioning, faster samplers and in-the-wild data collection [64–67]. Generalist robot policies followed: Octo used a diffusion action head [68], RDT-1B scaled a diffusion transformer for bimanual manipulation [69], and π0 and GR00T N1 attached flow-matching action experts to vision-language backbones [70,71]. In parallel, video diffusion models were turned into policies and simulators [72–74] and into "world foundation models" for physical AI [19], while diffusion and procedural generation began to supply scenes, traffic scenarios and augmented demonstrations [75–78].

3.2 Denoising diffusion

A diffusion model defines a forward process that adds Gaussian noise to a data sample x0 over T steps. With a variance schedule βt, αt = 1 − βt and ᾱt = ∏s≤t αs, the noisy sample at any step can be drawn in closed form [14,46]:

xt = √ᾱt x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) ε,    ε ~ N(0, I)

As ᾱT → 0, xT is pure noise. A network εθ(xt, t) is trained to predict the noise that was added, with the simple loss proposed for DDPM [14]:

L(θ) = Ex0, t, ε ‖ ε − εθ(√ᾱt x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) ε, t) ‖²

This is a re-weighted variational bound on the likelihood, and it is also denoising score matching: the optimal noise predictor is proportional to the score, ∇x log pt(xt) = −εθ(xt, t) / √(1 − ᾱt), a consequence of Tweedie's formula [15,44,82]. Generation runs the process backwards. DDPM's ancestral sampler removes the predicted noise and adds a little fresh noise at each of the T steps [14]; the deterministic DDIM sampler follows the probability-flow ODE and can skip steps [50]; higher-order ODE solvers and distillation reduce the number of network evaluations further [83–85]. Practical details matter: the cosine noise schedule [49], the parameterization of the network output and loss weighting [54], running diffusion in the latent space of an autoencoder [53] and using transformer denoisers [55] are all now standard.

Two features make diffusion especially useful for control. First, the loss is a plain regression, so training is as stable as supervised learning, yet the learned sampler covers all modes of the data, unlike a GAN [7,51]. Second, sampling is an iterative procedure that can be steered: by conditioning on observations or goals, by classifier-free guidance, which mixes conditional and unconditional predictions [52], by inpainting known parts of the output [86], or by adding the gradient of a cost or likelihood to each denoising step [18,51,87].

3.3 Flow matching and rectified flow

Flow matching starts from the ODE view. Choose a path that moves a noise sample x0 ~ N(0, I) to a data sample x1; the simplest is the straight line xt = (1 − t) x0 + t x1, whose velocity is x1 − x0. A network vθ(x, t) is trained to regress this velocity [16,56,57]:

L(θ) = Et, x0, x1 ‖ (x1 − x0) − vθ((1 − t) x0 + t x1, t) ‖²,    dx/dt = vθ(x, t)

Although each training pair moves in a straight line, many lines cross, and the minimizer is their conditional average, so the learned flow follows curved paths; Lipman and colleagues proved that this "conditional" objective has the same gradients as matching the true marginal velocity field [16]. Integrating the ODE from t = 0 to 1 transports the noise distribution to the data distribution. Diffusion's probability-flow ODE is one particular choice of path, so the two frameworks are closely related [15,59]. Paths can be made straighter, and sampling cheaper, by pairing noise and data with minibatch optimal transport [58] or by reflow: generate (noise, sample) pairs with the current model and retrain on them, which yields nearly straight trajectories that can be integrated in very few steps [56]. Flow matching extends to curved spaces such as rotations, which matters for robot poses [88,89].

4 Diffusion-based action generation

4.1 Policies as conditional generative models

A policy π(a | o) is a conditional distribution over actions given observations, so any conditional generative model can serve as one. Behavioural cloning with a Gaussian or squared-error loss is the special case of a unimodal distribution; when demonstrations are multimodal its mean can be an action nobody took [4,6,17]. Before diffusion, three remedies were common: mixture density outputs [4], discretizing actions into bins and predicting them with a transformer as in Behavior Transformers [6], and implicit, energy-based policies that choose the action of lowest learned energy [5]. Each has drawbacks: a fixed number of mixture components, discretization error, or unstable training and costly optimization at run time [7].

Diffusion policies represent π(a | o) by a conditional noise predictor εθ(at, o, t) and draw actions by iterative denoising, starting from Gaussian noise [7,62,63]. Pearce and colleagues showed that such policies reproduce the full spread of human behaviour in video games better than mixture or discretized policies [62], and Wang, Hunt and Zhou used them as an expressive policy class for offline reinforcement learning [63].

4.2 Diffusion Policy: action chunks and receding horizons

Diffusion Policy by Chi and colleagues established the recipe now used throughout robot manipulation [7]. Its key design choices are:

  • Action sequences. The model denoises a sequence of future actions (a prediction horizon of several steps) conditioned on a short history of observations, instead of one action at a time. The idea of predicting action chunks was introduced concurrently in ACT, which used a conditional VAE and averaged overlapping chunks [91].
  • Receding-horizon execution. Only the first part of the predicted sequence is executed before the policy re-plans, as in model predictive control. This commits the robot to one mode for several steps, giving temporally consistent behaviour, while still reacting to new observations [7].
  • Visual conditioning. Image features enter the denoiser as a conditioning signal (via FiLM layers in a 1-D temporal convolutional network, or via cross-attention in a transformer), so the expensive visual encoder runs once per decision, not once per denoising step [7].
  • Fast deterministic sampling. Training uses many noise levels, but inference uses DDIM with far fewer steps to meet real-time constraints [7,50].

Across 12 tasks from four benchmarks, Diffusion Policy reported an average improvement of 46.9% over the previous state of the art, on both simulated and real robots [7]. Subsequent work used point-cloud inputs (3D Diffusion Policy) to improve data efficiency and generalization [64], score-based goal-conditioned variants that sample in a few steps [65], distillation into one- or two-step consistency policies [66], and portable hand-held grippers to collect training data outside the lab [67]. Diffusion action heads also became a component of generalist policies trained on large multi-robot datasets [68,69,92], which the foundation models article discusses.

4.3 Diffusion over trajectories: planning by sampling

Diffuser models whole trajectories of states and actions with a diffusion model and turns planning into conditional sampling [18]. Constraints such as a start state and a goal are imposed by inpainting, overwriting the known parts of the trajectory at each denoising step, and objectives are imposed by guidance, adding the gradient of a reward or cost model to each step, a technique borrowed from classifier guidance in image generation [18,51]. Decision Diffuser instead conditions on returns, constraints or skills with classifier-free guidance and recovers actions with an inverse-dynamics model [52,61]. In robot motion planning, diffusion models learned from good trajectories serve as priors that are combined with collision and goal costs during sampling [93], diffusion over the group of rigid-body poses SE(3) produces grasp distributions usable as costs in trajectory optimization [94], and control barrier functions can be embedded in the sampler to guarantee constraint satisfaction [95]. In autonomous driving the same machinery generates and controls multi-agent futures [10,76].

Inpainting imposes the start and goal exactly, but without guidance about two-thirds of the paths pass through the new obstacle, roughly as often as the training paths do. Adding the gradient of a collision cost to the denoised estimate removes nearly all collisions at the cost of slightly rougher paths; the paths stay reasonably smooth because each guided step is followed by further denoising that pulls the trajectory back toward the learned distribution. This is the appeal of trajectory diffusion: the learned model supplies what typical, feasible behaviour looks like, and task-specific constraints are added only at sampling time [18,87,93]. Guidance gives no hard guarantee, which is why constraint-enforcing variants exist [95].

4.4 Speed, latency and reliability

The main practical cost of diffusion policies is inference time: every action chunk needs several network evaluations. Remedies include deterministic samplers with few steps [50,83], distillation into consistency models [66,85], shortcut and one-step models [90], and flow matching with straight paths (Section 5). Because a chunk is computed while the previous one executes, delays also create discontinuities between chunks; real-time chunking treats the next chunk as an inpainting problem that must agree with actions already committed [96]. Finally, the stochasticity that makes diffusion expressive also makes behaviour vary between runs, so evaluations need many trials and confidence intervals rather than a single rollout [17].

5 Flow-matching policies

5.1 Why flow matching for actions

Flow-matching policies replace the noise predictor by a velocity field vθ(a, t | o) and generate an action chunk by integrating da/dt = vθ from Gaussian noise for a fixed number of Euler steps [16,56]. For robotics they offer three practical advantages over diffusion. Training is the same simple regression, but there is no noise schedule to tune [59]. Paths from noise to actions are closer to straight, so fewer integration steps are needed, and reflow or consistency-style training can bring this down to one or two [56,90,97]. And the ODE formulation extends naturally to non-Euclidean action spaces such as orientations on SO(3) or SE(3) [88,89].

5.2 Systems

Early robotics papers applied flow matching to imitation learning on manifolds (Riemannian flow matching policies) [89], to multi-support manipulation with a humanoid robot [98], to affordance-conditioned manipulation [99] and to point-cloud-conditioned policies that generate actions in a single step using consistency flow matching [97]. At scale, π0 combined a pretrained vision-language model with a separate "action expert" trained by flow matching to output chunks of continuous actions at high control rates, and was trained on data from several robot platforms [70]. GR00T N1, a model for humanoid robots, pairs a vision-language model with a diffusion transformer trained with a flow-matching objective [71]. An alternative for transformer-based policies is to keep actions discrete and generate them autoregressively; compressing action chunks with a frequency-space tokenizer (FAST) narrowed the gap to flow-matching action experts [80,100]. Which representation is best remains an open question; the foundation models article compares these architectures.

6 Generative scene and environment models

6.1 Why generate environments

A policy trained in a handful of hand-built simulated rooms learns those rooms. Generating large numbers of varied, physically valid environments is one of the most effective ways to obtain policies that generalize, and generative models of scenes are the tool for doing so [11,75,101]. A scene generator must produce three things: plausible content (which objects appear), plausible layout (where they are and how they relate, for example a cup on a table rather than inside it) and simulation-ready assets with geometry, materials and physical properties. The representations involved, from meshes and scene graphs to radiance fields, are described in the representations article.

6.2 Procedural and grammar-based generation

The oldest approach writes the generative model by hand as a program with random choices. Example-based synthesis learned arrangements of objects from a few examples using probabilistic models of object co-occurrence and relative placement [102]; stochastic scene grammars generated indoor scenes together with pixel-perfect ground truth for training perception [103]. ProcTHOR procedurally generated 10,000 interactive houses for embodied AI and showed that agents pretrained on them transferred better to other benchmarks [75], and Infinigen generates photorealistic natural worlds, with every asset produced procedurally from mathematical rules [104]. Meta-Sim learned to adjust the parameters of a procedural scene grammar so that rendered data match the distribution of real images and improve a downstream task [101].

6.3 Learned layout and asset generators

Learned scene models treat a scene as a set of objects with category, position, size and orientation. ATISS generates indoor layouts autoregressively with a transformer, adding one object at a time conditioned on the room and the objects already placed [105], and DiffuScene denoises all object attributes jointly with a diffusion model, which also supports completion and rearrangement [106]. Large language models now act as scene planners: Holodeck turns a text request into a full 3D environment by having a language model propose objects and spatial constraints that a solver satisfies [107], and GenSim and RoboGen use language models to propose new tasks, generate their simulation code and assets, and produce training data for them [108,109]. Assets come from large 3D collections such as Objaverse [110] or from text-to-3D generation, which distils a 2D image diffusion model into a 3D representation [111].

6.4 Reconstruction-based environments

An alternative to inventing scenes is to reconstruct real ones. Neural radiance fields and 3D Gaussian splatting reconstruct photorealistic, renderable scenes from images [112,113]. In driving, neural sensor simulators built from recorded drives re-render camera and lidar data as actors are moved or inserted, creating closed-loop variations of real logs [114]. Real-to-sim pipelines reconstruct a specific workspace so that policies can be fine-tuned in simulation before deployment [115], and "digital cousins" generate a set of similar but not identical simulated scenes from one real scene to improve robustness [116]. Traffic scenes are generated in the same spirit by models learned from driving logs [117,118].

7 Learned dynamics models as generative models

7.1 From point predictions to distributions over futures

A learned dynamics model predicts the next state st+1 from the current state and action. The world models article treats such models in detail, including compounding error over multi-step rollouts [119] and planning with them [9,120,121]. Here the emphasis is on their generative character. When the outcome of an action is uncertain, because of unobserved contact geometry, friction or other agents, the right object to learn is p(st+1 | st, at). Three levels of modelling are common:

  • Deterministic regression predicts the conditional mean. It is adequate for nearly deterministic systems but predicts states between outcomes when the true transition branches [4].
  • Unimodal probabilistic models predict a mean and a variance, typically with ensembles that also capture uncertainty due to limited data, as in PETS [9,122]. They capture spread but not the shape of a branching distribution.
  • Expressive generative models (latent-variable models, autoregressive token models, diffusion or flow matching) represent arbitrary distributions over next states or whole future trajectories [40,81,123,124].

Diffusion has become prominent at the expressive end. DIAMOND trains agents entirely inside a diffusion world model and found that visual details preserved by diffusion matter for performance [123]; GameNGen simulates a complete computer game interactively with a diffusion model [125]; and diffusion world models can predict multiple future steps at once instead of rolling out one step at a time, which reduces compounding error [124]. The physical sciences offer a compelling demonstration of the same idea: GenCast, a conditional diffusion model of the global atmosphere, produces ensemble weather forecasts that outperformed the leading operational ensemble system on most of the targets evaluated [126]. For particle and mesh physics, graph neural network simulators remain the standard learned dynamics models [127,128].

8 Predictive video and interaction models

8.1 From video prediction to video generation

Cameras are the richest sensors most robots carry, so predicting future images is a natural general-purpose predictive task. Action-conditioned video prediction began with deterministic models of game screens [35] and of robot pushing, where Finn and colleagues predicted pixel motion from actions on a dataset of 59,000 robot interactions [36]. Stochastic variational video prediction added latent variables to represent multiple possible futures [8], and visual foresight used such models to plan pushing and grasping actions by imagining their visual outcome [37,38]. The world models article tells this story in detail.

Video diffusion models changed the quality of what can be predicted [72]. Large text-to-video models were presented as "world simulators" [129], and models trained for physical AI generate future video conditioned on text, actions or camera trajectories: UniSim learned an interactive simulator from diverse video data [74], Genie learned controllable environments from unlabelled video with latent actions [130,131], and NVIDIA's Cosmos platform provides pretrained diffusion and autoregressive world foundation models with tokenizers for robotics and driving [19].

8.2 Turning predicted video into action

A video model predicts what will happen, not what the robot should do, so an extra step is needed to obtain actions. UniPi generates a video of the task being completed from a text instruction and then infers actions from consecutive frames with an inverse-dynamics model [73]. Other systems extract dense correspondences or object motion from generated video and convert it to robot commands [132], generate only a goal image or sub-goal with an image-editing diffusion model and reach it with a low-level policy [133], or pretrain a policy network on video prediction before fine-tuning it on robot actions [134]. Latent action models learn an action-like code from unlabelled video so that large video datasets can pretrain policies [130,135]. Another option is to predict in a learned feature space instead of pixels, which avoids modelling irrelevant detail; feature-space world models built on self-supervised video or image encoders have been used for zero-shot planning [3,136,137].

8.3 How physical are video models?

Visually convincing is not the same as physically correct. Systematic tests found that video generation models trained on large data reproduce training-like scenarios well but fail to extrapolate simple physical laws, and appear to generalize by retrieving similar training cases rather than learning the underlying rules [138]. The Physics-IQ benchmark likewise found that current video models' physical understanding is severely limited and unrelated to how realistic their videos look [139]. Human physical intuition has been modelled as approximate probabilistic simulation [140], and combining generative video with explicit physics, object-centric structure or interaction with the real world remains an active research direction. For metrics, Fréchet video distance measures distributional similarity of generated and real video [141], but task success when a model is used for planning or policy evaluation is the more relevant test for robotics [17].

9 Models for generating synthetic training scenarios

9.1 The reality gap and domain randomization

Simulation can produce unlimited labelled data, but models trained on it often fail on real data because simulators differ from reality, the "reality gap" recognized early in evolutionary robotics [142]. Domain randomization addresses it by sampling simulator parameters, such as textures, lighting, camera pose, masses and friction, from wide distributions, so that the real world looks like just another sample [11,143,144]. Tobin and colleagues trained an object detector only on randomized synthetic images and used it to grasp real objects [11]; CAD2RL flew a real drone with a policy trained without any real images [143]; dynamics randomization transferred manipulation policies [144]; and with automatic domain randomization, which widens the parameter ranges as the policy improves, a robot hand trained in simulation solved a Rubik's cube [145]. Randomization ranges can also be adapted from real rollouts so that the simulated distribution moves toward reality [146], and the method has been reviewed comprehensively [147]. Synthetic data with randomized and photorealistic content also trains object detectors for robot perception [148]. A randomized simulator is itself a generative model of training scenarios, written by hand.

9.2 Learned generators: translation and augmentation

Learned generative models make synthetic data more realistic or make real data more varied. Adversarial image translation refined simulated images to look real [41] and, combined with domain adaptation, reduced the number of real grasps needed to train a grasping system [42]; randomized-to-canonical adaptation instead translated both real and randomized images into a canonical simulated appearance [149]. With text-conditioned diffusion models, real demonstrations can be augmented semantically: ROSIE inpaints new objects, distractors and backgrounds into robot videos [77], GenAug retargets demonstrations to unseen scenes and objects [150], and CACTI uses image generation to multiply the visual diversity of collected data [151]. Data can also be synthesized at the level of behaviour: MimicGen adapts a small number of human demonstrations to new object configurations to generate tens of thousands of new ones [78], and DexMimicGen extends this to bimanual dexterous hands [152]. World foundation models generate whole new training videos, either by conditioned "transfer" of simulator renders into photorealistic video [153] or by generating robot videos for new tasks and labelling them with inferred actions [154].

9.3 Generating safety-critical scenarios

For safety validation the aim is not typical data but rare, dangerous situations. Kalra and Paddock estimated that hundreds of millions to hundreds of billions of driven miles would be needed to demonstrate autonomous-vehicle reliability statistically by driving alone [155]. Accelerated evaluation therefore fits a generative model of naturalistic scenarios to driving data and then samples dangerous scenarios more often, correcting the estimate with importance weights; Zhao and colleagues applied this to lane changes [156], and O'Kelly and colleagues used adaptive importance sampling with the cross-entropy method to test end-to-end driving policies in simulation [157–159]. Feng and colleagues trained background vehicles with dense reinforcement learning to create an intelligent testing environment that accelerated evaluation by several orders of magnitude while keeping it unbiased [13]. Learned traffic models extend this: STRIVE optimizes in the latent space of a learned traffic model to produce realistic, accident-prone scenarios [160], AdvSim perturbs actor trajectories to attack a full autonomy stack [161], learned simulators reproduce realistic multi-agent behaviour [162], and guided diffusion generates controllable traffic that obeys user-specified rules [76]. A survey organizes these methods into data-driven, adversarial and knowledge-based generation [12].

10.2 Evaluation

Likelihood, sample quality and usefulness can disagree, so a generative model must be evaluated for the purpose it serves [79]. Image and video generators are usually scored with distribution distances such as the Fréchet inception distance and Fréchet video distance [141,163]. For robotics these are only proxies: a policy is judged by task success over many trials, a predictive model by planning performance and by whether its predicted outcomes are physically possible, a scene generator by the performance of policies trained on its scenes, and a scenario generator by the accuracy of the failure rates it estimates [17,75,139,157]. Because samples are random, results should be reported with confidence intervals.

10.3 Open problems

  • Speed versus quality. Iterative samplers remain expensive on embedded hardware; one-step generators often lose fidelity, as the reflow example shows [66,90,96].
  • Physical consistency. Generated videos, scenes and trajectories can look right and still violate physics; integrating simulators, constraints and object structure with generative models is unresolved [95,138,139].
  • Guarantees. Guidance steers samples but does not certify them; safety-critical uses need constraint enforcement, verification, or a classical fallback [12,95].
  • Data. Generative policies learn exactly what they are shown, including mistakes; augmentation and synthetic data help, but the reality gap persists and must be measured [77,78,147].
  • Evaluation at scale. Generated environments and world models are increasingly proposed as evaluators for policies; validating them against real-world outcomes is itself an open measurement problem [13,19,74].

Conclusion

Generative and predictive models give physical AI a way to learn distributions over actions, futures and worlds rather than single answers. The ideas are decades old, from Boltzmann machines and mixture density networks to score matching, but since 2020 denoising diffusion and flow matching have supplied a stable, scalable and steerable way to learn such distributions. In robotics they now generate action chunks for manipulation policies, plan trajectories under new constraints, predict branching dynamics and future video, build training environments and generate the rare scenarios used to test safety. The examples in this article show the core lessons in miniature: averaging multimodal data produces actions nobody would take; committing to a sampled chunk turns a distribution into a controller; constraints and costs can be added at sampling time; straighter flows trade a little fidelity for much faster sampling; relational structure makes generated scenes valid; generative dynamics predict possible outcomes rather than averages; randomized synthetic data beat narrow simulation; and steering a generator toward failures makes rare-event testing affordable.
