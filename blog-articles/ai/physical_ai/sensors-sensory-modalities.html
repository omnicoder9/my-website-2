1 Why sensing is the foundation of physical AI

Physical AI refers to learning-enabled systems that perceive, reason about and act on the physical world: mobile robots, manipulators, humanoids, drones and autonomous vehicles. Whatever the policy architecture, from a classical estimation-and-control stack to an end-to-end vision-language-action model that maps camera images directly to robot actions [1], every decision such a system makes is bounded by what its sensors can measure. A sensor is a transducer that converts a physical quantity (light, pressure, acceleration, temperature, concentration) into an electrical signal; the surrounding electronics condition, digitize and timestamp that signal so that software can use it [2].

Robotics texts traditionally divide sensors along two axes [3,4]:

  • Proprioceptive vs. exteroceptive. Proprioceptive sensors measure the robot's own state (joint encoders, inertial measurement units, motor currents, wrist force/torque); exteroceptive sensors measure the environment (cameras, lidar, radar, microphones, gas sensors).
  • Passive vs. active. Passive sensors capture energy already present in the scene (an RGB camera, a microphone). Active sensors emit energy and measure its return (lidar, radar, structured-light and time-of-flight depth cameras, ultrasonic rangers), which makes them largely independent of ambient illumination but introduces interference, power and eye-safety constraints.

Because no single modality is reliable in every condition, a physical AI system is almost always multimodal: it combines complementary sensors and fuses their outputs probabilistically [5,6]. The rest of this article walks through the major modality families, then closes with the cross-cutting problems of calibration, time synchronization and fusion.

2 Common foundations: measurement, noise, calibration, estimation

2.1 The measurement model

Every sensor is best understood through a measurement model z = h(x) + v that relates the true state x to the observation z through a (possibly nonlinear) function h and noise v [4]. Static characteristics such as range, resolution, sensitivity, linearity, hysteresis and offset, and dynamic characteristics such as bandwidth, latency and response time, determine how closely the hardware approximates an ideal transducer [2]. Practical error models separate deterministic errors that calibration can remove (offset, scale factor, misalignment, lens distortion) from stochastic errors that must be handled statistically (white noise, bias drift, quantization) [2,7].

2.2 Probabilistic state estimation

Probabilistic robotics treats perception as recursive Bayesian inference: a belief over the state is predicted forward with a motion model and corrected with each new measurement [4]. When the models are linear and the noise is Gaussian, the recursion has the closed-form solution published by Kalman in 1960 [8]; extended and unscented variants, particle filters and factor-graph smoothers generalize it to nonlinear, multimodal problems [4,9]. This single framework recurs throughout the article: GNSS/INS navigation, visual-inertial odometry, lidar SLAM and tactile state estimation are all instances of it.

2.3 Learned perception

Modern physical AI increasingly replaces hand-designed feature pipelines with learned encoders: convolutional and transformer networks for images, point-set networks for lidar [10,11], and multimodal fusion in a shared bird's-eye-view representation [12]. Vision-language-action models go further and consume raw camera frames as tokens alongside language [1]. Learning does not remove the need to understand sensors: calibration, synchronization, noise characteristics and failure modes still determine what information reaches the network, and large multimodal datasets such as KITTI and nuScenes exist precisely because sensor rigs had to be carefully calibrated and time-aligned [13,14].

3 RGB and stereo cameras

3.1 History of the image sensor

Solid-state imaging began with the charge-coupled device (CCD), described by Willard Boyle and George Smith of Bell Laboratories in 1970 [15]; the invention was recognized with a share of the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics [16]. In 1976 Bryce Bayer of Eastman Kodak patented the colour filter array that bears his name, a repeating 2×2 mosaic with two green, one red and one blue filter, which lets a single monochrome sensor capture colour after interpolation (demosaicing) [17]. During the 1990s the CMOS active-pixel sensor, championed by Eric Fossum's group at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, integrated amplification and readout on the same chip as the photodiodes, enabling the low-power "camera-on-a-chip" that now dominates robotics and consumer devices [18].

3.2 The pinhole model and lens distortion

The workhorse geometric model is the pinhole (central projection) camera [19,20]. A 3-D point Xw in world coordinates maps to pixel coordinates (u, v) by

s [u, v, 1]T = K [R | t] Xw,    K = [[fx, γ, cx], [0, fy, cy], [0, 0, 1]]

where K holds the intrinsic parameters (focal lengths in pixels, principal point and skew) and [R | t] the extrinsic pose [19]. Real lenses add radial and tangential (decentering) distortion; the polynomial model introduced by Brown in 1966 remains the standard parameterization in calibration software [21,22].

3.3 Camera calibration

Zhang's method, published in 2000, made calibration routine by requiring only a planar target observed in a few orientations: each view provides a homography that constrains the intrinsics in closed form, and a nonlinear least-squares refinement then minimizes reprojection error over all parameters including distortion [23]. It is implemented in OpenCV's calibrateCamera [22]. The example below synthesises checkerboard observations from a known camera, so the recovered parameters can be checked against ground truth.

3.4 Shutters, dynamic range and event cameras

Frame-based sensors integrate light over an exposure and read out pixel values at a fixed frame rate. Many CMOS sensors expose and read rows sequentially (rolling shutter), which skews fast-moving objects, whereas global-shutter sensors expose all pixels simultaneously [20]. Motion blur and limited dynamic range remain the main failure modes of frame-based cameras on fast robots [24]. Event cameras take a different approach: each pixel independently and asynchronously reports changes in log intensity. The dynamic vision sensor of Lichtsteiner, Posch and Delbruck (2008) achieved 120 dB dynamic range and 15 µs latency [25], and subsequent work has shown advantages for high-speed and high-dynamic-range robotics, at the cost of requiring new algorithms for a sparse, asynchronous data stream [24].

3.5 Stereo vision

Two calibrated cameras separated by a baseline B recover depth by triangulation. After rectification, which warps both images so that epipolar lines become horizontal scanlines, a scene point appears in the two images at the same row, offset horizontally by the disparity d = uL − uR, and depth follows as [19,20]

Z = f B / d,     σZ ≈ (Z² / f B) σd

The second expression, obtained by differentiating the first, shows why stereo depth error grows quadratically with distance and why wider baselines and longer focal lengths help at range [20].

The hard part is correspondence: finding, for each left pixel, the matching right pixel. Scharstein and Szeliski's taxonomy decomposes dense stereo algorithms into matching-cost computation, cost aggregation, disparity optimization and refinement, and their Middlebury benchmark standardized evaluation [26]. Hirschmüller's semi-global matching (SGM) approximates a global 2-D smoothness energy by aggregating costs along multiple 1-D paths through the image, with a small penalty P1 for disparity changes of one pixel and a larger penalty P2 for larger jumps; it offers near-global quality at a cost suitable for real-time and FPGA implementation [27]. 3.6 Cameras in robot pipelines

Beyond depth, cameras feed visual odometry and SLAM. ORB-SLAM2, for example, supports monocular, stereo and RGB-D input in a single feature-based system with loop closing and relocalization [29]. Monocular systems cannot observe metric scale, a key motivation for adding stereo, depth or inertial sensing [9,29].

4 Depth cameras, lidar and radar

Active range sensors measure distance directly by emitting energy and analysing its return. They differ in wavelength (near-infrared light for depth cameras and lidar, millimetre-wave radio for radar) and in how range is encoded: by triangulation, by time of flight, by phase, or by frequency modulation [30–32].

4.1 Structured-light depth cameras

Structured-light systems replace the second camera of a stereo pair with a projector. A known pattern (stripes, binary or phase-shifted codes, or a pseudo-random dot field) is projected onto the scene, and the distortion of the pattern seen by a camera at a known baseline is triangulated to recover depth [33]. The approach reached mass-market robotics with Microsoft's Kinect in 2010, whose infrared speckle projector and camera produced dense depth maps at video rate and low cost; it rapidly became a standard research sensor [34]. Dense depth at video rate enabled real-time dense reconstruction algorithms such as KinectFusion, which fuses successive depth frames into a volumetric truncated signed-distance function while tracking the camera with ICP [35].

Active stereo combines both ideas: a projector adds texture to the scene, and two infrared cameras perform conventional stereo matching. Intel's RealSense D400 cameras use this design so that they continue to work outdoors, where sunlight swamps the projected pattern but provides texture of its own [36].

4.2 Time-of-flight cameras

Time-of-flight (ToF) cameras illuminate the scene with modulated infrared light and measure, at every pixel, how long the light takes to return. Continuous-wave "lock-in" ToF sensors modulate the illumination at a frequency fmod and recover the phase shift φ of the returned signal from several correlation samples; range is then d = c φ / (4π fmod), which is unambiguous only up to c / (2 fmod) [32,37]. Characteristic error sources include multipath interference in concave scenes, "flying pixels" at depth discontinuities where a pixel integrates foreground and background, sensitivity to surface reflectivity and integration time, and systematic "wiggling" errors from non-sinusoidal modulation [32,37].

4.3 Lidar

Lidar (light detection and ranging) scans one or more laser beams across the scene and measures the range to each return. Three ranging principles dominate [30,38]:

  • Pulsed (direct) time of flight: range R = c Δt / 2 from the round-trip time of a short pulse; simple and long-range, it requires picosecond-class timing for centimetre resolution.
  • Amplitude-modulated continuous wave (AMCW): range from the phase of a modulated beam, as in ToF cameras.
  • Frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW): the optical frequency is chirped and mixed with the return, so range appears as a beat frequency and radial velocity as a Doppler shift, providing per-point velocity and strong immunity to interference from other emitters.

Beam steering is achieved by spinning mechanical assemblies, MEMS mirrors, rotating polygons or prisms, optical phased arrays, or by flash illumination without scanning; each trades field of view, resolution, reliability and cost [39]. Most automotive and robotic lidars operate near 905 nm, where inexpensive silicon detectors work, or near 1550 nm, where eye-safety limits allow higher power [30,39].

Lidar's role in autonomy was cemented by the DARPA challenges. Stanley, winner of the 2005 Grand Challenge, used five scanning laser rangefinders on its roof to classify terrain as drivable [40]. In the 2007 Urban Challenge, Stanford's entry Junior relied on a Velodyne HDL-64E spinning lidar with 64 beams providing 360° coverage [41], a sensor design that subsequently became common in autonomous-driving research and appears in the KITTI dataset [13].

Point cloud algorithms

Raw lidar output is a point cloud, an unordered set of 3-D points. Scan registration aligns two clouds to estimate relative motion; the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm of Besl and McKay alternates between finding nearest-neighbour correspondences and solving in closed form for the rigid transform that best aligns them [42]. Lidar odometry and mapping systems such as LOAM extract edge and planar features and split the problem into a high-rate odometry estimate and a lower-rate mapping refinement [43]; Google's Cartographer performs real-time 2-D lidar SLAM with submaps and branch-and-bound loop closure [44]. For learned perception, PointNet showed that a network built from shared per-point functions and a symmetric max-pooling operation can consume unordered point sets directly [10], and PointPillars made lidar 3-D object detection fast by encoding vertical columns of points into a pseudo-image processed by a 2-D CNN [11]. Libraries such as Open3D implement many of these primitives [45]. The example below implements point-to-point ICP with the singular value decomposition solution for the rigid transform.

4.4 Radar

Radar (radio detection and ranging) has the longest history of the active range sensors. Practical systems were developed in several countries in the years before and during the Second World War, and the field's foundational textbooks trace its development from early experiments on radio reflection to modern signal processing [46,47]. In robotics and driving, radar's attraction is robustness: millimetre-wave signals penetrate fog, rain, dust and darkness that defeat optical sensors, and radar measures radial velocity directly through the Doppler effect [31,48].

FMCW principles

Most automotive radars operate in the 76–81 GHz band and use frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) waveforms [31,48]. The transmitter emits a linear chirp of bandwidth B and duration Tc (slope S = B/Tc). Mixing the received echo with the transmitted chirp yields a beat signal whose frequency is proportional to range, fb = 2 S R / c. A frame of many chirps is processed with a two-dimensional FFT: the first FFT along each chirp resolves range, and the second across chirps resolves the phase progression caused by motion, i.e. radial velocity [31,47]. Key relations are [31,47]:

ΔR = c / (2B),    vmax = λ / (4 Tc),    Δv = λ / (2 N Tc)

Targets are then detected against noise and clutter using constant false alarm rate (CFAR) detectors, which estimate the local noise level from surrounding "training" cells and set an adaptive threshold; Rohling's 1983 analysis introduced ordered-statistic CFAR to cope with multiple-target and clutter-edge situations where simple cell averaging fails [49]. Angle is estimated from phase differences across a receive antenna array; multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) radars with Nt transmitters and Nr receivers synthesise a virtual array of Nt Nr elements, the basis of "4-D imaging radar" that resolves elevation as well as azimuth [50].

5 Microphones and acoustic sensors

5.1 Transduction and history

Microphones convert sound pressure into an electrical signal. In a condenser (capacitive) microphone, sound moves a thin diaphragm relative to a fixed backplate, changing the capacitance of the gap. Sessler and West at Bell Laboratories described the electret condenser microphone in 1962, in which a permanently charged polymer film supplies the bias that otherwise needs an external voltage; its low cost made it ubiquitous [51]. Micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) capacitive microphones fabricate the diaphragm and backplate in silicon, producing small, reflow-solderable parts with integrated electronics that are now standard in phones, smart speakers and robots; Zawawi and colleagues review their designs and performance trade-offs [52].

5.2 Ultrasonic ranging

Ultrasonic transducers emit pulses above the audible range and time their echoes, giving inexpensive short-range distance measurement. Wide-beam sonar was among the first exteroceptive sensors on mobile robots, and Moravec and Elfes's work on combining many uncertain sonar readings into a probabilistic grid introduced the occupancy-grid map that remains a basic robot map representation [4,53].

5.3 Microphone arrays and sound source localization

An array of microphones samples the sound field at several points, allowing a robot to estimate where sounds come from, enhance a desired talker and suppress noise [54,55]. For a microphone pair with spacing d and a far-field source at angle θ from broadside, the time difference of arrival (TDOA) is τ = d sin θ / c. The generalized cross-correlation with phase transform (GCC-PHAT) of Knapp and Carter whitens the cross-spectrum so that the correlation peak is sharp even in reverberant rooms [56]. Steered-response beamforming and subspace methods such as MUSIC, introduced by Schmidt, extend localization to multiple simultaneous sources [54,57]. Rascon and Meza review how these methods have been adapted to the moving platforms, ego-noise and real-time constraints of robots [55].

Output:

TDOA 166.7 us (true 167.2), DOA 34.9 deg (true 35.0)

With only two microphones the direction is ambiguous between front and back, and the maximum resolvable delay is d/c; larger planar or spherical arrays resolve full 3-D direction [54,55].

5.4 Audio understanding

Beyond localization, audio carries speech commands and event cues. Large-scale weakly supervised speech recognizers such as Whisper, trained on 680,000 hours of multilingual audio, have made robust spoken interaction practical for embodied agents [58]. Contact microphones and vibration sensors also capture the high-frequency signatures of touch and slip, a link to the tactile sensing discussed in Section 7 [59,60].

6 IMUs, GPS/GNSS and encoders

These three sensor families answer the question "where am I and how am I moving?" They are complementary: inertial sensors are fast and self-contained but drift; GNSS is absolute but slow and needs open sky; encoders measure joint and wheel motion precisely but know nothing about slip or the outside world [4,61].

6.1 Inertial measurement units

An inertial measurement unit (IMU) combines three accelerometers and three gyroscopes on orthogonal axes; a "MARG" or 9-axis unit adds a three-axis magnetometer [61,62]. Accelerometers measure specific force (acceleration minus gravity), so a static accelerometer reads the reaction to gravity, which is what makes it useful as a tilt sensor [7]. Classical navigation-grade systems used spinning-mass and later optical (ring-laser and fibre-optic) gyroscopes; robotics today relies mostly on MEMS devices, in which an accelerometer measures the deflection of a micromachined proof mass and a vibratory gyroscope senses the Coriolis force on a resonating structure [7,63]. In a strapdown system the sensors are fixed to the body and attitude, velocity and position are computed by integrating angular rate and specific force [7].

Error characteristics and Allan variance

Integration turns small errors into large ones: a constant gyro bias produces an attitude error that grows linearly with time, and an accelerometer bias produces a position error that grows quadratically [7,61]. IMU noise is characterized with the Allan variance, originally developed for atomic frequency standards [64]. Plotting the Allan deviation against averaging time τ on log–log axes separates noise processes by slope: angle random walk appears with slope −½ (its value at τ = 1 s gives the noise density), bias instability at the flat minimum, and rate random walk with slope +½ [65].

Attitude estimation

Attitude filters exploit complementary error spectra: the gyro is accurate over short intervals but drifts, while the accelerometer's gravity direction (and the magnetometer's heading) is noisy and corrupted by motion but does not drift. A complementary filter high-passes the integrated gyro and low-passes the accelerometer-derived angle; Mahony, Hamel and Pflimlin formulated nonlinear complementary filters directly on the rotation group SO(3) with provable stability [66], and Madgwick's gradient-descent filter offers a computationally light alternative for IMU and MARG sensors [62]. 6.2 GPS and GNSS

Global navigation satellite systems (GNSS) broadcast ranging signals from satellites with precisely known orbits and clocks. The U.S. Global Positioning System (GPS) was the first fully operational system, joined by Russia's GLONASS, Europe's Galileo and China's BeiDou [67,68]. A receiver measures pseudoranges, the signal travel time multiplied by the speed of light and including the receiver clock offset, so at least four satellites are needed to solve for three position coordinates and the clock bias [67]. Standalone positioning is typically accurate to metres, limited by satellite orbit and clock errors, ionospheric and tropospheric delays, multipath and receiver noise [67,68]. Differential corrections and carrier-phase techniques remove most of these errors: real-time kinematic (RTK) positioning resolves the integer ambiguities of carrier-phase measurements relative to a nearby base station and reaches centimetre-level accuracy, which is used in agricultural and surveying robots [68].

6.3 GNSS/INS and visual-inertial fusion

Integrating GNSS with inertial navigation produces a solution that is both high-rate and drift-free. In a loosely coupled architecture the GNSS position and velocity solutions update an INS error-state Kalman filter; tightly coupled designs use raw pseudoranges and continue to benefit from fewer than four satellites [61]. The same principle underlies visual-inertial odometry (VIO), in which camera features constrain IMU drift: the multi-state constraint Kalman filter (MSCKF) of Mourikis and Roumeliotis marginalizes feature positions to keep the filter efficient [69], and IMU preintegration on the manifold enables accurate optimization-based VIO [9]. 6.4 Encoders

Rotary and linear encoders measure the position of joints, motor shafts and wheels, and they are the primary proprioceptive sensors of manipulators [3]. Optical encoders shine light through a patterned disk; magnetic encoders sense a rotating magnet with Hall-effect or magnetoresistive elements, exploiting the Hall effect discovered by Edwin Hall in 1879 [2,70]. Incremental encoders output two square waves, A and B, in quadrature (90° apart), so that the sequence of transitions reveals direction and counting every edge gives four counts per line ("×4 decoding") [2]. Absolute encoders encode each position uniquely, often with the reflected binary code patented by Frank Gray of Bell Labs, in which consecutive values differ in a single bit so that misaligned tracks cannot cause large reading errors [2,71].

Output:

count=1000, angle=87.89 deg, errors=0

Wheel odometry integrates encoder counts through a kinematic model of the vehicle. It is precise over short distances but accumulates error from wheel slip, uneven terrain and uncertainty in wheel radius and track width, which is why it is fused with inertial, visual or lidar data [3,4].

7 Force, torque, tactile, pressure and proximity sensors

Manipulation is a contact-rich activity. Vision can tell a robot where an object is, but only contact sensing can tell it how hard it is pressing, whether the object is slipping and where on the fingertip contact occurred [60,72].

7.1 Transduction principles

Most force sensors measure the strain of an elastic element. Metal-foil strain gauges change resistance as they stretch; semiconductor gauges exploit the much larger piezoresistive effect in silicon and germanium reported by Smith in 1954, which also underlies MEMS pressure sensors [2,73]. Piezoelectric materials generate charge under stress, giving excellent sensitivity to dynamic forces and vibration but no true static response, while capacitive sensors measure the change in electrode gap or overlap under load [2,60].

7.2 Six-axis force/torque sensors

A wrist force/torque (F/T) sensor measures the full contact wrench w = [fx, fy, fz, τx, τy, τz]. Its elastic structure is instrumented with several strain-gauge bridges, and a calibration matrix maps the bridge voltages to the six wrench components [72]. Before the measured wrench can be used for control, the sensor bias and the gravitational load of the tool or gripper beyond the sensor must be removed. Both are linear in a small set of parameters (mass, mass times centre-of-mass position and the six offsets), so they can be identified by least squares from a handful of static poses [3,72].

Output:

mass 1.200 kg, CoM [1.   0.   7.99] cm
external wrench: [-0.01 -0.03  3.91  0.01 -0.03 -0.05] -> contact

Compensated wrench measurements close the loop in force-controlled behaviours. Raibert and Craig's hybrid position/force control partitions task space into directions that are position-controlled and directions that are force-controlled [74], while Hogan's impedance control regulates the dynamic relationship between motion and force, making the robot behave like a programmable spring–damper [75]. Joint torque sensors and motor-current estimates provide alternative routes to the same information [3].

7.3 Tactile sensing

Human fingertips contain four classes of mechanoreceptive afferents: slowly adapting types that signal sustained pressure and skin stretch, and fast-adapting types that respond to transients and vibration. Together they encode contact onset, slip and object properties that guide grasping [59]. Dahiya and colleagues' review links this biology to robot tactile-sensor requirements and catalogues transduction technologies including resistive, capacitive, piezoelectric, optical and magnetic designs [60]. Practical obstacles include wiring large arrays, robustness of the contact surface, hysteresis and drift, and covering curved surfaces [60,72].

Vision-based tactile sensors sidestep many of these issues by imaging the inside of a soft elastomer with a camera. GelSight, introduced by Johnson and Adelson as "retrographic sensing", uses a reflective skin and multicolour illumination so that photometric stereo recovers the contact surface's shape at very high resolution [76]; later robotic versions also estimate shear and normal force from the motion of printed markers [77]. DIGIT made the concept compact and inexpensive enough to mount on multi-fingered hands [78]. Learning-based work showed that combining vision and touch improves grasp success compared with vision alone [79]. 7.4 Pressure sensors

Pressure sensors measure force per unit area of a fluid or gas, typically through the deflection of a diaphragm read out piezoresistively, capacitively or piezoelectrically [2]. In robots they appear as barometric altimeters on drones (ambient pressure falls with altitude), as sensors in pneumatic and hydraulic actuators and soft grippers, and as distributed pressure-sensitive skins [2,60,61].

7.5 Proximity sensors

Proximity sensors detect objects before contact, which is valuable for collision avoidance in human–robot collaboration and for pre-grasp alignment. Lumelsky, Shur and Wagner proposed covering robots in a "sensitive skin" of distributed proximity sensors to enable whole-body awareness [80]. Navarro and colleagues survey the main technologies: capacitive sensors respond to the change in electric field caused by nearby conductive or dielectric objects (including humans); optical sensors measure reflected infrared intensity or time of flight; and ultrasonic and radar sensors measure echoes [81]. Each responds differently to material, so many systems combine principles [81].

8 Temperature, chemical and other specialized sensors

8.1 Contact temperature sensors

Robots monitor temperature to protect motors, batteries and electronics, to interact safely with people and objects, and in some cases as a sensing modality in its own right [2]. The three common contact sensors are [2]:

  • Thermocouples, junctions of dissimilar metals that generate a voltage proportional to the temperature difference between the measuring and reference junctions (the Seebeck effect); rugged and wide-ranging but low in output and requiring cold-junction compensation.
  • Resistance temperature detectors (RTDs), usually platinum, whose resistance rises nearly linearly with temperature. The standard Pt100 element has 100 Ω at 0 °C and follows the Callendar–Van Dusen equation with coefficients fixed by IEC 60751 [82].
  • Thermistors, semiconductor ceramics with a large, strongly nonlinear (usually negative) temperature coefficient; Steinhart and Hart's three-term equation 1/T = A + B ln R + C (ln R)3 fits them to millikelvin-level accuracy across wide ranges [83].

8.2 Thermal (infrared) imaging

Every object emits infrared radiation that depends on its temperature and emissivity. Uncooled microbolometer arrays, in which each pixel is a thermally isolated resistor that warms when it absorbs long-wave infrared radiation (about 8–14 µm), made thermal cameras small and affordable enough for robots, whereas cooled photon detectors remain the choice for the highest sensitivity [84]. Thermal cameras see people and animals in darkness, smoke and through some obscurants, and Gade and Moeslund survey their use in detection, tracking, inspection and search-and-rescue [85]. Because thermal images lack texture and their intensities depend on emissivity, they are usually fused with visible cameras [85].

8.3 Chemical sensing and machine olfaction

Chemical sensors let robots detect gas leaks, locate odour sources and monitor air quality. The main technologies are:

  • Metal-oxide semiconductor (MOX) sensors. Seiyama and colleagues showed in 1962 that the conductivity of heated semiconducting oxide films changes in the presence of reducing gases [86]. MOX sensors are cheap and sensitive but broadly selective, slow to recover and affected by humidity and temperature [87,88].
  • Electrochemical cells. Clark's oxygen electrode (1962) established amperometric sensing, in which a target gas is oxidized or reduced at an electrode and the resulting current is proportional to concentration [89].
  • Electronic noses. Persaud and Dodd showed in 1982 that an array of broadly tuned, partially selective sensors combined with pattern recognition can discriminate odours, mimicking the combinatorial coding of the mammalian olfactory system [90].

Robotic olfaction is difficult because odours are carried by turbulent airflow: plumes are patchy and intermittent, concentration gradients are unreliable over short times, and sensors respond slowly. Reviews by Lilienthal, Loutfi and Duckett and by Ishida, Wada and Matsukura cover gas-distribution mapping, odour-source localization strategies inspired by insects and the integration of anemometry with chemical sensing [87,88].

8.4 Other specialized sensors

  • Magnetometers measure the Earth's magnetic field for heading and are part of MARG sensor suites, but they are easily disturbed by nearby ferrous material and motor currents [61,62].
  • Event cameras, discussed in Section 3.4, are a specialized vision sensor for high-speed, high-dynamic-range perception [24].
  • Hall-effect current and position sensors measure motor current (a proxy for torque) and magnet position in brushless motors and magnetic encoders [2,70].

9 Putting it together: calibration, synchronization and fusion

9.1 Extrinsic and temporal calibration

Fusing sensors requires knowing the rigid transformation between their frames (extrinsics) and the offset between their clocks. Even a few milliseconds of timing error translate into significant spatial error on a fast-moving platform. Furgale, Rehder and Siegwart showed how to estimate spatial and temporal calibration jointly by representing the trajectory with continuous-time B-splines, an approach implemented in the widely used Kalibr toolbox for camera–IMU systems [91]. In hardware, sensors are synchronized by shared trigger lines, by GNSS pulse-per-second signals, or by network time protocols; the IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol provides sub-microsecond clock synchronization over Ethernet [92].

9.2 Levels of fusion

Fusion can happen at the level of raw data, features or decisions [5,6]. Classical robotics favours probabilistic state-level fusion in Kalman filters, particle filters and factor graphs [4,9]. Learned perception increasingly fuses at the feature level: BEVFusion, for example, projects camera and lidar features into a shared bird's-eye-view grid and outperforms single-modality detectors on the nuScenes benchmark [12,14]. Middleware such as ROS 2 standardizes message types, coordinate-frame trees and timestamps so that sensors from different vendors can be combined [93].

9.3 Failure modes worth designing for

  • Correlated failures: cameras and lidar both degrade in fog; radar and thermal sensing complement them [50,85].
  • Silent miscalibration: mechanical shocks shift extrinsics; online calibration checks and consistency tests between sensors catch this [6,91].
  • Unmodelled noise: IMU biases and GNSS multipath violate white-noise assumptions; estimating biases as states and using robust statistics helps [61,65].
  • Distribution shift for learned models: a network trained on one sensor configuration may fail when mounting positions or sensor models change, which is one motivation for large, diverse multimodal datasets [13,14].

When choosing sensors, start from the quantities the task must estimate, the required accuracy and update rate, and the operating conditions (lighting, weather, contact, temperature), then select modalities whose failure modes do not coincide and budget for calibration and synchronization from the outset [3,6].

Conclusion

The sensors of physical AI span light, radio, sound, inertia, contact, heat and chemistry. Each has a century or more of physics and engineering behind it, from the photoelectric charge transfer of the CCD to the Coriolis force in a MEMS gyroscope, and each comes with well-understood algorithms that turn raw signals into estimates: calibration, triangulation, Fourier processing, CFAR detection, Kalman filtering, ICP and learned encoders. Large learned models are changing how these signals are consumed, but they inherit the strengths and blind spots of the transducers that feed them. Engineers who understand the measurement model of every sensor in the loop are best placed to build embodied systems that are accurate, robust and safe.
