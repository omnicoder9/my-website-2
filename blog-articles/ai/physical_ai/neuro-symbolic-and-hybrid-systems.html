1 What neuro-symbolic and hybrid systems are, and why physical AI needs them

Two traditions have shaped artificial intelligence. The symbolic tradition represents knowledge explicitly, as logical statements, rules, programs, plans and equations, and computes with it by search and inference. It is precise, compositional and inspectable: a planner's output can be checked step by step, and a proof either holds or does not [1,2]. The connectionist or neural tradition learns distributed representations from data by adjusting the weights of networks of simple units. It copes with noisy, high-dimensional input such as images, sound and touch, and it generalizes statistically from examples, but its knowledge is implicit and its errors are hard to predict or explain [3,4]. Neuro-symbolic AI is the research programme that combines the two, so that learning and reasoning support each other [5–7]. A hybrid system, in the sense used in this article, is any physical AI system in which learned components are combined with explicit, model-based ones: symbolic planners, programs, physical laws, constraints, controllers with known properties, or formal specifications and proofs.

Physical AI makes the case for such combinations unusually strong. A robot must perceive a continuous, cluttered world, which neural networks do best, but it must also pursue goals over long horizons, obey the laws of physics, respect safety rules and be certified by people who need to understand why it is safe, which are strengths of explicit models [8,9]. Purely learned policies have achieved remarkable robot behavior, yet they can fail silently outside their training distribution, need large amounts of data, and offer few guarantees [10–12]. Purely symbolic systems, from Shakey onwards, could plan and explain but were brittle at the boundary with perception: deciding whether the symbol on(cup, table) is true in a camera image is itself a hard learning problem [13,14]. Hybrid systems aim for the best of both, and the field has converged on three broad ways of combining them, which organize this article:

  • Neural models with symbolic reasoning and planning. Logic is used as a training signal or as a reasoning layer on top of neural perception (Section 3); symbols are learned and grounded from sensor data and interaction (Section 4); and learned models guide, call or are distilled into planners and programs (Section 5).
  • Neural models with explicit physical constraints. Conservation laws, equations of motion, contact conditions and hard limits are built into learned models as structure, as losses, as residual corrections or as constraint layers (Section 6).
  • Neural models with formal verification. The behavior of networks and of the closed loops they control is proved or bounded mathematically (Section 7), specified in temporal logic, searched for counterexamples, and guarded at run time by verified monitors and fallback controllers (Section 8).

2 A short history: from logical neurons to the third wave

2.1 Two traditions from one origin (1943–1980s)

Neural networks and logic began together. In 1943 McCulloch and Pitts modelled neurons as threshold units and showed that networks of them could compute logical propositions; the first artificial neural network was a logic machine [18]. The 1955 Dartmouth proposal that named artificial intelligence included both "neuron nets" and the use of language and abstraction among its topics [19]. The traditions then diverged. Rosenblatt's perceptron learned from examples [20], but Minsky and Papert's analysis of its limitations helped turn attention towards symbolic methods [21], which dominated robotics for two decades: STRIPS and Shakey planned with logical state descriptions [13,22], and Newell and Simon's physical symbol system hypothesis held that symbol manipulation is necessary and sufficient for general intelligence [1]. Symbolic robots reasoned well in the planner and poorly at the interface with sensors; the frame problem and the difficulty of keeping a logical world model synchronized with reality were recognized early [23].

2.2 The connectionist debate and the first hybrids (1986–2000s)

Backpropagation revived neural networks in 1986 [24], and with them a debate about whether networks could represent structured knowledge. Fodor and Pylyshyn argued that cognition is systematic and compositional in a way that connectionist models could reproduce only by implementing a classical symbol system [25]; Smolensky replied with a theory of sub-symbolic computation and with tensor-product representations that bind roles to fillers inside vectors [26,27]. Harnad formulated the symbol grounding problem: symbols in a formal system mean nothing to the system unless they are connected to sensorimotor experience, and he proposed neural networks as the grounding mechanism [14]. Practical hybrids followed. Knowledge-based artificial neural networks (KBANN) compiled propositional rules into the initial weights of a network, refined them with data, and extracted revised rules [28], and the CILP family translated logic programs into networks and back [5]. Statistical relational learning combined logic with probability rather than with neural networks: Markov logic networks attached weights to first-order formulas [29], and ProbLog attached probabilities to the facts of a logic program [30]. Model checking of finite-state systems, formalized in the early 1980s, and theories of hybrid automata for systems with both discrete modes and continuous dynamics, developed in the 1990s, laid the foundations for verifying physical systems [31–34].

2.3 Deep learning, and the return of structure (2012–2020)

Deep learning transformed perception and, from the mid-2010s, robot control [3]. Its successes revealed its limits too: networks were fooled by imperceptible input perturbations [11], needed far more data than people for the same tasks, and generalized poorly outside their training distribution [4,35]. Several lines of work reintroduced structure. AlphaGo combined neural value and policy networks with Monte Carlo tree search [36]. Neural module networks and neural-symbolic visual question answering separated perception from executable reasoning programs [37,38], and the neuro-symbolic concept learner learned visual concepts from question–answer pairs alone [39]. DeepProbLog added neural predicates to ProbLog [40], and semantic loss turned logical constraints into differentiable training objectives [41]. Physics-informed neural networks put differential equations into the loss [42], and Hamiltonian and Lagrangian networks built conservation laws into the architecture [43,44]. In verification, Reluplex showed in 2017 that properties of deep networks could be proved exactly, using networks from the ACAS Xu airborne collision-avoidance system as its case study [45,46], and closed-loop reachability tools for neural-network controllers followed [47,48].

2.4 The third wave and foundation models (2020–)

By 2020 neuro-symbolic AI was being described as a "third wave" after symbolic AI and deep learning [7,15]. Kautz proposed a widely cited taxonomy of integration patterns, from neural networks that simply consume symbols to systems in which symbolic reasoning runs inside a network [15], and dual-process analogies with "fast" intuitive and "slow" deliberate thinking framed many architectures [49,50]. Large language models changed the landscape again. They can translate between natural language and formal languages such as PDDL, temporal logic and code, but they plan unreliably on their own, so a recurring pattern pairs a language model that proposes with a symbolic solver or verifier that disposes [51–54]. AlphaGeometry, which pairs a language model with a symbolic deduction engine, solved 25 of 30 olympiad geometry problems in its benchmark [55], and in robotics the same pattern appears in TAMP systems that use language models as translators and checkers [56,57]. 3 Patterns of integration: logic in the loss, logic in the loop

3.1 A taxonomy

There are many ways to connect a neural network to a symbolic component, and several taxonomies classify them [6,68,69]. Kautz's six types are the most cited [15]. They range from ordinary deep learning on symbolic inputs and outputs (type 1, written symbolic Neuro symbolic), through a symbolic algorithm that calls neural subroutines, as AlphaGo's tree search calls its value and policy networks (type 2, Symbolic[Neuro]) [36], a neural front end that hands symbols to a symbolic reasoner (type 3, Neuro | Symbolic), symbolic knowledge compiled into training data (type 4), and logical rules used as soft constraints or regularizers on a network (type 5, NeuroSymbolic), to a speculative type 6 in which a full reasoning engine runs inside the network. For a robot, a more practical question is where in the system the symbolic knowledge lives and when it acts: at training time, at inference time, or both. 3.2 Logic as a training signal

The lightest form of integration leaves the network architecture unchanged and adds a term to its loss that rewards outputs consistent with known rules. Rules expressed in fuzzy or real-valued logic replace Boolean connectives with differentiable operators such as t-norms, so that a formula's degree of truth can be maximized by gradient descent; semantic-based regularization and logic tensor networks follow this route, and logic tensor networks extend it to first-order formulas with quantifiers over the objects in a batch [66,82]. Hu and colleagues instead distilled rules into a network through a teacher–student scheme, in which a "teacher" distribution is obtained by projecting the network's predictions onto the rule-consistent set [73]. The choice of fuzzy operator matters: van Krieken and colleagues showed that some common operators give vanishing or misleading gradients, and that the product-based operators behave better in learning [83].

Semantic loss, introduced by Xu and colleagues, uses exact probabilistic semantics instead [41]. If a network outputs independent probabilities pi for Boolean variables xi, and a propositional formula α must hold, the semantic loss is the negative log-probability that a sample from the network satisfies the formula:

Ls(α, p) = − log Σx ⊨ α Πi: xi=1 pi Πi: xi=0 (1 − pi)

This is a weighted model count. It is zero exactly when the network puts all its mass on satisfying assignments, it is independent of how the formula is written, and it needs no labels, so it can be applied to unlabelled data. For large formulas the sum is computed by compiling the formula once into a tractable circuit such as a sentential decision diagram, after which evaluation and differentiation take time linear in the circuit size [41,84]. Giunchiglia and colleagues survey this and related methods for learning with logical constraints, including approaches that guarantee constraint satisfaction at inference time [85].

3.3 Example: semantic loss for gait-consistent contact estimation

Legged robots need to know which feet are on the ground, because state estimators fuse leg kinematics with inertial measurements only through the feet that are in contact [86,87]. The program below estimates the contact state of the four feet of a trotting quadruped from weakly informative, noisy per-leg features, with only 16 labelled time steps. It encodes a piece of domain knowledge, that in a trot the diagonal leg pairs move together, as the formula (FL ↔ RR) ∧ (FR ↔ RL) ∧ (FL ⊕ FR), and compares training with and without the semantic loss on 4000 unlabelled steps, decoding either by thresholding each foot or by choosing the most probable legal state (symbolic inference at test time).

The table separates the two places where the formula helps. As a training signal, the semantic loss raises per-foot accuracy from 85% to 92% using only unlabelled data, because it forces the four per-leg classifiers to agree with one another. As an inference rule, decoding to the most probable legal state helps even more, because the formula lets the evidence from all four legs be pooled: each foot's estimate is supported by its diagonal partner and contradicted by the other pair. Used together they reach 99.7%. Without either, only about half of the predicted states are physically possible gaits. The same gain from combining learned evidence with structural knowledge appears at much larger scale in robot state estimators that impose kinematic and contact constraints on learned measurements, as discussed in the sensor fusion article.

3.4 Logic in the loop: probabilistic logic programming with neural predicates

A tighter integration lets a logic program call neural networks during inference and passes gradients back through the reasoning. In DeepProbLog, a neural predicate such as digit(Image, D) is defined by a network whose softmax output gives the probability of each value of D; the rest of the program is ordinary probabilistic logic, and the probability of a query is computed exactly by knowledge compilation [40,88]. Because that computation is differentiable, the networks can be trained from labels on queries alone. The classic demonstration learns to recognize handwritten digits from examples labelled only with the sum of two digits. Scallop scales the idea with a Datalog-based language and approximate reasoning over the top-k proofs [67]; ∂ILP and neural theorem provers learn the rules themselves by making rule induction or backward chaining differentiable [74,89]; SATNet embeds a differentiable relaxation of MAXSAT as a network layer [75]; and abductive learning alternates between neural perception and logical abduction of the labels that would make observations consistent with background knowledge [90]. Marra and colleagues trace how these systems inherit the design dimensions of statistical relational AI: directed versus undirected models, proof- versus model-based semantics, and logic versus probability [69].

3.5 Example: learning to perceive from symbolic supervision

The program below is a physical analogue of the digit-addition task. A robot drops two parts into a bin and a scale reports only the total mass class. Each part's mass class is hidden and must be recognized from a noisy appearance feature. The relation between the parts and the scale is given as a one-line logic program, and a softmax classifier for the neural predicate mass/2 is trained by maximizing the probability of the observed totals, marginalizing over all pairs of part masses consistent with each total.

Although no individual part is ever labelled, the classifier learns to recognize part masses far above chance (20%), and with 200 bin totals (400 unlabelled parts) it is slightly better than a classifier trained on 200 directly labelled parts. The program supplies what the labels do not: the knowledge that totals are sums identifies which part classes are compatible with each observation, and the gradient (the network's prediction minus the posterior given the observed total) is an expectation step of EM computed by logical inference. This is the essence of learning from symbolic supervision: weak, high-level labels plus background knowledge can replace expensive low-level annotation, which matters for robots whose sensors produce far more data than anyone can label [40,90]. Its cost is computational: exact inference enumerates all consistent assignments, which grows quickly with the number of objects and values, and this is why practical systems use knowledge compilation, top-k proofs or sampling [67,84].

4 Grounding symbols in perception and action

4.1 The grounding problem for robots

A symbolic planner reasons about predicates such as holding(cup) or in(cup, drawer) and operators that change them. For a robot, each predicate must be computed from sensor data, each operator must be implemented by a controller, and the abstract model must predict well enough what those controllers will do. This is Harnad's grounding problem in engineering form [14], and it has three parts: perceptual grounding (classifying whether a predicate holds in the current observation), action grounding (linking an abstract operator to a skill or motion planner that can achieve its effects, the central problem of task-and-motion planning) and model learning (discovering the predicates and operators themselves) [76,91]. Hand-designed predicates and operators remain the norm in deployed systems, but they are expensive to write and often subtly wrong; learning them from data is one of the most active areas of neuro-symbolic robotics.

4.2 Perception that outputs symbols

Neural-symbolic visual question answering showed how cleanly perception can be separated from reasoning. NS-VQA parsed an image into a table of objects with attributes, parsed the question into a program, and executed the program on the table, reaching 99.8% accuracy on the CLEVR diagnostic dataset while remaining fully interpretable [38,92]. The neuro-symbolic concept learner removed the need for annotated programs and object attributes, learning visual concepts such as colours and shapes jointly with a semantic parser from images and question–answer pairs alone [39], and CLEVRER extended the approach to collisions and counterfactual questions about video [93]. For robots, the object table becomes a scene graph: objects, places and their relations, built online from posed images and depth and used as the state for planning and language grounding [94–96]. SayPlan, for example, lets a language model search a 3-D scene graph and then checks its plans with a scene-graph simulator before execution [72]. Object-centric learning methods such as slot attention discover objects from pixels without labels, providing a bridge from raw perception to discrete entities [97], as described in the representations article.

Predicates can also be learned through their role in planning rather than from labels. Migimatsu and Bohg trained predicate classifiers using only the knowledge of which actions were executed: a known PDDL domain implies that an action's preconditions held before it and its effects after it, which provides weak labels for every predicate in every image [98]. PDSketch lets a person write a domain "sketch" whose structure is symbolic and whose predicate groundings and transition functions are neural networks learned from data [99].

4.3 Learning symbols and operators from interaction

Further along, the symbols themselves are learned. Konidaris, Kaelbling and Lozano-Pérez showed which abstract symbols are necessary and sufficient for planning with a given set of skills: they must describe the skills' initiation sets and effects, so that symbols can be derived from the robot's own options rather than designed [76]. Latplan trained a discrete autoencoder whose binary latent variables served as propositions, learned an action model over them, and solved image-based puzzles with an off-the-shelf classical planner [61,100]. DeepSym and earlier work by Ugur and Piater learned object categories, effect categories and logical rules from a robot's exploratory interactions and compiled them into PDDL [101,102].

Once predicates exist, learning operators is a long-studied problem. Classic approaches include learning noisy, probabilistic relational rules from experience [103], inferring action models from plan traces by weighted MAX-SAT [104] and from action sequences alone [105]. In robotics, Silver and colleagues' LOFT learned symbolic operators from demonstrations for bilevel planning, in which a symbolic planner proposes abstract plans and a sampler refines them into continuous motions: transitions are clustered by their lifted effects, and each cluster's preconditions are the atoms common to all its examples [16]. Neuro-symbolic relational transition models (NSRTs) pair such operators with learned neural samplers [71], and predicate invention searches a grammar of candidate predicates for those that make planning fastest, rather than those that best predict the next state [65]. A recurring lesson is that the objective matters: an abstraction should be judged by how well it supports decisions, not by how accurately it reconstructs observations.

Yet with the first two languages, 24–28 of the 60 plans fail when executed, even with plenty of data. The reason is visible in the printed operators. 5 Neural models with planners and programs

5.1 Learning to guide symbolic search

The type-2 pattern, a symbolic algorithm that calls neural components, is the most successful form of integration in robotics today. Symbolic planners keep their soundness, completeness and ability to generalize to new goals, while learned components make them fast. Learned heuristics estimate the cost-to-go of symbolic states [106]; action schema networks learn generalized policies that transfer across problem sizes in a planning domain [107]; learned scoring of object importance lets a planner ignore irrelevant objects in large scenes [108]; and in task-and-motion planning, learned samplers propose grasps and placements that are likely to succeed, learned feasibility classifiers prune abstract plans that cannot be refined, and networks predict promising action sequences directly from a scene image [70,109,110]. Because the planner still checks each step against its model, a poor learned component makes planning slower but does not make plans wrong. These methods are covered in more depth in the task planning article.

5.2 Language models as translators, symbolic solvers as checkers

Large language models brought a new kind of neural component to planning: one that can read instructions and write formal descriptions. Their ability to plan by themselves is limited. On PlanBench, a benchmark of classical planning problems, language models produced many invalid plans, especially when domain names were obfuscated so that surface knowledge could not help [53,111]. A robust pattern is therefore to let the language model translate and let a symbolic system solve or verify. LLM+P translates a natural-language problem into PDDL and calls a classical planner [51]; other work builds PDDL world models with language models and corrects them with planner feedback [112], or has language models write generalized plans as programs that are then checked on held-out problems [113]. NL2TL and Lang2LTL translate instructions into temporal logic [54,114]; AutoTAMP translates to signal temporal logic and uses a syntax and semantics checker to correct the translation before a TAMP solver plans [56]; and the "safety chip" monitors a language-model agent's actions against LTL constraints and rejects violating ones [57]. Kambhampati and colleagues describe the general architecture as LLM-Modulo: a generator surrounded by sound external verifiers and critics [52]. Plan validators such as VAL, long used in the planning community, are exactly such verifiers [115]. Language-model planners grounded by affordances or code are discussed in the foundation models article [78,116,117].

5.3 Programmatic and interpretable policies

A policy can itself be symbolic. In programmatically interpretable reinforcement learning, Verma and colleagues searched a domain-specific language of controller programs, guided by a neural policy that served as an oracle, and obtained compact PID-like programs for a car-racing simulator that were easier to read and generalized better than the network [77]; PROPEL later framed the search as mirror descent with projection into program space [118]. VIPER extracted decision-tree policies from deep reinforcement-learning policies by imitation with a DAgger-style data aggregation, then verified properties such as correctness, stability and robustness of the trees, which is far easier than for the networks [62,119]. More broadly, neurosymbolic programming treats programs with neural components as the model class and combines program synthesis with gradient-based learning [120]; DreamCoder learns a library of reusable abstractions while synthesizing programs [121]; and Code as Policies uses language models to write robot policy code that calls perception and control APIs [78].

5.4 Example: distilling a neural policy into a verifiable program

The program below trains a small neural network by behaviour cloning to park a cart in front of a wall, distils it with DAgger into model trees of depth 2 and 4 (axis-aligned tests with a saturated linear control law in each leaf), and then checks a safety rule: when the cart moves towards the wall and its braking distance comes within 0.2 m of the wall, it must brake fully. For the network the rule can only be tested on samples; for each tree it is checked exactly, leaf by leaf. Violations are repaired symbolically by placing the rule ahead of the tree.

All the learned policies park every test start without crashing, so ordinary testing would not distinguish them. The safety rule does: the network violates it in about 2% of sampled states inside the rule's region, and the verifier finds two leaf boxes in each tree that do so. The violations are small (the policies brake hard, but not fully, near the boundary), but the point is that for the tree the check is a proof over the entire state space, not a sample. Because the tree is a program, the repair is a program edit, a guard placed in front of it, and the repaired policy is proved correct by construction with no loss of performance. The depth-2 tree, printed at the end, is small enough to read: a gain-scheduled linear controller with four regimes. This is the appeal of programmatic policies for physical systems, and their limit is equally clear: small trees and programs cannot match large networks on high-dimensional inputs such as images, which is why they are mostly used for low-dimensional decision layers on top of neural perception [62,120].

6 Explicit physical knowledge and constraints

6.1 Four places to put physics

For physical systems the most valuable "symbolic" knowledge is often physics itself: conservation laws, equations of motion, contact and friction laws, kinematic structure and hard limits on forces and speeds. This knowledge is exact where it applies, generalizes far beyond any dataset, and is what engineers use to argue that a system is safe. It can enter a learned model in four ways, which correspond to the levels of integration of Section 3 [122,123]:

  • As structure. The architecture guarantees a property for every input. Hamiltonian neural networks learn a scalar energy and obtain dynamics from Hamilton's equations, so that learned motion conserves energy by construction [43]; Lagrangian networks and deep Lagrangian networks learn a Lagrangian or a positive-definite mass matrix and use the Euler–Lagrange equations, which gives physically consistent inverse dynamics for robot control [44,124]; constrained formulations in Cartesian coordinates with explicit holonomic constraints make learning simpler and more accurate [125]; and graph networks and interaction networks build in the object-and-relation structure of physical scenes [126,127]. Stability can also be built in, by projecting learned dynamics so that a jointly learned Lyapunov function always decreases [128].
  • As a loss. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) add the residual of a differential equation, evaluated at collocation points by automatic differentiation, to the data loss, so that a network fits sparse data while approximately satisfying the governing equation [42,122]. Soft constraints are easy to add but can be hard to optimize: PINNs are known to fail on stiff or multi-scale problems because the loss terms compete and their gradients are badly balanced [129,130].
  • As a model with learned parts. Grey-box or hybrid models keep a physical model and learn what it misses: unknown parameters, residual forces, or terms in a differential equation [131,132]. TossingBot used a ballistic model to compute a nominal release velocity and a network to predict a residual correction for each object [80]; simulators have been augmented with stochastic neural residuals for pushing and bouncing [133]; and Neural Lander learned the aerodynamic ground effect with a spectrally normalized network whose Lipschitz bound allowed a stability guarantee for the nonlinear controller that used it [134]. Sparse regression can even discover the symbolic form of missing terms from a library of candidates [135].
  • As a hard constraint on outputs. A differentiable optimization layer computes the output as the solution of an optimization problem whose constraints encode physics or limits, so constraints hold exactly at inference while the layer is still trainable by backpropagation through the optimality conditions. OptNet introduced quadratic-program layers [79], cvxpylayers generalized them to disciplined convex programs [136], differentiable MPC put a whole controller inside a network [137], and DC3 handles hard equality and inequality constraints by completion and correction steps [138]. Contact is a case where the structure itself is a constraint problem: ContactNets learns contact geometry and friction while keeping the complementarity structure of rigid-body contact [139].

Differentiable simulators combine several of these: they implement physics exactly and expose gradients, so that parameters, policies or designs can be optimized through them [140–142]. The physical modeling article covers system identification and differentiable simulation in detail, and the hybrid control section shows learned residuals inside controllers. The examples below look at two questions those articles do not: how much each form of physical knowledge buys outside the training data, and what it takes for a learned output to satisfy a constraint every time.

6.3 Example: hard constraints on a learned policy

Safety limits on a robot are not preferences: a speed limit for collaborative operation or a keep-out zone must hold at every control step [143]. The program below trains a random-feature policy from fast, noisy demonstrations to move a gripper to goals on a table, subject to a speed limit, a barrier condition that keeps the gripper above the table (the discrete form of a control barrier function constraint [144]) and a workspace wall. It compares plain regression, a penalty on constraint violations during training, a projection layer applied at run time (the Euclidean projection onto the constraint set, solved exactly by Dykstra's algorithm) and training end to end through the projection, as a differentiable optimization layer would.

Output:

method       steps violating   lowest z  median final error
plain                 58.0%    -4.3 mm           13.6 mm

projection             0.0%     0.0 mm            9.1 mm

The demonstrations violate the limits, so the plain policy does too, in more than half of all steps, and it pushes the gripper 4 mm into the table. A penalty reduces violations but cannot eliminate them, because a soft constraint trades off against the data term and has no effect in states not seen during training. The projection layer makes violations impossible by construction, whatever the network outputs. Training through the projection does better still on the task (5.9 mm median final error instead of 9.1 mm), because the network learns to produce outputs that are useful after projection rather than being corrected after the fact; the gradient flows only through the components of the output that the active constraints leave free. This is the argument for optimization layers in robot learning [79,136,137]. Two caveats apply: the guarantee is only as good as the constraint model (here, a known table height and wall position), and if the constraint set can become empty, as with a barrier condition under actuator limits, the layer needs a defined fallback, as discussed for safety filters in the reactive behavior article [145].

7 Formal verification of learned components

7.1 What can be verified

Testing shows that a system works on the cases tried; formal verification proves that it works on all cases in a specified set, or produces a counterexample [8,146]. For a robot with a learned component, three kinds of property are common. Input–output properties of a network: for all inputs in a region, the output satisfies a condition, for example that a collision-avoidance network never advises a turn towards an intruder in a given geometry, or that a perception network's classification is robust to bounded perturbations [45,147]. Closed-loop properties: for all initial states in a set, the trajectories of the plant controlled by the network avoid an unsafe set or reach a goal, which requires reasoning about the network and the dynamics together [47,48]. And certificates: functions such as Lyapunov functions or barrier functions whose existence implies stability or safety, which may themselves be learned and then verified [148]. Seshia, Sadigh and Sastry identify the main obstacles to verified AI as modeling the environment, specifying perception-dependent properties, verifying learned components, and designing systems that are verifiable by construction [8,149].

7.2 Verifying networks: complete and incomplete methods

A ReLU network is a piecewise-linear function, so an input–output property can in principle be decided exactly. Complete verifiers do this. Reluplex extended the simplex algorithm with lazy case splitting on ReLUs and proved properties of the ACAS Xu networks, which had been trained to replace the lookup tables of a prototype airborne collision-avoidance system [45,46]; Marabou is its successor [150]. Planet combined satisfiability solving with linear relaxations [151], and mixed-integer linear programming encodes each ReLU with a binary variable [152]. Bunel and colleagues showed that most complete verifiers can be understood as branch-and-bound over the ReLU phases, with different bounding methods [153]. The problem is NP-hard in general [45], so complete methods are limited to modest networks.

Incomplete methods compute sound over-approximations of the reachable outputs: if the over-approximation satisfies the property, the property is proved; otherwise the result is "unknown". They are based on abstract interpretation, the theory of sound approximation of program semantics [154]. The simplest is interval arithmetic [155], applied layer by layer as interval bound propagation (IBP) [156]. AI2 used zonotopes [157], DeepPoly a polyhedral domain with one lower and one upper linear bound per neuron [158], and Wong and Kolter a convex outer polytope solved by linear programming duality [159]. CROWN propagates linear bounds backwards through the network with closed-form relaxations of each activation [160], and β-CROWN adds branch-and-bound with per-neuron split constraints, which makes it complete for ReLU networks and very fast on GPUs [161]; the auto_LiRPA library generalizes the approach to arbitrary computation graphs [162]. Lipschitz-constant estimation gives another route to robustness certificates [163]. Since 2020 the international Verification of Neural Networks Competition (VNN-COMP) has compared tools on common benchmarks, including ACAS Xu, image classifiers and control networks [64], and a monograph by Liu and colleagues reviews the algorithms [164]. Verification also feeds back into training: networks trained with abstract interpretation or IBP in the loop are far easier to verify afterwards [156,165].

7.3 Closed-loop verification

A network that controls a physical system must be verified together with the plant. Verisig transformed sigmoid networks into equivalent hybrid systems and used a hybrid-system reachability tool to analyze the closed loop [47]; NNV used star-set representations for networks and plants [48]; ReachNN, Sherlock-based methods and POLAR approximate the network by polynomials with rigorous error bounds and propagate Taylor models [166–168]; and Reach-LP propagates linear relaxations of the network through linear plant dynamics [169]. All of them must fight the wrapping effect: when a set is over-approximated by a simple shape at every step, the error compounds, and the bounds can explode even for a stable system [155]. Keeping the dependence of the state on the initial condition symbolically, as Taylor models and linear-bound methods do, is the main defence. Hamilton–Jacobi reachability, discussed in the control article, computes reachable sets on grids for low-dimensional systems and is used to synthesize safety controllers rather than to verify networks [170,171]. For image-based controllers, where the input set is hard to specify, Katz and colleagues verified the controller over the latent space of a generative model of the images instead [172]. The best-known applied result is again ACAS Xu: closed-loop reachability was used to identify encounter geometries in which the compressed network advisories could lead to near mid-air collisions, and to show safety elsewhere [173,174].

Monte Carlo simulation suggests that both properties hold, with the reachable set shrinking to a width of a few millimetres. It proves nothing, because 20000 samples cannot rule out a bad state between them. Plain IBP proves nothing either: its bounds grow to tens of metres, because every step forgets that the control is a function of the state and wraps the true set, a thin sliver, into an axis-aligned box. Splitting the initial set into 4096 cells barely helps, because each cell's box grows in the same way; re-gridding every step and using linear bounds help much more, but the error still accumulates. Only the symbolic variant succeeds: by carrying the correlation between the state and its initial condition from step to step, it tracks the true reachable set to within a few millimetres and proves the overshoot property with a single cell and both properties with 16 cells. The lesson matches what the verification tools above have found: the network bound (IBP or CROWN) matters less over long horizons than how dependencies are carried through the closed loop [168,169].

7.5 Learned certificates and counterexample-guided synthesis

A Lyapunov function proves stability without simulating any trajectory, and a barrier certificate proves that trajectories cannot cross from a safe set into an unsafe one [175–177]. Finding such functions is hard; sum-of-squares programming automated it for polynomial systems [177,178], and learning methods now extend it to neural networks and to systems with learned components [148]. The pattern that makes learned certificates trustworthy is counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS), which comes from program synthesis [179]: a learner proposes a candidate that satisfies the certificate conditions on a finite set of states, a verifier tries to prove the conditions everywhere, and any counterexample it finds is added to the learner's data. Neural Lyapunov control used this loop with the dReal SMT solver, which decides nonlinear real arithmetic up to a numerical tolerance [63,180]; Abate and colleagues and Ravanbakhsh and Sankaranarayanan developed related learner–verifier schemes [181,182]. Certificates learned together with policies are covered in the control article.

The first candidates, fitted to eight random states, are wrong, and the verifier finds states where they increase. Two features of the loop generalize. The division of labour is strict: the learner may be heuristic, approximate or neural, because nothing it produces is trusted until the sound verifier has checked it. And the margin matters: a certificate that only just satisfies its conditions is much harder to prove, so learners ask for slack that the verifier can then use. The same loop is used with neural candidates and SMT or MILP verifiers, at much higher computational cost [63,148,181].

8 Specifications, falsification and runtime assurance

8.1 Temporal logic as the interface

Every guarantee needs a precise statement of what is to be guaranteed. Temporal logics provide it. Linear temporal logic (LTL) describes properties of sequences of discrete states with operators such as "always" (G), "eventually" (F) and "until" [58]; it is used to specify robot missions, to synthesize correct-by-construction reactive controllers, efficiently for the GR(1) fragment, and to structure rewards in reinforcement learning [183–186], as described in the task planning article. Signal temporal logic (STL) adds real-valued signals and time intervals, so that a requirement such as "the gap to the vehicle ahead is always at least 3 m, and after any braking event the speed error returns below 1 m/s within 5 s" can be written directly [59]. Probabilistic temporal logics, checked by tools such as PRISM, express requirements on the probability of outcomes in stochastic models [187].

STL has a quantitative or robustness semantics: instead of true or false, a formula evaluated on a signal returns a real number whose sign says whether the formula is satisfied and whose magnitude says by how much it could be perturbed without changing the verdict [60,188]. Predicates μ(x) ≥ 0 return μ(x); conjunction and "always" become minima; disjunction and "eventually" become maxima. Robustness turns logic into a function that optimization can work with, which has made it the main bridge between temporal logic and learning. It is used as an objective or constraint in model predictive control by mixed-integer programming [189], smoothed for gradient-based control [190], computed as a differentiable computation graph so that specifications can be backpropagated into neural networks [191], and used as a reward for reinforcement learning [192]. Formulas can also be monitored online from streaming data, the basis of runtime verification [193,194].

8.2 Falsification and scenario-based testing

When a closed loop is too complex to verify, for instance because it contains a camera, a large network and a high-fidelity simulator, the robustness semantics still allows a systematic search for failures. Falsification treats the environment inputs (the behavior of other agents, initial conditions, disturbances) as decision variables and minimizes the robustness of the specification by simulation-based optimization; a negative value is a concrete counterexample. S-TaLiRo and Breach pioneered tools for hybrid systems [195,196], VerifAI and the Scenic scenario language extended the approach to AI-based autonomous systems with probabilistic scenario programs [197,198], and Corso and colleagues survey the black-box safety-validation algorithms involved: optimization, path planning, reinforcement learning and importance sampling [199]. Falsification cannot prove safety, but it finds bugs early and cheaply, and it pairs naturally with verification: counterexamples can be used to retrain or redesign, and falsification can be run first to avoid trying to prove false properties.

8.4 Runtime assurance: monitors, shields and verified fallbacks

Verification of every learned component may be out of reach, but it is often possible to verify a simpler safety envelope around them. The Simplex architecture runs a high-performance but unverified controller alongside a verified safety controller, with a decision module that switches to the safety controller whenever the state approaches the boundary of a region from which the safety controller is known to recover [17,200]. Shields for reinforcement learning compute, from a temporal-logic specification and an abstraction of the environment, the set of actions that keep the system safe, and override the agent's choice when it is not in that set [81]. Control barrier functions and predictive safety filters do the same in continuous state spaces by minimally modifying the learned action [144,145,201]. In differential dynamic logic, Platzer's theorem prover KeYmaera X verifies hybrid-system models, ModelPlex derives runtime monitors that check whether the real system still conforms to the verified model, and "justified speculative control" lets a learning agent act freely while the monitors hold and falls back to a verified controller when they do not [202–204]. Aviation has codified the pattern: ASTM F3269 describes run-time assurance architectures that bound the behavior of complex functions, including machine-learning functions, with a monitored recovery function [205,206].

Runtime assurance moves the burden of proof from the learned component to three simpler things: the monitor, the fallback controller, and the switching logic, all of which must be verified together with timing and sensing assumptions. It also exposes a dependency that verification of the controller alone hides: the monitor needs a correct estimate of the state, so the perception that feeds it must be trusted or bounded. Statistical methods such as conformal prediction can provide calibrated error bounds for learned predictors that a safety layer then uses, which gives probabilistic rather than worst-case guarantees [207,208].

9 Hybrid systems in practice

9.1 Patterns that recur

Across application domains, deployed physical AI systems that use learning almost always use it inside a hybrid architecture. Table 3 summarizes representative cases.

Three patterns recur. First, learned perception, explicit decision: networks estimate the state and its uncertainty, and an explicit layer of rules, planning or optimization decides, because that layer is where requirements and safety arguments are written. Responsibility-sensitive safety, for example, specifies safe longitudinal and lateral distances and proper responses as formulas that any driving policy can be checked against [209]. Second, learned proposal, explicit check: a learned component proposes plans, grasps or actions and a symbolic or physical model accepts or rejects them, from TAMP samplers to LLM-Modulo planners [52,70]. Third, learned performance, explicit envelope: a learned controller provides performance inside an envelope enforced by a verified filter or fallback [17,145].

9.2 Assurance and standards

Certification practice is adapting to these architectures. For road vehicles, ISO 21448 (safety of the intended functionality) addresses hazards from functional insufficiencies such as perception errors rather than faults, and UL 4600 sets out how to build a safety case for an autonomous product, including its machine-learning components [210,211]. In aviation, EASA's concept paper on machine-learning applications defines learning assurance objectives, and ASTM F3269 standardizes run-time assurance [205,215]. For industrial and collaborative robots, the safety functions themselves remain conventional and certified [214]. In all of them, explicit and verifiable structure, whether a specification, a monitor or a verified fallback, is what allows a learned component to be argued safe.

10 Evaluation, tools and open problems

10.1 Choosing an integration

The examples in this article suggest a few working rules. Use knowledge as structure when it is certain and universal (conservation laws, kinematics, hard limits), because structure generalizes everywhere and costs nothing at run time. Use it as a loss when it is certain but hard to build in, or when labels are scarce, and expect it to help learning rather than guarantee anything. Use a symbolic layer (planner, program or logic) where decisions must compose over long horizons or be explained, and invest in grounding its predicates. Use verification on the smallest component that carries the safety argument, often a filter, fallback or decision layer rather than the largest network, and design that component to be verifiable. 10.2 Tools

Mature open tools exist for most of the techniques in this article. Probabilistic logic programming with neural predicates is available in DeepProbLog and Scallop [67,88]; logic tensor networks have open implementations [66]; classical planners such as FF and plan validators such as VAL are standard components of PDDL pipelines [115,217]; physics-informed and differentiable-optimization layers are available for the main deep-learning frameworks [136]; network verifiers including Marabou, NNV and α,β-CROWN take part in VNN-COMP [48,64,150]; Drake combines rigid-body dynamics with optimization and verification tools for robotics [218]; and S-TaLiRo, Breach, VerifAI and Scenic support specification-based falsification [195–198].

10.3 Open problems

  • Scalable verification. Complete network verification remains NP-hard, and closed-loop verification multiplies the difficulty by the horizon; vision-based controllers with millions of parameters and high-dimensional inputs are far beyond current exact methods [45,64,172].
  • Specifying perception. Properties such as "the pedestrian is detected" have no formal definition over pixels, so verification of perception relies on proxies: robustness to perturbations, generative input models, or statistical bounds [149,172,207].
  • Learning abstractions that support decisions. Learned predicates and operators work in small domains; learning abstractions that are sound enough to plan with in open-ended environments, and knowing when they are wrong, is unsolved [65,76,100].
  • Language models and formal guarantees. Translating instructions into formal specifications makes language models checkable, but the translation itself can be wrong in ways that no downstream verifier detects [52,54,56].
  • Physics where models are uncertain. Contact, friction, deformable and fluid interactions are where both physical models and learned models are weakest, and where combining them has the most to offer [122,133,139].
  • Integration cost. Neuro-symbolic systems often have more components, more interfaces and more failure modes than either pure approach, and their evaluation needs both machine-learning benchmarks and formal criteria [69,216].

Conclusion

Neuro-symbolic and hybrid systems combine what learning does best, turning raw, noisy sensor data into estimates and skills, with what explicit models do best: composing decisions over long horizons, encoding the laws of physics, and supporting proofs. The examples in this article show the combination at work in small form. A logical formula about gait improved a learned contact estimator both as a training signal and as an inference rule; a one-line logic program taught a perception model from labels it never saw; the choice of a symbolic hypothesis language decided whether learned operators produced valid plans; a distilled program could be verified and repaired where a network could only be sampled; physical structure, not residual learning, gave generalization outside the data; a projection layer turned constraint violations from frequent to impossible; carrying symbolic dependencies through a closed loop made a neural controller provably safe; a learner–verifier loop produced a certificate that no single step could be trusted to find; and robustness-guided search found rare failures that random testing missed. As learned components grow more capable and are deployed in more safety-critical settings, the explicit, checkable structure around them becomes more important, not less.
