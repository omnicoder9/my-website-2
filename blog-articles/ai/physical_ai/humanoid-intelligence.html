1 What humanoid intelligence is, and why the body matters

A humanoid robot has a body organized like a person's: a torso, a head with cameras, two arms with hands, and usually two legs. Humanoid intelligence is the set of capabilities that let such a body act usefully in the world: perceiving its surroundings and its own state, walking over varied ground, keeping its balance when pushed, coordinating all of its joints so that hands, feet, head and centre of mass serve one task, manipulating objects, and interacting with people in ways they find natural and safe [1–3]. Each of these capabilities has its own literature, and many are covered elsewhere in this series. What makes humanoids a distinct research direction is that all of them must run at once, in one body, under constraints that couple them tightly.

Three properties of the humanoid body create that coupling. First, the robot is a floating-base system: no joint attaches it to the world, so it can move its body only by pushing on the ground or other surfaces through contacts that can push but not pull and can slip [4,5]. Every arm motion, head turn and step therefore changes the forces the feet must provide, and a manipulation plan that ignores balance can tip the robot over. Second, a biped standing on small feet with a high centre of mass is dynamically unstable: like an inverted pendulum, it falls unless it acts, and it has only a few hundred milliseconds to act after a disturbance [6,7]. Third, the body is highly redundant: thirty or more actuated joints give infinitely many ways to achieve a hand position, so the robot must constantly choose among them, trading off balance, reach, joint limits, collisions, energy and how its motion looks to people [8,9].

Why build robots in human form at all, when wheels are more stable and grippers simpler than hands? Three arguments recur. The world is built for human bodies, with stairs, doorways, handles, tools and vehicles designed around human reach and size, so a humanoid can in principle use it without modification [1,10]. People read human-like bodies easily, which helps communication and teaching, although human likeness also raises expectations and can disturb people when it is imperfect [11,12]. And human motion is the largest available source of demonstrations: motion capture, teleoperation and video of people can be mapped onto a humanoid much more directly than onto other bodies [13–15]. That third argument has become central since 2023, as learning-based methods trained on human data and massive simulation have made humanoids walk, run and manipulate far more robustly than before [16–18]. Embodied-cognition researchers add a fourth: that intelligence itself is shaped by the body and its interaction with the environment, so a human-like body is a route to human-like learning [19–21].

This article follows the six capabilities in the title and then the question of how to integrate them. Section 2 traces the history. Section 3 sets out the dynamics that make humanoids hard: centroidal momentum, contact wrench constraints and redundancy. Sections 4 to 9 cover perception, locomotion, balance, whole-body coordination, manipulation and social interaction, each from the humanoid's point of view. Section 10 discusses architectures that combine them, from optimization-based control stacks to dual-system vision-language-action models, and Section 11 covers evaluation, safety and open problems.

2 A short history: from WABOT-1 to humanoid foundation models

2.1 Walking machines and the first humanoids (1960s–1990s)

The theory that made biped walking controllable came before the robots. In the late 1960s Miomir Vukobratović and colleagues in Belgrade introduced the zero-moment point (ZMP), the point on the ground about which the horizontal moments of the ground reaction force vanish; as long as the ZMP stays strictly inside the support polygon of the feet, the feet do not tip over, and walking patterns could be designed by planning a ZMP trajectory [24]. In 1973 Ichiro Kato's group at Waseda University completed WABOT-1, generally regarded as the first full-scale anthropomorphic robot: it walked slowly with its lower limbs, gripped objects with hands that had tactile sensors, measured distances with artificial eyes and ears, and conversed in Japanese [25].

A different line of research asked how little control walking needs. Raibert's hopping and running machines at Carnegie Mellon and MIT in the 1980s balanced dynamically with three simple decoupled controllers for hopping height, forward speed and body attitude [26]. McGeer showed in 1990 that a purely mechanical biped can walk down a gentle slope with a stable, human-like gait and no control at all, its legs swinging like pendulums [27], and Collins and colleagues later built powered robots on this passive-dynamic principle that walked with energy efficiency comparable to humans [28]. These results shaped the idea, central to modern humanoids, that good control exploits the body's natural dynamics rather than fighting them [29,30].

2.2 Honda, HRP and the ZMP era (1996–2012)

Honda's humanoid program, secretly under way since 1986, surprised the field in 1996 with P2, a self-contained 1.8 m biped that walked, climbed stairs and pushed carts; its successors P3 and ASIMO (2000) made the humanoid a public symbol of robotics [31,32]. Honda's walking combined ZMP-based pattern generation with posture and ground-reaction force feedback, and ASIMO added recognition of faces, gestures, voices and moving objects, integrated so that the robot could greet people, follow them and respond to commands [31,32]. In Japan's Humanoid Robotics Project, AIST and Kawada Industries developed the HRP series of research humanoids, including HRP-2, which could lie down and get up [33]; the same group published the preview-control method for generating CoM trajectories from planned ZMP trajectories that became a standard walking pattern generator [1,34]. Linear inverted pendulum models, ZMP preview control and model predictive control of the ZMP defined humanoid locomotion for more than a decade [35–37].

In parallel, research on humanoid cognition grew from the behavior-based robotics movement. Brooks's Cog project at MIT (from 1993) built an upper-body humanoid to study how human-like intelligence could develop from embodied interaction, with work on saccades, reaching, joint attention and imitation [38]. Breazeal's Kismet, an expressive robot head, explored social interaction driven by models of emotion and drives [39]. The European RobotCub project produced the iCub, a child-sized open-source humanoid designed for research on developmental cognition, with dexterous hands and a tactile skin [21,40,41]. In Japan, ATR's Robovie studies took humanoids into schools and museums to measure how people respond to them over time [42].

2.3 Torque control, optimization and the DARPA Robotics Challenge (2005–2015)

Position-controlled humanoids walking on flat floors could not cope with uneven ground or unexpected contact. Torque-controlled humanoids such as Sarcos's CB, DLR's TORO and Boston Dynamics' hydraulic Atlas, together with whole-body controllers that optimize contact forces, changed this [43–45]. The capture point (2006) and the divergent component of motion gave a simple, general account of when and where to step to avoid falling [6,46,47], and task-priority whole-body controllers solved as quadratic programs became fast enough to run at 1 kHz on a full humanoid [8,9,48].

The DARPA Robotics Challenge (2012–2015), motivated by the Fukushima nuclear accident, asked robots to drive a vehicle, open doors, turn valves, use a drill, cross rubble and climb stairs in a disaster scenario. Most finalists were humanoids, and the teams that used Atlas built optimization-based planning, estimation and control stacks that are still a reference design [22,45,49]. The finals in 2015 were won by KAIST's DRC-HUBO, which could switch from walking to rolling on wheels at its knees, and the competition's widely viewed falls showed how far humanoids were from robust autonomy; most tasks were executed with heavy human supervision [50].

2.4 Learning and the humanoid boom (2018–present)

Deep reinforcement learning in simulation, which had produced robust quadruped controllers [51–53], was applied to bipeds from about 2018, first to Agility Robotics' Cassie [54–57] and then to full humanoids. In 2024 Radosavovic and colleagues showed a transformer policy trained entirely in simulation walking a full-size humanoid outdoors over varied terrain [16], Haarnoja and colleagues trained small humanoids to play one-versus-one soccer with agile, emergent recovery behaviors [58], and humanoid parkour policies learned to jump and climb [59]. Physics-based character animation contributed motion-imitation methods that let policies reproduce human motion capture [60–62], which were adapted into learned whole-body controllers that track human motion for teleoperation and skill learning [15,17,63–67].

At the same time, a wave of commercial humanoids appeared from companies such as Agility Robotics, Tesla, Figure, Unitree and Boston Dynamics, alongside open research platforms such as the MIT humanoid and the Berkeley Humanoid [3,10,68,69]. Vision-language-action models brought web-scale knowledge to robot control [70,71], and humanoid-specific foundation models such as NVIDIA's GR00T N1 and Figure's Helix adopted a dual-system design: a vision-language model that interprets the scene and instruction at a few hertz, and a fast visuomotor policy that outputs whole upper-body actions at high rate [18,23]. 3 The humanoid body as a control problem

3.1 Floating base and centroidal dynamics

A humanoid's configuration is the pose of its base (pelvis or torso) in the world, six unactuated degrees of freedom, plus its joint angles. Its rigid-body dynamics, computed with the recursive algorithms described in the physical modeling article [5], take the form

M(q) q̈ + h(q, q̇) = ST τ + Σi Ji(q)T fi

where S selects the actuated joints and fi are the contact wrenches at the feet, hands or other contacts. The first six rows, those of the floating base, contain no joint torques at all: the robot can change its overall motion only through contact forces [4]. These six rows are equivalent to Newton's and Euler's laws for the whole body. Writing hG = (m ċ, kG) for the centroidal momentum, the linear momentum of the centre of mass (CoM) c and the angular momentum kG about it, they read

m c̈ = Σi fi + m g, k̇G = Σi (pi − c) × fi + ni

where pi are contact points and ni contact moments. Orin, Goswami and Lee showed that the centroidal momentum is a linear function of the joint velocities, hG = AG(q) q̇, through the centroidal momentum matrix AG, which can be computed efficiently [72,73]. This gives a compact model of everything that matters for balance and locomotion: the robot's limbs can be moved in many ways, but their combined effect on the body's motion is captured by six numbers whose rates of change are set entirely by contact forces. Planning with centroidal dynamics plus full kinematics, rather than full dynamics, has become a standard compromise for humanoid motion generation [74,75].

3.2 Contacts: what the ground can and cannot do

A contact can only push, and only within its friction cone; a flat foot can resist moments only as long as the centre of pressure (CoP) stays inside the foot. The set of wrenches a contact can transmit is the contact wrench cone [76,77]. For a single flat contact on level ground, the CoP condition is the ZMP criterion: the ZMP of the whole robot coincides with the CoP and must lie in the support polygon [24,78]. Popovic, Goswami and Herr clarified the relations between the ZMP, CoP, the foot-rotation indicator and the centroidal moment pivot (CMP), the point where a line through the CoM parallel to the ground reaction force meets the ground; the CMP leaves the support polygon exactly when the body's angular momentum changes, which humans exploit in balance recovery [79,80]. With multiple non-coplanar contacts, as when a robot climbs a ladder, leans on a table or pushes a heavy object, the ZMP criterion no longer applies and feasibility must be checked against the combined contact wrench cones [76,81,82].

3.3 Redundancy, limits and hardware

Humanoids have far more joints than any one task needs: research humanoids range from about 20 actuated joints to more than 50 when hands are included [40,83]. Redundancy is what lets a humanoid reach while staying balanced, or look at a person while walking, but it also means that the controller must resolve it continually, subject to joint position, velocity and torque limits, self-collision and actuator heating [9,84]. The hardware determines what control can achieve. Series elastic actuators, as on NASA's Valkyrie, give accurate torque control and absorb impacts [83,85]; low-ratio "proprioceptive" actuators like those of the MIT Cheetah and MIT humanoid allow fast, transparent force control and robust contact [68,86]; and hydraulics provided the power density of the original Atlas [22]. These trade-offs are discussed in the actuators section of the hardware article. For the rest of this article, the important point is that most humanoid intelligence is expressed through a controller that turns desired motions of the CoM, feet, hands and head into joint torques that respect all of these constraints at once (Section 7).

4 Embodied perception: gaze, body and people

A humanoid perceives the world through cameras in a head that moves with its body, so its perception is active: what it sees depends on where it looks, and where it looks is a decision. Bajcsy defined active perception in 1988 as the problem of controlling the sensors to gather the information a task needs [87], and Ballard's animate vision argued that gaze control simplifies vision by making the camera's frame of reference do much of the work [88,89]. The detection, segmentation and tracking algorithms themselves are covered in the computer vision article; this section concerns what is specific to perception in a humanoid body.

4.1 Where to look

Studies of people performing everyday tasks show that gaze is tightly coupled to action: people fixate the object they are about to grasp, typically just before the hand moves, and rarely look at things irrelevant to the current step [90]. When walking over rough terrain, people look about two steps ahead and adjust how far ahead they look to the difficulty of the terrain [91]. A humanoid faces the same trade-off: a narrow, high-resolution camera can look at the footholds ahead, a person crossing its path or the object it is about to grasp, but not all at once, and each gaze shift costs time during which images are blurred. Uncertainty about what is not being watched grows with time: the person may have moved, odometry drift has shifted the footholds, a held object may have slipped. Gaze control can therefore be framed as scheduling observations to keep task-relevant uncertainty within bounds [89].

4.3 Sensing the body: proprioception and state estimation

A humanoid needs an accurate estimate of its own base pose and velocity, which no sensor measures directly. Humanoid state estimators fuse an inertial measurement unit in the pelvis or torso with joint encoders and the assumption that feet in contact do not slip, and add vision or lidar to bound drift [92,93]. The contact-aided invariant extended Kalman filter exploits the geometry of this problem to obtain better convergence than a standard EKF and is used on Cassie and other bipeds [94,95]. The general principles of these filters are covered in the sensor fusion article, and the legged-robot variant in its motion estimation section [96]. Contact detection itself, deciding which feet are in contact, combines force sensing, kinematics and the gait phase, and errors in it are a common cause of falls [92].

Beyond state, a humanoid benefits from a body schema, a model of its own body that can be learned and updated, analogous to the body representations studied in neuroscience [97]. Bongard, Zykov and Lipson showed that a robot can infer its own morphology from its actions and adapt its gait after damage [98]. Tactile skins, such as the modular skins developed for iCub and other humanoids, give a sense of contact over the whole body, and the iCub has been used to learn a "peripersonal space" around its body from the association between seen objects and touch, so it can anticipate and avoid contact [99–101].

4.4 Perceiving people

For a humanoid working among people, the most important objects are people themselves: their position and motion, their body pose, their gaze direction and gestures, and what they are attending to. Real-time multi-person 2D and 3D pose estimation and parametric body models such as SMPL provide the skeleton-level description [102–104], and hand pose estimators recover finger configurations [105,106]; these methods are described in the human perception section of the vision article. Two humanoid-specific uses stand out. The first is social: head pose, gaze and body orientation tell the robot whom a person is addressing and what they are looking at, the inputs to the joint attention and turn-taking behaviors of Section 9 [107,108]. The second is learning: the same estimators turn videos of people into motion data that can be retargeted to the robot (Section 8.3) [15,109].

5 Locomotion

Bipedal locomotion alternates between single support, when the robot balances on one foot, and double support, when weight transfers between feet. It is a hybrid dynamical system: continuous dynamics between contact events and discrete jumps at foot strike [110,111]. Three families of methods have been used to control it: reduced-model pattern generation, full-model optimization and hybrid zero dynamics, and learned policies [3,4,30].

5.1 Reduced models: the linear inverted pendulum

If the CoM moves at constant height zc and the body's angular momentum is neglected, the centroidal dynamics of Section 3 reduce to the linear inverted pendulum (LIP) of Kajita and colleagues [35]:

c̈x = ω2 (cx − px), ω = √(g / zc)

with p the ZMP. The same equation can be read the other way: the ZMP is p = c − c̈ / ω2, the "cart–table" model. A walking pattern generator must find a CoM trajectory whose ZMP follows a reference that moves from foot to foot. Because the LIP is unstable, the CoM must start moving before the ZMP does, and Kajita and colleagues solved this with preview control: an optimal servo that uses the planned ZMP reference over the next one to two seconds [34]. Wieber reformulated the problem as linear model predictive control with explicit ZMP constraints [36], Herdt and colleagues added the footstep positions as decision variables so the robot adjusts its steps automatically [37], and the divergent component of motion gave a three-dimensional formulation that handles height changes [47]. The MPC section of the control article covers the general method.

5.3 Full-model optimization and hybrid zero dynamics

More dynamic walking uses more of the robot's model. Optimization-based controllers compute CoM and footstep plans with centroidal models and track them with whole-body quadratic programs, as on Atlas in the DARPA Robotics Challenge [22,45], and whole-body nonlinear MPC is now feasible in real time [112,113]. Footstep placement on rough terrain can be planned as a mixed-integer convex program that chooses which safe region each foot goes into [114]. Hybrid zero dynamics (HZD), developed by Grizzle, Westervelt and colleagues, takes a different approach: it designs virtual constraints, output functions of the joints that are driven to zero by feedback, so that the closed-loop robot evolves on a low-dimensional surface whose periodic orbits are provably stable [110,111]. HZD controllers produced fast, efficient walking and running on underactuated bipeds such as MABEL and Cassie [30,115].

5.4 Learned locomotion

Since about 2018, deep reinforcement learning has become the most effective way to obtain robust bipedal and humanoid locomotion. Policies are trained in massively parallel simulation with randomized dynamics, terrain and sensor noise, often with a privileged teacher policy distilled into one that uses only onboard sensors, and then transferred to hardware without fine-tuning [53,116,117]; the method is covered in the sim-to-real section of the reinforcement learning article. On Cassie, learned policies walked blind up and down stairs [56], produced all common gaits from a single reward composed of periodic phase terms [55], and in later work jumped and ran with a dual-history architecture that combines long- and short-term memory of inputs and states [57]. On full-size humanoids, a causal transformer trained with large-scale RL walked outdoors over varied terrain and adapted in context to disturbances [16]; the same group showed that humanoid locomotion can be learned as next-token prediction over sensorimotor sequences, including data from human motion capture and videos that lack actions [118]. Policies that use depth perception can climb, jump onto boxes and leap gaps [59].

Learned and model-based locomotion are converging. Motion priors from human data make learned gaits look natural and reduce reward engineering [61,119]; model-based footstep plans can be given to learned trackers as commands [120,121]; and models such as the capture point are used to shape rewards and to check learned policies. The integration section returns to this hybrid design.

6 Balance and fall management

6.1 Strategies from human balance

Human balance research describes a small repertoire of strategies. For small perturbations people use an ankle strategy, moving the CoP within the feet; for larger or faster ones a hip strategy, bending at the hips to generate a ground reaction force that pulls the CoM back; and for the largest a stepping or "change-in-support" strategy [122–124]. Winter's review of human posture control explains these in terms of the CoP–CoM relationship [125], and measurements of walking humans show that whole-body angular momentum is kept small and tightly regulated in normal gait, suggesting that changes in angular momentum are a reserve for recovery [79,80]. Stephens mapped the three strategies onto robot models: the LIP for the ankle strategy, a LIP with a flywheel for the hip strategy, and the capture point for stepping [6,126].

The reactive behavior article develops the stepping strategy in detail, with a runnable example of capture-point push recovery. Its essential result is that the instantaneous capture point ξ = c + ċ / ω, also called the divergent component of motion, determines whether the robot can stop: if the CoP can be placed at ξ the robot comes to rest above it, and if ξ lies outside the reachable support region the robot must step or fall [6,46,47]. The hip strategy is the subject of the next example.

6.2 Example: ankle and hip strategies

Pratt and colleagues extended the LIP with a flywheel representing the upper body's rotational inertia [6]. A hip torque τ shifts the CMP to p + τ / (m g), so spinning the torso briefly moves the effective push point beyond the foot. 6.3 Momentum-based and force-based balance

Hyon, Hale and Cheng showed that a torque-controlled humanoid can balance under unknown external forces by computing a desired total ground reaction force from CoM error and distributing it to the contact points, with no model of the disturbance [43]. Ott, Roa and Hirzinger formulated the distribution as an optimization over contact forces subject to friction and CoP constraints [44], and Stephens and Atkeson computed contact forces from desired CoM dynamics for compliant humanoids [128]. Lee and Goswami's momentum-based controller prescribes desired rates of linear and angular momentum and finds ground reaction forces and CoPs for each foot that achieve them as closely as possible, prioritizing linear momentum when both cannot be met; it works on non-level and moving ground [127]. Herzog and colleagues implemented momentum control within a hierarchical inverse-dynamics QP on a torque-controlled humanoid and showed recovery from pushes while balancing on one foot [48].

6.4 When balance fails

No controller prevents every fall, and falls were frequent at the DARPA Robotics Challenge [50]. Fall management has three parts: predicting a fall early, avoiding it if possible, and otherwise falling safely. Learned classifiers over state features can predict an impending fall early enough to react [129]. For unavoidable falls, the UKEMI technique of Fujiwara and colleagues, named after judo breakfalls, controlled HRP-2P to fall backward with reduced impact by squatting and extending the body [130], and Ha and Liu planned sequences of contacts that spread the impact over several body parts [131]. Getting up again is also a whole-body skill; learned policies now produce robust fall recovery and stand-up behaviors on small humanoids and on quadrupeds [58,132]. Safety aspects of falling are discussed in the safe stopping section of the reactive behavior article.

7 Whole-body coordination

Whole-body control (WBC) is the layer that turns many simultaneous goals, such as keep the CoM over the feet, put the right hand on the handle, keep the left foot flat, look at the person, stay away from joint limits, into joint torques or velocities for every joint at once [8,75]. It is where the humanoid's redundancy is resolved and where locomotion, balance, manipulation and gaze meet.

7.1 Task-priority control

The basic tool is the task Jacobian Ji, which maps joint velocities to the velocity of task i; resolved-rate control inverts it to obtain joint motions that achieve a desired task velocity [133]. When tasks conflict, a robot can either weight them, solving one least-squares problem whose solution compromises between all of them, or prioritize them, satisfying lower-priority tasks only in the null space of higher-priority ones. Nakamura, Hanafusa and Yoshikawa formulated task-priority redundancy resolution in 1987 [134], and Siciliano and Slotine gave the recursive form for any number of priority levels [135]:

q̇k = q̇k−1 + (Jk Nk−1)+ (ẋk − Jk q̇k−1), Nk = Nk−1 (I − (Jk Nk−1)+ Jk Nk−1)

Khatib's operational-space formulation extended the idea to dynamics and forces, and Sentis and Khatib applied it to humanoids with a hierarchy of contact, balance, manipulation and posture tasks [8,136]; with multiple contacts, internal forces between the contacts become additional quantities to control [137]. Real robots also have inequality constraints, joint limits, friction cones and collision avoidance, which pseudo-inverse projections cannot express. Kanoun, Lamiraux and Wieber generalized the priority framework to inequality tasks by solving a sequence of quadratic programs [138], and Escande, Mansard and Wieber's hierarchical QP solver made this fast enough for online humanoid control [9].

7.3 Whole-body controllers on real humanoids

On torque-controlled humanoids, WBC is solved at the level of dynamics: a QP finds joint accelerations, joint torques and contact forces that satisfy the equations of motion, friction cones and torque limits while tracking task accelerations, either with weights or with strict priorities [45,48,139]. The controllers used on Atlas during the DARPA Robotics Challenge solved such QPs at about 1 kHz to track CoM and footstep plans from a centroidal planner [22,45]. Because a QP looks only at the current instant, it is usually combined with a predictive layer: an MPC over centroidal dynamics for locomotion and balance [75,140], or full whole-body MPC, which Koenemann and colleagues ran on the HRP-2 using the iterative LQG approach of Tassa and colleagues [113,141].

7.4 Multi-contact and loco-manipulation

Humans use more than their feet: they lean on tables, use handrails, kneel and brace against walls. Multi-contact planning chooses a sequence of contact sets and the motion between them, a combinatorial problem that Hauser, Bretl and Latombe tackled for climbing and legged robots on varied terrain [81] and Bouyarmane and Kheddar extended to combined locomotion and manipulation steps [82]. Mordatch, Todorov and Popović's contact-invariant optimization showed that contact sequences for complex whole-body behaviors can emerge from continuous optimization [142]. Loco-manipulation, moving the body while exerting forces on objects, requires the balance controller to account for the reaction forces at the hands: Harada and colleagues introduced a generalized ZMP that includes hand forces to let a humanoid push heavy objects while walking [143]. The flow-matching imitation-learning policies of Rouxel and colleagues learn multi-support manipulation in which the robot braces with one hand while working with the other [144].

7.5 Learned whole-body controllers

A newer approach trains a single neural network to track whole-body motion targets. Starting from physics-based character animation, where DeepMimic and adversarial motion priors trained simulated humanoids to imitate motion capture [60,61] and Perpetual Humanoid Control tracked almost all of the AMASS motion-capture archive and recovered from failures [14,62], researchers trained policies that track retargeted human motion on real humanoids. H2O and OmniH2O teleoperate a full-size humanoid in real time from an RGB camera or VR headset by tracking the operator's motion [17,63]; Expressive Whole-Body Control tracks upper-body motion while a robust lower body walks [64]; HumanPlus "shadows" a human from a single camera and uses the collected data to train autonomous skills [15]; and HOVER distils several command modes (joint angles, keypoints, root velocity) into one controller [65]. ASAP learns a correction for the gap between simulated and real dynamics from real-world rollouts, enabling agile motions such as jumps and kicks [66], and BeyondMimic combines precise motion tracking with a diffusion model that composes tracked skills for new tasks [67]. These controllers play the role that the QP plays in optimization-based stacks, a general-purpose interface between "what the body should do" and the actuators, and they are the action space of many humanoid foundation models (Section 10) [18,145].

8 Manipulation with a humanoid body

Manipulation by humanoids inherits everything in the general manipulation literature, from grasp synthesis to visuomotor policies [146,147], and adds three things: two arms that can cooperate, human-like hands with many degrees of freedom, and a body that must balance while the hands exert forces.

8.1 Hands and bimanual skills

The design of humanoid hands, from the Utah/MIT and DLR hands to underactuated and soft hands, is covered in the end effectors section of the hardware article [148]. Control of many-fingered hands is simplified by the observation that human grasps occupy a low-dimensional space: Santello, Flanders and Soechting found that two principal components, or postural synergies, explain more than 80% of the variance of hand postures when grasping imagined objects [149], and Ciocarlie and colleagues used eigengrasps to plan dexterous grasps in a low-dimensional space [150]. Grasp taxonomies such as the GRASP taxonomy organize the 33 human grasp types into a hierarchy that guides hand design and grasp selection [151]. Learned in-hand manipulation with multi-fingered hands, trained in simulation and transferred to hardware, is now well established [152,153], and recent work transfers vision-based dexterous manipulation policies trained with RL to humanoids with two multi-fingered hands [154].

Bimanual manipulation, whether both hands holding one object or each hand doing a different part of a task, requires coordinated control of the relative motion and of the internal forces in the closed chain formed by the arms and the object [155]. Learning-based approaches have made bimanual skills much more accessible: low-cost bimanual teleoperation systems and action-chunking transformers learned fine skills such as threading and battery insertion [156], diffusion policies represent multimodal bimanual actions [157], and Mobile ALOHA extended this to whole-body mobile bimanual tasks [158]. On humanoids, 3D diffusion policies trained on data collected with a whole-upper-body teleoperation system generalized to new scenes [159], and immersive VR teleoperation systems give the operator stereo vision from the robot's head [160].

8.2 Manipulating while balancing

Every force a humanoid applies with its hands is felt at its feet. Lifting a heavy box shifts the combined CoM forward; pushing a cart requires leaning into it; opening a heavy door creates a moment the feet must resist. Whole-body controllers handle this by including the hand wrenches in the centroidal dynamics and the contact wrench constraints, so that the feet's CoP and friction limits bound what the hands can do [127,137,143]. Impedance and force control at the hands (see the force control section of the control article) make contact with uncertain objects safe [161], and the controller can trade arm forces against stance width or step adjustments. Learned policies acquire similar behavior implicitly: whole-body teleoperation data contain the operator's own balance strategy, and policies trained on it lean and brace as people do [15,17].

8.3 Learning from people: retargeting

Human motion is the most natural source of humanoid skills, but a person's body differs from any robot's in proportions, joint ranges, mass distribution and contacts. Motion retargeting maps human motion onto the robot while preserving what matters for the task. In computer graphics, Gleicher formulated retargeting as a spacetime optimization that keeps important constraints, such as foot contacts, while staying close to the original motion [162]. Pollard and colleagues adapted captured human motion to the joint and velocity limits of a humanoid's upper body [163], Nakaoka and colleagues made HRP-2 perform a traditional Japanese dance by extracting leg task primitives from motion capture and regenerating balanced leg motions [164], and Ayusawa and Yoshida optimized the motion and the human-to-robot morphing parameters together [165]. Real-time retargeting for teleoperation solves a whole-body inverse kinematics problem at each frame [145,166], and learned controllers increasingly retarget implicitly by tracking keypoints in simulation [17,63]. The teleoperation and learning from video sections of the imitation learning article describe the data side.

9 Embodied social interaction

A humanoid's body is itself a communication channel. People interpret where its head points, how it moves its arms, how close it stands and how it times its actions as signals of attention, intention and even emotion, whether or not the designers intended them [11,167]. Socially interactive robots therefore need two things: the perception of people's social signals described in Section 4.4, and control of their own body so that the signals it sends are the right ones. This section concerns the second, the social behavior a single humanoid expresses through its body. Coordination among teams of robots and people is a separate topic. The broader field of human–robot interaction is surveyed by Goodrich and Schultz [168].

9.1 Gaze and joint attention

Gaze is the most studied social behavior in robots. Admoni and Scassellati's review describes how robot gaze affects people's understanding, engagement and task performance: people follow a robot's gaze to objects, use it to predict what the robot will do, and feel more engaged when it looks at them at appropriate moments [107]. Mutlu and colleagues showed that a humanoid can shape the roles of participants in a conversation, addressee, bystander or overhearer, purely through how it distributes its gaze [169]. Joint attention, two agents attending to the same object and knowing that they do, underlies much of human teaching and cooperation; it has been studied in robots as a set of skills (following gaze, directing attention by gaze or pointing, checking that the partner attends) and as something that can develop through learning [170–172]. Engagement, the process by which people start, maintain and end an interaction, is signalled largely through gaze and head orientation, and Sidner and colleagues built robots that track and produce these cues [108].

For a humanoid, social gaze competes with task gaze: Section 4.2 showed the robot needs its cameras on footholds and objects. Humans resolve this by looking at the task most of the time and glancing at partners at moments that matter, such as turn transitions and before acting on a shared object [90,107]. Models learned from human interaction data can generate such multimodal behavior, coordinating gaze, gesture and speech timing [173].

9.2 Gesture, posture and expression

Co-speech gestures, the hand and arm movements people make while talking, carry part of the message: they point, depict shapes and sizes, and mark emphasis [174]. Salem and colleagues built a system that generates gestures for the ASIMO humanoid from the same representation as its synthesized speech, keeping the two synchronized, and evaluated how people perceive the result [175]. Expressive heads and faces, from Kismet's display of emotion-like states [39] to android robots, raise the question of how human-like a robot should look. Mori's uncanny valley hypothesis, first published in 1970, proposed that affinity rises with human likeness until near-human appearance or motion becomes eerie [12]; the hypothesis remains debated, but it is a reason many humanoids use stylized rather than realistic faces. Large language models have recently been used to generate expressive robot behaviors from social context, composing gestures, gaze and motion from language descriptions [176].

9.3 Space and motion around people

People regulate distance socially. Hall's proxemics divided interpersonal space into intimate, personal, social and public zones whose sizes depend on relationship and culture [177]. Experiments with robots show that people apply similar rules: comfortable distance depends on the robot's gaze, on whether the person likes the robot, and on the person's experience with robots and pets [178,179]. For a humanoid this affects where it stands to hand over an object, how it approaches a group and how it passes people in a corridor; socially aware navigation, which predicts and respects human motion, is covered in the dynamic environments section of the motion planning article [180,181].

Motion itself communicates intent. Dragan, Lee and Srinivasa distinguished predictable motion, which matches what an observer expects given the goal, from legible motion, which lets an observer infer the goal quickly and confidently [182]. The two can conflict: when two objects are close together, the most efficient reach to one is ambiguous for most of its duration, while a reach that exaggerates its direction early reveals the goal sooner at the cost of a slightly longer path. Legibility can be formalized with a model of the observer as a Bayesian inferring the goal from the motion so far.

This mirrors human behavior, in which the eyes typically lead the hand to the target [90,107], and it shows why humanoid social behavior is best designed together with motion and gaze control rather than added afterwards.

9.5 Conversation and turn-taking

Spoken interaction with a humanoid is now usually driven by large language models, but the embodiment adds timing problems that text chat does not have. People take turns with gaps of a few hundred milliseconds, predicting the end of the other's turn from prosody, syntax and gaze, and they signal wanting the turn with breath, gaze and gesture [183]. A robot that waits for silence and then pauses to think seems slow, and one that interrupts seems rude. Turn-taking models predict turn ends continuously from multimodal cues, and a humanoid can fill processing delays with gaze aversion and gestures, as people do [173,183]. Long-term field studies, from Robovie's weeks in a Japanese elementary school onward, show that novelty fades quickly and that sustained interaction depends on the robot remembering people and adapting to them [42,167].

9.6 Measuring social behavior

Social behavior is evaluated with people. Standardized questionnaires such as the Godspeed series measure perceived anthropomorphism, animacy, likeability, perceived intelligence and perceived safety [184], and objective measures, such as task time, gaze-following rates, how early observers predict a goal, and approach distances, complement them [107,178,182]. Because results depend strongly on context, culture and the population studied, findings from one setting transfer to another only with care [11,167].

10 Putting it together: architectures and learning at scale

10.1 Modular, optimization-based stacks

The classical humanoid architecture is a hierarchy of modules with explicit interfaces: perception and state estimation produce a world model and a base-state estimate; a task planner or operator chooses goals; footstep, contact and motion planners produce reference trajectories; a whole-body controller tracks them; and joint servos execute torques [22,45,49]. The DARPA Robotics Challenge systems were built this way, with humans supervising at the task level. Such stacks are interpretable, each module can be tested on its own, and constraints such as balance and joint limits are enforced explicitly. Their weakness is the interfaces: each module's assumptions (flat ground, rigid contacts, known object poses) must hold for the next one to work, and errors in one cascade [50]. Behavior trees and execution monitoring, described in the task planning article, help detect and recover from such failures.

10.2 Learned controllers inside a structured stack

Many current systems replace one or two modules with learned components while keeping the structure. A learned locomotion policy may take velocity or footstep commands from a planner [16,120]; a learned whole-body tracker may take keypoint or joint targets from a teleoperator or a high-level policy [17,65]; and learned residuals may correct a model-based controller [66]. This keeps the interfaces of the classical stack, so that a user or a planner can still command "walk there" or "put the hand here", while gaining the robustness of policies trained on millions of simulated episodes. Simulation frameworks with GPU-parallel physics, such as Isaac Gym, Isaac Lab and MuJoCo Playground, make this training practical [185–187], and benchmarks such as HumanoidBench test whole-body locomotion and manipulation in simulation [188]. Hierarchical world models offer another route: Puppeteer learns a high-level visual world model that commands a low-level tracking world model for a simulated 56-degree-of-freedom humanoid [189]; world models in general are discussed in the world models article.

10.3 Dual-system foundation models

Vision-language-action models map images and language instructions directly to robot actions, inheriting semantic knowledge from web-scale pretraining [70,71,190]; they are covered in detail in the foundation models article. Humanoids pose two difficulties for them: the action space is large (two arms, two multi-fingered hands, torso, head and legs), and control must be fast and smooth while a large vision-language model is slow. The response has been a dual-system architecture, named by analogy with Kahneman's fast and slow thinking. In GR00T N1, a vision-language model (System 2) interprets the image and instruction, and a diffusion-transformer action module (System 1) trained with flow matching generates continuous motor actions at a higher rate, conditioned on the VLM's features; the model was trained on a mixture of real teleoperation data, human videos and synthetic data and evaluated on several humanoid and arm embodiments [18]. Figure's Helix similarly pairs a slower VLM for scene and language understanding with a fast visuomotor policy that controls the whole upper body, hands included, at 200 Hz, and has been demonstrated on two robots collaborating on a household task [23]. Gemini Robotics and π0 follow related designs for general robots [71,191].

In these systems the boundaries between the capabilities of this article blur. A single network perceives the scene, decides where to reach, coordinates both hands and, in some systems, the torso and head; balance and locomotion are usually still delegated to a separate learned or model-based lower-body controller [18,65]. Whether one model will eventually control the whole body from pixels to torques, or whether a structured interface at the level of whole-body motion targets will persist, is an open question; current evidence favours the interface, because it lets the fast balance loop guarantee stability while slower models decide what to do [3,75].

10.4 Data

The main bottleneck for learned humanoid intelligence is data. Robot data from teleoperation are high quality but expensive; whole-body teleoperation systems based on VR headsets, motion capture suits, exoskeletons or a single camera reduce the cost but still need a human for each hour of data [17,145,160]. Human video is plentiful but lacks actions and differs in embodiment; retargeting, hand and body pose estimation and latent-action models help bridge the gap [15,109,118]. Simulation provides unlimited data for locomotion and whole-body control but less faithful data for contact-rich manipulation, and generative models are increasingly used to augment both [66,192]. The demonstration data section of the imitation learning article discusses collection methods in detail.

11 Practice, evaluation and open problems

11.1 Evaluating humanoid intelligence

Humanoid capabilities are evaluated at several levels. Component metrics include walking speed and cost of transport, largest recoverable push, ZMP or CoM tracking error, success rates on manipulation tasks and teleoperation tracking error [17,28,126]. Integrated evaluations, such as the DARPA Robotics Challenge's timed task courses, measure what a system can actually do and expose failures at the interfaces between components [50]. Simulated benchmarks make comparisons cheap and repeatable but do not capture hardware failures, sensor noise or the sim-to-real gap [188,193]. Social behavior requires user studies with validated measures (Section 9.6) [184]. Statistical care matters: humanoid experiments are expensive, so trial counts are small, and success rates should be reported with confidence intervals, as discussed in the practice section of the foundation models article.

11.2 Safety

Humanoids combine the hazards of mobile robots and manipulators with a new one: a dynamically balanced robot cannot simply stop when something goes wrong, because cutting power makes it fall. Existing standards such as ISO 13482 for personal care robots and ISO 10218 for industrial robots were not written for dynamically stable legged machines, and safety arguments for humanoids must address falls, collisions while balancing and contact during physical interaction [194–196]. Collision detection and reaction methods developed for arms apply to humanoid limbs [197], and controlled falling (Section 6.4) and safe-stop behaviors that bring the robot to a stable posture before cutting power are active research topics. Learned controllers raise the further question of how to verify behavior outside the training distribution [3,198].

11.3 Open problems

  • Integration under one body. Most demonstrations show one capability at a time. Long tasks that require walking, balancing, manipulating and interacting in sequence and in parallel, with recovery from failures, remain rare [3,50].
  • Contact-rich whole-body manipulation. Using the whole body, arms, torso and legs, to carry, push and brace against objects, as people do, is only beginning to be learned and controlled reliably [65,82,144].
  • Dexterity and touch. Humanoid hands lag far behind human hands in robustness, sensing and control, and tactile sensing over hands and body is still costly and fragile [99,148,154].
  • Data and generalization. Foundation models for humanoids are limited by the amount and diversity of whole-body data; learning from human video at scale and transferring across embodiments are central problems [18,118,145].
  • Energy and reliability. Battery life, actuator heating and mechanical wear limit how long humanoids can work, and the reliability needed for deployment is far above current research platforms [84,199].
  • Social competence over time. Producing gaze, gesture, proxemics and conversation that remain appropriate over long-term interaction, across cultures and individuals, is unsolved [42,107,167].
  • Safety assurance. Certifiable safety for dynamically balancing robots near people, including for learned controllers, does not yet exist [196,198].

Conclusion

Humanoid intelligence is the problem of making one floating-base, dynamically unstable, highly redundant body perceive, move, balance, manipulate and interact at the same time. Its foundations are physical: centroidal dynamics and contact wrench cones say what the body can do at each instant, the linear inverted pendulum and the capture point say how far ahead it must plan and where it must step, and task-priority control says how to share its joints among competing goals. On top of these foundations, learning has changed what is achievable: policies trained in simulation walk and recover more robustly than hand-designed controllers, learned trackers turn human motion into robot motion, and dual-system foundation models link language and vision to whole upper-body actions. The examples in this article illustrate the recurring lessons in small form: attention must be scheduled by uncertainty and task; an unstable body must act on the future, not the present; brief changes of angular momentum extend balance but must be paid back; strict priorities protect balance where weights cannot; retargeting trades human likeness for task success; and a robot's motion and gaze are messages that people read. The frontier lies in putting these pieces together, robustly, for long tasks among people.
