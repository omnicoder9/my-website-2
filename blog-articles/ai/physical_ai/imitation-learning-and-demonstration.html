1 What imitation learning is, and why physical AI relies on it

Writing a controller by hand for a task such as folding a shirt, inserting a connector or clearing a table is slow and brittle, and specifying a reward function precise enough for reinforcement learning (RL) to discover the behaviour on its own is often just as hard. Showing the robot what to do is frequently easier. Imitation learning, also called learning from demonstration (LfD) or programming by demonstration, is the family of methods that derive a policy from examples of the desired behaviour supplied by a teacher, usually a person [1–3]. In robotics the idea goes back to the 1980s and 1990s, when it was seen both as a practical way to program robots without expert programmers and as a route to more general, human-like learning [4,5].

Formally, the world is modelled as a Markov decision process (MDP), or a partially observable one (POMDP) when the robot sees only observations ot of the underlying state st [6,7]. (The MDP formalism and value functions are developed in the companion article on reinforcement learning, so they are only summarized here.) A demonstrator, usually called the expert, acts according to an unknown policy πE(a | s) and produces a dataset of trajectories

𝒟 = { τi } ,   τi = (o0, a0, o1, a1, …, oT)

and the goal is a policy πθ whose behaviour, when it runs on the robot, is as good as the expert's under the expert's (unknown) objective [3,8]. Three broad answers to "how" structure the field and this article [1,3,9]:

  • Behavioral cloning (BC) treats the problem as supervised learning, regressing actions on observations: θ* = argmaxθ Σ log πθ(at | ot) [10,11].
  • Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) instead infers the reward function the expert appears to be optimizing, then derives a policy from it by planning or RL [12,13].
  • Interactive imitation keeps the teacher in the loop during learning, so the learner can ask for labels, receive corrections, or be told when it is doing well or badly [14,15].

Cutting across these is the question of what the teacher provides. Demonstrations may be given by physically guiding the robot (kinesthetic teaching), by driving it through a teleoperation interface, or simply by performing the task while being recorded, which yields observations without the robot's actions [1,16,17]. 1.1 Why imitation is central to physical AI

Imitation learning has become the dominant way of training manipulation policies, and the large "generalist" robot models of 2023–2026 are trained mostly by behavioral cloning on teleoperated data [18–21]. Several properties of physical systems explain why.

  • Exploration is costly and unsafe. RL learns by trial and error, which on real hardware means wear, resets by hand and the risk of damage; demonstrations put the learner near good behaviour from the start [4,22].
  • Rewards are hard to write. For tasks such as cloth folding or cooking, a reward function that captures everything the user cares about is difficult to specify, whereas demonstrating the task is natural [3,13].
  • People already know how. Humans can teach by showing, and the infant literature suggests imitation is one of the earliest learning mechanisms people use: newborns imitate facial and manual gestures within weeks of birth [23], and the discovery of mirror neurons, which fire both when a monkey acts and when it watches the same action, motivated much early robot imitation work [4,24,25].
  • Scale. Supervised learning scales predictably with data and model size, so collecting more demonstrations is a dependable, if expensive, way to improve a policy [26,27].

Imitation also has well-known weaknesses, which much of this article is about: a cloned policy can drift into states its teacher never visited and compound its own errors (Section 4.2); demonstrations are often multimodal and inconsistent (Section 4.3); and the teacher's body, sensors and viewpoint usually differ from the robot's, which is the correspondence problem [25,28] (Sections 6 and 7). Each technique that follows can be read as a response to one of these difficulties.

2 A short history: from teach pendants to vision-language-action models

2.1 Programming by guiding (1970s–1980s)

The earliest form of robot "demonstration" was lead-through or teach-pendant programming: an operator moved an industrial arm through a sequence of poses, either by hand or with a handheld control box, and the controller recorded and replayed them. Lozano-Pérez's 1983 review classified this guiding approach alongside robot-level and task-level programming languages and noted its central limitation: a replayed trajectory cannot adapt to variations in the scene [5]. Much of the subsequent field can be read as an effort to generalize from demonstrations instead of merely replaying them.

2.2 Learning by watching and behavioural cloning (late 1980s–1990s)

In 1989 Pomerleau's ALVINN (Autonomous Land Vehicle In a Neural Network) steered the Carnegie Mellon Navlab using a small three-layer neural network that mapped camera and laser range-finder input to a steering direction [10]. It was later trained "on the fly" from a person's driving, and Pomerleau identified the problem that would later be formalized as covariate shift: a network that only sees a good driver never sees how to recover from drifting off the road, so he synthesized shifted and rotated views of the road with corrective steering labels [29]. In the same period Sammut, Michie and colleagues trained decision trees on logs of human pilots in a flight simulator ("learning to fly") and introduced the term behavioural cloning for reconstructing a skill from records of a person's actions [11,30].

In robotics, Kuniyoshi, Inaba and Inoue's "learning by watching" system observed a person assembling blocks with a vision system, segmented the performance into actions and generated a robot program that reproduced the task in a different initial configuration [31]. Atkeson and Schaal showed that a single demonstration of a pendulum swing-up was not enough to copy directly on a robot arm, but that the demonstration could be used to learn a task model and a reward, which the robot then optimized with a few of its own trials, foreshadowing the combination of imitation and RL [32]. Schaal's 1999 review "Is imitation learning the route to humanoid robots?" framed the agenda for the next decade: movement primitives, the correspondence problem and the relation between imitation and learning from reward [4].

2.3 Primitives, statistics and inverse RL (2000s)

The 2000s produced compact, generalizable skill representations. Dynamic movement primitives (DMPs) encoded a demonstration as a stable nonlinear dynamical system that could be re-targeted to new goals and speeds [33,34]. Gaussian mixture models and Gaussian mixture regression captured the variability across several demonstrations [35], and later probabilistic movement primitives represented whole distributions over trajectories [36]. Surveys by Argall and colleagues and by Billard and colleagues consolidated the field under the name robot learning from demonstration [1,2].

In parallel, Ng and Russell formalized inverse reinforcement learning in 2000, following Russell's 1998 proposal [12,37], building on a much older control-theory question posed by Kalman in 1964: when is a given linear control law optimal, and for which cost? [38]. Apprenticeship learning by IRL [13], maximum margin planning [39] and maximum-entropy IRL [40] followed, with high-profile applications in autonomous helicopter aerobatics learned from an expert pilot [41] and in learning terrain costs for an off-road vehicle from a driver's demonstrations [42].

2.4 Interactive imitation and deep learning (2010s)

Ross and Bagnell's analysis of 2010 proved that behavioral cloning's errors can grow quadratically with the task horizon, and DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) fixed this by querying the expert in the states the learner itself visits [8,14]. Deep networks then made end-to-end imitation from pixels practical: a convolutional network trained by NVIDIA in 2016 steered a car directly from camera images [43], visuomotor policies were trained for manipulation [44], and virtual-reality teleoperation made it feasible to collect demonstrations for deep imitation [45]. Generative adversarial imitation learning (GAIL) connected IRL to generative adversarial networks [46], and self-supervised methods began to learn from human videos [47,48].

2.5 Expressive policies and robot foundation models (2020s)

From about 2021, attention turned to policy classes that can represent the multimodal, temporally correlated actions humans produce: implicit (energy-based) policies [49], behavior transformers [50], diffusion policies [51] and transformers that predict chunks of future actions [52]. Low-cost bimanual teleoperation rigs such as ALOHA [52,53] and handheld data-collection grippers [54] lowered the cost of demonstrations. Large multi-robot datasets, RT-1's 130,000 episodes [18], BridgeData V2 [55], DROID [27] and the Open X-Embodiment collection of more than a million trajectories from 22 robot embodiments [19], fed generalist policies and vision-language-action models (VLAs) such as RT-2, Octo, OpenVLA, π0, GR00T N1 and Gemini Robotics, all of which are trained largely by behavioral cloning [20,21,56–59]. 3 Learning from human demonstrations

3.1 Ways of demonstrating

The way a demonstration is given determines what is recorded and how much work is needed to turn it into robot behaviour. Argall and colleagues distinguished demonstrations by whether the recorded states and actions are those the robot itself would experience (the record mapping) and whether the demonstrator's body is the robot's (the embodiment mapping) [1]. Four modes are common [1,2,16]:

  • Kinesthetic teaching. The teacher physically moves a compliant or gravity-compensated robot through the task while its joint encoders record the motion. States and actions are in the robot's own coordinates, so there is no correspondence problem, but the teacher must be co-located and it is hard to demonstrate fast, bimanual or forceful motions [61,62]. Forces can be taught by adding a haptic interface or force sensor [62].
  • Teleoperation. The teacher drives the robot remotely with a joystick, a kinematic replica, a VR controller or hand tracking (Section 6). Data are again in the robot's action space and observations come from the robot's own cameras, which is why most large datasets are collected this way [18,27,52].
  • Observation of the human. The teacher simply does the task while being recorded by cameras, motion capture or wearable sensors. This is the most scalable and natural mode but yields neither the robot's actions nor its viewpoint (Section 7) [17,31].
  • Keyframes and sketches. Instead of continuous trajectories the teacher provides a few key poses, which reduces the effort and noise of demonstrating and leaves interpolation to the robot [61].

Human demonstrations are rarely clean. Different people and even the same person solve a task in different ways; they hesitate, make and correct mistakes, and vary in skill. Benchmarks of offline learning from human demonstrations found that policies trained on data from several operators of mixed quality perform worse than those trained on the same number of demonstrations from a proficient operator, and that design choices such as observation space and history length matter a great deal [63]. Belkhale and colleagues formalized two properties of a "good" dataset: low action divergence (the demonstrator acts consistently in a given state) and sufficient state diversity to cover the states the learned policy will visit [64].

3.2 What to imitate: levels of abstraction

A demonstration can be imitated at several levels. At the lowest it is a trajectory to reproduce; at the next it is a skill (a reaching or pouring motion) to be generalized to new start and goal positions; higher still it is a task made of a sequence of skills, whose structure can be learned by segmenting demonstrations into primitives and identifying the conditions under which each is used [2,65]. At the highest level it is an intention or goal to be achieved by whatever means suit the robot's body, which is the view taken by inverse reinforcement learning (Section 5) [3,12]. Meta-learning and one-shot imitation methods aim to infer a new task from a single demonstration, conditioning a policy trained on many tasks on a demonstration of the new one [66,67].

3.3 Movement primitives

For single skills, the most influential representations have been movement primitives: compact, parameterized trajectory generators that can be fitted to one or a few demonstrations and then adapted [34,36,68].

Dynamic movement primitives represent each degree of freedom as a critically damped spring pulling toward the goal g, perturbed by a learned forcing term that shapes the path [33,34]:

τ ż = αz(βz(g − y) − z) + f(x)(g − y0),    τ ẏ = z,    τ ẋ = −αx x,    f(x) = x Σi wiψi(x) / Σi ψi(x)

The phase variable x decays from 1 to 0, so the forcing term vanishes and the system is guaranteed to converge to g; the weights wi of the Gaussian basis functions ψi are fitted to a demonstration by linear regression. Changing g re-targets the movement, changing τ changes its speed without changing its path, and perturbations are rejected by the attractor dynamics [34,69]. DMPs have been used for tasks from tennis swings to pouring and pick-and-place, and as a policy parameterization for subsequent improvement by RL [34,69,70].

Probabilistic approaches instead model the variability across several demonstrations. Gaussian mixture models fitted to (time, position) or (position, velocity) data, with Gaussian mixture regression to generate motion, capture which parts of a task must be precise and which can vary [35]; task-parameterized variants attach the mixture to several coordinate frames (for example, an object and a target) so the skill adapts when those frames move [68]. Probabilistic movement primitives (ProMPs) place a Gaussian distribution over the weights of a trajectory basis, so new via-points or goals are imposed by Gaussian conditioning and several primitives can be blended or co-activated [36]. The SEDS method learns a mixture-model dynamical system with a guarantee of global asymptotic stability at the target [71].

4 Behavioral cloning

4.1 Imitation as supervised learning

Behavioral cloning fits a policy to demonstration pairs (ot, at) by maximizing their likelihood, or equivalently minimizing a regression or classification loss [3,11]:

θ* = argminθ 𝔼(o,a)~𝒟 [ ℓ(πθ(o), a) ]

It needs no reward, no simulator and no interaction with the environment during training, which makes it simple, stable and easy to scale. These properties explain its use in the earliest learned driving systems [10,43] and in today's generalist robot policies [18,20,21,57]. Conditioning the policy on a command, goal image or language instruction turns one network into a multi-task policy: conditional imitation learning for driving took the navigation command ("turn left at the next intersection") as input [73], and BC-Z and RT-1 conditioned manipulation policies on natural-language instructions [18,74].

4.2 Covariate shift and compounding errors

Supervised learning assumes the test inputs come from the training distribution. A cloned policy violates this assumption by construction: its own small mistakes take it to states the expert never visited, where it has had no training and makes larger mistakes, and so on. Ross and Bagnell showed that if the learned policy errs with probability ε on the expert's state distribution, its total cost over a horizon T can exceed the expert's by O(T2ε), whereas a learner trained on its own state distribution achieves O(Tε) [8,14]. Because the policy's actions determine which observations it sees, this is a covariate shift problem, and the errors compound over time.

Practical remedies fall into three groups. The first adds recovery data offline: ALVINN and NVIDIA's end-to-end driving network synthesized off-centre views with corrective labels [29,43], ChauffeurNet perturbed logged trajectories and added losses that penalize collisions and leaving the road [75], and DART injects noise into the expert's actions during data collection so that the expert demonstrates recoveries from the resulting perturbations [76]. The second collects labels interactively in the learner's own state distribution, as DAgger does (Section 8.1) [14]. The third reduces the effective horizon, for example by predicting chunks of several actions at once (Section 4.5) [52], or by combining imitation with a planner or reward so the policy can recover from states the expert never demonstrated [3,46].

4.3 Multimodality and causal confusion

The second difficulty is that human demonstrations are multimodal: in the same situation, one demonstrator passes an obstacle on the left and another on the right, or the same person grasps a mug by the handle one time and by the rim the next. A policy trained with mean-squared error predicts the conditional mean of the actions, which may be an action that no demonstrator took and that fails, such as driving straight into the obstacle. Classical remedies include mixture density networks, which output a Gaussian mixture [77], and discretizing actions into bins and treating control as classification, as RT-1 and RT-2 do with 256 bins per action dimension [18,56].

A subtler problem is causal confusion. A policy that sees more information is not necessarily better: de Haan and colleagues showed that when the input includes features correlated with the expert's actions but not their cause (for example, a brake indicator on the dashboard that lights up because the driver braked), BC can latch onto the wrong feature and fail when deployed [78]. In driving, Codevilla and colleagues documented related issues, including sensitivity to dataset bias and high variance across training runs [79].

4.4 Expressive policy classes

Since about 2021, the most effective manipulation policies have used generative models that can represent complex, multimodal action distributions [49–51]:

  • Implicit BC trains an energy-based model Eθ(o, a) and acts by minimizing it over actions; Florence and colleagues showed that such implicit policies outperform explicit regression on tasks with discontinuities and multimodality [49].
  • Behavior Transformers cluster actions with k-means, predict a categorical distribution over clusters plus a continuous offset with a transformer, and so "clone k modes with one stone" [50].
  • Diffusion Policy represents the action distribution with a denoising diffusion model [81] conditioned on observations, generating a sequence of future actions by iterative denoising. It improved success on manipulation benchmarks by an average of 46.9% over prior methods in the original report, and has become a standard baseline [51]; variants condition on 3D point clouds [82].
  • Flow matching learns a velocity field that transports noise to actions in a few integration steps [83]; π0 uses it as a dedicated "action expert" attached to a vision-language model, producing chunks of continuous actions at up to 50 Hz [21].
  • Action tokenization turns continuous actions into discrete tokens for autoregressive transformers; the frequency-space FAST tokenizer compresses smooth, high-frequency action sequences more efficiently than per-step binning [84].

The architectures of large VLA policies built from these components, and how they are pre-trained and fine-tuned, are covered in the foundation models article; what matters here is that they are, in the end, behavioral cloning with very expressive function approximators and very large datasets.

4.5 Temporal structure and action chunking

Human demonstrations are temporally correlated and often non-Markovian: operators pause, hesitate and act on information they remember rather than see. Predicting one action per observation forces the policy to resolve these ambiguities at every step. Zhao and colleagues' Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) instead predicts the next k actions as a chunk, which reduces the effective horizon of the task by a factor of k, and smooths execution by temporal ensembling, averaging the overlapping predictions from successive chunks with exponential weights [52]. ACT is trained as a conditional variational autoencoder so that it can also represent multimodal styles [52]. Diffusion Policy likewise predicts action sequences and executes them in a receding-horizon fashion [51], and chunking has become standard in VLAs [21,84]. Section 6.4 returns to chunking with an example drawn from teleoperated data.

4.6 Learning from play and from heterogeneous data

Not all useful demonstrations are of the target task. Lynch and colleagues collected "play" data, in which an operator teleoperated a robot to interact freely with a scene, and trained goal-conditioned policies on short windows of it, relabelling the end of each window as its goal [85]. Large datasets mix tasks, robots, operators and quality levels [19,27]. MimicGen showed that a few hundred human demonstrations can be expanded automatically into tens of thousands by adapting object-centric segments of them to new scenes in simulation [86]. Data scaling studies find that, for a fixed budget, diversity of environments and objects matters more than the number of demonstrations per environment [26].

5 Inverse reinforcement learning

5.1 Learning the objective instead of the actions

Behavioral cloning copies what the expert did. Inverse reinforcement learning asks why: given demonstrations assumed to be (near-)optimal for some unknown reward r, find r, and then compute a policy that optimizes it [12,13,87]. Russell proposed the problem in 1998, and Ng and Russell argued that the reward function, rather than the policy, is "the most succinct, robust, and transferable definition of the task" [12,37]. A reward learned in one environment can be re-optimized in another with different obstacles, dynamics or even a different robot body, and a policy derived by planning on it can recover from states the demonstrator never visited, which BC cannot do [3,13,40]. The price is that IRL needs a model or simulator of the environment, or interaction with it, to solve the forward RL or planning problem repeatedly; the RL background is developed in the reinforcement learning article.

The problem is ill-posed. Many rewards, including the trivial reward r ≡ 0, make any behaviour optimal [12]; potential-based shaping transformations leave the optimal policy unchanged [88]; and without assumptions about how rational the demonstrator is, preferences cannot be identified from behaviour at all [89]. Each IRL algorithm is defined largely by how it resolves this ambiguity.

5.2 Feature matching, margins and maximum entropy

Most classical methods assume a reward linear in features, r(s) = w⊤φ(s), so a policy's expected return is w⊤μ(π) where μ(π) = 𝔼[Σt γtφ(st)] are its feature expectations [13].

  • Ng and Russell (2000) posed IRL as a linear program that chooses the reward under which the expert's policy beats alternatives by the largest margin, with a penalty favouring simple rewards [12].
  • Apprenticeship learning (Abbeel and Ng, 2004) observed that a policy whose feature expectations match the expert's performs as well as the expert under any reward in the linear class, and gave an iterative algorithm that alternates between finding a reward that separates the expert from previously found policies and solving RL for it [13].
  • Maximum margin planning (MMP) cast the problem as structured prediction: the demonstrated path should be cheaper than every other path by a margin that grows with how different the paths are [39]; its functional-gradient extension LEARCH learned nonlinear cost functions [90].
  • Maximum-entropy IRL (Ziebart and colleagues, 2008) resolved the ambiguity probabilistically: among all distributions over trajectories that match the expert's feature counts, choose the one with maximum entropy, which gives P(τ) ∝ exp(Σt r(st)) [40]. Demonstrations are then explained as noisy, "Boltzmann-rational" choices, and the reward is fitted by maximum likelihood, with gradient equal to the difference between the expert's and the model's expected feature counts. The forward problem becomes "soft" value iteration, in which the maximum over actions is replaced by log-sum-exp [40].
  • Bayesian IRL places a prior over rewards and infers a posterior from the demonstrations [91]; Gaussian-process IRL learns nonlinear rewards with a nonparametric prior [92].

The MaxEnt model has been particularly influential in modelling people: it was used to predict the routes of taxi drivers from GPS traces [93], to forecast the paths of pedestrians from video [94] and to make mobile robots navigate in a socially compliant way among people [95].

5.3 Deep and adversarial IRL

Linear rewards over hand-designed features limit what IRL can express. Maximum-entropy deep IRL replaced the linear reward with a convolutional network over map features and trained it with the same gradient [96]. Guided cost learning made MaxEnt IRL practical for high-dimensional robot control with unknown dynamics by estimating the partition function with samples from a policy that is optimized alongside the cost [97].

Generative adversarial imitation learning (GAIL) took a different route: it showed that running RL on the reward recovered by MaxEnt IRL is equivalent to matching the occupancy measure of the expert, and implemented the matching with a discriminator that distinguishes expert from policy state-action pairs, in the style of generative adversarial networks [46,98]. GAIL imitates without ever producing an explicit, transferable reward; adversarial IRL (AIRL) restored one by constraining the discriminator so that its reward term is disentangled from the environment's dynamics, making it transferable to changed dynamics [99]. IQ-Learn avoids the adversarial game by learning a single soft Q-function from which both reward and policy follow [100].

5.4 IRL and imitation-derived rewards in robotics

The best-known robotic applications of IRL combine a reward learned from demonstration with a strong planner or controller. Abbeel, Coates and Ng learned the target trajectories and reward weights for aerobatic helicopter manoeuvres (flips, rolls, tic-tocs and the "chaos" manoeuvre) from an expert pilot's flights, and flew them autonomously with a learned dynamics model and optimal control [41]. Silver, Bagnell and Stentz used MMP-style learning to set the cost functions of an off-road robot's planner from examples of how an expert would drive, replacing laborious manual tuning [42,90].

A related family uses demonstrations to define a reward for RL without full IRL. DeepMimic rewarded a simulated character for tracking motion-capture clips while performing tasks [101], and the same idea transferred dog motion-capture gaits to a real quadruped [102]. Adversarial motion priors (AMP) replaced the tracking reward with a GAIL-style discriminator trained on a motion dataset, rewarding motion that "looks like" the data rather than matching a specific clip [103], and gave natural gaits for real legged robots [104]. These methods sit between imitation and RL; the RL machinery they rely on, and the sim-to-real transfer that brings them to hardware, are covered in the reinforcement learning article.

Finally, IRL frames a general problem in human–robot interaction: a robot that helps a person should treat that person's behaviour as evidence about what they want. Cooperative IRL formalizes this as a game in which only the human knows the reward and both are rewarded by it, so the robot has an incentive to learn and the human to teach [105]. Section 8 returns to this view when corrections and preferences are treated as evidence about the reward.

6 Learning from teleoperation

6.1 From master–slave manipulators to data-collection rigs

Teleoperation predates autonomous robots: mechanical master–slave manipulators were built by Goertz and colleagues at Argonne National Laboratory from the late 1940s so that operators could handle radioactive material from behind shielding walls, and electrically coupled versions with force feedback followed [106,107]. A large literature addresses stable bilateral control, in which forces are reflected back to the operator, in the presence of communication delays [106,108], and supervisory control, in which the operator issues higher-level commands to a partly autonomous robot [109].

For imitation learning, teleoperation has a decisive advantage: the recorded actions are in the robot's own action space and the observations come from the robot's own sensors, so a policy can be trained on exactly what it will see and do, with no correspondence problem [1,45]. Teleoperation has consequently become the main source of data for robot learning, from RT-1's 130,000 episodes collected over 17 months with a fleet of 13 robots [18] to DROID's 76,000 trajectories (about 350 hours) collected in 564 scenes [27].

6.3 Shared control and the human in the loop

Teleoperation need not be all-or-nothing. In shared autonomy, the robot infers what the operator is trying to do and blends its own assistance with the operator's commands; policy blending formalized the arbitration between user and robot [121], and hindsight optimization lets the robot assist while still uncertain which goal the user has in mind [122]. The same infrastructure that lets an operator drive the robot also lets them take over when an autonomous policy is failing, which is the basis of the interactive methods of Section 8 [123,124].

6.4 Properties of teleoperated data

Teleoperated demonstrations carry the signature of the interface and of the human operator. Latency and limited visual feedback make operators slower and more hesitant than when they act directly; demonstrations contain pauses, small corrective motions and idiosyncratic strategies [52,63]. Operators also differ: in the robomimic study, mixing demonstrations from operators of different proficiency lowered the success of learned policies relative to proficient-only data, and the effect depended on the learning algorithm [63].

Pauses are a particular problem for single-step policies. When the robot is stationary the observation does not change, so a Markovian policy cannot tell how long it has already waited; Zhao and colleagues cited such pauses as a motivation for action chunking [52]. The single-step policy stalls at the object: neighbours in the holding state disagree about whether to wait or go, their average is a slow creep, and the robot ends about 0.3 short of the place location. Predicting chunks lets one prediction made on arrival encode "wait about 15 steps, then go", but executing chunks open loop is sensitive to where the chunk boundaries fall, and a chunk queried mid-hold is as ambiguous as a single step. Temporal ensembling, which re-queries every step and averages the overlapping chunks, reproduces the hold reliably when the oldest predictions dominate (m = 0.5) and gives the smoothest motion when weights are nearly uniform, but heavily weighting old predictions makes the policy slow to react, so it ends farther from the place location. This trade-off between temporal consistency and reactivity is exactly what the exponential weight in ACT controls [52]; diffusion and flow-matching policies make the same trade-off through the number of actions executed from each predicted chunk [21,51].

7 Learning from video and first-person observation

7.1 The promise and the gaps

Teleoperated data are expensive: every trajectory needs a robot, an operator and time. Video of people doing things is abundant. Egocentric (first-person) datasets alone include EPIC-KITCHENS, with 55 hours of unscripted kitchen activity recorded in 32 kitchens [125], Ego4D, with 3,670 hours of daily-life video from 931 camera wearers [126], and Ego-Exo4D, which pairs first-person and third-person views of skilled activities [127]. Learning robot skills from such data is often called imitation from observation (IfO) [17,48]. It has to bridge three gaps:

  • Missing actions. Video shows states, not the motor commands that produced them [60,128].
  • Embodiment. A human hand has more degrees of freedom, different kinematics and different contact properties than a parallel-jaw gripper; this is the correspondence problem again [28].
  • Viewpoint and domain. Third-person videos see the demonstrator from outside, while the robot sees its own arm from its cameras; scenes, lighting and objects differ [47,48,129].

7.2 Recovering actions with inverse dynamics models

If the robot can collect its own experience, it can learn an inverse dynamics model (IDM) a ≈ g(st, st+1) that predicts which action causes a given state change, and then use it to label the transitions in an observation-only demonstration. Behavioral cloning from observation (BCO) does exactly this: self-supervised exploration trains the IDM, the IDM labels the demonstrations, and BC is applied to the inferred actions [128]. Video PreTraining (VPT) scaled the idea to Minecraft: an IDM trained on about 2,000 hours of gameplay recorded with keyboard and mouse actions labelled 70,000 hours of unlabelled internet video, and a policy cloned from the result could be fine-tuned with RL to craft diamond tools [60]. Because an IDM can look at future frames, it is a much easier prediction problem than the policy itself [60].

When no action labels exist at all, a model can learn latent actions: an IDM and a forward model are trained jointly so that a low-dimensional code inferred from two frames suffices to predict the second from the first [130,131]. Latent-action pretraining from human and robot videos has been used to initialize VLA policies that are then fine-tuned with a small amount of real action data [132].

This illustrates why IDM labelling works: action errors that do not change the resulting state change much do not matter for imitation.

7.3 Learning what matters from video: representations and rewards

Video can also teach a robot what to look at and what progress looks like, without supplying actions. Time-contrastive networks learned viewpoint-invariant embeddings from synchronized multi-view video, so that a robot could imitate human poses and pouring motions by matching embeddings [47]. Third-person imitation learning used domain confusion to learn features invariant to the demonstrator's viewpoint [129], and context translation converted a demonstration into the robot's viewpoint before using it as a reward [48]. AVID translated human video frames into images of the robot performing the task with a CycleGAN-style model and used them as goals for multi-stage tasks [133]. SFV reconstructed human poses from monocular video and used them as reference motions for simulated characters [134].

Pretrained visual representations are now the most common use of human video in robot learning. R3M trained a ResNet on Ego4D with time-contrastive and video–language alignment objectives [126,135]; MVP used masked-autoencoder pretraining on egocentric and internet images and video for real-robot control [136]; and VIP learned an embedding whose distances act as a goal-conditioned value function and so provide dense rewards [137]. These representations are compared in the foundation models article.

7.4 Hands, objects and the embodiment gap

For manipulation, the most informative part of a human video is often the hand and what it does to objects. Modern pipelines detect hands and their contact state [138], reconstruct 3D hand pose with a parametric hand model such as MANO [139,140], and estimate object poses, then retarget the hand motion to the robot. Several approaches follow:

  • Retarget into demonstrations. DexMV reconstructed hand and object poses from videos and retargeted them into demonstrations for a simulated dexterous hand, which were then used with imitation and RL [141]. Robotic telekinesis trained a hand retargeter on YouTube videos to drive a robot hand from a single camera [142].
  • Learn priors and affordances. VideoDex used internet videos as visual, action and physical priors for dexterous policies [143]; WHIRL imitated people in the wild by extracting hand-object interaction priors from a single video and improving through the robot's own trials [144]; and affordance models trained on human videos predict where and how to contact objects [145].
  • Predict motion, not actions. Any-point trajectory modelling (ATM) trains a model on video to predict the future 2D tracks of points in the image, and a small policy trained on robot data follows the predicted tracks [146]. UniPi generates a video of the task from a text instruction and recovers actions with an IDM [147].
  • Treat the human as another embodiment. MimicPlay learned high-level latent plans from human play video and low-level control from a small amount of teleoperated data [148]. EgoMimic recorded people wearing head-mounted cameras with hand tracking and co-trained one policy on human and robot data as if the human were another robot [149].

First-person video is attractive because the camera roughly matches a robot's head camera and the hands are usually visible, and wearable capture systems make it possible to collect embodied human data with action-like signals (head and hand poses) at a scale that teleoperation cannot reach [115,126,149]. The remaining gap is in contact: video shows where the hand went but not how hard it pressed, which is why video-derived priors are usually combined with some robot data or robot practice [144,148,149].

8 Corrections and feedback from human operators

A demonstration is a large, up-front teaching signal. People can also teach while the robot acts: by taking over when it errs, by physically pushing it onto a better path, by saying "good" or "too fast", by choosing between two alternatives, or by giving language instructions. These interactive imitation learning methods target the states the learner actually reaches, which addresses covariate shift directly, and they often require far less of the teacher's time than full demonstrations [1,14,15]. Figure 2 shows the main kinds of feedback.

8.1 DAgger and its human-friendly variants

DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) is the canonical interactive algorithm [14]. Starting from a BC policy, it repeatedly (i) runs the current policy, possibly mixed with the expert, (ii) asks the expert what it would have done in each visited state, (iii) adds these labels to the dataset and (iv) retrains on all data so far. Because training data come from the learner's own state distribution, the reduction to no-regret online learning gives error that grows linearly rather than quadratically in the horizon [14].

DAgger asks a lot of a human expert: labelling every state of a trajectory the expert is not controlling is unnatural, because the right action depends on what happens next, and letting a poor policy drive can be unsafe. Variants address this [15]:

  • Gated querying. SafeDAgger learns a classifier that predicts when the learner is about to deviate from the expert and hands control to the expert only then [150]; EnsembleDAgger uses disagreement in an ensemble of policies as the gate [151]. Confidence-based autonomy had earlier let a robot ask for a demonstration when its classifier was unsure [152].
  • Human-gated intervention. In HG-DAgger the human watches and takes over when they judge the robot unsafe; only the takeover segments are recorded as new labels, and the human retains full control while giving them [123].
  • Learning from the intervention itself. The moment of intervention is also information: it says the robot's recent behaviour was unacceptable. Expert intervention learning used both the corrective actions and the implicit signal of when the human did not intervene [153]; intervention-weighted regression and Sirius up-weight intervention data and down-weight the segments that preceded them during training on deployment data [124,154]. Human-in-the-loop RL systems combine interventions with RL rewards to reach near-perfect success on precise real-world tasks [155].

BC matches the expert on short drives, but its failure rate grows with the horizon, reaching 15% failures over 800 steps, because the rare states it has never seen, reached after large gusts, become likely over long drives: the compounding effect of Section 4.2. DAgger, which collects labels in the learner's own state distribution, roughly halves the failure rate with the same number of labels. In this example DART does best: its random perturbations explore the rare far-off-centre states more systematically than the learner's own mistakes do. Which method is best depends on the task and learner; the general lesson is that where the training data are collected matters as much as how many there are [14,76,123].

8.2 Evaluative feedback: rewards from people

Instead of telling the robot what to do, a person can tell it how well it is doing. TAMER modelled a human trainer's scalar feedback as a signal about the immediate value of the most recent action, learned a model of it, and acted greedily on that model [156]; Deep TAMER extended the approach to learning from pixels [157]. COACH argued instead that people give feedback relative to the agent's current policy, more like an advantage than a reward, and used it directly as a policy-gradient signal [158]. Studies of how people actually teach show that they use the reward channel to give guidance about future actions as well as to evaluate past ones, and have a positive bias, which naive RL formulations misinterpret [159].

8.3 Preferences and comparisons

People are often better at comparing than at rating or demonstrating. In preference-based learning the robot shows two trajectories and the person picks the better one. The standard model is the Bradley–Terry model of paired comparisons [160], in which the probability of preferring trajectory A to B is a logistic function of the difference of their returns, so a reward model can be fitted by logistic regression. Christiano and colleagues used this to train deep RL agents from about 900 human comparisons, including teaching a simulated robot to do a backflip that would be hard to specify as a reward [161]; the same reward-modelling recipe underlies reinforcement learning from human feedback for language models [162]. In robotics, active preference learning chooses the queries that are most informative about the reward [163], and demonstrations and preferences can be combined, using demonstrations for a coarse initial reward and preferences to refine it [164].

8.4 Physical, trajectory and language corrections

Some corrections are given in the robot's own medium. In coactive learning, a user improves a trajectory the robot proposed, for example by moving a few waypoints, and the robot treats the improved trajectory as evidence that it scores higher under the user's preferences [165]. Bajcsy, Losey and colleagues treated a person's physical push on a robot arm during execution as an observation about the objective: the robot updates the weights of its cost function online, so that it not only yields to the push but also changes how it moves for the rest of the task, for example keeping a cup upright or staying farther from a person [166,167]. Tactile corrections were used earlier to refine demonstrated policies [168].

Language is the most natural channel of all. Language corrections such as "go around the bowl" have been mapped to cost terms for a motion planner [169], and in hierarchical policies a person's verbal correction ("move a bit to the left") can redirect a high-level language policy on the fly and then be used to fine-tune it [170].

8.5 Designing for human teachers

Interactive learning depends on people, so the human side matters. Robots that ask well-chosen questions get better data from naive teachers [171], teachers adapt their teaching when they can see what the robot has learned [159], and every query or intervention has a cost in attention and time that the learning algorithm should account for [15,123].

9.2 Evaluating imitation-learned policies

Evaluation is harder than it looks. The quantity of interest is task success on the real robot under the conditions it will face, which is expensive to measure and noisy: with a few dozen trials per condition, confidence intervals on success rates are wide, and results vary with the training seed, the checkpoint chosen and small changes in the scene [63,79,172]. Offline metrics such as action prediction error on held-out demonstrations correlate poorly with closed-loop success, because they do not capture compounding errors (Section 4.2) [8,79]. Recommended practice includes reporting the number of trials and uncertainty, blind and randomized evaluation order, fixed protocols for scene variation and analysis of failure modes [172].

Simulation benchmarks make comparisons cheaper and reproducible: RLBench provides 100 simulated manipulation tasks with demonstrations [173], robomimic standardizes offline learning from human demonstrations [63], LIBERO targets knowledge transfer across task suites [174], and SIMPLER builds simulated environments whose results correlate with the real-world performance of policies trained on real data [175].

9.4 Open problems

  • Data cost and coverage. Even the largest robot datasets are small compared with text or image corpora, and their diversity matters more than their size [19,26,27]. Cheaper collection (handheld devices, human video, simulation-generated demonstrations) is one of the most active areas of work [54,86,149].
  • Exceeding the demonstrator. A cloned policy is at best as good as its data. Combining imitation with RL, whether offline from logged data or online on the robot, is the main route to better-than-demonstrator performance [22,155,176].
  • Data quality. Inconsistent or low-skill demonstrations degrade policies, and methods to measure and curate demonstration quality are still immature [63,64].
  • Embodiment and contact. Learning from people requires bridging differences in kinematics, viewpoint and especially forces, which video does not record [28,144,149].
  • Specification and intent. Demonstrations underdetermine the objective: the robot must infer which aspects of the demonstrated behaviour matter, a question that IRL, causal analysis and interactive feedback each address only partly [78,89,105].
  • Reliability and evaluation. Imitation-learned policies can fail unpredictably out of distribution; measuring and certifying their reliability remains difficult [79,172].

9.5 Summary

Imitation learning turns human skill into robot behaviour by three routes: copying actions (behavioral cloning), inferring objectives (inverse reinforcement learning) and learning interactively from corrections and feedback. Its practical form in physical AI is shaped by how demonstrations are obtained: kinesthetic teaching gives data in the robot's own body, teleoperation gives scalable robot-centric data, and video gives abundant but action-free observations of people. The main technical challenges, compounding errors, multimodal and non-Markovian demonstrations, the correspondence problem and the ambiguity of intent, each have well-understood remedies: interactive data collection, expressive and chunked policies, inverse dynamics models and retargeting, and probabilistic models of the teacher. The result is that imitation learning, scaled up, is now the backbone of generalist robot policies, and the frontier lies in making demonstrations cheaper, combining them with self-improvement, and making the resulting systems reliable.
