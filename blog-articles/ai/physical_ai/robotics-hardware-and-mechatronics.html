1 Why hardware matters for physical AI

A robot is a machine that senses, decides and acts on the physical world, and every one of those functions is carried by hardware: a structure that holds its shape under load, actuators that turn electrical or fluid energy into motion, transmissions that trade speed for force, hands and tools that make contact, batteries and power electronics that feed it, computers that run its control loops on time, and sensors that report what actually happened [1,2]. Mechatronics is the name engineers gave to designing these parts together rather than one after another. The word was coined at the Japanese motor and drive maker Yaskawa Electric in 1969 to describe machines in which mechanical and electronic functions are integrated, and it has since become a discipline that treats mechanism, actuator, electronics, sensing and software as one design problem [3,4].

Physical AI makes this integration more important, not less. A learned policy can only command what its body can deliver: the torque limits, speed limits, backlash, latency, thermal limits and failure modes of the hardware are part of the environment the policy learns in. Legged-robot controllers trained in simulation transferred to real robots only after the simulators modelled the actuators properly, for example by learning a network that maps commands to the torques a real series-elastic actuator produces [5,6]. Low-cost but well-designed arms and teleoperation rigs made it affordable to collect the demonstration data that modern imitation-learning and foundation-model policies need [7–9], and actuators that can be pushed back by hand, sense their own torque and survive impacts made it possible to let robots learn by trial and error on real hardware [10–12]. Conversely, a body that overheats, drifts out of calibration or breaks after a few hundred hours limits any learning system that runs on it.

This article covers robot hardware from that perspective. It is the first article in the series' part on robotics and physical platforms, and it connects to earlier articles rather than repeating them: sensors such as encoders, inertial measurement units, force/torque and tactile sensors are covered in sensors and sensory modalities; rigid-body kinematics and dynamics in physical modeling; PID, impedance and force control in classical and modern control; and fast reflexes such as grasp correction and safe stopping in the companion article on reactive behavior. Here we follow the energy and information path through a robot joint (Figure 1): from the battery, through the motor driver and motor, through the gearbox to the link, and back through encoders and current sensors to the embedded controller.

2 A short history of robot hardware

The industrial robot began as a hardware invention. George Devol's patent for "programmed article transfer", granted in 1961, described a manipulator that played back taught motions, and the resulting Unimate, built with Joseph Engelberger's company Unimation, went to work handling hot die-cast parts at a General Motors plant in 1961 [16–18]. Early Unimates were hydraulically actuated, because hydraulics offered high force from compact actuators at a time when electric motors and their drives were heavy and hard to control [18,19]. Electric robots followed: Victor Scheinman's Stanford Arm of 1969 was an all-electric, computer-controlled six-axis arm whose design lineage led to the PUMA family of industrial arms [18,20].

The decades since can be read as a sequence of answers to one problem: electric motors spin fast with little torque, while robot joints need high torque at low speed. Strain wave (harmonic) gearing, patented by C. Walton Musser in 1959, offered high reduction ratios with nearly zero backlash in a compact, hollow package and became the standard robot-joint reducer [21,22]. At Carnegie Mellon, Asada and Kanade went the other way and built direct-drive arms with no gears at all, using large high-torque motors to eliminate backlash and friction [23,24], and Townsend and Salisbury's cable-driven Whole Arm Manipulator showed that low-friction, low-ratio cable transmissions make an arm sensitive enough to control contact forces through the motors themselves [25]. In 1995 Pratt and Williamson introduced the series elastic actuator, which deliberately puts a spring between gearbox and load so that force can be measured from the spring's deflection and shocks are filtered [26].

Legged robots pushed actuation hardest. Raibert's hopping machines of the 1980s established dynamic balance as an engineering discipline [27]; Honda's humanoids of the 1990s showed reliable electric biped walking [28]; Boston Dynamics' BigDog and the Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia's HyQ used hydraulics for power-dense, rough-terrain quadrupeds [29,30]; and NASA's Valkyrie humanoid used series elastic actuators throughout [31,32], as did ETH Zurich's ANYmal quadruped, built from identical series elastic joint modules [33]. In the 2010s the MIT Cheetah robots introduced "proprioceptive" actuators: large-diameter brushless motors with a single-stage planetary reduction, transparent enough that the motor current is a good estimate of the joint torque and robust enough to absorb landing impacts without force sensors [10,11]. The design was documented in detail for the smaller Mini Cheetah [13,34], and together with direct-drive designs such as Minitaur [35] and open platforms such as Solo and Stanford Doggo [14,36], it shaped a generation of quadrupeds and humanoids. On the manipulation side, the German Aerospace Center's (DLR's) lightweight robot III integrated torque sensors in every joint and achieved a payload close to its own weight, and its technology became a commercial collaborative arm [15,37]. The term "cobot" itself dates from 1996, when Colgate, Peshkin and colleagues proposed robots designed to work hand in hand with human operators [38].

The latest shift is towards hardware designed for data and learning: low-cost bimanual teleoperation arms such as ALOHA [7,39], handheld data-collection grippers [8], open-source software and low-cost arm kits [40], and research humanoids built to be repaired and re-trained cheaply [41]. 3 Mechanical structures and kinematic architectures

A robot's structure is a set of rigid (or deliberately flexible) links connected by joints. Most joints are revolute (rotary) or prismatic (sliding), and the arrangement of joints determines the robot's degrees of freedom, its workspace and how loads flow through it [1,2]. The kinematics of these chains, including the Denavit–Hartenberg convention for describing them [48], are covered in the article on physical modeling; here the concern is what the choice of architecture means for the hardware.

Serial and parallel mechanisms. A serial arm stacks joints one after another, giving a large workspace and simple kinematics, but each actuator must carry the mass of everything after it, and errors and compliances accumulate along the chain. A parallel mechanism connects the end effector to the base through several chains, as in the six-legged Stewart platform used in flight simulators and the three-armed Delta robot used for fast pick-and-place [42,46,49]. Parallel mechanisms can keep all motors on the base, which makes the moving mass small and the structure stiff, at the price of a smaller workspace, more complex kinematics and singularities inside the workspace [49]. Legged robots mix both: each leg is a short serial chain, but when several feet are on the ground the body is supported by a parallel structure.

Where the mass goes. Because every actuator must accelerate the links after it, good designs keep distal parts light. The MIT Cheetah robots mount the knee motor at the hip, next to the hip motors, and transmit its motion down the thigh to the knee, which keeps the leg itself light, which lowers the forces needed to swing the leg and the energy lost in each foot impact [10,11]. Cable- and tendon-driven arms and hands go further, placing motors in the base or forearm and routing cables to the joints [25,45]. A cost is elasticity: cables stretch, belts and long shafts twist, and thin links bend.

Stiffness and flexibility. Real joints and links are springs. Elastic-joint models, in which a stiff motor is connected to the link through a torsional spring, describe robots with harmonic drives or torque sensors [50], while long, light links, as in space manipulators, need flexible-link models [51]. The lowest structural resonance limits how fast a position controller can be made before it excites vibration, a trade-off revisited in the design section (Section 10). Compliance is not only a defect: series elastic and variable-stiffness actuators add it on purpose to protect the gearbox and the people nearby (Section 4), and soft robots replace rigid links with elastomers altogether [52].

Redundancy and modularity. Arms with seven or more joints are kinematically redundant, which lets them avoid obstacles and joint limits while holding the hand fixed, at the cost of more actuators to buy, power and maintain [1]. Many current robots are built from identical joint modules, each with motor, gearbox, encoders, driver and microcontroller in one housing on a shared power and communication bus (Figure 1) [13–15]. Modularity simplifies manufacturing, spares and repair, and it makes the actuator, rather than the whole robot, the unit that is characterized, modelled and replaced.

4 Actuators, gears and transmissions

An actuator converts stored energy into controlled motion. A robot designer judges actuators by a handful of figures: force or torque density (per unit mass and volume), power density, efficiency, bandwidth (how fast force can change), stroke or speed range, controllability, and impedance, that is, how the actuator responds when the environment pushes back [19,53]. No technology wins on all of them, which is why robots use several (Table 2).

Electric actuation dominates because electricity is easy to store, switch, measure and route through a robot, and because a brushless motor driven by a modern inverter is efficient and precisely controllable (Section 5). Its weakness is that motors produce their best power at high speed and low torque, whereas joints need high torque at low speed. Muscle-like actuators are an active research area because biological muscle combines moderate stress with large strain, good efficiency and built-in compliance, but no artificial muscle yet matches electric motors for overall maturity in robots [53,58]. Hydraulic actuation remains attractive where force density matters most, but most new legged and humanoid robots are electric [10,13,41].

Gears and transmissions

A transmission of ratio N multiplies motor torque by N (less losses) and divides speed by N. It also changes what the link "feels": the motor's rotor inertia Jm appears at the joint multiplied by N2, and friction inside the motor and gearbox is amplified when the joint is driven backwards from the outside [59,60]. For a load inertia JL and motor torque τm, the joint acceleration is

q̈ = N η τm / (JL + N2 Jm),

which is largest at the inertia-matching ratio N* = √(JL/Jm), where the reflected rotor inertia equals the load inertia [59]. Real choices also weigh speed limits, continuous torque against gravity, efficiency, size and cost [60]. The main transmission types are compared in Table 3.

Strain wave gears deserve a note because nearly every industrial and collaborative arm uses them. An elliptical wave generator deforms a thin flexible steel cup (the flexspline), whose external teeth mesh with a rigid internal-toothed ring that has two more teeth; each turn of the wave generator advances the flexspline by two teeth, giving ratios of 50–160 in a single stage [21,22]. The flexing that makes this possible also makes the gear a nonlinear torsional spring with friction, hysteresis and kinematic error, which must be modelled for precise torque control [22,61].

Impedance, impacts and safety

What matters during a collision, a foot touchdown or an unexpected contact with a person is the actuator's mechanical impedance in the first few milliseconds, before any controller can react. A high-ratio gearbox couples the large reflected rotor inertia rigidly to the link, so the impact force grows with it [64,65]. Three hardware strategies reduce this. Proprioceptive or quasi-direct-drive actuators keep the ratio low so the reflected inertia is small and the motor current measures the joint torque directly [10,11]. Series elastic actuators put a spring between gearbox and load: the spring decouples the rotor's inertia during impacts, and its measured deflection gives a clean force signal for force control, at the price of lower force bandwidth [26,32]. Variable impedance actuators can change their stiffness, softening for fast or safe motion and stiffening for precision [66–68]. The series elastic actuator has the same large rotor inertia as the harmonic drive, but the soft spring passes it on slowly: the force builds over 24 ms rather than 12 ms, long enough for a 1 kHz controller to detect the contact and back off (see Section 9 and the article on reactive behavior). How these actuators are used in impedance and force control is covered in classical and modern control [70].

5 Electric motors, servos and motor drivers

Almost every electric robot joint is driven by a permanent-magnet motor. Its behaviour is captured by two constants that, in SI units, are numerically equal: the torque constant Kt, relating torque to current (τ = Kt i), and the back-EMF constant Ke, relating the voltage the motor generates to its speed (e = Ke ω) [54,71]. The winding resistance R converts current into heat at a rate i2R, so the quantity that tells how much torque a motor can make for a given heat load is the motor constant Km = Kt/√R. Torque is limited by heat, and speed is limited by the supply voltage, because the back-EMF eventually uses up all of it [54]. These two limits shape everything below.

Motor types

Brushed DC motors switch current between windings with a mechanical commutator and brushes. They need only a DC voltage to run, which keeps drives simple and cheap, but brushes wear, spark and limit speed and continuous current, so they appear mainly in small, low-cost mechanisms [54]. Brushless DC (BLDC) and permanent-magnet synchronous motors (PMSM) move the windings to the stator and the magnets to the rotor, and replace the commutator with electronic switching driven by a rotor position sensor. Without brushes they are more efficient, more durable and easier to cool, since the heat is generated in the stator, which is attached to the housing [71]. Robot designers often buy them frameless, as a separate stator and rotor that are built directly into the joint housing. A motor's torque grows with its air-gap radius, which is why quasi-direct-drive legged robots use flat, large-diameter motors [10,11]. Permanent-magnet motors also produce cogging and torque ripple from the interaction of magnets and stator teeth, which matters for smooth slow motion and for torque sensing through current; it can be reduced by motor design and compensated in software [72].

Stepper motors move in fixed angular steps and can position a load without any feedback, which makes them common in 3D printers, laboratory automation and low-cost arms. Driven open-loop, they lose position silently if the load exceeds their torque, and they draw full current even when holding still; closed-loop steppers add an encoder to address both problems [73]. Servos, in the robotics-kit sense, package a small motor, a gearbox, a position sensor and a controller in one housing that accepts a position command. Hobby servos take a pulse-width command and report nothing back, while "smart" servos on a serial bus report position, load, voltage and temperature; such servos power many low-cost research arms and hands, including the arms used in ALOHA [7].

Motor drivers

A motor driver turns a low-power command into controlled current. A brushed motor needs an H-bridge of four transistors; a brushless motor needs a three-phase inverter of six. The transistors, usually MOSFETs at robot voltages, are switched on and off by pulse-width modulation (PWM) at tens of kilohertz, typically above the audible range, and the motor's inductance smooths the switched voltage into a nearly continuous current [74]. Gate drivers, dead time to keep the upper and lower switches of a leg from conducting together, current sensing with shunt resistors or Hall-effect sensors, and protection against over-current, over-voltage and over-temperature complete the design [74,75]. Gallium nitride (GaN) transistors switch faster with lower losses than silicon, which allows higher PWM frequencies and smaller, cooler drivers that fit inside joints [76]. Open designs such as the Mini Cheetah and Open Dynamic Robot Initiative drivers have made compact, high-bandwidth joint drives available to researchers [14,34].

Field-oriented control

To make a brushless motor behave like an ideal torque source, the driver controls the stator current so that its magnetic field is always perpendicular to the rotor magnets. Field-oriented control (FOC), introduced by Blaschke for induction machines [77], does this by expressing the three phase currents in a frame that rotates with the rotor. The Clarke transform maps the three phase quantities onto two stationary axes (α, β), and the Park transform, introduced in 1929 for synchronous machines, rotates them by the electrical angle into direct (d) and quadrature (q) components [75,78]. For a surface-magnet motor the torque is

τ = (3/2) p λ iq,

where p is the number of pole pairs and λ the magnet flux linkage, so the controller regulates iq to the torque command and id to zero with two PI controllers. The output voltages are transformed back and synthesized by space-vector PWM, which uses the DC bus about 15% more effectively than simple sinusoidal PWM [71,79]. In the rotating frame the two current loops are cross-coupled through terms proportional to speed, and the back-EMF acts as a disturbance; both can be cancelled by feedforward, and choosing the PI gains as Kp = Lωc, Ki = Rωc places the closed-loop bandwidth at ωc [80]. The rotor angle comes from an encoder or Hall sensors, or, at higher speeds, can be estimated from the back-EMF without a sensor [81].

Sizing motors for heat

A motor's peak torque is limited by magnetic saturation and the driver's current limit, but its continuous torque is limited by temperature. Copper losses heat the winding, the heat flows through the stator into the housing and then into the air or a cooling plate, and the winding insulation sets the upper limit; class F insulation, for example, is rated for 155 °C [82,83]. Lumped thermal networks of a few thermal resistances and capacitances predict winding temperature well enough to size motors and to protect them in real time [83,84]. A practical sizing rule compares the root-mean-square (RMS) torque of the duty cycle with the motor's continuous rating, and the peak torque with its peak rating, but the RMS rule hides how long a high load can last, which the thermal model shows.

The two time constants explain why short peaks are tolerable (the winding needs about a minute to heat up) while sustained overloads are not (the housing keeps heating for half an hour). High-power humanoids have used such thermal models, with temperature sensors and liquid cooling, to let motors exceed their continuous rating safely for short periods [84]. 6 End effectors, grippers and compliant mechanisms

The end effector is where a robot meets the objects it works on, and it is often the part that decides whether a task succeeds. Industrial practice favours simple, task-specific tools such as two-finger parallel grippers, vacuum cups, magnets, welding torches and screwdrivers, often exchanged automatically with tool changers [85]. Research on general-purpose manipulation has pursued human-like hands for more than a century, from early prostheses to tendon-driven research hands [86]. Table 4 summarizes the main families.

Grasp mechanics. A grasp is secure when the contact forces the fingers can apply, each limited to lie inside its friction cone, can resist any disturbing force and torque on the object; this is the condition of force closure [98,99]. For a parallel-jaw gripper the practical version is simple: the pads must squeeze hard enough that friction carries the object's weight and inertial loads. Squeezing too little drops the object; squeezing too much crushes it or wastes motor current. Humans solve this by sensing incipient slip with their fingertips and adjusting grip force within about a tenth of a second [100]; robots can do the same with tactile sensors, which are covered in sensors and sensory modalities [101,102], and the slip-reflexes that use them are covered in reactive behavior.

Underactuation and compliance. Hirose's soft gripper of 1978 used a single wire and pulleys to curl a multi-link finger around objects of any shape [43]. The same idea, more joints than actuators with springs or differential mechanisms distributing the motion, underlies adaptive hands that conform to objects passively and tolerate positioning errors [90–92], and "synergy"-based hands that drive all fingers with one motor along a human-like motion pattern [93]. Soft grippers take this further with fingers made of elastomers inflated by air [94,95]. Compliance also helps at the wrist: Whitney's remote centre compliance device lets a peg slide into a chamfered hole without a precise position controller, an early demonstration that a passive mechanism can solve part of a control problem [103].

Compliant mechanisms get their motion from the bending of flexible members rather than from hinges and bearings. They have no backlash, friction or wear in their joints, need no assembly or lubrication, and can be made in one piece by moulding or 3D printing, which suits low-cost grippers and fingers [104]. Their large deflections are nonlinear, but the pseudo-rigid-body model replaces a flexible beam by a rigid link on a torsion spring placed at about 85% of the beam length, with a stiffness coefficient chosen to match exact solutions [104,105].

Grippers as data-collection tools. Because a learned policy imitates the hardware that recorded its demonstrations, several groups now design the end effector and the data-collection device together. 7 Batteries, power management and thermal management

Mobile robots carry their energy with them, and lithium-ion batteries are the default choice because they offer the best combination of energy density, power density, cycle life and cost among rechargeable chemistries [107,108]. A robot's battery pack connects many cells in series to reach the bus voltage (for example 14 cells in series for a nominal 50 V) and in parallel to reach the required capacity and current. Energy is therefore a hard design constraint: run time equals usable energy divided by average power, and every gram of battery must itself be carried. A common figure of merit for locomotion efficiency is the dimensionless cost of transport, the energy used per unit weight and distance travelled [109]. Passive-dynamic walkers reached costs of transport comparable to human walking, while conventional actuated humanoids used roughly an order of magnitude more [110]. Electric legged robots can recover some energy by running motors as generators during braking, which the MIT Cheetah design explicitly exploited [10].

Battery management. A battery management system (BMS) measures every cell's voltage, the pack current and temperatures; it protects against over-charge, over-discharge, over-current and over-temperature, balances the cells so that they stay at the same state of charge, and estimates the state of charge (SOC) and state of health [111]. SOC cannot be measured directly. Counting coulombs, that is integrating current over time, drifts with any current-sensor bias and depends on a correct starting value; the open-circuit voltage indicates SOC but is hidden behind resistive and polarization voltage drops while current flows. Equivalent-circuit models represent the cell as a voltage source in series with a resistor and one or more resistor–capacitor pairs [112], and an extended Kalman filter can fuse the current integral with the voltage measurement through such a model, as Plett showed for vehicle packs [113]. The general Kalman filter machinery is explained in sensor fusion and state estimation.

Safety and ageing. Lithium-ion cells can enter thermal runaway when overheated, overcharged, crushed or internally shorted: exothermic reactions raise the temperature further, the cell vents flammable gases, and the failure can spread to neighbouring cells [114]. Pack design therefore combines cell-level fusing, mechanical protection, thermal barriers and venting, and lithium batteries must pass the United Nations transport tests before they may be shipped [115]. Capacity fades with cycling and calendar time, and high temperatures and fast charging accelerate the degradation mechanisms [116,117]; for robot fleets, battery replacement is a maintenance item to be planned like any other wear part (Section 10).

Power distribution. Between the battery and the motor drivers sits a DC bus with fuses or electronic switches, a pre-charge circuit that limits the inrush current into the drivers' capacitors, voltage converters for the computers and sensors, and an emergency-stop circuit. Regenerative braking returns current to the bus; if the battery cannot absorb it, for example when it is full or disconnected, the bus voltage rises, so drives include braking resistors or clamps to dump the energy [74]. Modern drives implement a certified safe torque off function that removes power from the motor in hardware, independently of software [118].

Thermal management. Heat limits motors (Section 5), power electronics and batteries alike, and in a sealed, compact robot it is often the factor that limits sustained performance. Designers conduct heat from windings and transistors into the structure, which then acts as a heat sink, add fans or liquid cooling where needed, and monitor temperatures to derate current before limits are reached [83,84]. A thermal model running alongside the controller lets the robot know how much more effort it can afford, which can also be exposed to planners and learned policies so that they avoid behaviours the hardware cannot sustain.

8 Embedded computers and real-time microcontrollers

A robot's computing is distributed across several layers that differ in speed, determinism and power (Table 5). Close to each motor, a microcontroller samples currents and encoders and updates the PWM duty cycles tens of thousands of times per second; a few hundred microseconds of delay here would destabilize the current loop. Further up, a real-time computer runs whole-body control and state estimation at hundreds of hertz to a few kilohertz. At the top, general-purpose processors and GPUs run perception, planning and learned policies at tens of hertz, where occasional delays can be tolerated if the layers below keep the robot safe [1,13,14].

Real-time means predictable, not fast. A real-time system is correct only if its results arrive before their deadlines. On a single processor running periodic tasks with fixed priorities, the classical result of Liu and Layland is that assigning higher priority to tasks with shorter periods (rate-monotonic scheduling) is optimal among fixed-priority schemes, and that all deadlines are met if the total utilization stays below n(21/n−1), about 69% for many tasks [128]. The bound is sufficient but pessimistic; exact response-time analysis computes each task's worst-case response time by adding its own execution time to the preemptions it can suffer from higher-priority tasks [129–131]. Shared resources need care: a low-priority task holding a lock can block a high-priority one while medium-priority tasks run, a priority inversion that priority-inheritance protocols bound [132]. General-purpose Linux is not designed for hard deadlines, but the PREEMPT_RT patches make most of the kernel preemptible and reduce worst-case latencies to tens of microseconds on suitable hardware [122]. Robot middleware adds its own latencies, which must be measured rather than assumed [127].

A delay of 1 ms in a 40 Hz position loop adds a little overshoot; at 2 ms the overshoot is 35%, and at 3 ms the loop is unstable, although the controller code is unchanged. Random jitter of up to 1 ms on top of a 1 ms delay is almost as harmful as a fixed 1.5–2 ms delay. Delays and jitter belong in the hardware specification, alongside torque and speed.

Safety-related computing. Functions whose failure could hurt someone, such as emergency stops, speed and force limiting and safe torque off, are implemented according to functional-safety standards that demand redundancy, diagnostics and systematic development processes [118,135,136]. In practice this produces a split architecture: a simple, certified safety controller with independent hardware monitors a few critical quantities and can cut power, while the complex autonomy software, including any learned policy, runs on non-certified computers above it. Hardware watchdogs that stop the motors if a controller stops sending commands, and drivers that fall back to a damped, low-torque mode when communication is lost, are the minimal versions of the same idea.

Compute for learned policies. Neural network policies, especially vision–language–action models, need far more computation than classical controllers. Onboard GPU modules can run moderately sized networks at control-relevant rates when models are optimized and quantized [124], while the largest models are often run off-board or split so that a slower high-level model guides a fast low-level controller on the robot, an architecture discussed in foundation models for physical AI. Either way, the real-time layers below must remain responsible for stability and safety when the policy is late.

9 Motor feedback, calibration and hardware diagnostics

Feedback in the joint. A typical electric joint measures motor phase currents (for torque control), motor angle (for commutation and velocity), often the output angle after the gearbox, the bus voltage and several temperatures. Encoders, resolvers and Hall sensors are described in sensors and sensory modalities [137]; here what matters is how a joint uses them. Velocity is usually computed from encoder positions, which trades noise against delay, and observer-based estimators do better than simple differences at low speed [138]. With a low-ratio transmission, motor current is a good joint-torque estimate, which is what makes proprioceptive actuators sensitive to contact [11]; with a high-ratio gearbox, friction hides much of the external torque, so arms such as the DLR lightweight robot add dedicated joint torque sensors [15,139]. Two encoders, one on each side of a compliant gearbox or spring, measure its deflection and hence the transmitted torque, as in series elastic actuators [26,32].

Calibration turns a robot built to tolerances into one whose models match reality, and it happens at several levels:

  • Sensors and commutation: current-sensor offsets, encoder nonlinearity, and the electrical zero of each motor, without which field-oriented control loses torque (Section 5).
  • Actuators: torque constant, cogging and friction, with friction usually the largest error at low speed; Coulomb, viscous and Stribeck terms, or dynamic friction models such as LuGre, are identified from experiments [72,140,141]. Learned "actuator networks" map commands and recent history to output torque when physics models fall short [5].
  • Kinematics: joint zero offsets and link lengths, identified by measuring the end effector with an external instrument such as a laser tracker and solving a least-squares problem; this can improve absolute accuracy several-fold, although repeatability is a property of the hardware that calibration cannot improve [142–145].
  • Dynamics: masses, centres of mass and inertias of the links and payload, identified from joint torques along optimized excitation trajectories while keeping the parameters physically consistent [146–149]; this is covered in more depth in physical modeling.
  • Sensors relative to the robot: camera-to-gripper (hand–eye) transforms [150] and the extrinsic and temporal calibration of other sensors, discussed in the fusion article.

Diagnostics. Hardware fails gradually and suddenly: bearings and gears wear, cables chafe, connectors loosen, encoders get contaminated, batteries age and motors overheat. A field study of tracked ground robots found that they failed often, with effectors, power, control systems and communications all contributing, and that many failures could have been anticipated by monitoring [151]. Fault detection and isolation compares measurements with what a model predicts and analyzes the residuals [152,153]. Two techniques are particularly useful for joints. The generalized-momentum observer estimates external joint torques from motor torques and velocities alone, without acceleration measurements, and is widely used for collision detection and actuator fault isolation in collaborative arms [154–156]. Vibration and current signature analysis detects bearing and gear damage from periodic features: a damaged tooth or bearing race produces impacts at a characteristic frequency that ring structural resonances, and band-pass filtering around a resonance followed by envelope analysis and spectral kurtosis reveals them [157–160]. Applied to repetitive robot motions, such methods have detected gearbox wear in industrial robots [161].

10 Mechanical design, manufacturing and maintainability

Design process. Systematic engineering design moves from requirements (payload, reach, speed, accuracy, run time, environment, cost, lifetime) through concept selection to detailed design, and keeps checking the design against the requirements at each step [164]. For robots the requirements are coupled: payload sets joint torques, which set motor and gearbox sizes, which add mass to the links, which raises the torques again. Practical design therefore iterates between the mechanical layout, the actuator sizing of Sections 4 and 5, the power budget of Section 7, and dynamic simulation.

Materials and structure. For links that must be stiff and light, the relevant property is specific stiffness, Young's modulus divided by density. It is almost the same for steel, aluminium and titanium, so among metals the choice is driven by manufacturability, strength, cost and how thin a wall can be made; fibre composites can do several times better along their fibres [165]. Topology optimization distributes material where loads need it [166], and its organic shapes have become manufacturable with additive processes. Structural resonance matters as much as static deflection, because the lowest natural frequency limits the bandwidth of position control and the joint's own torsional stiffness, often from the gearbox, is in series with the link's bending stiffness.

Manufacturing. Robot parts are made by CNC machining, sheet-metal work, casting and injection moulding, and increasingly by additive manufacturing, which builds parts layer by layer from polymers, composites or metals and allows complex internal channels, lattice structures and consolidation of many parts into one [168,169]. Robotics has also produced its own processes: shape deposition manufacturing embeds sensors, actuators and compliant joints within a single moulded part, as in the fast, robust hexapod robots of Cham and colleagues [170]; pop-up laminate fabrication folds laser-cut multi-material sheets into mechanisms, enabling insect-scale flying robots [171,172]; and soft robots are cast from elastomers in printed moulds [52,173]. Whatever the process, design for manufacture and assembly reduces part count, uses standard fasteners and parts, and makes assembly possible in one direction with self-locating features, which cuts cost and errors [174].

Maintainability and reliability. A robot that works in a laboratory for a demonstration and a robot that works in a warehouse for five years are different products. Reliability engineering describes component lifetimes with distributions such as the Weibull distribution, whose shape parameter distinguishes early failures, random failures and wear-out [175,176]. Rolling bearings, for example, are rated for a statistical life under a given load [177]. A robot with many actuators is a series system: any one failure stops it, so its reliability is the product of its components' reliabilities. Maintenance can be reactive (fix on failure), preventive (replace at fixed intervals) or condition-based (replace when diagnostics show wear, Section 9), and the optimal preventive replacement interval for a wearing part follows from its lifetime distribution and the relative costs of planned and unplanned replacement [162,178,179].

Safety standards. Industrial robot design is governed by ISO 10218, whose 2025 revision covers the robot itself (Part 1) [182]; collaborative operation, including limits on the forces and pressures a robot may apply to body regions during contact, is specified in ISO/TS 15066 [183]; and personal care robots, including mobile servant and person-carrier robots, are covered by ISO 13482 [184]. These standards connect directly to hardware: they favour low moving mass, rounded surfaces, backdrivable or torque-sensing joints, certified safety functions and verified stopping performance [65,156].

11 Hardware for learning robots

The idea that intelligence depends on the body, that good morphology and materials can make control simpler, has a long history in robotics and cognitive science [185]. It has practical consequences for physical AI.

The body is part of the learning problem. Policies trained in simulation inherit every mismatch between the simulated and real hardware. For legged robots, the main gaps were actuator dynamics and latency, and they were closed by identifying actuators carefully, learning actuator models from data, and randomizing dynamics parameters during training [5,6,186]; the general methods are covered in reinforcement learning. Policies trained on real hardware face a different problem: trial and error wears and breaks hardware, so practical systems limit torques and speeds, add automatic resets and design robots to survive falls and collisions [12,187]. Learned dexterous manipulation with tendon-driven hands relied heavily on simulation partly because real trials are slow and stress the hardware [188,189]. Robust, backdrivable, impact-tolerant actuators (Section 4) and good thermal models (Section 5) are therefore enabling technologies for learning, not just for control.

Hardware for data. Imitation learning and robot foundation models need large demonstration datasets, and data is collected through hardware. Low-cost, open designs such as ALOHA and Mobile ALOHA, which pair kinematically matched leader and follower arms [7,39], GELLO teleoperation controllers [9], hand-held grippers such as UMI [8] and open software and arm kits [40] have made data collection cheaper and more widespread. A recurring design rule is consistency: if the data-collection device and the deployed robot share gripper geometry, camera placement and control interfaces, demonstrations transfer with little adaptation [7,8].

Open and repairable platforms. Open-hardware robots, from the iCub humanoid [190] to open quadrupeds and humanoids built from common parts [13,14,36,41], let research groups modify, repair and replicate hardware, which matters when learning experiments run for months.

Co-design. Finally, the body itself can be optimized along with its controller. Evolutionary systems designed virtual creatures and then physical robots automatically [191,192], and gradient-based methods co-optimize link lengths, gear ratios and trajectories for real robots [193]. As differentiable simulators and fast fabrication improve, design and learning are converging: the actuator, transmission and gripper become parameters to be learned alongside the policy, within the limits set by manufacturing, power, heat and reliability.

Tools. Mechanical design relies on CAD with integrated finite-element analysis; robot descriptions exported from CAD feed simulators such as MuJoCo or Isaac (see physical modeling); and the hardware interface is commonly provided by ROS 2 with real-time control frameworks [123,126,194]. Open motor-driver hardware and firmware, open actuator designs and open robot platforms provide tested starting points [14,34,40].

Open problems. Several hardware limits still bound what physical AI can do. Electric actuators remain heavier, hotter and less robust than biological muscle for the same work, and artificial muscles are not yet mature enough to replace them [53,58]. Dexterous hands combine many small actuators, tendons and sensors that are costly and fragile compared with grippers [86]. Battery energy density limits the working time of legged robots and humanoids [107]. Robots that learn need hardware that tolerates thousands of hours of exploratory use and reports its own condition [12,151]. And as fleets grow, reliability, maintainability, cost and safety certification, rather than peak performance, become the constraints that decide which designs survive [176,182].

Summary. Robot hardware is a chain of conversions, from chemical energy in a battery to electrical power in a driver, torque in a motor, multiplied torque in a transmission and contact force at a gripper, closed by sensors and embedded computers that run on time. Each conversion has limits and side effects: gear ratios trade force against inertia and backdrivability; motors are limited by heat and voltage; grippers by friction; batteries by energy and safety; control loops by delay and jitter; calibration by what can be measured; and every part by wear. The examples show these trade-offs in miniature: inertia matching sets the best acceleration ratio, but backdrivability and impact safety favour low ratios or springs; feedforward and calibration let field-oriented control reach its design bandwidth; RMS torque hides thermal risk; pseudo-rigid-body models make compliant fingers designable; voltage-based estimation fixes coulomb-counting drift; response-time analysis proves timing that utilization bounds cannot; separating forward and backward motion isolates friction; residual observers catch collisions in milliseconds; joint compliance and angular errors dominate arm stiffness and accuracy; and preventive replacement can halve the cost of keeping a robot fleet running. Physical AI runs on these bodies, and the best learning systems are designed with them in mind.
