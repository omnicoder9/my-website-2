1 What "traditional" AI and machine learning mean for physical AI

Artificial intelligence has always had two broad strategies. The first writes knowledge down: an engineer or domain expert encodes facts, rules and procedures in a symbolic form that a program can reason with. Philosopher John Haugeland called this "good old-fashioned AI" (GOFAI) [1]. The second lets the program acquire the knowledge from data. Tom Mitchell's textbook gives the standard definition: "A computer program is said to learn from experience E with respect to some class of tasks T and performance measure P, if its performance at tasks in T, as measured by P, improves with experience E" [2]. Both strategies are covered in the standard references on AI and machine learning [3–6].

This article uses traditional AI and machine learning for the methods that came before, or that sit beside, large deep networks and foundation models. They include rule-based and expert systems, fuzzy logic, linear and kernel models, nearest neighbours, decision trees and ensembles, clustering and dimensionality reduction, classical anomaly detectors, and probabilistic graphical models with Bayesian inference. "Traditional" does not mean obsolete. In a typical robot these methods still do a large share of the work for several reasons:

  • Small data. Robot data are expensive. Each labelled grasp, terrain patch or fault example costs robot time, and some failures cannot be produced on purpose. Methods with strong structure or priors, such as physics-based regression, Gaussian processes and Bayesian networks, learn from tens or hundreds of examples rather than millions [7,8].
  • Tabular and low-dimensional signals. Much of what a robot measures is a vector of joint currents, temperatures, vibration features and estimator residuals, not an image. On data of this kind, tree ensembles still match or beat deep networks [9,10].
  • Embedded compute and latency. A decision tree, a linear model or a rule table runs in microseconds on a microcontroller, with predictable timing.
  • Inspection and certification. Rules, sparse models and explicit probabilistic models can be read, reviewed and tested against requirements. This matters for safety cases and for the growing set of AI regulations and standards [11–13].
  • Calibrated uncertainty. Probabilistic models say how sure they are. Robots need that to decide when to act, when to slow down and when to ask for help [14,15].

Within the series, this article belongs to the Learning and AI Models part. It builds on the earlier articles on sensors, computer vision, sensor fusion and state estimation, physical modeling and world models. Some topics already have a home elsewhere in the series. Kalman and particle filters are in the fusion article, and the detection and segmentation models used in perception are in the vision article. Here the focus is on the general learning and reasoning methods and on how they are used in robots. 2 A short history: from Shakey and MYCIN to Stanley

Alan Turing asked in 1950 whether machines can think. He proposed replacing that question with an imitation game, and he suggested that a "child machine" might be educated rather than programmed [16]. The term "artificial intelligence" was introduced in the 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth summer research project, held in 1956 [17]. Learning was present from the start. Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) learned linear decision boundaries from examples [18], and Arthur Samuel's checkers program (1959) improved its own play through experience, in one of the first papers with "machine learning" in its title [19]. In 1969 Minsky and Papert analysed the limits of single-layer perceptrons [20], and interest moved towards symbolic methods.

Robotics was part of that symbolic programme. Shakey, built at SRI between 1966 and 1972, was a mobile robot that perceived its surroundings with a camera and a rangefinder, represented them in logic, and planned its own actions [21]. Two lasting algorithms came out of the project: the A* search algorithm [22] and the STRIPS planner, whose operator representation of preconditions, add lists and delete lists still underlies planning languages such as PDDL [23,24]. In the UK the 1973 Lighthill report criticised AI's failure to scale beyond toy problems, and funding was cut [25]. That was the first of the downturns later called "AI winters".

The 1970s and 1980s were the era of the expert system, which captured the knowledge of human specialists in rules. DENDRAL, started at Stanford in 1965, inferred molecular structure from mass-spectrometry data and is generally regarded as the first expert system [26]. MYCIN (mid-1970s) recommended antibiotic therapy using several hundred backward-chained rules and introduced certainty factors for reasoning under uncertainty [27,28]. R1, later called XCON, configured VAX computer orders at Digital Equipment Corporation and became one of the first commercially important AI systems [29]. Forgy's Rete algorithm made large rule bases fast enough to run in production [30]. Expert systems also exposed the knowledge acquisition bottleneck: extracting and maintaining thousands of rules from experts was slow and brittle [3,31].

Three developments in the mid-1980s changed the field. Back-propagation made it practical to train multi-layer neural networks [32]. Decision-tree learners such as CART and ID3 induced rule-like classifiers directly from data [33,34]. Judea Pearl's Bayesian networks replaced ad-hoc certainty calculi with sound probabilistic reasoning [35]. In robotics, Rodney Brooks's subsumption architecture rejected central symbolic world models in favour of layers of reactive behaviours [36,37]. Moravec and Elfes introduced probabilistic occupancy grids for mapping with noisy sonar [38,39]. In 1989 Pomerleau's ALVINN steered the Navlab vehicle with a small neural network trained by imitating a human driver, an early example of end-to-end supervised learning on a robot [40].

The 1990s and 2000s brought statistical learning theory and a practical toolbox. Valiant's PAC framework and Vapnik's theory of capacity gave learning a theoretical basis [41,42]. Support vector machines [43,44], boosting [45,46] and random forests [47,48] became standard tools. Probabilistic methods took over mobile robotics: localisation, mapping and SLAM were recast as Bayesian inference [14]. The 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge winner, Stanford's Stanley, brought these strands together. It combined probabilistic state estimation, classical classifiers and a self-supervised vision system that learned what drivable road looked like from its own laser scans [49,50]. In 2012 deep convolutional networks trained on ImageNet cut image-classification error sharply [51,52]. Deep learning has since become the default for perception [53]. The methods in this article remain in use alongside it, as Table 1 and the rest of the article show.

3 Rule-based systems and expert systems

Production systems

A rule-based or production system has three parts [3,31]:

  • A working memory of facts, for example wheel_speed_zero or gripper_open.
  • A rule base of condition-action pairs: IF premises THEN conclusion or action.
  • An inference engine that runs the recognise-act cycle. It finds the rules whose premises match working memory (the conflict set), picks one using a conflict-resolution strategy such as specificity, recency or priority, fires it, and repeats.

Forward chaining is data-driven: it starts from observations and derives everything that follows, which suits monitoring and control. Backward chaining is goal-driven: it starts from a hypothesis and searches for rules and facts that support it. MYCIN used backward chaining so that it asked the clinician only the questions relevant to the hypothesis being tested [28]. Matching every rule against every fact on every cycle is expensive. Forgy's Rete algorithm compiles the rule conditions into a network that caches partial matches, so each cycle costs time proportional to what changed rather than to the size of working memory [30]. Rete and its successors are still used in production-rule engines.

Expert systems add two things to a production system. One is a knowledge base elicited from specialists. The other is an explanation facility that can say which rules led to a conclusion. MYCIN handled uncertainty with certainty factors (CFs) between −1 and 1. When two rules support the same conclusion with positive CFs x and y, the combined belief is x + y(1 − x) [27]. CFs are easy to elicit and compute. Heckerman later showed that they are consistent with probability theory only under strong independence assumptions that rarely hold, which is one reason diagnosis moved to Bayesian networks (Section 7) [58].

Fuzzy logic and fuzzy control

Many rules that operators state in words, such as "if the obstacle is near, go slowly", use vague categories. Zadeh's fuzzy sets let an element belong to a set to a degree between 0 and 1 [54]. In 1975 Mamdani and Assilian controlled a laboratory steam engine with linguistic fuzzy rules. Their method fuzzifies the inputs, evaluates the rules with min for AND, clips each rule's output set, aggregates the clipped sets with max and defuzzifies them, for example by taking the centroid [59]. Takagi-Sugeno models replace the output sets with local linear functions, which makes them easier to identify from data and to analyse for stability [60]. Fuzzy controllers became common in consumer products and vehicles, and fuzzy rule bases were used widely for behaviour blending in mobile-robot navigation [61]. Behaviours, state machines and behaviour trees

Brooks's subsumption architecture builds a robot from layers of simple behaviours (avoid, wander, explore), each a network of finite-state machines. Higher layers can suppress or override the outputs of lower layers [36]. Behaviour-based robotics, surveyed by Arkin, generalised this approach to blending and arbitrating between behaviours [62]. In practice, robot decision logic is still mostly written by hand as rules and state machines. Boss, the winner of the 2007 DARPA Urban Challenge, used hierarchical finite-state machines in its behavioural layer to handle lane driving, intersections and parking-lot manoeuvres [63]. Industrial cells are programmed in the IEC 61131-3 languages for programmable logic controllers, such as ladder diagrams, structured text and sequential function charts, which are rule-based systems in a different notation [64].

Behaviour trees (BTs) came from computer-game AI and have become a popular alternative to large state machines in robotics. A BT is a tree of sequence, fallback and parallel nodes over condition and action leaves. Each tick, the tree is traversed, and every node returns success, failure or running. Because subtrees can be reused and composed, BTs scale better than flat state machines [65,66]. The ROS 2 navigation stack, Nav2, configures its whole navigation behaviour as a behaviour tree [67].

Symbolic knowledge and planning

Symbolic AI also survives in task planning and knowledge representation. Planners that take PDDL domain descriptions, the successors of STRIPS, sequence high-level actions such as "pick", "place" and "open drawer". Integrated task and motion planning connects these symbolic actions to geometric feasibility checks [24,68]. KnowRob gives robots a logic-based knowledge base, built on Prolog and ontologies, about objects, actions and their properties, which the robot can query during a task [69]. In automated driving, formal rule sets such as Responsibility-Sensitive Safety define provably safe longitudinal and lateral distances. They act as a checkable safety layer around learned driving policies [70].

Rule-based methods are the right choice when the knowledge is explicit, the cases are few enough to enumerate, and behaviour must be auditable. Interlocks, safety monitors, mode logic, protocol handling and fault trees are examples. They are the wrong choice when the mapping from sensors to decisions is too complex for anyone to write down, as with recognising a pedestrian in an image. That is the job of learning.

4 Supervised learning

The learning problem

In supervised learning we are given n labelled examples (xi, yi) drawn from an unknown distribution, and we look for a function f in a hypothesis class 𝓕 that predicts y from x on new data. Regularised empirical risk minimisation chooses

f̂ = argminf ∈ 𝓕  (1/n) Σi ℓ(f(xi), yi) + λ Ω(f)

Here ℓ is a loss, such as squared error for regression or cross-entropy for classification, and Ω penalises complexity [5,42]. Least squares, the oldest instance, was published by Legendre in 1805 and by Gauss in 1809 [71]. Ridge regression adds Ω = ‖w‖² to stabilise ill-conditioned problems [72]. The lasso uses ‖w‖1, which sets many weights to exactly zero and so selects features [73].

The central difficulty is generalisation. A flexible model can fit the training data perfectly and still predict badly. Geman and colleagues described this as a trade-off between bias, the error from a model too simple to represent the truth, and variance, the sensitivity of the fit to the particular sample [74]. Learning theory bounds the gap between training and test error in terms of model capacity and sample size [41,42]. The no-free-lunch theorems show that no learner is best for every problem, so prior knowledge about the problem has to enter through the choice of model [75]. In practice, hyperparameters such as λ are chosen by cross-validation. The data are split into k folds, the model is trained on k − 1 of them and tested on the held-out fold, and the errors are averaged. Kohavi's study recommended stratified 10-fold cross-validation for model selection [76,77].

Supervised learning in robots

Supervised learning appears throughout a robot:

  • Model learning for control. A robot can learn its forward or inverse dynamics from logged joint positions, velocities, accelerations and torques [7]. Rigid-body dynamics is linear in the inertial parameters, so the classical approach is linear least squares on physics-derived features [78]. Unmodelled effects such as friction, cable forces and gear backlash motivated nonparametric learners. Locally weighted learning and LWPR fit many local linear models online [79,80], and local Gaussian processes add uncertainty estimates [81]. The physical modeling article treats system identification from the physics side.
  • Learning from demonstration. Behaviour cloning regresses actions on observations from expert demonstrations, as ALVINN did for steering [40,82]. Because the learned policy's own small errors take it into states the expert never visited, errors compound over time. DAgger addresses this by asking the expert to label the states the learner actually reaches [83].
  • Perception and grasping. Before deep learning, classifiers trained on engineered features predicted grasp points and grasp success. Saxena and colleagues trained a model on synthetic images to find grasp points on novel objects [84,85]. Detection and segmentation models are covered in the vision article.
  • Calibration and sensor models. Regression maps raw sensor readings to physical quantities, for example tactile voltages to forces or thermistor readings to temperature, and models how sensor errors depend on operating conditions.

The physics-structured model recovers the inertia and gravity terms almost exactly. Its small friction error comes from approximating the smooth friction curve with a sign function. It also extrapolates to faster motions without losing accuracy. The kernel model interpolates well but fails badly outside its training range, because its basis functions vanish there. Extrapolation failure is one form of distribution shift. The standard guarantees assume test data come from the training distribution, and robots break that assumption all the time: new payloads, worn parts, other floors, other weather. Under covariate shift, where the input distribution changes but the conditional p(y | x) does not, reweighting the training loss by the density ratio ptest(x)/ptrain(x) corrects the bias [87,88]. More often the answer is to detect the shift, using the anomaly detectors in Section 6 or the predictive uncertainty in Section 7, and to fall back to safer behaviour when it occurs.

5 Unsupervised and self-supervised learning

Finding structure without labels

Robots generate far more data than anyone will ever label: hours of joint telemetry, camera streams, lidar sweeps and force traces. Unsupervised learning looks for structure in these data, such as clusters, low-dimensional subspaces or densities, without labels [4,5]. The main tools are old:

  • Principal component analysis (PCA) finds the orthogonal directions of maximum variance. It was introduced by Pearson in 1901 and developed by Hotelling in 1933 [89,90]. PCA compresses correlated sensor channels, removes noise and underlies the PCA-based monitoring statistics in Section 6.
  • k-means clustering alternates between assigning points to the nearest centroid and moving each centroid to the mean of its points. This is Lloyd's algorithm, written in 1957 and published in 1982; MacQueen named the method in 1967 [91,92]. It converges only to a local optimum, and the k-means++ seeding rule gives a provable approximation guarantee and better results in practice [93].
  • Gaussian mixture models fitted by the expectation-maximisation (EM) algorithm give soft assignments and a separate covariance for each cluster. EM alternates between computing the posterior responsibility of each component for each point (E-step) and re-estimating the parameters (M-step) [55]. The number of components can be chosen with the Bayesian information criterion, BIC = −2 log L + p log n [94].
  • Density-based and graph-based clustering. DBSCAN groups dense regions and labels isolated points as noise, which makes it a common choice for segmenting lidar point clouds into objects [95]. Spectral clustering partitions a similarity graph [96].
  • Nonlinear dimensionality reduction with self-organising maps, Isomap, t-SNE and autoencoders finds curved low-dimensional structure for visualisation and compression [97–100].

Uses in robotics include discovering operating modes and gaits in telemetry, segmenting demonstrations into motion primitives, compressing high-dimensional sensor data, and building visual vocabularies for place recognition. In learning from demonstration, Calinon and colleagues encoded demonstrated trajectories as Gaussian mixtures and reproduced them with Gaussian mixture regression, generalising a task from a handful of demonstrations [101]. Self-supervised learning: the robot labels its own data

A robot is in an unusual position for a learning system: it can often produce its own labels. In self-supervised learning the supervisory signal comes from the data itself, or from another sensor, another time step or the outcome of the robot's own action, instead of a human annotator. Robotics used this idea early:

  • Near-to-far learning for off-road driving. On Stanley, laser scanners labelled the nearby ground as drivable or not, and a vision model trained online on those labels extended the road estimate far beyond laser range. That allowed higher speeds [49,50]. Stavens and Thrun learned to predict terrain roughness from the vehicle's own shock measurements [102]. In the DARPA LAGR programme, several teams learned long-range traversability from stereo-labelled near-field data [103,104].
  • Learning from the robot's own trials. Pinto and Gupta collected 50,000 grasp attempts in 700 robot hours. Each attempt was labelled automatically as success or failure by checking whether the gripper still held an object [105]. Levine and colleagues scaled this to about 800,000 grasp attempts across up to 14 robots [106].
  • Cross-modal and temporal prediction. Time-contrastive networks learn viewpoint-invariant representations by pulling together simultaneous frames from different cameras and pushing apart frames from different times [107]. Lee and colleagues learned joint vision-and-touch representations by predicting whether contact would occur and whether the two streams were time-aligned, then used them for peg insertion [108].

Outside robotics, self-supervised learning became the main way to pre-train visual representations. Contrastive methods such as SimCLR and MoCo learn embeddings that are invariant to augmentations of the same image and that separate different images [109,110]. Masked methods such as MAE reconstruct hidden image patches [111]. Self-distillation methods such as DINOv2 produce general-purpose features without any labels [112]. Robot systems often freeze such encoders and train only a small policy or classifier on top. R3M, for example, pre-trained a representation on human videos and then learned manipulation tasks from a few demonstrations [113]. Balestriero and colleagues survey the design choices of these methods [114]. The learned latent models in the world models article are also trained self-supervised, by predicting the future.

6 Classification, regression and anomaly detection

Most learning problems in a robot reduce to three tasks. Classification predicts a discrete label: terrain type, grasp success, contact or no contact, human activity. Regression predicts a continuous quantity: torque, slip velocity, distance, remaining battery life. Anomaly detection flags inputs that do not look like normal operation, usually without examples of every way things can go wrong.

Classifiers

The classical classifiers differ mainly in the shape of decision boundary they can represent and in what they assume about the data [4–6]:

  • Linear discriminants and logistic regression. Fisher's linear discriminant (1936) projects the data onto the direction that best separates the classes [115]. Logistic regression models p(y = 1 | x) = σ(wᵀx + b), is fitted by maximum likelihood, and gives probabilistic outputs [116]. The multiclass version uses the softmax function.
  • Naive Bayes assumes that features are independent given the class. It is often accurate for zero-one classification even when that assumption is false [117].
  • k-nearest neighbours predicts the majority label among the k closest training points. As the training set grows, the 1-NN error is at most twice the Bayes error [118]. The method needs good feature scaling and degrades in high dimensions.
  • Support vector machines find the maximum-margin separating hyperplane. With kernels they produce nonlinear boundaries, and the soft-margin form tolerates overlapping classes [43,44,119].
  • Decision trees split the feature space recursively, choosing at each node the threshold that most reduces impurity, for example the Gini index in CART or information gain in ID3 [33,34]. Trees are readable and handle mixed feature types, but a single tree has high variance.
  • Ensembles. Bagging averages trees trained on bootstrap samples [47]. Random forests also choose a random subset of features at each split, which decorrelates the trees [48]. Boosting fits models sequentially to the examples or residuals that earlier models got wrong. Examples are AdaBoost, gradient boosting and XGBoost [45,46,120].

Large empirical comparisons favour ensembles of trees and kernel SVMs. Fernández-Delgado and colleagues evaluated 179 classifiers on 121 datasets and found random forests best on average, followed by SVMs with Gaussian kernels [10]. Caruana and Niculescu-Mizil found boosted trees and random forests at the top across several metrics [121]. On medium-sized tabular data, tree-based models still outperform deep networks [9]. Robotics has its own examples:

  • Terrain classification from wheel vibration on planetary rovers [122,123].
  • Grasp stability predicted from tactile and joint data [124].
  • Kinect body-part recognition. Randomised decision forests trained on large numbers of synthetic depth images label every pixel with a body part in real time [57].

Evaluating classifiers honestly

Accuracy hides a lot when classes are imbalanced, which is the normal case for failures, collisions and other rare events.

  • Curves instead of single numbers. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves trade true-positive rate against false-positive rate across thresholds [125]. Precision-recall curves are more informative when positives are rare; one curve dominates another in ROC space exactly when it dominates in precision-recall space [126].
  • Rebalancing the training data. Oversampling methods such as SMOTE synthesise extra minority-class examples [127].
  • Calibrating the scores. SVMs and boosted trees produce distorted scores that need Platt scaling or isotonic regression before they can be read as probabilities [128]. Modern neural networks tend to be overconfident [129].
  • Guaranteed coverage. Conformal prediction wraps any model and gives prediction sets with a guaranteed coverage rate, assuming only that the data are exchangeable [130].

For robot data, the most common evaluation error is leakage. Consecutive samples from one run are nearly identical, so a random split puts near-copies of test samples into the training set. Splits should be made by run, by robot, by day or by site [131].

Regression

Regression uses the same families with continuous outputs: linear and polynomial models, kernel ridge regression, k-NN averaging, regression trees and boosted trees, locally weighted regression and Gaussian processes [5,56,79]. Section 4 compared three of them on inverse dynamics. Two points matter especially for robots. First, noise is often heteroscedastic: a range sensor's error grows with distance, and friction is noisier near zero velocity. A model that predicts its own variance is more useful than one that predicts only a mean. Second, regression outputs feed controllers and planners, so the error has to be small and also smooth and bounded, because a jumpy torque model makes a jumpy controller. Gaussian processes (Section 7) handle both points naturally, at a computational cost.

Anomaly detection and fault detection

Chandola and colleagues distinguish point anomalies (a single abnormal reading), contextual anomalies (normal in general but abnormal here, such as high motor current while the robot is stationary) and collective anomalies (a sequence that is abnormal as a whole) [132]. In robotics, anomaly detection protects hardware, detects collisions, catches perception failures and triggers safe stops [133]. The classical toolbox is broad:

  • Statistical models of normal data. Fit a Gaussian and score new points by Mahalanobis distance (x − μ)ᵀΣ−1(x − μ) [134]. Alternatively, fit PCA and use the squared prediction error (SPE, or Q statistic) of the residual outside the principal subspace, with control limits from Jackson and Mudholkar [135].
  • Sequential change detection. CUSUM accumulates small deviations to detect a persistent drift quickly [136].
  • Geometric and density methods. The local outlier factor compares a point's density with that of its neighbours [137]. One-class SVMs learn a boundary around normal data [138]. Isolation forests use the observation that anomalies are isolated by fewer random axis-aligned splits [139].
  • Model-based residuals. Fault-diagnosis engineering compares measurements with a physical model through observers, parity equations or parameter estimation, and analyses the residual [140]. Collision detection on torque-controlled arms, for example, uses a momentum observer whose residual estimates the external joint torque [141].
  • Learned reconstruction. Autoencoders and LSTM variational autoencoders flag inputs they reconstruct poorly. Park and colleagues used this approach to detect failures in robot-assisted feeding [142].

Benchmarks show that no detector is best on every dataset. The right choice depends on whether anomalies are global or local and on the structure of the normal data [143]. 7 Probabilistic models and Bayesian inference

Probability as the logic of uncertainty

Bayes' theorem, published after Thomas Bayes's death by Richard Price in 1763 [144], updates a prior belief about an unknown θ with the likelihood of observed data D:

p(θ | D) = p(D | θ) p(θ) / p(D),    p(D) = ∫ p(D | θ) p(θ) dθ

Jaynes argued that probability theory is the unique consistent extension of logic to reasoning under uncertainty [145]. Ghahramani described the probabilistic approach as a framework for representing and manipulating uncertainty in models and predictions, which is central to systems that act in the world [15]. Robots are the clearest case. Every sensor is noisy, much of the state is hidden, and actions have uncertain effects. Probabilistic robotics recasts localisation, mapping and control as inference over these unknowns [14]. Recursive Bayesian state estimation, including Kalman and particle filters and factor-graph smoothing, has its own article in this series, the sensor fusion article. This section covers the other probabilistic models a robot uses: Bayesian networks, hidden Markov models, Gaussian processes, Bayesian optimisation, and the approximate inference methods that make them practical.

Bayesian networks and graphical models

A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph whose nodes are random variables. Each node stores a conditional distribution given its parents, so the joint distribution factorises as p(x1, …, xn) = Πi p(xi | parents(xi)) [35,146]. The graph encodes conditional independences that make large models compact. Exact inference is possible by variable elimination or by the junction-tree algorithm of Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter, which was developed for medical expert systems [147]. Factor graphs and the sum-product algorithm generalise these message-passing schemes [148], and the same machinery underlies modern SLAM back ends [149]. When effects have several possible causes, a noisy-OR model gives each cause an independent chance of producing the effect, which keeps the number of parameters linear in the number of parents [35].

Bayesian networks took over the role of rule-based expert systems in diagnosis because they support reasoning in both directions. They can predict effects from causes and diagnose causes from effects, and they handle explaining away correctly [35,58]. The program below diagnoses a failed robot grasp. An abnormal force reading then raises the probability of a sensor fault from about 0.10 to 0.33, but a slippery object remains the leading explanation, because slipping also disturbs the force signal. Certainty factors cannot produce this kind of interacting, non-monotonic reasoning consistently [58]. Exact inference in general networks is intractable, which motivates the approximate methods described below [146].

Hidden Markov models

A hidden Markov model (HMM) is the simplest dynamic Bayesian network. A discrete hidden state evolves as a Markov chain, and each state emits an observation. Three algorithms answer the standard questions [150]:

  • Forward-backward computes the posterior probability of each state at each time step.
  • Viterbi finds the single most probable state sequence by dynamic programming [151].
  • Baum-Welch, an instance of EM, learns the parameters from unlabelled sequences [55,152].

HMMs dominated speech recognition for decades. In robotics, Hannaford and Lee used them in 1991 to segment force/torque signals from telemanipulation into subtasks [153], and they have since been used for contact-state estimation, gesture recognition and grasp-stability assessment [124]. Gaussian processes

A Gaussian process (GP) is a prior over functions: any finite set of function values is jointly Gaussian, with covariance given by a kernel k(x, x′). With Gaussian noise the posterior is available in closed form. The predictive mean is k*ᵀ(K + σn²I)−1y and the predictive variance is k(x*, x*) − k*ᵀ(K + σn²I)−1k* [56]. Kernel hyperparameters are chosen by maximising the log marginal likelihood. This "type-II maximum likelihood", or evidence, approach trades data fit against model complexity automatically [56,154].

GPs are popular in robotics because they are accurate with little data and know where they are uncertain. They have been used to learn inverse dynamics [81], as the transition and observation models inside Bayesian filters [155] and as the dynamics model in PILCO, which learned control policies in a few trials by propagating model uncertainty through long-horizon predictions [8]. Exact GPs cost O(n³) time in the number of training points. Sparse approximations based on a small set of inducing points reduce the cost [156]. Where data exist, the GP is accurate and its standard deviation matches the sensor noise. Inside the gap around zero velocity the true friction curve jumps steeply, which a smooth, stationary kernel with a single length-scale cannot represent. The GP is wrong there, at 0.1 rad/s, and confident at the same time, and the overall 95 % intervals cover only 87 % of test points. Bayesian uncertainty is only as good as the model's assumptions. A mis-specified kernel produces overconfident posteriors, which is why GP predictions in safety-relevant loops are often combined with physical structure or checked with empirical calibration methods such as conformal prediction [56,130].

Bayesian optimisation

The GP's ability to say where it is uncertain makes it the standard surrogate for Bayesian optimisation (BO). BO tunes an expensive black-box objective by repeatedly fitting a GP to the evaluations so far and choosing the next query to maximise an acquisition function, such as expected improvement or an upper confidence bound, that balances exploitation against exploration [157]. Each evaluation of a robot controller can take minutes of hardware time, and BO has been used to learn bipedal walking gaits with a small number of hardware trials [158]. SafeOpt adds a GP model of a safety constraint and evaluates only parameters that are safe with high probability, which allowed controller parameters to be tuned on a real quadrotor while avoiding unsafe parameters [159].

Approximate inference

Most posteriors have no closed form. Two families of approximation dominate:

  • Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) constructs a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior, then averages over its samples. The family runs from the Metropolis algorithm (1953) and Hastings's generalisation (1970) [160,161], through Gibbs sampling, which brought Bayesian computation into mainstream statistics [162], to Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the No-U-Turn sampler [163], the default in the probabilistic programming language Stan [164].
  • Variational inference (VI) turns inference into optimisation. It picks the member of a tractable family closest to the posterior in Kullback-Leibler divergence, by maximising the evidence lower bound [165,166]. VI is faster than MCMC and scales to large models, but it tends to underestimate posterior variance [166].

Particle filters are sequential Monte Carlo methods for dynamic models and are covered in the fusion article. Bayesian deep learning applies the same ideas to neural networks; Monte Carlo dropout, for example, is an approximate variational posterior [167]. Planning under uncertainty with partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs) is the decision-theoretic end point of the probabilistic approach: the robot plans over its beliefs rather than over states [14,168]. The world models article covers how learned models represent that uncertainty.

Choosing a method

From notebook to robot

Fitting the model is often the smallest part of a working system. Sculley and colleagues described the "hidden technical debt" of machine-learning systems: data dependencies, feedback loops, configuration sprawl and the glue code around the model [169]. Several practices matter particularly for robots:

  • Split data by run, robot, site or day, never at random across correlated samples, and fit preprocessing such as scaling, PCA and feature selection only on the training split, as all the programs above do [131].
  • Test under the conditions the robot will meet: new payloads, worn parts, other floors and lighting. Treat simulation-trained models with care, using domain randomisation or real-world fine-tuning to close the gap [88,170].
  • Monitor in operation. Track input distributions and model confidence, and route out-of-distribution inputs to conservative fallback behaviour.
  • Prefer simple, interpretable models where they perform as well, especially in high-stakes decisions [11]. The mature library implementations in scikit-learn make strong traditional baselines cheap to establish before reaching for a deep network [171].

Safety and regulation

Standards increasingly expect evidence about learned components:

  • ISO 21448 (SOTIF) addresses hazards from functional insufficiencies and performance limitations of the intended function, including perception, rather than from faults [12].
  • ISO/PAS 8800:2024 covers safety-related AI elements in road vehicles: data, training, verification and monitoring [172].
  • The EU Artificial Intelligence Act (Regulation 2024/1689) imposes requirements on high-risk AI systems, which include many safety components of machinery and vehicles. The requirements cover risk management, data governance, transparency, human oversight, accuracy and robustness [13].

Traditional methods help meet these expectations. Rules can be verified formally, as with the formal distance rules of Responsibility-Sensitive Safety [70]. Small models can be inspected, and probabilistic models expose calibrated uncertainty that a safety monitor can act on. A common architecture places a learned perception or policy component inside a rule-based or model-based safety envelope.

Open problems and where the field is going

Sutton's "bitter lesson" argues that general methods that scale with computation eventually beat methods built on human knowledge [173], and the history of perception largely bears it out [53]. Physical AI keeps several older questions open:

  • Data efficiency and extrapolation. Robots cannot always collect millions of examples. The inverse-dynamics example showed how much a little physical structure buys.
  • Calibrated uncertainty under distribution shift. The GP example showed that even Bayesian models can be confidently wrong when their assumptions fail. Combining probabilistic models, conformal methods and runtime monitoring is an active research area [130].
  • Neurosymbolic integration. Combining learned perception with symbolic reasoning and planning, the "third wave" of AI, is an open research programme [68,174]. Large language models have revived symbolic interfaces in robotics. SayCan uses a language model to propose steps and grounds them with learned value functions of pretrained skills [175]. Code as Policies has a language model write robot programs, effectively generating the rules and control code that engineers once wrote by hand [176].
  • Learning from the robot's own experience. Self-supervision, the robot's main advantage as a learner, is still underused, and making it robust to biased label sources is an open problem.

Deep learning, reinforcement learning and foundation models build on these foundations rather than replacing them. A practical physical AI system is a hybrid, and engineers need to know these traditional methods well to know when a deep network is warranted and when a decision tree, a Gaussian process or a handful of rules will do the job better.
