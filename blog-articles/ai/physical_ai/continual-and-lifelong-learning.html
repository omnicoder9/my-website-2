1 Why physical AI needs continual learning

Most learned robot capabilities today are produced in a fixed pipeline: collect a dataset or run a simulator, train a model, freeze it and deploy it. The world the model meets afterwards does not stay fixed. Objects, rooms and users change; tools are swapped; joints wear, gearboxes loosen and batteries age; a household robot is asked for chores nobody demonstrated; a fleet encounters weather, lighting and terrain that were rare in its training data [1–3]. Continual learning (also called lifelong, incremental or never-ending learning) is the study of systems that keep learning from such a non-stationary stream of experience, accumulating and refining knowledge over time instead of being retrained from scratch [4–7].

The central obstacle has a name. When a neural network trained on one task is trained further on another, gradient descent changes the same shared weights that encoded the first task, and performance on it can collapse: catastrophic forgetting, or catastrophic interference [8–10]. Avoiding forgetting by freezing the network is easy, but then nothing new is learned. The tension between the two, between keeping old knowledge stable and staying plastic enough to learn new knowledge, is the stability–plasticity dilemma, a term from Grossberg's work on adaptive resonance theory that now frames the whole field [11–13]. Brains face the same dilemma and appear to solve it with several mechanisms at once: synapses whose strength is consolidated at different rates, a fast-learning hippocampus that replays experience to a slow-learning neocortex, and neuromodulation that gates when learning happens [14–17].

For physical AI the problem has particular features (Figure 1) [1,2]:

  • The data are a correlated stream, not a shuffled dataset. A robot spends hours in one room or on one task, so consecutive samples are highly correlated and its training distribution drifts with whatever it happens to be doing. Learning "online" on such data is the regime in which forgetting is worst [1,18].
  • Task boundaries are rarely announced. A change of payload, floor surface or user is often discovered only through rising prediction error, so methods that need a task label at training or test time do not always apply [19,20].
  • Experience is expensive and memory is finite. Real robot data cost time, wear and supervision; onboard storage and compute are limited; and privacy or bandwidth can prevent sending everything to the cloud [21–23].
  • Forgetting can be dangerous. A robot that learns a new grasp and silently loses a safety-relevant behavior, such as a careful placement or a stop reaction, is worse than one that never learned. Updates to a deployed system therefore need regression testing and, increasingly, regulatory approval [24–26].
  • Change happens on several time scales. Some changes call for adaptation within seconds (a gust of wind, a heavy box), some over days (wear, a new kitchen) and some over months (new skills, new product lines). Different mechanisms suit each, from adaptive control and online parameter estimation to fine-tuning a foundation model [27–29].

This article concentrates on the learning methods themselves: how a model improves from experience while retaining earlier capabilities. Several neighbouring topics are covered elsewhere in the series and are linked rather than repeated: online parameter estimation and adaptive control in classical and modern control; sim-to-real transfer and offline RL in reinforcement learning; learning from human corrections in imitation learning; parameter-efficient adaptation of large models in foundation models for physical AI; and the basics of supervised learning and model evaluation in traditional AI and machine learning. Loops that search for failures and generate test scenarios before deployment are touched on only briefly in Section 10.

1.1 Terminology

Usage varies, but the following distinctions are common [4,7,20,30]. Continual or lifelong learning denotes learning from a sequence of tasks or a drifting stream while retaining and reusing earlier knowledge; "lifelong" often emphasizes transfer between many tasks over an agent's life, "continual" the avoidance of forgetting. Online learning processes each sample once, usually in a single pass. Incremental learning adds classes, tasks or data over time. Concept drift is the statistics literature's term for a changing data distribution, where the usual goal is to track the present rather than to remember the past. Transfer and multitask learning reuse knowledge across tasks that are available together, and meta-learning learns how to adapt quickly. Adaptation in robotics often means updating a small set of parameters to the current condition, which may or may not be retained afterwards. A complete continual learner combines several of these.

2 A short history: from catastrophic interference to lifelong robot learning

2.1 Connectionism discovers forgetting (1980s–1990s)

Interest in the problem began with cognitive models. McCloskey and Cohen trained a backpropagation network on arithmetic facts, first the "ones" problems and then the "twos", and found that learning the second set almost completely erased the first, whereas people learning the same material forget gradually; they called the effect catastrophic interference [8]. Ratcliff reached the same conclusion for connectionist models of recognition memory [9]. Grossberg had already identified the underlying tension: a learning system must remain plastic for new inputs yet stable for old ones, and his adaptive resonance theory (ART) networks addressed it by creating a new category node only when an input did not resonate with an existing one closely enough, leaving established categories untouched [11,12].

The 1990s brought the first remedies that are still in use in modern form. Robins showed that interleaving old items with new ones (rehearsal) prevents forgetting, and that when the old data are gone, pseudorehearsal on items generated by feeding random inputs through the old network works almost as well, an ancestor of generative replay [31]. French reviewed the field and traced forgetting to overlapping, distributed representations, proposing that sparser, less overlapping codes reduce it [10]. McClelland, McNaughton and O'Reilly gave the problem a neuroscientific interpretation: the brain has complementary learning systems, a hippocampus that learns individual episodes quickly and a neocortex that learns structure slowly by interleaved replay of those episodes, so that new knowledge is integrated without overwriting the old [14]. Recordings showing that hippocampal place-cell sequences experienced while awake are reactivated during subsequent sleep supported the replay idea [32].

2.2 Lifelong robot learning (1990s)

Robotics contributed the term "lifelong learning" itself. Thrun and Mitchell argued in 1995 that a robot facing a long sequence of related tasks should not learn each from scratch, and that knowledge which is task-independent, such as a model of the effects of the robot's own actions, should be learned once and reused to make every later task cheaper to learn [2]. Thrun's explanation-based neural network (EBNN) learning used such previously learned action models to provide slopes (derivatives) that guide learning of a new target function from few examples [33,34]. At the same time, Caruana showed that tasks learned together in a shared representation help one another (multitask learning) [35], and Ring's CHILD system, one of the first to use the phrase continual learning, built up a hierarchy of behaviours in reinforcement-learning agents incrementally, each level reusing those below it [36].

Robot model learning met the same problem in a different guise. A global function approximator trained online on whatever region of state space the robot currently visits forgets the regions it visited earlier. Schaal and Atkeson's receptive-field weighted regression and its successor, locally weighted projection regression (LWPR), avoided this by learning many local linear models, each updated only by data in its own receptive field, so that learning in one region cannot disturb another; LWPR learned inverse dynamics models of anthropomorphic arms incrementally in real time [37–39]. Local Gaussian process models later followed the same principle [40,41]. Locality, replay and stable representations are therefore old ideas in robot learning.

2.3 Never-ending learning and deep networks (2010s)

The 2010s turned lifelong learning into a mainstream research area. The Never-Ending Language Learner (NELL) read the web continuously from 2010, extending its knowledge base and using what it had learned to improve how it learned [42], and lifelong machine learning was consolidated as a field with its own textbook [7]. Learning many tasks with a shared latent basis, as in ELLA and its policy-gradient version PG-ELLA, made lifelong transfer computationally efficient for both supervised learning and control [43,44].

Deep learning made forgetting acute again. Goodfellow and colleagues measured catastrophic forgetting in modern networks and found that dropout training helped [45]. Deep reinforcement learning had already relied on replaying stored transitions to break temporal correlations [46,47]. In 2016–2017 a cluster of influential methods appeared: progressive neural networks, which add a new column per task [48]; elastic weight consolidation (EWC), which slows learning on weights important for earlier tasks [49]; synaptic intelligence [50]; learning without forgetting, based on distillation [51]; gradient episodic memory, which also introduced the standard metrics for the field [52]; iCaRL for class-incremental learning [53]; and deep generative replay [54]. Progressive networks were soon used to transfer robot manipulation skills learned in simulation to a real arm without forgetting the simulated ones [55]. Reviews in 2019–2022 organized the field into regularization, replay and architectural families and showed that the evaluation scenario matters greatly [1,4,20,56].

2.4 Plasticity, adaptation and foundation models (2020s)

Three developments shaped the current decade. First, attention turned to the plasticity side of the dilemma: networks that are trained for a long time on changing data gradually lose the ability to learn at all, a phenomenon demonstrated across supervised and reinforcement learning and addressed by continually re-initializing little-used units [57–59]. Second, legged robots and drones showed that fast adaptation to the current condition, through learned adaptation modules or online updates of a small set of coefficients, can be separated from slow learning of the underlying skill [27,28]. Third, large pretrained models changed the question from "how do we learn task after task from scratch" to "how do we keep extending a generalist model without damaging it", with parameter-efficient adapters, data co-training and weight interpolation as the main tools [60–63]. Benchmarks such as Continual World and LIBERO brought these questions to robot manipulation [64,65], and research programmes on lifelong learning machines asked what biology still has to teach [16,66].

3 Problem settings, metrics and why networks forget

3.1 The continual learning problem

Formally, a continual learner receives a sequence of data distributions D1, D2, …, DT, either as explicit tasks or as a stream whose distribution drifts. At each stage it may update its parameters θ using the current data and whatever it has chosen to keep, such as a bounded memory of past samples, a copy of an earlier model or importance estimates. The goal is to perform well on everything seen so far, and ideally on what comes next:

minimize over θ:   Σt=1…T E(x,y)~Dt [ ℓ(f(x; θ), y) ]   subject to bounded memory and compute

If all data could be stored and the model retrained on their union at every stage, the problem would reduce to ordinary multitask learning, which is why joint training is the usual upper bound in experiments [4,52,56]. Knoblauch and colleagues showed that this is not merely a convenient baseline: in general, an optimal continual learner must be able to reconstruct its past data perfectly, and deciding what to keep is NP-hard, so every practical method trades some optimality for memory and computation [67].

Van de Ven, Tuytelaars and Tolias distinguished three scenarios that differ in what the model must infer at test time, and showed that methods ranked very differently across them [20]:

  • Task-incremental: the task identity is given at test time, so separate output heads or modules can be used. For a robot, this corresponds to knowing which tool is attached or which skill has been invoked.
  • Domain-incremental: the task structure stays the same but the input distribution changes, and the identity of the domain is not given. Examples are a grasp detector moving between kitchens, a dynamics model meeting new terrain, or a policy facing new lighting.
  • Class-incremental: new classes are added and the model must discriminate among all classes seen so far without being told which stage they came from, as when a robot learns new object categories one at a time.

Physical systems add the task-free or online setting, in which no boundaries are announced and samples arrive one at a time, often highly correlated [1,19]; continual reinforcement learning, in which the agent's own actions determine which data it sees [18,68]; and drift in the robot itself, which overlaps with the statistics of concept drift [30].

3.2 Measuring forgetting and transfer

Most metrics derive from a matrix R whose entry Ri,j is the performance on task j after training on tasks 1…i. Lopez-Paz and Ranzato defined three summaries that remain standard [52]:

  • Average performance after the last task, ACC = (1/T) Σj RT,j.
  • Backward transfer, BWT = 1/(T−1) Σj<T (RT,j − Rj,j): how much learning later tasks changed earlier ones. For accuracy, negative BWT is forgetting; positive BWT means later learning improved earlier tasks.
  • Forward transfer, FWT = 1/(T−1) Σj>1 (Rj−1,j − bj): how much earlier learning helps a task before it is trained, relative to a reference bj such as a randomly initialized model.

Chaudhry and colleagues complemented these with forgetting (the drop from the best performance ever achieved on a task) and intransigence (the inability to learn a new task, compared with a model trained on it alone), which measure the two horns of the dilemma separately [69]. Díaz-Rodríguez and colleagues argued for also reporting memory size, computational cost and sample efficiency, since a method that stores everything trivially avoids forgetting [21], and the DARPA Lifelong Learning Machines programme proposed a domain-agnostic set including performance maintenance, forward and backward transfer, performance relative to a single-task expert, and sample efficiency [66]. For robots, these are best computed on closed-loop task success with confidence intervals rather than on offline loss, for the reasons discussed in the evaluation section of the reinforcement learning article [70,71].

3.3 Why networks forget

Forgetting is a direct consequence of how neural networks learn. All tasks share the same parameters, and a gradient step that lowers the loss on the current task changes the network's function everywhere, not only near the current data. If the gradient of the new task's loss has a negative inner product with that of an old task's loss, the step increases the old loss: the tasks interfere [10,52,72]. Representations drift too: Ramasesh and colleagues found that forgetting is concentrated in the deeper layers, whose features are reorganized for the new task, while early layers change little [73]. Training choices matter: dropout, a large initial learning rate with decay and small batches lead to wider minima in which the old tasks' loss is less sensitive to parameter changes, and reduce forgetting [45,74]. Scale helps as well; larger pretrained models forget less when fine-tuned on sequences of tasks [75], although large language models fine-tuned on a sequence of instruction tasks still forget noticeably [76].

4 Protecting what matters: regularization methods

4.1 Elastic weight consolidation

Regularization methods keep a single network and add a penalty that discourages changing what earlier tasks rely on. The simplest is an L2 penalty that ties all weights to their values after the previous task, but this treats every weight as equally important and quickly stops the network from learning anything new. Kirkpatrick and colleagues' elastic weight consolidation (EWC) instead weights the penalty by an estimate of each parameter's importance [49]. Its motivation is Bayesian. After task A, the posterior over parameters p(θ | DA) summarizes everything learned; learning task B should maximize log p(DB | θ) + log p(θ | DA). Approximating the old posterior by a Gaussian centred on the old solution θ*A (a Laplace approximation) with a diagonal precision given by the Fisher information F gives the loss

L(θ) = LB(θ) + (λ/2) Σi Fi (θi − θ*A,i)2,   Fi = E[ (∂ log p(y | x, θ) / ∂θi)2 ]

Weights to which the old task's predictions are sensitive become stiff; the rest stay free. EWC let one network learn several Atari games in sequence and was explicitly inspired by evidence that synapses involved in a learned skill are protected from later change: in mice, a small fraction of the dendritic spines formed during motor learning are stably maintained long afterwards, providing a possible structural basis for lifelong memories [49,77]. With many tasks, the penalties should be combined into a single running quadratic rather than kept separately, as Huszár pointed out; this online EWC is used in the progress-and-compress framework, which learns each new task in an active network and then distills it into a knowledge base protected by online EWC [78,79]. Better posterior approximations, such as Kronecker-factored Laplace approximations or variational continual learning, in which each task's approximate posterior becomes the prior for the next, follow the same principle more accurately [80,81].

4.2 Other importance measures and functional regularization

Importance can be estimated without the Fisher. Synaptic intelligence (SI) accumulates, for every parameter, its contribution to the decrease of the loss along the whole training trajectory, an online quantity that each "synapse" can compute locally [50]. Memory aware synapses (MAS) measures the sensitivity of the network's output, not its loss, to each parameter, so importance can be estimated on unlabelled data from the robot's own environment [82]. Biologically motivated variants model each synapse as a chain of variables consolidating at different rates, which in reinforcement learning reduced forgetting without task boundaries [83,84].

Instead of anchoring parameters, functional regularization anchors the network's outputs. Learning without forgetting (LwF) records the old model's outputs on the new task's inputs and adds a knowledge-distillation loss that keeps them unchanged while the new task is learned [51,85]. Its weakness is that it protects behaviour only where the new data are; if old and new inputs differ, as when a robot moves to a new workspace, the constraint says little about the old region. A third family constrains the direction of updates: orthogonal gradient descent projects new-task gradients away from the gradients of earlier tasks' outputs, and orthogonal weight modification and gradient projection memory restrict updates to the subspace orthogonal to the input or activation subspace that earlier tasks used [86–88].

5 Remembering by rehearsal: replay and episodic memory

5.1 Experience replay

The most reliable way to avoid forgetting old data is to keep training on some of it. Replay or rehearsal methods store a small episodic memory of past samples and interleave them with new data, in the spirit of the complementary learning systems account, in which the hippocampus replays recent episodes to the neocortex [14,31,90]. Replay has deep roots in robotics and reinforcement learning: Lin introduced experience replay in 1992 for reactive agents learning from their own interaction, and replay buffers are part of every off-policy deep RL algorithm [46,47]. In continual learning it is consistently among the strongest baselines. Chaudhry and colleagues found that even a tiny episodic memory, as little as one example per class, replayed alongside new data, beats many more elaborate methods [91]; GDumb, which does nothing but fill a class-balanced memory greedily and retrain on it from scratch, matched or outperformed many published methods [92]; and under realistic computation budgets, simple replay has proved hard to beat [22]. In reinforcement learning, CLEAR combined replay of old experience with behaviour cloning of the policy's past actions and greatly reduced forgetting across sequences of tasks [93].

What to store matters when memory is small. Reservoir sampling keeps a uniform random sample of an unbounded stream without knowing its length: the n-th item replaces a random slot with probability M/n, so every item seen so far is in the memory with equal probability [94]. Alternatives choose samples that maximize the diversity of their gradients [95], that cover the state space or match the distribution of past experience in lifelong RL [96], or, for classification, that are close to each class mean, as in iCaRL's herding [53]. Storing the network's own outputs (logits) with the inputs and replaying both, as in dark experience replay, adds a distillation signal at almost no cost [97].

5.2 Using memory as a constraint

Instead of adding old samples to the loss, gradient episodic memory (GEM) uses them to constrain each update: it solves a small quadratic program so that the step does not increase the loss on any earlier task's memory [52]. A-GEM simplifies this to a single constraint on the average memory gradient gm: if the proposed gradient g conflicts with it (g·gm < 0), g is projected onto the half-space where it does not, g ← g − (g·gm / gm·gm) gm [98]. Meta-experience replay goes further and uses a meta-learning objective that rewards gradient alignment between old and new samples, encouraging transfer as well as preventing interference [72].

5.3 Generative and latent replay

When raw data cannot be kept, because of privacy, storage or the sheer volume of camera streams, a generative model can produce pseudo-samples of the past instead. Deep generative replay trains a generator alongside the task network and rehearses its samples, labelled by the previous network [54]; it is the deep-learning descendant of pseudorehearsal [31]. Generating raw images or point clouds well enough is hard, so brain-inspired replay rehearses internal representations rather than inputs, generated by a model fed back from the hidden layers and gated by context, which scaled generative replay to harder class-incremental benchmarks [99]. The generative models used for scene and trajectory synthesis, described in the generative and predictive models article, are natural candidates for such replay in robotics; CRIL, for example, uses a generative model and a predictive model to replay demonstrations of earlier manipulation tasks in continual imitation learning [100].

6 Giving each skill its own room: architectural methods

6.1 Isolating parameters

A third family avoids interference by construction: different tasks use different parameters. Progressive neural networks add a new column of layers for every task, freeze the earlier columns and connect them laterally to the new one, so that old features can be reused but never overwritten [48]. Rusu and colleagues used them to transfer a manipulation policy learned in simulation to a real robot arm: the real-world column reused the simulated one's features and learned faster than training from scratch, while the simulated skill remained intact [55]. The cost is growth: parameters increase with every task. PackNet fits many tasks into one fixed network by iterative pruning: after a task is learned, its least important weights are released, the remaining ones are frozen for that task, and the next task trains only the released weights [101]. Hard attention to the task (HAT) learns, for every task, nearly binary masks over the units of each layer and blocks gradients to units that earlier tasks rely on [102]. PathNet uses evolution to select which modules of a large network each task uses [103]; dynamically expandable networks grow only when the existing capacity is insufficient [104]; and supermasks in superposition store a binary mask per task over a fixed random network and can even infer which mask to use from the network's output entropy, scaling to thousands of tasks [105].

6.2 Experts, adapters and prompts

Mixtures of experts, introduced by Jacobs, Jordan, Nowlan and Hinton, divide a problem among specialist networks selected by a gating network [106]. Expert Gate applies the idea to lifelong learning, training one expert per task and an autoencoder per task that decides which expert should handle a new input [107]. In the era of pretrained models, isolation is usually achieved with small task-specific modules attached to a frozen backbone: adapters inserted between layers [108], low-rank adaptation (LoRA), which adds a trainable low-rank matrix to each frozen weight matrix [109], and learned prompts selected from a pool, as in learning to prompt [110]. TAIL applied task-specific adapters to continual imitation learning with a large pretrained robot policy on LIBERO, found LoRA the most effective adapter type and avoided forgetting while training only a small fraction of the parameters [63,65].

A related line of work represents tasks as compositions of reusable modules or a shared basis. ELLA represents each task's model as a sparse combination of shared latent components that are refined as tasks arrive [43], PG-ELLA extended it to policy-gradient control [44], and modular lifelong RL composes neural modules for new tasks and adds modules only when needed [111,112]. Skill libraries in robotics follow the same idea at a coarser grain (Section 9.2).

Architectural methods are therefore attractive where tasks are discrete, known and important enough to protect absolutely, for example certified skills in an industrial cell, and less so where change is gradual and unlabelled [20,56].

7 The other half of the dilemma: loss of plasticity

7.1 Networks that stop learning

Most continual-learning research has concentrated on stability. A robot that is meant to learn for years also needs the opposite property: it must still be able to learn the thousandth task as well as the first. Deep networks trained for a long time on changing data turn out to lose this ability. Ash and Adams observed that a network warm-started from a solution on part of a dataset generalizes worse after training on all of it than a freshly initialized one, and proposed shrink and perturb, scaling the weights down and adding a little noise before further training [113]. In deep RL, Nikishin and colleagues described a primacy bias: agents overfit to their earliest experience and learn poorly from later data, which periodically resetting the last layers fixes [59]; Sokar and colleagues found a growing fraction of dormant neurons and proposed recycling them [114]; and Lyle and colleagues linked plasticity loss to the changing curvature of the loss landscape rather than to any single cause such as weight norm [58].

Dohare and colleagues showed in 2024 how general the phenomenon is [57]. On long sequences of tasks built from ImageNet classes, and in reinforcement learning on a simulated legged robot whose ground friction changed periodically, standard deep-learning methods gradually lost the ability to learn, eventually performing no better than a linear model or a shallow network on new tasks. The degradation came with growing weights, more dead units and less diverse representations. Their remedy, continual backpropagation, keeps re-initializing a very small fraction of the hidden units that contribute least, so the network permanently retains some of the variability it had at initialization, and it maintained plasticity indefinitely in their experiments [57]. Utility-based perturbed gradient descent combines both halves of the dilemma, protecting useful units from change and perturbing the less useful ones [115].

These results connect to a broader view of continual reinforcement learning, in which the right objective is not to converge but to keep tracking. Sutton, Koop and Silver showed that even in stationary environments, a learner that keeps adapting can outperform one that converges, when its representation cannot capture the environment exactly [116]; Abel and colleagues formalized continual RL as the setting in which the best agents never stop learning [68]; and the Alberta Plan places continual learning at the centre of a research programme for agents that learn from a lifetime of interaction [18,117].

8 Fast adaptation without overwriting

8.1 Two time scales

Much of what a deployed robot must cope with is not a new skill but a new condition: a heavier payload, a different floor, wind, a worn joint. Control engineering has long handled such changes with adaptive control and online parameter estimation, which re-estimate a small set of physically meaningful parameters continuously, as described in the adaptive control section of the control article and the digital twin section of the representations article. Neuroscience offers a similar picture of motor learning, in which internal models of the body and of tools are adapted continually and the cerebellum may hold several such models for different contexts [118,119]. Learning-based robotics has converged on the same separation of time scales: a slowly learned, general representation and a quickly adapted, low-dimensional part.

Rapid motor adaptation (RMA) trains a legged-robot policy in simulation together with an adaptation module that infers a compact vector of environment properties (friction, payload, terrain) from the recent history of states and actions; on the real robot the module updates this vector within a fraction of a second, without changing any network weights [28]. Neural-Fly learns, offline and from flight data in different winds, a basis of features shared by all wind conditions, and adapts only the linear coefficients on that basis online, with a composite adaptation law; this let a quadrotor track trajectories precisely in strong, changing winds [27]. Meta-learning makes the same split explicit: model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML) learns initial weights from which a few gradient steps adapt to a new task [120], and meta-learned dynamics models let a legged millirobot adapt online to a missing leg, a slope or a payload [121].

8.2 Remembering contexts

Fast adaptation alone does not accumulate knowledge: if the coefficients are re-estimated each time conditions change, a condition seen yesterday must be re-learned today, and the transient errors recur. A recursive estimator with a forgetting factor is in effect a continual learner tuned for maximal plasticity [30]. The remedy is to keep what was adapted. Nagabandi, Finn and Levine's MOLe maintains a mixture of dynamics models, instantiates a new one when none explains the current data (using a Chinese restaurant process prior) and recalls old ones when their conditions recur [122]. Task-free continual learning methods similarly detect changes from the loss and consolidate when it plateaus [19]. The change-detection part is classical statistics: cumulative-sum tests and Bayesian online change-point detection decide when the data stop fitting the current model [123,124].

The single estimator never stops paying for change. Its error in steps 16–100 of a segment is 1.5–1.9, about 40 times the noise level, and it is no better for a returning condition than for a new one: nothing learned earlier is reused, and after the first segment its covariance has shrunk, so it trusts outdated coefficients and re-adapts slowly. Two design choices carry most of the benefit. Updates are committed with a delay, so the samples that reveal a change never corrupt the head of the previous condition; and old heads are frozen rather than slowly forgotten. Real systems must also handle conditions that change gradually rather than abruptly, and the memory needed to store many heads, but the principle of separating fast inference of the context from slow learning of the shared representation is the same one used in RMA, Neural-Fly and MOLe [27,28,122].

9 Lifelong learning on robots and robot foundation models

9.1 Continual reinforcement learning on real robots

Robot reinforcement learning has largely been practised as "train, then deploy", but several studies show what learning that never stops can achieve. Julian and colleagues took a vision-based grasping policy pretrained with QT-Opt on hundreds of thousands of grasps by a fleet of robots [125] and fine-tuned it, off-policy, to a series of changes such as new lighting, backgrounds, transparent objects and an extended gripper; each adaptation needed only a few hundred to about a thousand new grasp attempts, a small fraction of the original data, and the policy could be fine-tuned repeatedly as conditions kept changing [29]. Smith and colleagues pretrained quadruped locomotion in simulation and then let the robot keep learning in the real world, with an autonomous recovery policy, on surfaces such as grass and memory foam [126], and later showed that a quadruped can learn to walk from scratch in about 20 minutes of real-world model-free RL [127]. MT-Opt learned a dozen manipulation tasks on a fleet of robots, sharing data across tasks and adding new tasks over time by bootstrapping from the experience collected for earlier ones [128]. Xie and Finn kept the experience of all earlier tasks and selectively reused it to speed up learning of each new manipulation task [129], an instance of the replay principle of Section 5. Model-based methods such as DayDreamer, which learn a world model online and train behaviour in imagination, are also continual learners in this sense [130,131].

Benchmarks have exposed the difficulties. Continual World chains ten or twenty robotic manipulation tasks from Meta-World and found that, while several methods limit forgetting, few achieve positive forward transfer, the ability to learn later tasks faster [64,132]. Wołczyk and colleagues identified a subtler problem in fine-tuning pretrained RL agents: the agent forgets pretrained capabilities in the parts of the state space it does not visit early in fine-tuning, and must later relearn them; knowledge-retention techniques such as behavioural cloning on old data, replay or EWC recover most of the benefit of pretraining [133]. For physical systems, continual RL must also respect the safety constraints of the safe RL section of the reinforcement learning article: a learner that explores after deployment needs a safety filter that is not itself being relearned [24,134].

9.2 Continual imitation and skill libraries

For manipulation, much new learning comes from demonstrations and corrections. LIBERO provides 130 language-conditioned manipulation tasks organized into suites that isolate the transfer of spatial, object and goal knowledge, specifically to study lifelong learning in decision making; among its findings were that sequential fine-tuning can outperform dedicated continual-learning methods in forward transfer, that no single architecture or algorithm is best across suites, and that naive pretraining can hurt later lifelong learning [65]. On LIBERO and similar benchmarks, task-specific adapters on a large pretrained policy (TAIL) avoid forgetting entirely while training few parameters [63], generative replay of earlier demonstrations reduces forgetting without storing them [100], and LOTUS discovers recurring skills in new demonstrations without supervision, adds them to a growing skill library and trains a meta-controller to compose them, which improved success over earlier continual imitation methods [135].

Deployment itself produces demonstrations. In Sirius, human operators monitor a robot and take over when it is about to fail; the corrections are added to the training data with weights that emphasize the interventions, and the policy is retrained in rounds, so performance improves with deployment time [136,137]. The techniques for collecting and weighting such corrections are covered in the corrections section of the imitation learning article. In open-ended embodied agents, the skill library can be code: Voyager, an LLM-driven agent in Minecraft, stores each successfully verified skill as an executable program, retrieves and composes them for new tasks, and keeps expanding the library without forgetting the old programs, because they are never retrained [138]. Robot skill libraries built from behaviour trees and parameterized primitives play the same role in classical systems.

9.3 Continual perception

A robot's recognition system must learn new objects and adapt to new appearance conditions. CORe50 was built for this: 50 household objects recorded as short video sequences in eleven sessions with different backgrounds and lighting, for benchmarking continual object recognition in the new-instances, new-classes and mixed scenarios that a robot meets [139]. OpenLORIS-Object was recorded by robots and varies illumination, occlusion, object size, camera-object distance and clutter in a controlled way [140]. On the iCub humanoid, Camoriano and colleagues learned new objects incrementally with a recursive regularized least-squares classifier on fixed deep features, with constant update time and a correction for the class imbalance that incremental learning creates [141]. Self-supervision provides labels for free: the autonomous car Stanley continually updated an appearance model of drivable road from the laser-verified area just in front of the vehicle, letting it see farther than its lasers [142], an example of continual adaptation that is also discussed in the self-supervised learning section of the traditional AI and machine learning article.

9.4 Keeping robot foundation models current

Vision-language-action models are pretrained on large multi-robot datasets and then post-trained for particular robots and tasks [62,143–145]. Every post-training step is a continual-learning problem: the model should gain the new skill without losing its general visual, linguistic and physical knowledge. Several findings from language and vision models carry over:

  • Fine-tuning forgets, and scale does not prevent it. Large language models fine-tuned on a sequence of instruction tasks forget general knowledge and earlier tasks [76]; fine-tuning a pretrained model can also distort its features so that it generalizes worse out of distribution, which training the head first (linear probing, then fine-tuning) reduces [146].
  • Parameter-efficient methods learn less and forget less. LoRA forgets less of the base model's capabilities than full fine-tuning, but also learns less of the target domain [60,109]. Adapters that are switched on only for the tasks they were trained for avoid forgetting entirely [63,108]; parameter-efficient adaptation of robot foundation models is reviewed in the foundation models article.
  • Co-training is the workhorse. Mixing a fraction of pretraining data into every fine-tuning batch is replay at scale. RT-2 was co-fine-tuned on robot data together with the original web vision-language data to retain its semantic knowledge [62]; π0.5 co-trains on heterogeneous robot, web and verbal-instruction data [147]; and language models fine-tuned with a small rehearsal buffer behave as good continual learners [148]. Continual pretraining with learning-rate re-warming and a small amount of replay can match retraining from scratch at a fraction of the compute [149].
  • Protecting the backbone architecturally. Knowledge insulation stops the gradients of a newly added action expert from flowing into the vision-language backbone, so the backbone's knowledge is not overwritten while the robot learns to act [150].
  • Weights can be combined after training. Interpolating the weights of a pretrained and a fine-tuned model (WiSE-FT) often keeps much of the robustness of the first and the accuracy of the second [61], and task vectors, the differences between fine-tuned and pretrained weights, can be added or subtracted to combine or remove skills [151].

Generalist agents are also beginning to improve themselves from their own deployment. RoboCat fine-tuned copies of a generalist agent on new tasks from a few demonstrations, used them to generate more data, and folded that data back into the next version of the generalist [152]. π*0.6 trains a VLA with reinforcement learning from its own autonomous experience and expert corrections, using an advantage-conditioned policy, and improved throughput and robustness on long real-world tasks such as making espresso and folding laundry [153]. In both cases the consolidation step, retraining the generalist on accumulated data, is what prevents forgetting.

Some practical guidance follows from the evidence. Start with replay if any data can be kept, because it is simple and strong [22,91,92]. Add regularization or distillation when memory is very small or data cannot be stored [49,51]. Use separate modules for skills that must never regress, such as certified behaviours, and for discrete, identifiable contexts such as tools [63,101]. Separate fast context adaptation from slow skill learning where the physics allows it [27,28]. And for long-running online learners, watch for loss of plasticity as well as forgetting [57].

10.2 Evaluating continual learners honestly

Continual-learning results are easy to overstate. Farquhar and Gal showed that common protocols, such as permuted-image benchmarks and multi-head evaluation with known task identities, favour some methods and do not reflect realistic use [89]; the scenario (task-, domain- or class-incremental) changes rankings completely [20]; simple baselines such as GDumb and plain replay are often not reported [92]; and methods that look good when computation is unconstrained lose their advantage under a fixed compute budget [22]. Kemker and colleagues found that none of the methods they tested solved forgetting across all of their benchmarks [154]. A useful evaluation reports the whole performance matrix, forgetting and intransigence, the memory and compute used, the scenario, and several random seeds with confidence intervals [21,52,69,70]. For robots, these must be measured as closed-loop task success on the real system or a validated simulator, including the old tasks after every update, which is the continual-learning version of regression testing [1,66].

10.3 Deployment: fleets, versions and update gates

Most deployed robots that "learn" do so at the fleet level rather than individually. Data from many robots are pooled, a model is retrained or fine-tuned centrally, and new versions are rolled out, as in large-scale grasping and multi-task RL fleets [125,128,155]. This turns continual learning into a release process: every candidate model is evaluated on the old tasks as well as the new, compared with the current version, and deployed only if it does not regress, with the ability to roll back. Federated learning lets robots contribute updates without sending raw data, at the price of less control over what is learned [23]. Onboard learning is used mostly for fast adaptation within a fixed representation (Section 8), where the space of possible changes is small enough to bound. Systems that close this loop automatically, searching for their own failures, generating training scenarios and validating candidates before release, are an active research direction of their own; for the learning step at the centre of that loop, the methods in this article determine whether the robot improves without losing what it could already do.

10.4 Safety and regulation

A system that changes after deployment raises specific safety concerns: distributional shift and unsafe exploration, feedback loops in which the system's own outputs bias its future training data, and the possibility that a safety-relevant behaviour is forgotten [24,30]. Engineering practice keeps the safety-critical layer out of the learning loop: a fixed, verified safety filter or monitor bounds whatever the learned components do, as described in the safe stopping section of the reactive behavior article and in the safety-filter literature [134]. Regulators have begun to address learning after deployment explicitly. The EU Artificial Intelligence Act requires high-risk AI systems that continue to learn after being placed on the market to be developed so as to eliminate or reduce the risk of biased outputs influencing future inputs (feedback loops), and treats changes that the provider predetermined and documented at the initial conformity assessment as not constituting a substantial modification [25]. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration's guidance on predetermined change control plans lets manufacturers of AI-enabled medical device software describe in advance the modifications a model may undergo and how each will be validated, so that updates within the plan need no new marketing submission [26]. Both approaches reward continual learners whose possible changes are bounded and whose updates are tested against previous capabilities.

10.5 Open problems

  • Learning without boundaries, at the speed of experience. Most methods still assume task boundaries or many passes over each task. Real robots need single-pass, task-free learning on correlated data, with change detection, consolidation and memory management integrated [1,19,90].
  • Forward transfer and compositionality. Preventing forgetting is not enough; a lifelong learner should learn each new skill faster than the last, by composing and reusing what it knows. Robot benchmarks still show little positive forward transfer [64,65,112].
  • Plasticity over a lifetime. Keeping networks trainable for years of continual learning, without periodic resets that themselves cause forgetting, is unsolved at scale [57,58].
  • Continual learning for foundation models. How to add skills, embodiments and knowledge to VLAs continually, with predictable effects on everything else, at acceptable compute cost and without the full pretraining data, is open; so is how to audit what changed [3,149,150].
  • Memory: what to keep and for how long. Deciding what to store, compress, generate or deliberately forget, under privacy, storage and regulatory constraints, has strong theoretical limits and few practical answers [21,67].
  • Lessons from biology. Brains combine consolidation at many time scales, replay during sleep, neuromodulated plasticity, sparse codes and neurogenesis; only some of these have been translated into machine learning [16,83,90,156].
  • Verification of systems that change. Certifying a learner rather than a fixed model requires bounding its possible changes and the effect of each on safety-relevant behaviour, which current methods cannot do beyond narrow cases [25,26,134].

10.6 Summary

Continual and lifelong learning asks a physical AI system to keep improving from its own experience without losing what it already knows. The obstacle, catastrophic forgetting, was identified in connectionist models in 1989, and the dilemma behind it, between stability and plasticity, is shared with biological brains. Three families of remedies have matured: regularization, which protects the parameters or outputs that earlier tasks depend on; replay, which keeps rehearsing a memory of the past and is the strongest general-purpose tool; and architectural isolation, which gives each skill its own parameters at the cost of growth or capacity. Loss of plasticity, the gradual inability of a network to learn, is the other half of the dilemma and matters for learners that run for a long time. Robotics adds structure that helps: separating fast adaptation to the current context from slow learning of shared representations, storing and recalling context-specific models, learning from corrections during deployment, and consolidating fleet experience into new model versions. For robot foundation models, co-training with pretraining data, task-specific adapters and protected backbones are the current practice. Underneath it all, evaluation must measure old capabilities after every update, and safety-critical behaviour should be kept outside the part of the system that learns.
