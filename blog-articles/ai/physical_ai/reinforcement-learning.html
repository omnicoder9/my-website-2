1 What reinforcement learning is, and why physical AI uses it

Reinforcement learning (RL) is the study of agents that learn to act by interacting with an environment and receiving a scalar reward signal. In the standard formulation, at each time step t the agent observes the state st (or an observation of it), chooses an action at, receives a reward rt+1 and finds itself in a new state st+1; its goal is to find a policy, a rule mapping states to actions, that maximizes the expected cumulative reward over the long run [1]. Unlike supervised learning, nobody tells the agent which action was correct; it must discover good behaviour by trial and error, and it must solve the credit assignment problem of working out which of its many earlier decisions caused a reward that arrived much later [1,2]. Unlike classical optimal control, RL does not require the dynamics to be known in closed form: it can learn from sampled experience alone [3,4].

Those two properties explain RL's appeal for physical AI. Many robot skills, such as running over rough terrain, in-hand manipulation of a cube, racing a quadrotor or recovering from a push, are easy to score but hard to program or demonstrate, and involve contact dynamics that are difficult to model analytically [5,6]. Since the mid-2010s, RL in simulation combined with deep neural networks has produced quadrupeds that traverse mountains and stairs [7,8], a robot hand that reorients a Rubik's cube [9,10], a drone that beat human world champions [11], a humanoid that plays soccer [12] and controllers for systems as unusual as stratospheric balloons [13] and tokamak fusion plasmas [14]. RL also has well-known weaknesses for physical systems: it is sample-hungry, it explores by trying things that may be dangerous, its reward functions are easy to misspecify, and policies trained in simulation must survive the gap between simulation and reality [15–17]. Much of this article is about methods developed to address exactly those weaknesses.

It builds on the article on sensor fusion and state estimation, whose Bayes filters supply the belief states that partially observable RL relies on, and on the article on world models, which treats learned dynamics models and planning with them (Dyna, Dreamer, MuZero, model predictive control) in depth; here model-based RL is discussed from the RL perspective and the reader is referred there for detail. 2 A short history: from trial and error to champion-level robots

RL draws on two older threads. The first is the psychology of animal learning: Thorndike's "law of effect" (1911) stated that responses followed by satisfaction become more strongly connected to the situation, the core idea of learning by trial and error [1,19]. The second is optimal control and dynamic programming. Bellman introduced the principle of optimality, the recursive equation that now bears his name and the Markov decision process (MDP) formalism in the 1950s [20,21], and Howard's policy iteration algorithm followed in 1960 [22]. Minsky's 1961 survey named the credit assignment problem [2], and Samuel's checkers program (1959) learned an evaluation function by adjusting it toward the values of later positions, an early form of temporal-difference learning [1,23].

Learning control of physical systems appeared early. Michie and Chambers' BOXES system (1968) learned to balance a pole on a cart by partitioning the state space into boxes and keeping statistics on which action prolonged balance in each [24]. Barto, Sutton and Anderson's 1983 actor-critic solved the same cart-pole task with two "neuronlike" elements, one selecting actions and one predicting reinforcement [25]. Sutton formalized temporal-difference (TD) learning in 1988 [26], Watkins introduced Q-learning in his 1989 thesis with a convergence proof published with Dayan in 1992 [27,28], and Williams' REINFORCE algorithm (1992) established likelihood-ratio policy gradients [29]. Tesauro's TD-Gammon, which learned backgammon at close to world-class level by self-play with a neural network trained by TD learning, showed in the early 1990s that RL could scale with function approximation [30]. Neuroscience later found that the firing of dopamine neurons resembles a TD reward-prediction error, connecting the algorithm back to biology [31].

In robotics, the 2000s brought policy search on real hardware: policy gradient methods tuned the gait of a Sony AIBO quadruped for speed [32] and a simple 3D biped learned to walk [33]; Ng, Abbeel and colleagues used a learned helicopter model and policy search, and later apprenticeship learning, to fly autonomous aerobatics [34,35]; and Peters and Schaal developed natural actor-critic methods for motor skills [36,37]. Surveys from this period emphasized that robot RL worked when it used good policy representations, demonstrations, models and prior knowledge to cope with high-dimensional continuous spaces and expensive real-world samples [5,38].

The deep RL era began when the deep Q-network (DQN) learned to play 49 Atari games from pixels at a level comparable to a professional games tester across the set [39], followed by AlphaGo's defeat of a professional Go player [40] and AlphaZero's mastery of chess, shogi and Go by self-play [41]. Continuous-control algorithms (DDPG, TRPO, PPO, SAC) made deep RL practical for simulated robots [42–45], and end-to-end visuomotor policies and large-scale grasping experiments brought it to real arms [46,47]. GPU-parallel simulators and domain randomization then enabled the sim-to-real successes of the late 2010s and 2020s in legged locomotion, dexterous manipulation and agile flight [7,9,11,48,49]. 3 Markov decision processes

3.1 Definition

A Markov decision process is a tuple (S, A, P, R, γ): a set of states S, a set of actions A, a transition model P(s′ | s, a) giving the probability of reaching s′ after taking a in s, a reward function R(s, a), and a discount factor 0 ≤ γ ≤ 1 [1,56]. The defining Markov property is that the next state and reward depend only on the current state and action, not on the earlier history. A (stationary, possibly stochastic) policy π(a | s) induces a distribution over trajectories, and the objective is to maximize the expected discounted return

Gt = rt+1 + γ rt+2 + γ2 rt+3 + … = Σk≥0 γk rt+k+1.

The discount factor makes infinite sums finite and expresses a preference for earlier reward; 1/(1−γ) is often read as an effective planning horizon [1]. For a robot, the state might be joint angles and velocities plus object poses; actions are motor torques, target joint positions or velocity commands; transitions are given by physics; and the reward encodes the task, for example progress toward a goal minus penalties for energy use or collisions [5]. Episodic tasks end in terminal states (the part is placed, the robot falls); continuing tasks do not.

3.2 Value functions and the Bellman equations

The state-value function Vπ(s) = Eπ[Gt | st = s] is the expected return from s when following π, and the action-value function Qπ(s, a) is the expected return after taking a in s and then following π. They satisfy the Bellman expectation equation, and the optimal value functions satisfy the Bellman optimality equation [1,20]:

Vπ(s) = Σa π(a|s) [ R(s,a) + γ Σs′ P(s′|s,a) Vπ(s′) ]      Q*(s,a) = R(s,a) + γ Σs′ P(s′|s,a) maxa′ Q*(s′,a′).

Any policy that is greedy with respect to Q* is optimal, and for finite MDPs a deterministic stationary optimal policy always exists [56]. The Bellman operator is a γ-contraction in the max norm, which is why repeatedly applying it converges.

3.3 Dynamic programming: value and policy iteration

When P and R are known, the MDP can be solved by dynamic programming. Value iteration applies the Bellman optimality backup to every state until the values stop changing [20]. Policy iteration alternates exact policy evaluation (solving a linear system for Vπ) with greedy policy improvement and terminates in a finite number of iterations, often very few [22,56]. Linear programming is a third exact method, and finite MDPs can be solved in polynomial time in the number of states and actions [56,57]. The catch, which Bellman called the curse of dimensionality, is that the number of states grows exponentially with the number of state variables, so exact dynamic programming is limited to small or coarsely discretized problems [4,20]. Reinforcement learning can be viewed as approximate dynamic programming from samples [1,4].

4 Partially observable MDPs

4.1 Belief states

Real robots rarely observe the full state. Cameras are occluded, objects' masses and frictions are unknown, other agents' intentions are hidden, and sensors are noisy. A partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) adds a set of observations Ω and an observation model O(o | s′, a) to the MDP [50,58]. The agent cannot condition on s; instead it can maintain a belief b(s), a probability distribution over states, updated after each action and observation by Bayes' rule:

b′(s′) ∝ O(o | s′, a) Σs P(s′ | s, a) b(s).

Åström showed in 1965 that the belief is a sufficient statistic for optimal control, so a POMDP is equivalent to a fully observable MDP over the continuous space of beliefs [50]. This update is exactly the Bayes filter of the sensor fusion article, of which the Kalman and particle filters are special cases [59]; the traditional AI and machine learning article covers the underlying Bayesian inference. What POMDPs add is that the agent chooses actions knowing how they will change its future information: optimal POMDP policies trade off acting to gain reward against acting to reduce uncertainty, such as looking again before grasping [58,60].

4.2 Solving POMDPs

Smallwood and Sondik showed that the finite-horizon optimal value function over beliefs is piecewise linear and convex, representable by a finite set of "alpha vectors" [61], but exact solution is intractable in general: finite-horizon POMDPs are PSPACE-complete [57]. Practical solvers approximate. QMDP assumes that all uncertainty disappears after one step, which gives fast policies that never take purely information-gathering actions [62]. Point-based methods such as PBVI and SARSOP back up the value function only at a set of sampled beliefs that the robot is likely to reach, and scaled POMDP planning to robotic problems with thousands of states [63,64]. Online Monte Carlo planners such as POMCP search from the current belief using a particle representation and a simulator, and need only a generative model of the environment [65]. Robotic applications include grasping under pose uncertainty, navigation among pedestrians with unknown intentions, and active sensing; recent reviews survey them [60,66].

4.3 Partial observability in deep RL

Deep RL agents usually avoid explicit beliefs. Common approaches are to stack recent observations, or to give the policy a recurrent memory, as in the deep recurrent Q-network [67], so that the network's hidden state acts as a learned belief. Dreamer-style world models learn a latent state filter jointly with the policy (see the world models article) [68,69]. A family of methods exploits the fact that, in simulation, the full state is known: asymmetric actor-critic trains a critic on privileged simulator state while the actor sees only images [70], and "learning by cheating" or teacher–student training first trains a teacher with privileged information and then distils it into a student that sees only onboard sensors [7,71]. Lee and colleagues' quadruped controller used this to learn, from proprioception history alone, implicit estimates of terrain properties that the teacher had been given directly [7], and RMA trained an adaptation module that estimates a latent encoding of the environment's physical parameters online [72].

5 Value functions and policy learning

When the model is unknown, the agent must learn from sampled transitions. RL algorithms differ in what they learn: a value function from which a policy is derived (value-based methods), the policy directly (policy-search and policy-gradient methods), or both (actor-critic methods) [1,38].

5.1 Temporal-difference learning

Monte Carlo methods estimate Vπ(s) by averaging complete returns, which is unbiased but has high variance and must wait until the end of an episode. Temporal-difference methods instead bootstrap: after each step they move the estimate toward a target built from the observed reward and the current estimate of the next state's value [26]:

V(st) ← V(st) + α [ rt+1 + γ V(st+1) − V(st) ].

The bracketed term is the TD error δt. TD(λ) and eligibility traces interpolate between one-step TD and Monte Carlo, trading bias for variance [1,26]. For control, the agent learns action values. SARSA updates Q(st, at) toward rt+1 + γ Q(st+1, at+1) using the action it actually takes next, so it evaluates the policy it follows, exploration included (on-policy) [73]. Q-learning uses rt+1 + γ maxa Q(st+1, a), learning the greedy policy's values regardless of how the data were collected (off-policy); in the tabular case it converges to Q* provided every state-action pair is visited infinitely often and step sizes decay suitably [28]. Off-policy learning is what makes experience replay and offline RL possible (Sections 7 and 8).

5.2 Function approximation

Tables do not scale to continuous robot states, so values are approximated with parameterized functions Qw(s, a): linear features, tile coding, radial basis functions or neural networks [1,4]. TD learning with linear function approximation converges when on-policy [74], but the combination of function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy updates, which Sutton and Barto call the "deadly triad", can diverge, as Baird's counterexample showed [1,75]. Much of deep RL engineering (target networks, replay, clipped double estimates) exists to tame this instability (Section 7).

5.3 Policy gradients

Policy-gradient methods parameterize a stochastic policy πθ(a | s), for example a Gaussian over joint torques whose mean is a neural network, and ascend the gradient of the expected return J(θ). The likelihood-ratio form of the policy gradient theorem gives [29,76]

∇θJ(θ) = Eπθ[ Σt ∇θ log πθ(at | st) · (Gt − b(st)) ],

which can be estimated from sampled trajectories without a model and without differentiating the dynamics. REINFORCE uses this estimator directly; subtracting a baseline b(s) reduces variance without introducing bias [1,29]. Policy gradients handle continuous actions naturally, can represent stochastic and constrained policies, and change the policy smoothly, properties that suit robotics and explain their early use on real legged robots [32,33,36]. Their weakness is high gradient variance, hence poor sample efficiency.

5.4 Actor-critic methods and trust regions

Actor-critic methods replace the Monte Carlo return with a learned critic: the advantage A(s, a) = Q(s, a) − V(s) is estimated from the TD error or from a weighted sum of TD errors, the generalized advantage estimator (GAE) [25,77,78]. Because a single large gradient step can collapse a policy's performance, a line of work constrains each update. The natural policy gradient preconditions the gradient with the Fisher information matrix [36,37]; trust region policy optimization (TRPO) bounds the KL divergence between successive policies [43]; and proximal policy optimization (PPO) approximates this with a clipped surrogate objective that removes the incentive to move the probability ratio ρ = πθ(a|s)/πold(a|s) outside [1−ε, 1+ε], allowing several epochs of minibatch updates on each batch of data [44]:

LCLIP(θ) = E[ min( ρt Ât, clip(ρt, 1−ε, 1+ε) Ât ) ].

PPO's simplicity and robustness made it the default algorithm for training locomotion policies in massively parallel simulators [8,9,49]. 6 Model-free and model-based reinforcement learning

Model-free methods (Section 5) learn values or policies directly from experience. Model-based methods learn, or are given, a model of the transition dynamics and reward, and use it to plan or to generate synthetic experience [1,80]. The distinction maps onto a classic trade-off. Model-based methods are usually far more sample-efficient, because every transition teaches the model something about the dynamics everywhere it generalizes, and models can be reused for new tasks; but the policy inherits the model's errors, and a planner will actively exploit them [80–82]. Model-free methods are asymptotically unbiased with respect to the true system but can need orders of magnitude more interaction.

6.1 Families of model-based RL

Sutton's Dyna architecture interleaves real experience, model learning and "imagined" updates from the model [51]. PILCO learned a Gaussian-process dynamics model, propagated its uncertainty through long-term predictions and optimized a policy by analytic gradients, learning cart-pole swing-up on hardware from a few seconds to minutes of data [52]. PETS combined ensembles of probabilistic neural networks with sampling-based model predictive control [83]; MBPO uses short model rollouts branched from real states to train a SAC agent [84]; and Dreamer learns behaviours inside a learned latent world model, with DreamerV3 working across many domains with fixed hyperparameters [68,69]. DayDreamer applied Dreamer directly on physical robots, a quadruped learning to walk from scratch in about an hour of real-world interaction [85]. TD-MPC2 combines a learned latent model, short-horizon planning and a learned terminal value [86], and MuZero plans with a model trained only to predict quantities relevant to decisions [87]. These systems, and the question of which parts of a model to learn and how to plan with them, are treated in detail in the world models article.

From the RL perspective, three issues dominate. Model bias and exploitation: policies optimized against an imperfect model find regions where the model is optimistically wrong; ensembles, uncertainty penalties and short rollouts limit this [52,83,84]. Objective mismatch: a model trained for prediction accuracy is not necessarily the model that yields the best policy [82]. Combining both worlds: many robot systems use a model-based controller or planner as a starting point and fine-tune with model-free RL, as in Nagabandi and colleagues' model-based initialization of a model-free learner [88]. In robotics a "model" is also often an analytical or simulated one. Training model-free RL in a physics simulator, the dominant recipe for legged robots, is in effect model-based at the level of the whole system: the simulator is the model, and its errors are the sim-to-real gap of Section 10 [48,89].

7 Deep reinforcement learning

Deep RL uses deep neural networks to represent value functions, policies or models, so that agents can learn directly from high-dimensional inputs such as images, depth maps, point clouds and long proprioceptive histories [39,46]. Function approximation with neural networks predates deep learning, as TD-Gammon showed [30], but the combination of large networks, GPUs, stabilizing algorithmic devices and fast simulators made it broadly practical in the 2010s.

7.1 Value-based deep RL

DQN trains a convolutional network to approximate Q(s, a) with two devices that address the instability of Section 5.2 [39]. Experience replay stores transitions in a buffer and trains on random minibatches, breaking the correlation between consecutive samples and reusing data, an idea due to Lin [93]. A target network, a periodically updated copy of the Q-network, supplies the bootstrap targets so that they do not move with every gradient step. Later refinements include double Q-learning to reduce the overestimation caused by the max operator [94], dueling architectures [95], prioritized replay [96] and distributional value functions [97]; Rainbow combined six such extensions [98]. QT-Opt scaled the Q-learning approach to continuous grasping actions, optimizing over actions with the cross-entropy method, and trained a vision-based grasping policy from 580 000 real grasp attempts collected on seven robots, reaching 96% grasp success on unseen objects [47].

7.2 Deep actor-critic methods for continuous control

Robots act in continuous spaces, where the max over actions in Q-learning is itself an optimization problem. The deterministic policy gradient (DPG) and its deep version DDPG learn a deterministic actor that ascends the critic's action gradient [42,101]. TD3 reduced DDPG's overestimation with clipped double Q-learning, delayed actor updates and target policy smoothing [102]. Soft actor-critic (SAC) maximizes reward plus policy entropy, which encourages exploration and robustness, and is off-policy and therefore relatively sample-efficient [45]; Haarnoja and colleagues used it to train a Minitaur quadruped to walk directly in the real world in about two hours [103]. On-policy methods (TRPO, PPO) [43,44] are less sample-efficient but simple to parallelize: distributed architectures such as A3C and IMPALA run many actors in parallel [104,105], and GPU simulators such as Isaac Gym, Brax and MuJoCo-based pipelines run thousands of robot instances at once [106–108]. Rudin and colleagues trained a quadruped locomotion policy with PPO and thousands of simulated robots in parallel on a single GPU in under 20 minutes [49]. Evolution strategies and random search are competitive alternatives when parallel compute is abundant [92,109].

8 Offline reinforcement learning

8.1 Learning from fixed datasets

Online RL needs the agent to collect its own data, which on a physical robot is slow, expensive and potentially dangerous. Offline (or batch) RL instead learns a policy from a fixed, previously logged dataset of transitions, with no further interaction [115,116]. Early batch methods include fitted Q iteration with tree ensembles and neural fitted Q iteration [117,118]. Offline RL is attractive for physical AI because robotics is accumulating large datasets of teleoperated and autonomous experience, such as the Open X-Embodiment collection pooled from many robots [119], and because it allows a policy to be improved and evaluated before it is deployed.

8.2 Distribution shift and extrapolation error

Applying an off-policy algorithm such as Q-learning naively to a fixed dataset usually fails [116,120]. The Bellman target maxa′ Q(s′, a′) queries actions the behaviour policy never took; their Q-values are pure extrapolation and are often overestimated; the greedy policy then chooses exactly those actions; and since the agent can never try them, the error is never corrected. Fujimoto and colleagues named this extrapolation error [120]. Offline RL algorithms counter it with some form of conservatism:

  • Policy constraints keep the learned policy close to the behaviour policy: BCQ generates only actions similar to those in the data [120], BEAR constrains the policy's support [121], and TD3+BC simply adds a behaviour-cloning term to the actor loss [122].
  • Pessimistic values penalize uncertain actions: conservative Q-learning (CQL) learns a Q-function that lower-bounds the true value by pushing down Q-values of out-of-distribution actions [54]; model-based variants such as MOPO penalize rewards by model uncertainty [123]; and theory shows that pessimism, acting on a lower confidence bound, is what makes offline RL provably efficient [124,125].
  • In-sample learning avoids querying unseen actions at all: implicit Q-learning (IQL) uses expectile regression over dataset actions and extracts a policy by advantage-weighted regression [126].
  • Sequence modelling treats RL as conditional generation: the Decision Transformer predicts actions conditioned on desired return [127], and Q-Transformer brings offline Q-learning to transformer policies trained on large robot datasets [128].

Evaluation is a further difficulty, because the value of a new policy must be estimated from data collected by another; importance-sampling off-policy evaluation is unbiased but high-variance [116,129]. Benchmarks such as D4RL standardized comparison [130]. In practice offline RL is often a pre-training stage followed by online fine-tuning: AWAC and RLPD showed that prior data can greatly accelerate subsequent online learning [131,132].

9 Safe and constrained reinforcement learning

An RL agent learns by trying actions whose consequences it does not yet know. In a simulator that costs only compute; on a robot near people, fragile objects or its own joint limits it can be dangerous. Safe RL covers methods that keep the agent's behaviour within acceptable bounds during learning, deployment or both [18,133]. García and Fernández divided early approaches into those that modify the optimization criterion (constraints, risk sensitivity) and those that modify the exploration process (external knowledge, safe exploration) [133]; Brunke and colleagues' review connects these with learning-based control and its certificates [18]. Safety requirements for robots working beside people are also set by standards such as ISO/TS 15066 on collaborative robots [134], which any learned controller must respect.

9.1 Constrained MDPs and Lagrangian methods

A constrained MDP (CMDP) adds cost functions ci(s, a) with budgets di: maximize JR(π) subject to JCi(π) ≤ di, where each JCi is an expected (discounted) cumulative cost [135]. Separating costs from rewards avoids hand-tuning penalty weights and states requirements in natural units, such as expected contacts with a person or time spent near a ledge. Optimal CMDP policies may need to be stochastic [135]. The most common solution is Lagrangian relaxation: optimize the policy for R − λC while adjusting the multiplier λ by gradient ascent on the constraint violation, as in reward-constrained policy optimization [136]; PID control of the multiplier reduces the oscillation and overshoot of plain dual ascent [137]. Constrained policy optimization (CPO) instead solves a trust-region problem with linearized constraints, aiming to satisfy constraints approximately at every policy update [138]. Risk-sensitive formulations replace expectations with tail measures such as conditional value at risk (CVaR), focusing on bad outcomes rather than averages [139].

The example solves a small CMDP with the primal-dual method: an inner loop computes an entropy-regularized optimal policy for r − λc, and the outer loop raises λ while the expected discounted cost exceeds its budget.

9.2 Safety filters, shields and barrier functions

Hard, per-step safety requires a different tool: a safety filter that sits between the learned policy and the actuators and modifies only those actions that would lead to unsafe states [140,141]. Variants include shields synthesized from temporal-logic specifications that block unsafe discrete actions [142], a learned linear "safety layer" that projects actions onto a constraint set [143], predictive safety filters that solve a model predictive control problem to verify that a safe backup trajectory exists [141], and Hamilton–Jacobi reachability-based frameworks that intervene only near the boundary of the safe set [144]. Control barrier functions (CBFs) are among the most widely used: a function h(x) that is non-negative on the safe set defines a condition on the control input, typically ḣ(x, u) ≥ −α(h(x)), that keeps the set forward-invariant, and the filter solves a small quadratic program for the input closest to the nominal one that satisfies it [145,146]. Cheng and colleagues combined CBF filters with model-free RL so that exploration stays safe during training [147]. Model-based safe exploration methods instead learn the dynamics with uncertainty, for example with Gaussian processes, and expand the region of state space certified as safe as data accumulate [148,149].

The example wraps an exploring policy for a mobile base approaching a shelf in a discrete-time CBF filter. The barrier is the distance remaining after braking at full deceleration, so it accounts for the robot's actuator limit.

9.3 Safety in practice

Beyond formal methods, practical robot learning relies on engineering safeguards: joint and torque limits enforced in low-level controllers, workspace boundaries, automatic resets and fall detection, and human supervision with emergency stops [15,16]. Learning to reset, so that the agent itself returns to safe start states and aborts when it cannot, reduces both manual resets and hazardous states [150]. A wider class of safety problems concerns the specification itself: agents optimize the reward they are given, not the one intended, and may find unintended "reward hacks" or cause negative side effects [17]. For physical systems this makes reward design and validation in simulation part of the safety case.

10 Sim-to-real transfer and domain randomization

10.1 The reality gap

Because real-world interaction is slow, costly and risky, most RL for robots is done in simulation, where millions of episodes can run in parallel faster than real time [49,106,108]. A policy trained in simulation, however, exploits whatever the simulator gets wrong: unmodelled actuator dynamics and latency, inaccurate friction and contact, unrealistic rendering, sensor noise and mis-estimated masses. The resulting drop in performance on hardware is the reality gap, a term from evolutionary robotics, where Jakobi and colleagues proposed adding noise to simulations to cross it [151,152]. Surveys group the remedies into better simulation (system identification), robustness through randomization, adaptation, and domain adaptation of perception [89,153]. The physical modeling article discusses simulators and system identification themselves.

10.2 Better models of the robot

The first remedy is to make the simulator more accurate. Hwangbo and colleagues trained a neural network on measured data to model the ANYmal robot's series-elastic actuators and inserted it into the simulator, which was critical for transferring agile locomotion policies [48]. Tan and colleagues improved the simulated actuator model and latency for the Minitaur quadruped and combined this with randomization [154]. The drone-racing system Swift added residual models of the drone's dynamics and perception fitted to real flight data [11]. Closing the loop between real rollouts and simulator parameters, as in SimOpt, adapts the randomization distribution itself to match reality [155].

10.3 Domain randomization

The second remedy is to make the policy robust to the gap rather than closing it. Domain randomization trains across many simulated variants with randomized parameters, so that, in Tobin and colleagues' words, the real world appears to the model as just another variation [53]. Tobin and colleagues randomized textures, lighting and camera positions to train an object detector for grasping that transferred from synthetic images alone [53], and CAD2RL learned collision-free indoor drone flight from randomized rendered scenes without any real images [156]. Peng and colleagues randomized dynamics, including masses, friction, damping, actuator gains and observation latency, and used a recurrent policy so that it could implicitly identify the current dynamics; the policy transferred to a real arm pushing a puck [157]. EPOpt trained on an ensemble of models while emphasizing the worst-performing ones [158]. OpenAI's Dactyl and Rubik's cube work randomized physics and vision at large scale, and automatic domain randomization (ADR) widened each parameter's range whenever the policy mastered the current one [9,10]. Randomization is now standard in legged and humanoid locomotion [7,8,49,114] and in dexterous manipulation, as in DeXtreme [159].

10.4 Adaptation and privileged learning

Randomization trades nominal performance for robustness: a policy that must work for every mass is conservative for any particular one. Adaptive approaches recover performance by inferring the current parameters online. Universal policies with online system identification (UP-OSI) feed explicitly estimated physical parameters to the policy [160]; RMA trains a base policy with access to an encoding of environment parameters in simulation and an adaptation module that estimates that encoding from recent states and actions on the real robot [72]; and teacher–student training, introduced in Section 4.3, distils a privileged policy into one that uses only onboard sensors [7,71]. For vision, domain adaptation translates simulated images toward real ones or both toward a canonical rendering, as in GraspGAN and RCAN for grasping [161,162].

11 Practice, evaluation and open problems

11.1 Rewards, exploration and prior knowledge

The reward function is the task specification, and designing it is often the hardest part of applying RL to a robot [5,16]. Sparse rewards (1 on success, 0 otherwise) are easy to specify but give almost no learning signal; dense shaped rewards guide learning but can be exploited [17]. Potential-based shaping, which adds γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) for any potential function Φ, is guaranteed not to change the optimal policy [164]. Hindsight experience replay relabels failed attempts with the goal that was actually achieved, so that goal-reaching tasks can be learned from sparse rewards [165]. Intrinsic motivation adds exploration bonuses, such as the prediction error of a learned forward model ("curiosity") [166]. Rewards can also be learned: inverse RL infers a reward from demonstrations [167,168], preference-based RL learns it from human comparisons of behaviour [169], and large language models have been used to write reward code that is iteratively refined from training results [170]. Demonstrations are also used to initialize and guide RL, as in DAPG for dexterous manipulation [113], motion-capture imitation rewards for physics-based characters [112], and human interventions during training [111]; imitation learning on its own is limited by compounding errors when the policy drifts from the demonstrated states [171,172]. Some argue that maximizing reward is sufficient to produce general intelligent behaviour [173]; for engineers the practical lesson is narrower: the agent will optimize exactly what is measured.

11.2 Tools and benchmarks

The OpenAI Gym interface (now maintained as Gymnasium) standardized how agents and environments interact [174,175], and the DeepMind Control Suite provides continuous-control benchmarks built on MuJoCo [108,176]. GPU-accelerated simulators and frameworks for robot learning, such as Isaac Gym and the Orbit framework built on it, and Brax, enable massively parallel training [106,107,177]. Reference implementations such as Stable-Baselines3 reduce the risk of subtle implementation errors [178]. Offline RL has D4RL and large real-robot datasets [119,130].

11.3 Evaluating RL results

Deep RL results are notoriously sensitive to random seeds, hyperparameters, code-level details and evaluation protocols; Henderson and colleagues showed that different random seeds of the same algorithm could produce statistically different learning curves [99]. Agarwal and colleagues recommended reporting interval estimates and robust aggregate statistics such as the interquartile mean rather than point estimates from a few runs [100]. For physical AI, simulated benchmark returns are only a proxy: what matters is real-world success rate, robustness to perturbations, safety incidents and the human effort needed for resets and supervision, which should be reported alongside learning curves [6,15,16].

11.5 Open problems

  • Sample efficiency in the real world. Real-world RL remains limited by data; model-based, off-policy and offline methods and better use of prior data narrow the gap [15,85,110,132].
  • Generalization and foundation models. Policies trained for one robot and task rarely transfer; large multi-robot datasets and vision-language-action models trained mostly by imitation are a complementary route, and how best to improve them with RL is an active question [119,128,179].
  • Reward specification. Learned, preference-based and language-generated rewards ease design but introduce their own misspecification risks [17,169,170].
  • Verified safety. Connecting learned policies to certificates that hold under model error and partial observability is unsolved for general robots [18,140].
  • Partial observability at scale. Belief-space methods are principled but expensive; learned memory is scalable but its uncertainty is hard to interpret [60,66].
  • Reproducible evaluation. Standard real-world protocols and statistics are needed to compare methods meaningfully [6,99,100].

Conclusion

Reinforcement learning gives physical AI a general way to turn a task specification, the reward, into behaviour through experience. Its foundations are the Markov decision process and the Bellman equations; partial observability adds belief states and the value of information; value functions, policy gradients and actor-critic methods make learning from samples possible; and models trade sample efficiency against bias. Deep networks and parallel simulation have taken RL from cart-poles to legged robots in the wild, dexterous hands, champion drones and humanoids. Offline RL lets robots learn from logged data, constrained and filtered RL keeps learning within safe bounds, and domain randomization, system identification and adaptation carry policies from simulation to hardware. The examples in this article show the recurring lessons in miniature: exploration has a cost that on-policy methods account for; information-gathering actions fall out of belief-space planning; reusing data improves sample efficiency; models are efficient but biased; extrapolated values must be treated pessimistically; constraints and filters make safety explicit; and robustness has to be trained in. The most capable robot systems combine these ideas rather than relying on any one of them.
