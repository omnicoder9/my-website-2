1 What a self-improving physical system is

Every robot that leaves the laboratory meets conditions its designers did not anticipate: heavier payloads, worn tyres, new floor coatings, unfamiliar objects, different lighting, other road users who behave in unexpected ways. Traditionally such surprises were handled by engineers who read incident reports, reproduced the problem, changed the software and shipped a new release months later. A self-improving physical system automates as much of that cycle as can be automated safely. It notices when it fails or nearly fails, works out what kind of situation caused the failure, creates many more situations of that kind in simulation or in controlled practice, updates its policy on them, and then checks, before the update reaches any customer, that the new version is better where it was weak and no worse anywhere else [1–3].

The idea is not new in control. An adaptive controller adjusts its parameters continuously to track a changing plant, and an iterative learning controller improves a repeated motion from one trial to the next [4,5]. What is new is the scale and scope. Modern robot policies are large learned models, often trained on data from fleets of robots, and they can fail in ways that no parameter adjustment can fix: a grasp strategy that does not work on transparent objects, a perception stack that drops stationary obstacles, a locomotion policy that has never seen ice [6–8]. Improving them means changing what the system has learned, and every such change can break something that used to work. A self-improving system is therefore as much a validation system as a learning system: the faster it learns, the more it depends on being able to tell a real improvement from a regression [9,10].

2 A short history: from self-tuning regulators to fleet learning

2.1 Adaptive and learning control

The first self-improving machines were controllers. In the late 1950s, aircraft autopilots that had to work across a wide flight envelope motivated model-reference adaptive control, in which controller gains are adjusted online so that the closed loop follows a reference model; the gradient-based "MIT rule" for doing so dates from this period [4]. In 1973 Åström and Wittenmark analysed the self-tuning regulator, which identifies a plant model recursively from input–output data and redesigns its controller from the current estimate at every step, and showed conditions under which it converges to the controller that would be designed if the plant were known [12]. Adaptive control established two lessons that recur throughout this article: improvement requires informative data (a regulator that holds a plant perfectly still learns nothing about it), and adaptation can destabilize a system that was stable before, so guarantees matter [4].

A second strand learned across repetitions instead of within one. In 1984 Arimoto, Kawamura and Miyazaki proposed "bettering" a robot's operation by learning: after each repetition of a motion, the feedforward command for the next is corrected by the tracking error just observed, so that a manipulator repeating the same task improves trial by trial [13]. Iterative learning control (ILC) grew into a mature field with convergence theory and industrial applications [5], and later work used optimization-based ILC to make quadrocopters fly aggressive trajectories precisely after a few practice runs [14]. ILC is self-improvement in its purest form, but it improves one task under fixed conditions; it does not discover new failures or generalize to new situations.

2.2 Lifelong and never-ending learning

Machine learning brought the ambition of systems that keep learning over their whole operating life. Lin's 1992 study of "self-improving reactive agents" introduced experience replay, storing past transitions and reusing them for learning, which became a cornerstone of deep reinforcement learning [15]. In 1995 Thrun and Mitchell argued for lifelong robot learning: a robot facing a stream of tasks should transfer knowledge between them, so that learning becomes easier the more it has learned [1]. The never-ending language learner NELL, which read the web continuously from 2010 and kept extending its knowledge base, showed that a system could improve itself for years, and also how errors could accumulate when a system learns from its own outputs without enough correction [16]. The same period identified the central obstacle to learning continuously with neural networks: catastrophic forgetting, in which training on new data overwrites what was learned before [17,18].

2.3 Robot fleets and data engines

Large-scale robot learning turned these ideas into practice. Levine and colleagues collected over 800,000 grasp attempts with up to 14 robot arms and used them to train a convolutional network that predicted grasp success, so that the fleet's experience improved every robot [6]. QT-Opt trained a vision-based grasping policy with off-policy reinforcement learning on 580,000 real grasps from seven robots and reached 96% success on unseen objects [19], and MT-Opt extended the approach to a fleet learning many tasks at once, with new tasks bootstrapped from data collected for old ones [20]. Simulation offered an alternative source of experience: with automatic domain randomization, the simulated training distribution widened automatically whenever the policy mastered it, which let a robot hand trained only in simulation solve a Rubik's cube [21]. RoboCat closed a full self-improvement loop for manipulation: a generalist agent was fine-tuned on a few demonstrations of a new task, practised the task autonomously to generate more data, and the data were folded back into the next version of the generalist [7]. Robot foundation models are now trained and post-trained in the same iterative way [22–24].

2.4 Testing, falsification and safety assurance

A parallel line of work asked how to find failures before customers do. Formal-methods researchers defined quantitative semantics for temporal logic specifications, so that a simulation could report not just whether a requirement was violated but by how much [25–27], and tools such as Breach and S-TaLiRo used that "robustness" as an objective for optimization-based falsification of cyber-physical systems [28,29]. Adaptive stress testing used Monte Carlo tree search, and later reinforcement learning, to find the most likely sequences of disturbances that led an aircraft collision avoidance system into a near midair collision [30,31]. For automated driving, Kalra and Paddock showed that demonstrating reliability by driving alone would take hundreds of millions of miles or more, which made simulation-based and accelerated testing a necessity [32,33]. Standards followed: ISO 21448 on the safety of the intended functionality treats unknown unsafe scenarios as something to be discovered and reduced systematically, ISO/PAS 8800 addresses AI components in road vehicles, and UL 4600 requires a safety case for autonomous products that stays valid as the product changes [34–36]. Regulators have begun to accept systems that change after approval if the change process itself is approved in advance, as in the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's guidance on predetermined change control plans for AI-enabled medical devices [37]. 3 Automatically identifying failures

3.1 What counts as a failure

A system can only improve on failures it can recognize, so the first design decision is what to count. Useful signals range from unambiguous to subtle. Task failures (a dropped object, a missed delivery) and safety violations (a collision, a protective stop, an excessive contact force) are the clearest. Near misses (a minimum distance or time to collision below a threshold, a grasp that slipped but held) are far more frequent and carry most of the information about where the system is fragile. Human interventions, such as a safety driver taking over or a teleoperator correcting a robot, are both a failure signal and a source of corrective labels; the disengagement reports filed by holders of California's autonomous-vehicle testing permits, for example, classify each disengagement by cause, from perception and planning discrepancies to hardware faults [41,42]. Finally, degradation (rising tracking error, longer cycle times, more retries) indicates that the system is drifting towards failure before it fails [43,44]. Reviews of failures in human–robot interaction add that some failures are social rather than technical, such as a robot that completes a task in a way that confuses or alarms people around it [45].

Signals of this kind become machine-checkable when requirements are written as formal specifications. Signal temporal logic (STL) expresses requirements over real-valued signals with time bounds, for example "always keep at least 1 m from the car ahead" or "after a door-open command, the door is open within 5 s and stays open for 10 s" [25]. STL has a quantitative robustness semantics: for a trace, the robustness ρ of a formula is a real number whose sign says whether the trace satisfies the formula and whose magnitude says by how much, so "always gap ≥ 1 m" has robustness mint gap(t) − 1 [26,27]. Robustness turns failure detection into a continuous measurement: a run with ρ = 0.2 m passed, but only just, and is worth learning from. It also turns failure search into optimization, which Section 4 exploits.

3.2 Runtime monitors and calibrated alarms

Offline analysis of logs finds failures after the fact; runtime monitors try to detect them, or their precursors, while the robot is operating, so that it can slow down, hand over to a fallback or ask for help, and so that the episode is flagged for learning. Classical fault detection compares measurements with a model's predictions and raises an alarm when the residual exceeds a threshold, with statistical change detectors such as the cumulative sum (CUSUM) test deciding when a shift in the residual's mean is real [44,46]. Learned systems face an additional problem: their inputs drift away from the training distribution, through covariate shift in what they see or concept drift in how the world responds [47,48]. Anomaly detection, which models what normal data look like and flags what does not fit, is the general tool [49]; Park and colleagues, for example, used a variational autoencoder over force, sound and joint signals to detect anomalies during robot-assisted feeding [50].

For neural networks, the simplest out-of-distribution (OOD) signal is the model's own confidence, such as the maximum softmax probability of a classifier, which is lower on average for misclassified and OOD inputs [51]. Confidence from a single network is often poorly calibrated, however, and degrades under common image corruptions that a deployed robot will certainly meet [52]. Ensembles of independently trained networks and Monte Carlo dropout give better estimates of epistemic uncertainty, the part that comes from lack of data: ensemble members agree where training data were dense and disagree where they were not [53,54]. The same disagreement signal is used in interactive imitation learning to decide when a robot should hand control to a human [55].

The hard part of any monitor is the threshold. Too low, and false alarms halt the fleet; too high, and failures pass unnoticed. Conformal prediction gives a distribution-free way to set it. Given n calibration scores from nominal operation and a new score that is exchangeable with them, the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉-th smallest calibration score is a threshold that a new nominal score exceeds with probability at most α, whatever the score function and whatever the distribution [56,57]. Robotics has adopted the idea widely: to bound the rate at which a learned perception or prediction module violates a safety margin [58], and to let a language-model planner ask for help exactly when its set of plausible plans contains more than one option, with a guaranteed success rate [59].

Monitors for generative and foundation-model policies watch the policy's own behaviour. Agia and colleagues detected imminent failures of diffusion policies from the temporal consistency of successive sampled action chunks, which become erratic when the policy is out of its depth, and used a vision-language model to judge task progress for slower failures; both thresholds were calibrated conformally [60]. Sinha and colleagues paired a fast anomaly detector based on the distance of an observation's embedding from nominal data with a slower large-language-model reasoner that decided whether a detected anomaly actually required a fallback [61].

3.3 Explaining and triaging failures

A failure log is only useful for improvement once someone, or something, has said what kind of failure it is. Triage clusters failures into modes that share a root cause, ranks the modes by frequency and severity, and decides which to fix first. In robotics this used to be entirely manual. Foundation models now automate part of it. REFLECT converts multisensory robot logs into a hierarchical textual summary and asks a large language model to locate and explain the failure and propose a corrected plan [62]. AHA fine-tuned a vision-language model on automatically generated failure trajectories to detect manipulation failures and describe them in language, and used the explanations to improve task plans and reward functions downstream [63]. Such explanations are hypotheses, not ground truth; Section 6 returns to the danger of a self-improving system that grades its own homework. Human interventions remain the most trusted source: in fleet learning, the moments at which a remote supervisor had to step in are both the failure labels and the training data for the fix [64,65], as described in the corrections section of the imitation learning article.

On nominal episodes the observed false-alarm rate is close to the 5% target; the guarantee is on the expected rate, and 300 test episodes estimate it only to within about ±2.5 percentage points. The payload and actuator faults are flagged in essentially every episode, mostly by the residual, including the large majority in which they did not (yet) cause a task failure: the monitor detects the changed condition, not just its consequences, which is exactly what a self-improving system needs in order to collect data about it. Every one of those failures was preceded by an alarm, typically by most of a second. Faster commands are a different kind of change: the dynamics model is still right, so the residual is less useful, but the policy is operating where it has never been validated, and the ensemble's disagreement flags it in two thirds of the episodes, again before every failure, though with less warning, because once the motions are fast enough the tracking error grows within a few hundred milliseconds. In practice, residual-type and novelty-type monitors are complementary, and both need calibrated thresholds [53,57,58].

4 Generating training scenarios from failures

4.1 From one failure to a distribution of scenarios

A single field failure is an anecdote. Training on it alone teaches the policy to handle one exact situation, which will never recur exactly, and invites overfitting. What a self-improving system needs is a distribution of scenarios around the failure: the same structure with different speeds, distances, objects, lighting and timing, including variations that are harder than the one observed, so that the fix generalizes and its limits are known. Producing that distribution takes three ingredients: a parameterized description of the scenario, a simulator or practice setup that can execute it, and a search or sampling procedure that decides which parameter values to try [3,39].

The first ingredient often comes from reconstruction. Logged sensor data can be turned into a simulated replica of the place where a failure happened, using the mapping and neural scene representations described in the digital twins section of the representations article. RialTo builds such a "digital twin" from a quick phone scan of a real scene and fine-tunes a policy in it with reinforcement learning before transferring it back [66], and ACDC generates "digital cousins", similar but not identical scenes, so that the policy learns the family of situations rather than one replica [67]. For driving, logged traffic can be replayed with the behaviour of other agents perturbed, adversarially or by sampling from learned traffic models, to produce realistic variants of a near miss [68,69]; the generative models used for this are described in the generative and predictive models article.

4.2 Falsification and adaptive stress testing

Given a parameterized scenario space and a specification, falsification searches for parameter values that violate the specification. Corso and colleagues' survey of black-box safety validation organizes the methods into optimization, path planning, reinforcement learning and importance sampling, and distinguishes three goals that are easily confused: finding any failure (falsification), finding the most likely failure, and estimating the probability of failure [3]. Because STL robustness is a continuous objective, falsification can use any black-box optimizer. Breach and S-TaLiRo used simulated annealing, nonlinear optimization and related methods [28,29]; Bayesian optimization models the robustness as a Gaussian process and chooses each new simulation to balance exploring uncertain regions against exploiting promising ones, finding failures of automotive controllers in few simulations [70,71]; and population methods such as the covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES) and the cross-entropy method (CEM) iteratively refit a sampling distribution to the best scenarios found so far [72–74].

Sequential problems call for sequential search. Adaptive stress testing (AST) treats the disturbances that the environment can apply at each time step (sensor noise, pilot responses, other agents' actions) as the actions of an adversary, and searches with Monte Carlo tree search or deep reinforcement learning for the disturbance sequence that maximizes the likelihood of the path while still reaching a failure. Applied to an aircraft collision avoidance system, AST found the most likely encounters leading to a near midair collision, which made the failures interpretable and relevant rather than merely possible [30,31]. Feng and colleagues trained background vehicles with "dense" reinforcement learning, which edits only the safety-critical states of naturalistic driving, to create an intelligent testing environment in which automated vehicles met dangerous situations far more often than in naturalistic traffic while the failure-rate estimate remained unbiased [75]. Estimating failure probabilities with importance sampling, including the cross-entropy method, is treated with an example in the synthetic scenarios section of the generative models article [33,76]. For self-improvement, the goal is usually different: not an estimate, but a diverse collection of failures that covers every mode the system has.

4.3 Scenario languages and coverage

Handwritten simulation scripts do not scale to thousands of scenario families. Scenic is a probabilistic programming language for scenarios: a program declares objects, their spatial relations ("a car ahead of the ego vehicle, between 10 and 30 m away, facing roughly along the lane") and distributions over their properties, and the Scenic sampler produces concrete scenes and behaviours that satisfy the constraints [39]. VerifAI connects such programs to falsifiers, samplers and specification monitors and analyses the results, for example by clustering counterexamples into failure modes [77], and Fremont and colleagues used both to select test cases in simulation and then executed them on a closed test track with a real autonomous vehicle, comparing simulated and real outcomes [78]. Whatever the tooling, generated scenarios must be judged on coverage (have all relevant regions and modes been exercised?) as well as on the number of failures found; a falsifier that finds ten thousand variants of the same failure has found one failure [3,9].

4.4 Curricula and open-ended scenario generation

When scenarios are used for training rather than testing, the question becomes which scenarios teach the most. Domain randomization samples simulator parameters from wide, fixed ranges, as described in the sim-to-real section of the reinforcement learning article [79,80]. Adaptive variants move those ranges: automatic domain randomization widens a parameter's range whenever the policy performs well at its boundary [21], and Chebotar and colleagues adjusted randomization distributions so that simulated trajectories matched a few real rollouts [81]. Legged-robot training uses game-inspired terrain curricula in which each simulated robot is promoted to harder terrain when it succeeds and demoted when it fails [82].

Unsupervised environment design generalizes these ideas by making scenario generation itself a learning problem. POET co-evolves a population of environments and of agents that solve them, transferring agents between environments to find stepping stones to ever harder challenges [83]. PAIRED trains an adversarial environment generator to maximize the regret of a protagonist agent, the gap between what an antagonist agent achieves in the generated environment and what the protagonist achieves, so that it proposes environments that are hard but solvable rather than impossible [84]. Prioritized level replay keeps a buffer of previously seen environments and replays those with the highest estimated learning potential [85], and ACCEL combines that buffer with small edits to the best levels to evolve a curriculum [86]. A self-improving physical system can use the same machinery with field failures as seeds: start from the scenario that failed, mutate it, and train preferentially on variants that are solvable but not yet solved.

5 Adapting policies without forgetting

5.1 Three time scales of adaptation

Policies can change at three time scales, and a self-improving system usually uses all three. Online adaptation changes the policy's behaviour within an episode, in milliseconds to seconds, without changing its learned weights: an adaptive controller retunes its gains, or a learned policy infers the current conditions from recent history and conditions its actions on them. Incremental updates change weights between episodes or shifts using freshly collected data: corrections, interventions or practice. Release-level retraining builds a new version from all data collected so far, typically offline, and is the update that goes through the full validation of Section 6. The faster the time scale, the less validation is possible before the change takes effect, so the fast scales are usually restricted to variations the policy was designed and validated for, and genuinely new behaviour is introduced at the slow scale [2,4,87].

5.2 Online adaptation without changing weights

Adaptive control, described in the adaptive and robust control section of the control article, is the classical form [4]. Learned policies achieve the same effect by conditioning. Rapid motor adaptation (RMA) trains a legged-robot policy in simulation with access to privileged environment parameters such as friction, payload and motor strength, compressed into a latent "extrinsics" vector, and then trains an adaptation module that estimates that vector from the recent history of states and actions; on hardware, the adaptation module tracks changes in terrain and load within a fraction of a second, with no weight updates [88]. Meta-learning trains models explicitly to adapt quickly: model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML) finds initial weights from which a few gradient steps on a new task give good performance [89], and Nagabandi and colleagues meta-trained dynamics models that adapted online, from the last few time steps, to a legged robot with a damaged leg, a new slope or a changed payload [90]. Online adaptation has a great advantage for self-improvement, which is that it cannot forget, because nothing permanent changes. Its limitation is the mirror image: it only covers the range of variation anticipated during training, so failures outside that range still need the slower loops.

5.3 Learning from deployment data

Field data for incremental updates come in several forms. Human interventions and corrections are the most informative per sample. HG-DAgger records the actions of a human expert who takes over when the robot is about to fail [42]; Sirius uses the intervention data from deployments of a manipulation system, reweighting it during training so that the policy learns most from the moments when a human had to help [65]; and Fleet-DAgger studies how a limited number of remote humans should allocate their attention across a fleet of robots that request help, so that the fleet improves fastest [64]. Combined with reinforcement learning, interventions brought real-world manipulation policies to near-perfect success rates within a few hours of training [91]. These methods are developed in the corrections section of the imitation learning article.

Logged autonomous experience can be used with offline reinforcement learning, which learns from fixed datasets while guarding against overestimating actions that the data do not support, as described in the offline RL section of the reinforcement learning article [92]. Residual learning is a conservative way to apply any of these signals: instead of replacing a working controller, a small learned policy adds a correction on top of it, so the original remains intact and the residual can be switched off if it misbehaves [93,94].

Finally, robots can generate data by autonomous practice. RoboCat practised newly learned tasks on its own and folded the resulting experience into the next version of its generalist model [7]. SOAR used a vision-language model to propose language-specified tasks that a robot should practise and to judge whether each attempt succeeded, and used the resulting autonomously collected data to improve the policy [95]. Ghasemipour and colleagues fine-tuned robot foundation models to predict, in addition to actions, how many steps remained until success, and used that prediction as a self-generated reward for autonomous practice on real robots [24]. Self-generated labels and rewards are efficient but risky: a system that judges its own success can reinforce its own errors, as NELL's designers observed in a different domain [16], so autonomous practice should be anchored by some independent signal, such as a calibrated success detector, human spot checks or the validation suite of Section 6.

5.4 Updates that break old behaviour

Every incremental update carries the risk that McCloskey and Cohen called catastrophic interference: a network trained sequentially on new data loses some of what it learned from old data, because the same weights serve both [17,18]. The continual-learning literature offers three families of remedies, regularizing the weights that mattered for earlier data (as elastic weight consolidation does), replaying stored or generated old data, and adding new parameters while freezing existing ones [38,96,97], and benchmarks such as LIBERO measure forgetting in lifelong robot manipulation [2,98]. For a self-improving system the practical consequences are two. First, the data engine should keep the data behind earlier fixes, so that each candidate can be trained with replay of what already works. Second, and more fundamental, no training method removes the need to test old behaviour again: forgetting in a deployed robot reintroduces failures that were fixed in earlier releases, which is why the validation stage in Section 6 runs regression tests over every previously fixed failure mode.

Fine-tuning on the new data fixes condition B completely and breaks condition A just as completely: 70% of light-payload runs, which never failed before, now fail. A validation suite that only checked the newly fixed failures would have released this version. Replay of a buffer only a tenth the size of the original data solves both conditions, consistent with the robustness of replay reported in the continual-learning literature [2,96], and joint retraining does too, at the cost of keeping and reprocessing all old data. The general point is the one that matters for deployment: an update that fixes the newest failure can silently break old behaviour, and only testing the old behaviour again reveals it.

6 Validating improvements before deployment

6.1 What a release decision must establish

A candidate policy produced by the loop has to clear three bars before it replaces the incumbent. It must be better where the incumbent was weak, on the failure modes that triggered the update. It must be no worse anywhere else, on every behaviour that already worked, including every previously fixed failure. And it must be acceptable in absolute terms, meeting the safety requirements of the system regardless of how it compares with its predecessor [9,36]. Machine-learning engineering practice turns these bars into infrastructure: Breck and colleagues' "ML test score" rubric for production systems asks, among other things, that models be tested against a previous version before release, rolled out through a canary process, and capable of being rolled back quickly and safely [10,99].

The workhorse is a regression suite: a curated library of scenarios, in simulation and on test rigs, that grows with every failure the system has ever had, every scenario family generated in Section 4, and the nominal operating conditions. Simulation makes such suites cheap to run, but simulated results are only evidence about the real world to the extent that the two correlate. Kadian and colleagues measured this "sim-to-real predictivity" for visual navigation and found that the correlation between simulated and real performance of different agents was weak with the default simulator settings and improved substantially after the simulator was tuned to the real setup [100]. Li and colleagues built simulated counterparts of real manipulation evaluation setups and showed that, once visual and control gaps were reduced, the ranking of policies in simulation tracked their ranking on real robots [101]. A suite's simulator therefore needs its own validation, and real-world tests remain the final check.

6.2 Statistical acceptance tests

Physical tests are expensive, so the number of trials is always limited, and the question is what a limited number of trials can prove. When n independent trials produce no failures, the one-sided 95% upper confidence bound on the failure probability is approximately 3/n, the "rule of three" [102]; with failures observed, the exact Clopper–Pearson interval applies [103]. Turned around, demonstrating a failure rate below p needs about 3/p failure-free trials, which is why Kalra and Paddock concluded that demonstrating automated-vehicle fatality rates better than human drivers by driving alone would take hundreds of millions of miles or more [32]. Comparisons between versions face the same arithmetic, plus a multiple-comparison problem: a gate that checks many scenario slices at the 5% level will find a spurious "regression" somewhere unless the family-wise error is controlled, for example with Holm's step-down procedure [104]. Deep reinforcement learning results have repeatedly been shown to be fragile under such scrutiny, which has led to recommendations to report interval estimates over runs rather than point estimates [105,106].

Sequential tests reduce the cost. Wald's sequential probability ratio test (SPRT) updates the log-likelihood ratio between two hypotheses after every trial and stops as soon as it crosses one of two thresholds set by the desired error rates; it minimizes the expected number of trials at the two hypothesized values among tests with the same error rates [107]. For release decisions this means that a clearly better or clearly worse candidate is decided quickly, and trial budget is spent only on close calls.

6.4 Off-policy evaluation from logged data

Not every candidate has to be tested in new trials. If the incumbent's logs record the probability with which it chose each action, the value of a different policy can be estimated from those logs by importance sampling: each logged outcome is reweighted by the ratio of the probability that the candidate would have chosen the logged action to the probability that the incumbent did [108]. Importance sampling is unbiased but can have very high variance when the candidate differs from the logging policy. The direct method instead fits a model of outcomes and evaluates the candidate in it, which has low variance but is biased when the model is wrong. Doubly robust estimators combine the two and remain accurate if either the model or the importance weights are good [109]. High-confidence off-policy evaluation turns such estimates into lower bounds on performance that hold with a chosen probability [110], and Seldonian algorithms use the same bounds to return a new policy only if it is unlikely to violate user-specified constraints, otherwise reporting "no solution found" [111]. Benchmarks of deep off-policy evaluation show, however, that estimates for continuous-control robot policies can be unreliable, especially when the candidate is far from the logging policy [112]. Running a candidate in "shadow mode", computing its actions on live data without executing them, raises the same issue: it shows where the candidate would have acted differently, but what would have happened next is counterfactual and has to be estimated in the same way.

6.6 Formal verification, safety filters and safety cases

Testing samples behaviour; verification aims to cover it. Reluplex extended the simplex method to verify properties of neural networks with ReLU activations and was used to prove properties of a prototype airborne collision avoidance network [113]. Verification of whole learned policies in closed loop with realistic dynamics and perception remains far harder, so practical systems rely on safety filters whose guarantees do not depend on the learned policy: control barrier functions, predictive safety filters and reachability-based shields that minimally modify any command that would leave a verified safe set, as described in the reactive behavior and safe RL articles [11,114,115]. A self-improving system gains a great deal from this split: the learned policy can change frequently, while the filter, which changes rarely, carries the safety argument.

That argument is written down as a safety case: a structured argument, supported by evidence, that a system is acceptably safe in a given context. Functional-safety standards such as ISO 26262 and IEC 61508 require one for safety-related electronic systems [116,117]; ISO 21448 adds the safety of the intended functionality, including the discovery of unknown unsafe scenarios, and ISO/PAS 8800 adds AI-specific requirements on data, training and monitoring [34,35]. UL 4600 is written for autonomous products and requires the safety case to be maintained through the product's life, including after updates and in response to field data, which matches the loop of Figure 1 [36]. Webb and colleagues describe how an automated-driving developer combined hazard analyses, simulation, closed-course and on-road testing into a readiness determination before driverless operation [118], and Koopman and Wagner discuss why machine-learning components make such arguments harder: their requirements are implicit in training data, and their behaviour can change in non-obvious ways when they are retrained [9,119].

7 Closing the loop: architectures and governance

7.1 Data engines for physical systems

In practice the loop of Figure 1 is implemented as a data engine: infrastructure that logs fleet data, selects what is worth keeping (triggered by monitors, interventions, near misses or disagreement between the policy and a reference), routes it to labelling or reconstruction, generates scenario families, trains candidates, runs them through validation, and manages staged release and rollback. Its components are ordinary machine-learning infrastructure, and so are its characteristic failure modes: Sculley and colleagues warned that the code of a learned model is a small fraction of a production system, and that hidden feedback loops, entangled data dependencies and undeclared consumers of a model's outputs make such systems costly to maintain [10,99]. Physical systems add hardware in the loop. Practice needs resets, so autonomous practice systems learn reset or recovery behaviours to keep going without a human [120,121], and exploration during practice must stay within safe limits, for which the safe-learning literature offers constrained optimization, safe Bayesian optimization of controller parameters and safety filters [11,87,122].

Published robot systems show the range of designs. Fleet grasp learning and QT-Opt pooled the data of several robots to train one policy, with the robots collecting more data under the improved policy [6,19]. Fleet-DAgger and Sirius put remote humans in the loop, so that their interventions are both safety mechanism and training signal [64,65]. RoboCat, SOAR and self-improving embodied foundation models replace part of the human effort with autonomous practice judged by learned success detectors or progress estimates [7,24,95]. Automated-driving developers combine fleet data, scenario-based simulation and staged testing into a release process with formal readiness determinations [75,118].

7.2 Pitfalls of systems that learn from themselves

  • Feedback loops. A deployed policy shapes the data it will later learn from. Perdomo and colleagues formalized this as performative prediction and showed that repeated retraining can converge to a stable point that is not optimal, or fail to converge at all [123]. A robot that learns to avoid a difficult corridor will stop collecting data about that corridor, and may later lose the ability to traverse it.
  • Self-confirmation. When the same model proposes, executes and judges its own practice, errors in the judge become errors in the policy, as the never-ending learner's designers observed [16]. Independent signals, human spot checks and held-out validation suites are the countermeasure.
  • Specification gaming. Optimizing a proxy, such as a learned success detector, a robustness margin or a simplified reward, invites solutions that score well without doing what was intended [124]. Falsifiers can game simulators in the same way, finding "failures" that are artefacts of simulator physics rather than real hazards, which is why generated failures need plausibility checks [3,9].
  • Drift of the world, not just the policy. Operating conditions change continuously, so a validation suite that is never refreshed measures a world that no longer exists [47,48].
  • Regression through forgetting. As Section 5 showed, every update can reintroduce fixed failures; regression suites must grow monotonically.

7.3 Governance: who decides what ships

The more autonomous the loop, the more important it is to specify in advance which changes it may make on its own. Regulators have started to provide this structure. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's guidance on predetermined change control plans lets manufacturers of AI-enabled medical devices obtain authorization for a plan that describes the modifications they intend to make, the methods for developing and validating them, and an assessment of their impact, so that changes within the plan do not require a new marketing submission [37]. The EU AI Act regulates high-risk AI systems, a category that includes AI safety components of products such as machinery, and treats changes to a system that continues to learn after being placed on the market as not constituting a substantial modification if they were predetermined by the provider and assessed at the initial conformity assessment [40]. UL 4600 requires the safety case of an autonomous product to be updated in response to field data and design changes [36]. For a self-improving physical system, these frameworks suggest a concrete design rule: write down the envelope of changes the loop may make automatically (which parameters, which data, which validation thresholds), and route everything outside that envelope to human review.

8.2 Evaluating a self-improving system

A self-improving system should be evaluated as a process, not only as a sequence of models. Useful measures include the time from the first occurrence of a failure mode to its detection and to a released fix; the field failure rate over time under drifting conditions, as in Section 7.4; the regression rate, meaning the fraction of releases that reintroduce a previously fixed failure; the coverage of failure modes by the scenario library; the false-alarm rate of monitors; and the human effort per fix [2,3,10]. Statistical reporting matters as much here as for any learning result: intervals over seeds and over deployment rounds, not single runs [105,106].

8.3 Open problems

  • Coverage you can measure. There is no agreed way to say when a scenario library covers the operating domain well enough; falsification finds failures but cannot prove their absence [3,9,34].
  • Trustworthy self-assessment. Foundation-model failure detectors and success judges make autonomous improvement possible, but calibrating and validating the judges themselves is largely unsolved [24,60,63].
  • Sim-to-real validity of validation. Simulation-based regression suites are only as good as their predictivity, which must itself be monitored as the world and the simulator change [100,101].
  • Guarantees under change. Safety filters keep guarantees independent of the learned policy, but scaling verified safe sets to high-dimensional, contact-rich, perception-driven tasks remains hard [11,87,113].
  • Learning in the loop without feedback pathologies. Performative effects, self-confirmation and drifting suites can make a loop converge to the wrong place, and there is little theory for when a real data engine is stable [16,48,123].
  • Regulation of continuous change. Predetermined change envelopes are a promising start, but translating them into technical criteria for robot policies that keep learning is still open [36,37,40].

Conclusion

A self-improving physical system turns deployment into a source of improvement without turning customers into test subjects. It detects failures and near misses with monitors whose alarm rates are calibrated, explains and clusters them into modes, multiplies each mode into families of training scenarios by falsification, perturbation and reconstruction, adapts its policy using those scenarios together with replay of what already works, and releases a candidate only after regression suites, statistical gates and safety arguments show that it is better where it was weak and no worse anywhere else. The examples in this article show the recurring lessons in small form: residual and novelty monitors catch different problems, and conformal calibration makes their thresholds meaningful; optimization finds failures quickly but collapses onto one mode unless pushed toward coverage; a fix trained on new data alone can break old behaviour; rare-failure claims are expensive, sequential tests and slice-wise gates spend trials wisely, and logged data can screen candidates that stay close to what was logged; and a loop that generates scenarios around near misses keeps up with drift that a frozen policy cannot. The hardest open questions are not about learning faster but about knowing, with evidence a regulator and the public can accept, that each change made the system better.
