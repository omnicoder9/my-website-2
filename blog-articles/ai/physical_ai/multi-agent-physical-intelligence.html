1 What multi-agent physical intelligence is

Most of this series has looked at one robot: how it senses, models the world, plans, controls its body and reacts when things go wrong. Many of the most valuable physical-AI systems, however, are not one robot. A fulfilment centre runs hundreds of drive units that carry shelves to pickers [1]; a city's roads are shared by human drivers, cyclists, pedestrians and, increasingly, automated vehicles [2]; search-and-rescue teams combine legged, wheeled and flying robots that must map a mine or a cave together [3]; and in factories, collaborative robots work beside people at the same bench [4,5]. Multi-agent physical intelligence is the ability of such collections of embodied agents, artificial and human, to perceive, decide and act so that the group achieves what no member could achieve alone, safely and efficiently, in the physical world [6–9].

Three features distinguish the multi-agent problem from the single-robot one. First, coupling: agents share space, resources and goals, so one agent's action changes what the others can and should do. The joint state and action spaces grow exponentially with the number of agents, which is why even planning collision-free paths for many robots is computationally hard [10,11]. Second, distributed information: each agent sees only part of the world through its own sensors and learns about the rest through communication that is limited in bandwidth, range and reliability. Optimal decentralized decision-making under partial observability is, in the worst case, far harder than its single-agent counterpart (NEXP-complete for finite-horizon decentralized POMDPs, against PSPACE-complete for POMDPs) [12,13]. Third, other minds: when other agents are people or independently designed machines, they have their own objectives and models, and a good agent must predict, influence and adapt to them rather than command them [14,15].

A useful way to organize the field is by three questions, illustrated in Figure 1. Who decides? A centralized system computes everyone's actions in one place; a decentralized one lets each agent decide from local information; distributed systems sit in between, with agents computing together by exchanging messages [16,17]. What do the agents want? Teams are fully cooperative and share one objective; traffic is mixed, with individual objectives and a shared interest in not colliding; adversarial settings such as pursuit or security are competitive [7,18]. Who are the agents? Homogeneous swarms of identical robots, heterogeneous teams of different machines, and human–robot teams in which some members cannot be programmed at all [19–21].

Every architecture trades three things against each other. Centralized coordination can be optimal and is easy to reason about, but it needs a reliable network, has a single point of failure, and its computation grows with the team. Decentralized coordination scales and degrades gracefully, but local decisions can be inconsistent, myopic or deadlocked. Distributed algorithms recover some global optimality through iteration and communication at the cost of latency [8,22,23]. The rest of this article follows these trade-offs through the main technical layers of a multi-agent system. Several neighbouring topics are covered elsewhere in the series and are linked rather than repeated: multi-agent path finding, conflict-based search and reciprocal collision avoidance [24–26] in Motion Planning, Section 9; multi-robot task allocation and auctions [27,28] in Task Planning, Section 7; speed and separation monitoring around people in Reactive Behavior, Section 4; multi-object tracking in Sensor Fusion, Section 6; and the single-agent foundations of learning in Reinforcement Learning.

2 A short history: from contract nets to robot fleets

2.1 Distributed AI and the first robot collectives

Multi-agent systems began in distributed artificial intelligence. Smith's contract net protocol (1980) let nodes announce tasks, bid on them and award contracts, the ancestor of every market-based robot allocator since [22,29]. In robotics, the late 1980s brought the first explicitly multi-robot architectures: Fukuda's CEBOT, a dynamically reconfigurable system of cooperating cells [30], and Asama's ACTRESS, which coordinated autonomous robots through explicit communication [31]. In the 1990s, behaviour-based robotics, already influential for single robots, was extended to groups. Matarić showed that simple local behaviours such as following, aggregation and dispersion could be combined into flocking and foraging in physical robot groups [32]; Parker's ALLIANCE architecture used motivational behaviours (impatience and acquiescence) so that robots would take over tasks from failed teammates without central control [33]; and Balch and Arkin built formation keeping out of motor schemas [34]. Cao, Fukunaga and Kahng's 1997 survey framed cooperative mobile robotics as a field [8], and Stone and Veloso's 2000 survey connected it to machine learning [9].

Games became benchmarks. RoboCup, launched in 1997, proposed robot soccer as a standard problem for artificial intelligence and robotics and made real-time teamwork among physical robots, under uncertainty and with limited communication, a research staple [35]. In parallel, physicists and biologists studied collective motion: Reynolds's boids reproduced flocking from three local rules [36], and Vicsek's self-propelled particle model showed a phase transition from disorder to aligned motion as noise decreases [37].

2.2 Control theory of networked systems

The 2000s gave multi-agent coordination a mathematical backbone. Jadbabaie, Lin and Morse proved that Vicsek-style nearest-neighbour averaging makes headings converge if the interaction graph is connected often enough [38]. Olfati-Saber and Murray analyzed consensus with switching topologies and delays [39], Ren and Beard gave conditions for directed, changing graphs [40], and Fax and Murray linked formation stability to the eigenvalues of the graph Laplacian [41]. These results, synthesized in surveys and textbooks [16,17,42], drew on older work on averaging opinions [43] and on distributed asynchronous optimization [44]. Cortés and colleagues' coverage control turned Lloyd's quantization algorithm into a distributed controller for sensor deployment [45,46].

2.3 From laboratories to fleets

Multi-agent physical systems reached industrial scale in the same decade. Kiva Systems coordinated hundreds of mobile drive units in warehouses, a deployment that later motivated lifelong multi-agent path finding [1,47]. The 2007 DARPA Urban Challenge required autonomous cars to obey traffic rules while interacting with other autonomous and human-driven vehicles at intersections and in merges [48], and research on platooning that began in California's PATH programme matured into cooperative adaptive cruise control tested in real traffic [49,50]. Swarm robotics demonstrated scale with a thousand-robot Kilobot swarm [51], termite-inspired construction robots [52], outdoor drone flocks [53] and micro-aerial swarms flying through forests [54]. In the DARPA Subterranean Challenge (2018–2021), teams of heterogeneous robots explored mines, tunnels and caves with intermittent communication, a demanding test of collaborative mapping and autonomy [3].

2.4 Learning and foundation models

Machine learning reshaped the field after 2015. Deep multi-agent reinforcement learning produced centralized-training, decentralized-execution methods such as MADDPG, COMA and QMIX [55–57], and large-scale self-play reached grandmaster level in the real-time strategy game StarCraft II [58]. Learned policies moved onto physical teams for decentralized collision avoidance [59,60], quadrotor swarms [61] and, in simulation, humanoid football played as a team [62]. Most recently, large language models have been used to plan and negotiate among robots [63–65], and fleets of robots now collect shared data for generalist policies, turning a fleet itself into a learning system [66,67].

3 Foundations: graphs, consensus and formations

3.1 The communication graph

The basic object of networked multi-agent control is a graph whose vertices are agents and whose edges say who can sense or talk to whom. For an undirected graph with adjacency matrix A and degree matrix D, the graph Laplacian L = D − A is symmetric and positive semidefinite, its smallest eigenvalue is zero with the all-ones eigenvector, and the second-smallest eigenvalue λ2, Fiedler's algebraic connectivity, is positive exactly when the graph is connected [17,68]. Most of what follows rests on this one fact: λ2 measures how well information mixes across the team, and so how fast distributed algorithms converge and how strongly a formation resists disturbances [41,42]. For robots, the graph is not fixed. Range-limited radios and sensors make it depend on positions, so motion changes the graph and the graph constrains motion; connectivity-maintenance controllers keep λ2 above a threshold while the team moves [69].

3.2 Consensus

Many coordination problems reduce to agreement: on a rendezvous point, a heading, a clock, a sensor average or a task assignment. In the average consensus protocol each agent repeatedly replaces its value by a weighted average of its own and its neighbours' values,

xi(k+1) = Wii xi(k) + Σj ∈ N(i) Wij xj(k),

or in continuous time ẋ = −Lx. If the graph is connected and the weight matrix W is symmetric and doubly stochastic, every agent converges to the exact average of the initial values, and the error shrinks geometrically at a rate set by the second-largest eigenvalue modulus of W (in continuous time, by λ2) [39,70]. The same analysis extends to directed and switching graphs, which converge when the union of graphs over bounded intervals contains a spanning tree [38,40], and to asynchronous random pairwise gossip [71]. Metropolis–Hastings weights, Wij = 1/(1 + max(di, dj)), need only the degrees of the two endpoints, so each robot can compute them locally [72]. Xiao and Boyd showed that weights can also be optimized for speed by semidefinite programming [70].

Consensus is a building block for distributed optimization. In distributed subgradient methods each agent mixes its neighbours' estimates and then takes a step on its own local cost, so the team minimizes a sum of private costs without anyone collecting all the data [44,73]; the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) achieves the same with faster, more robust convergence and underlies many distributed model predictive controllers [74,75].

For a robot team the practical lesson is that topology is a design variable: adding a few long links (or a relay) can cut agreement time by an order of magnitude.

3.3 Formation control

Formation control keeps a group in a prescribed geometric pattern while it moves, for convoys, aerial imaging, cooperative transport and sensing arrays [76,77]. Oh, Park and Ahn classify approaches by what each agent senses and controls [76]. In position-based schemes every robot tracks an absolute slot and needs global positioning. In displacement-based schemes robots control relative positions pj − pi to desired offsets; this is consensus on the deviations from the shape, so all the graph results apply, and robots need a common sense of direction but no global frame [40,41]. In distance-based schemes robots control only inter-robot distances, which needs no common frame but requires a rigid graph to pin down the shape and has only local convergence guarantees [78]. Leader–follower designs, in which followers keep a set range and bearing to a leader, are common on nonholonomic ground robots [79], and behaviour-based formations blend formation keeping with obstacle avoidance [34]. Formations must also stay collision-free; control barrier functions provide minimally invasive safety filters for robot teams [80].

Two lessons emerge. Without feedforward, the followers learn about the leader's motion only through the graph, so they lag behind it, and the lag is roughly halved when two chords raise λ2 from 1.0 to 1.44. Broadcasting the reference velocity removes most of the lag; what remains is the response to the drifting robot, which a better-connected graph again rejects more strongly. When links are lost the formation degrades gracefully as long as the graph stays connected, but when it splits, the part that contains the drifting robot has no way to learn that it is wrong and wanders away, with an error that grows without bound. This is the networked version of an old control lesson: feedback from neighbours rejects disturbances, feedforward tracks references, and neither works across a disconnected graph [41,42,69].

Event-triggered communication, in which a robot transmits only when its state has drifted enough from what neighbours last heard, reduces bandwidth while preserving these properties [81]. At very large scale, the same ideas appear as flocking with separation, alignment and cohesion terms [36,53,82] and as model-predictive swarm controllers that share predicted trajectories [75,83].

4 Collective perception, estimation and coverage

4.1 Cooperative localization

Robots that can measure each other can localize better together than apart. Kurazume and colleagues' cooperative positioning let robots take turns acting as landmarks for each other [84]; Fox and colleagues extended Monte Carlo localization so that a robot that detects another robot can transfer its belief and sharply reduce both robots' uncertainty [85]; and Roumeliotis and Bekey decomposed a team-wide Kalman filter so that each robot runs its own propagation and the team exchanges information only when relative measurements are taken [86]. The single-robot estimators behind these methods are covered in Sensor Fusion, Section 3.

4.2 Decentralized data fusion and the double-counting problem

The central difficulty of decentralized estimation is correlation. When robot A sends its estimate to B, and B later sends a fused estimate back, A's own information returns disguised as new evidence. A filter that treats it as independent becomes overconfident: its covariance shrinks while its actual error does not, and it will eventually reject correct measurements [87,88]. Three families of solutions exist. Information filters on trees track exactly what has been shared along each channel and subtract it, which is optimal but requires a tree-shaped network [87]. Consensus filters run average consensus on information vectors and matrices so that every node approaches the centralized estimate [72,89]. Covariance intersection (CI) fuses estimates with unknown correlation by a convex combination of their information matrices, P−1 = Σ ωi Pi−1 with Σ ωi = 1, which is guaranteed consistent for any correlation [88]. Fast heuristics choose the weights in proportion to each input's inverse trace [90], and CI is used for decentralized cooperative localization on real robot teams [91]. The single-sensor treatment of unknown correlations is in Sensor Fusion, Section 8.

In multi-robot systems, a consistent estimate that is slightly worse is almost always preferable to an inconsistent one that is slightly better [87,88,91].

4.3 Multi-robot SLAM and shared maps

Collaborative SLAM merges partial maps built by different robots into one consistent map. Early systems used particle filters and required knowing or discovering the robots' relative poses [92]. Modern systems are built on factor graphs: DDF-SAM had each robot share condensed summaries of its local graph [93]; distributed Gauss–Seidel optimization reached near-centralized accuracy while exchanging little data [94]; DOOR-SLAM added peer-to-peer communication and robust rejection of wrong inter-robot loop closures [95]; and Kimera-Multi built dense metric-semantic maps across robots with a distributed robust solver [96]. Outlier rejection is essential: one false inter-robot loop closure can corrupt every robot's map. A survey by Lajoie and colleagues reviews the landscape [97], and single-robot SLAM is covered in Sensor Fusion, Section 6.

Connected vehicles extend shared perception to the road. Cooperative perception lets vehicles and roadside units exchange raw point clouds, intermediate features or object lists to see around occlusions; early fusion is most accurate but costs the most bandwidth [98]. Datasets and benchmarks such as OPV2V and DAIR-V2X, and attention-based fusion models such as V2X-ViT, have made this an active area [99–101], together with learned policies for deciding when and with whom to communicate [102].

4.4 Coverage, exploration and persistent monitoring

Where should a team of robots be? Coverage control answers for sensing: given a density φ(q) describing how important each point is, place robots to minimize H = Σi ∫Vi ‖q − pi‖² φ(q) dq, where Vi is robot i's Voronoi cell. Cortés and colleagues showed that moving each robot toward the centroid of its cell, a continuous-time version of Lloyd's algorithm, decreases H monotonically and needs only Voronoi neighbours' positions [45,46]; Schwager and colleagues let robots learn the density online from their own measurements [103].

Exploration asks how a team should map an unknown environment. Yamauchi's frontier-based exploration sends robots to the boundary between known and unknown space [104]; Burgard and colleagues coordinated robots by trading off a frontier's utility against its cost and discounting frontiers near those already assigned to teammates, which spreads the team and reduces overlap [105]. Such assignment problems are instances of the multi-robot task allocation methods described in Task Planning, Section 7.3.

5 Communication: what to share, when, and with whom

Communication is what makes a group of robots a team rather than a crowd, and it is usually the binding constraint. Radio links have limited range and bandwidth, are blocked by walls, rock and bodies, suffer latency and loss, and their quality depends on where the robots are [23]. Gielis, Shankar and Prorok's review argues that multi-robot research often abstracts communication as perfect or as a fixed disk model, and that system design should instead treat what, when and to whom to communicate as decisions [23].

Several strategies address this. Implicit coordination avoids messages altogether: reciprocal velocity obstacles and buffered Voronoi cells need only neighbours' observed positions and velocities [106,107], and robots can infer teammates' intentions from their motion. Communication-aware planning keeps the team connected [69] or plans periodic rendezvous so that robots can work out of range and meet to share data, as in Hollinger and Singh's periodic-connectivity planner [108]; in the Subterranean Challenge, robots deployed their own communication relay nodes as they advanced underground [3]. Event-triggered schemes send only when information has changed enough to matter [81]. Learned communication treats messages as part of the policy: CommNet and DIAL learned continuous messages end to end [109,110], TarMAC added attention so that agents choose recipients [111], and graph neural networks, which aggregate features over a communication graph, learn decentralized controllers for flocking and multi-robot path planning that generalize to larger teams than they were trained on [112,113].

Middleware matters too. ROS 2 uses the Data Distribution Service, whose quality-of-service settings let designers choose between reliable and best-effort delivery per topic, which is important on lossy wireless links [114]. A common engineering pattern is to separate a small, reliable, low-rate channel for coordination state (roles, task claims, reservations) from a lossy, high-rate channel for perception data that can be dropped when bandwidth is short [23,114].

6 Multi-agent reinforcement learning

6.1 Models: stochastic games and decentralized POMDPs

Reinforcement learning extends to many agents through game theory. Shapley's stochastic games (1953) generalize Markov decision processes to several players, each with its own reward [115]; Littman brought them into reinforcement learning as Markov games [116]. The solution concept is usually an equilibrium rather than an optimum. In a Nash equilibrium no agent can gain by changing its policy alone [117], and general-sum games can have many equilibria, so agents must also coordinate on which one to play [7]. Fully cooperative teams with a shared reward and private observations are modelled as decentralized POMDPs (Dec-POMDPs) [13]. Their worst-case complexity, NEXP-complete for finite horizons, reflects the fact that each agent must reason about what the others have seen [12]. The single-agent MDP and POMDP foundations are in Reinforcement Learning, Sections 3–4.

6.2 Independent learners and their failure modes

The simplest approach lets each agent learn as if the others were part of the environment. Tan showed in 1993 that such independent learners can work but that sharing observations, episodes or policies helps [118]. The difficulty is that each learner's environment is non-stationary: as teammates learn, the transition and reward distributions it sees change, which breaks the convergence guarantees of single-agent methods [18,119]. Claus and Boutilier's climbing game exposes a specific failure called relative overgeneralization: an independent learner evaluates each of its actions averaged over its partner's exploratory actions, so an action that is optimal only when the partner coordinates looks worse than a safe one [120]. Lenient learners forgive a teammate's exploration early in learning by updating on optimistic samples [121].

Lenient learners and a learner over joint actions both find the optimum (a,a) every time. The joint learner pays for this with a table whose size grows as mn for n robots with m actions each, which is why modern methods learn joint value functions only during training and execute decentralized policies.

6.3 Centralized training with decentralized execution

The dominant paradigm in deep multi-agent RL is centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE): during training in simulation, a learner may use global state and all agents' actions; at execution, each agent acts on its own observations. MADDPG gives each agent a centralized critic that sees everyone's actions, which removes the non-stationarity from the critic's point of view [55]. COMA uses a counterfactual baseline that asks how much better the team did than if one agent had acted differently, addressing credit assignment [56]. Value decomposition methods learn per-agent utilities whose sum (VDN) or monotonic mixture (QMIX) equals the team value, so that each agent's greedy action is consistent with the joint greedy action [57,122]. Simple baselines are surprisingly strong: multi-agent PPO with a centralized value function matched or beat specialized methods on standard benchmarks [123]. For very large populations, mean-field methods replace interactions with many neighbours by interaction with their average [124]. Textbooks and surveys cover the theory [125,126].

Multi-agent learning also produces behaviour that nobody designed. In OpenAI's hide-and-seek environment, competing teams developed a sequence of strategies and counter-strategies, including using boxes and ramps as tools [127]; AlphaStar combined imitation and league-based self-play to reach grandmaster level in StarCraft II [58]; and DeepMind trained simulated humanoids from motor control up to coordinated team play in football [62].

6.4 Learning for physical teams

On real robots, multi-agent RL has been most successful when the learned part is local and the team structure is simple. Decentralized collision-avoidance policies trained with RL on many simulated robots transfer to physical teams and crowds [59,60,128]; end-to-end policies have controlled quadrotor swarms in close proximity [61]; and graph neural network policies coordinate decentralized path planning [113]. Competitive physical play is also within reach: deep RL trained a small bipedal robot to play agile one-versus-one soccer, transferred zero-shot from simulation [129]. Vectorized simulators such as VMAS make it practical to train collective robot behaviours with thousands of parallel environments [130]. The sim-to-real techniques that make single-robot RL deployable, from domain randomization to privileged teachers, carry over (see Reinforcement Learning, Section 10). The challenges particular to teams are partner diversity (a policy trained with copies of itself may fail with different partners), credit assignment over long horizons, safety during learning, and the gap between simulated and real communication [23,119,131].

7 Autonomous vehicles as interacting agents

7.1 Prediction, planning and the frozen robot

Driving is the largest multi-agent physical system that people use every day, and it is mixed-motive: each road user has its own destination, but all share an interest in safety and flow [2]. The conventional autonomy stack predicts other agents' future trajectories and then plans against those predictions. Multi-agent trajectory forecasting has advanced rapidly with graph and transformer models trained on large interactive datasets [132–134], and planning-oriented end-to-end models predict and plan jointly [135]. Treating predictions as fixed, however, ignores that other agents respond to the robot. A planner that assumes others will not yield can find every option unsafe and stop, the frozen robot problem that Trautman and Krause identified in dense crowds; modelling cooperation, the fact that people make room for each other, resolves it [136,137].

7.2 Game-theoretic interaction

Game-theoretic planners model interaction explicitly. Sadigh and colleagues modelled the human driver as optimizing a reward learned from data and the robot car as a leader that chooses actions anticipating the human's best response, a Stackelberg game; the robot learned to influence humans, for example by nudging toward a human's lane so that the human slows down, or by backing up slightly at an intersection so that the human proceeds first [14]. Hierarchical game-theoretic planning combines a long-horizon simplified game with short-horizon detailed planning [138]; iterative linear-quadratic methods (iLQGames) and augmented Lagrangian solvers (ALGAMES) compute approximate Nash equilibria of general-sum differential games fast enough for online use [139,140]; and social value orientation estimates how altruistic or egoistic other drivers are and uses that to predict their behaviour in merges [141]. Safety rules can be formalized as well; Responsibility-Sensitive Safety defines safe distances and proper responses so that a car following the rules is never to blame for a collision [142].

If the robot assumes the other car will hold its speed, it brakes hard and yields. If both cars reason that way, both brake and neither crosses within five seconds: a deadlock in miniature, inefficient for both. The game has three pure Nash equilibria, two in which the human goes first and one in which the robot does, and which one iterated best response finds depends only on how it is initialized. A Stackelberg leader picks the equilibrium that favours it: when the robot leads, it accelerates and the human yields, with a safe 8.8 m minimum gap. Equilibrium selection, not just equilibrium computation, is the practical problem. Courteous or socially compliant planners deliberately bias selection toward outcomes that respect other road users, and conventions such as right of way exist precisely to make the selection predictable [14,138,141].

7.3 Cooperative driving: platoons and string stability

When vehicles cooperate explicitly, they can drive closer together and smooth traffic. A key property is string stability: a disturbance, such as the lead car braking, should shrink rather than grow as it propagates down a column of vehicles [143,144]. Human drivers are not string stable at typical headways; in Sugiyama's experiment, cars driving on a circular track formed stop-and-go waves with no bottleneck at all [145]. Adaptive cruise control (ACC), which uses only on-board radar, needs a relatively large time gap for string stability. Cooperative adaptive cruise control (CACC) adds the preceding vehicle's acceleration, received by vehicle-to-vehicle radio, as a feedforward term, which allows much shorter gaps [50,146,147]. A few automated vehicles can also help human traffic. In a field experiment, a single automated vehicle driving around a ring with about twenty human-driven cars dampened the stop-and-go waves that formed without it [148]; reinforcement learning in the Flow framework has been used to design such mixed-autonomy controllers [149]. At intersections, Dresner and Stone's autonomous intersection management replaced traffic lights with a reservation protocol in which vehicles request space-time slots from an intersection manager, sharply reducing delays in simulation [150]. Traffic simulators such as SUMO and data-driven multi-agent simulators make these designs testable at scale [151–153].

8 Human–robot teams

8.1 What makes a robot a teammate

Human teamwork has been studied for decades, and its theories shaped multi-agent AI. Cohen and Levesque defined joint intentions: team members are committed not only to a goal but to letting each other know when it is achieved, impossible or irrelevant [154]. Grosz and Kraus's SharedPlans formalized how agents build and refine a common plan from individual intentions and beliefs [155], and Tambe's STEAM turned these ideas into a reusable teamwork model for agent teams [156]. Klein and colleagues set out ten challenges for making automation a "team player": it must enter a basic compact with its human partners, keep its state and intentions observable and predictable, be directable, and help maintain common ground [21].

Human factors research adds the human side. Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens described automation in terms of levels and of the information-processing stages it supports [157]. Trust should be calibrated to the automation's actual reliability; both over-trust and under-trust cause failures [158], and robot performance is the strongest factor affecting human trust in robots [159]. Supervisors must maintain situation awareness [160], and when one person supervises several robots, each robot's neglect tolerance, how long it can work well unattended, limits how many robots one person can manage [161,162]. Scaled autonomy learns when a fleet's robots most need a human's attention [163]. Surveys of human–robot interaction and of safe human–robot interaction cover the wider field [164,165].

8.2 Predicting and inferring people

To work with people, a robot must anticipate them. Baker, Saxe and Tenenbaum modelled human action understanding as inverse planning: observers assume that agents act approximately rationally toward goals and invert that model with Bayes' rule to infer the goal from the observed actions [166]. Robots use the same principle for intention recognition. Early prediction of a person's reaching target, from the first few hundred milliseconds of motion, lets a robot arm plan around where the person is about to be [167,168], and integrating motion prediction with planning in time made a mobile manipulator a faster, more fluent assistant in collaborative assembly [169]. In shared autonomy, the robot infers the user's goal and assists toward it [170,171], and cooperative inverse reinforcement learning formalizes a robot that acts to learn and serve a human's objective [172].

8.3 Legibility, fluency and shared work

Inference works in both directions: people must also infer what the robot intends. Dragan, Lee and Srinivasa distinguished predictable motion, which matches what an observer expects given a known goal, from legible motion, which lets an observer infer an unknown goal quickly and confidently; the two often conflict, and legible motion exaggerates away from competing goals [173]. Humanoid robots add gaze and body language to these cues (see Humanoid Intelligence). Hoffman and Breazeal showed that anticipatory action selection makes human–robot collaboration more fluent [174], and Hoffman later proposed objective fluency metrics such as idle time and concurrent activity [175]. Training matters: in human–robot cross-training, people and robots switch roles during practice, which improved team performance and human ratings compared with standard reinforcement-learning-style training [176]. How work is allocated also matters. Gombolay and colleagues found that letting a robot schedule a mixed human–robot team was more efficient and was preferred by workers to allocating all tasks themselves [177].

The confidence threshold trades speed against errors. With a steady hand, committing at 60% confidence lets the robot act 38% of the way through the human's reach with no mistakes, while waiting for 90% delays it until 70% of the reach. For the robot's own reach, the straight path is the most efficient and predictable, but an observer is only 50% sure of its goal after a fifth of the motion. Bending the path by 0.2 m raises that to 71% for a 6% longer path, and further exaggeration keeps improving legibility while costing ever more effort; with an explicit effort penalty, the best trade-off is a moderate bend, as Dragan and colleagues found when they constrained legible trajectories to stay close to efficient ones [173].

8.4 Physical collaboration and safety

Physical human–robot collaboration, in which a robot and a person jointly carry an object or work on the same part, requires the robot to read intent through forces as well as motion; impedance-based co-manipulation dates back to Kosuge's work in the early 1990s [4,178]. Safety is governed by collaborative-robot standards: ISO/TS 15066 sets out power and force limiting and speed and separation monitoring [179], whose reactive implementation is covered in Reactive Behavior, Section 4.2. Human–robot teams in industry are surveyed by Villani and colleagues and by Ajoudani and colleagues [4,5].

8.5 Ad hoc teamwork and zero-shot coordination

Robots will increasingly meet partners they were not designed with: other companies' robots, or people they have never seen. Stone and colleagues posed ad hoc teamwork as a challenge: create an agent that can collaborate with unknown teammates without pre-coordination [15,180]. In learning, policies trained by self-play often fail with new partners because they rely on arbitrary conventions. Other-play trains policies that avoid symmetry-breaking conventions and so coordinate better zero-shot [131]; in the cooperative cooking game Overcooked, agents trained with models of human behaviour worked much better with people than self-play agents [181]; and Fictitious Co-Play trains against a diverse population of partners, at several skill levels, to collaborate with humans without human data [182].

9 Research frontiers: resilience, heterogeneity and foundation models

9.1 Resilience to failures and attacks

A team is only as trustworthy as the information its members exchange. Distributed algorithms that average neighbours' values are fragile: a single faulty or compromised robot that broadcasts wrong values can pull the whole team wherever it likes. Classic distributed computing studied this as the Byzantine generals problem, in which some participants may send arbitrary, even inconsistent, messages [183]. For networked control, LeBlanc and colleagues introduced W-MSR (weighted mean-subsequence-reduced) consensus, in which each robot discards the F most extreme neighbour values above and below its own before averaging; on graphs with a sufficient property called r-robustness, the normal robots provably agree on a value within the range of their initial values despite up to F malicious neighbours [184]. Saldaña and colleagues extended resilient consensus to time-varying networks of moving robots [185], and Gil and colleagues used physical properties of wireless signals to detect spoofed robot identities, so that a single attacker cannot pose as many robots [186].

With plain averaging, a single Byzantine robot that tells its neighbours different extreme values splits the honest team: after 200 rounds their values span −7 to 14, outside anything any honest robot measured. Resilience costs connectivity: the graph must be dense enough that discarding extremes never cuts a robot off from honest information, so it trades directly against the sparse, cheap communication of Section 5 [184,185].

9.2 Heterogeneous teams

Real teams mix capabilities: legged robots that climb stairs, drones that see from above, wheeled robots that carry loads, and people who judge. Prorok, Hsieh and Kumar showed that the distribution of capabilities across a swarm, its diversity, affects the best achievable performance and how tasks should be allocated [20]. The Subterranean Challenge teams combined legged, wheeled and aerial robots, each assigned the parts of the environment it was best suited to explore [3]. Formal specifications in temporal logic can describe missions for heterogeneous teams and synthesize controllers that provably satisfy them, at a computational cost that grows with team size [187]. Some tasks physically need several robots at once, such as carrying an object too large or heavy for one, which ground and aerial teams have done with coordinated force and motion control [188,189]. Allocating tasks that require several robots with complementary skills at once (the MR and TA classes of the allocation taxonomies) remains hard and mostly heuristic [190,191].

9.3 Foundation models as coordinators

Large language and vision–language models are a new kind of coordinator. They can turn a natural-language mission into per-robot subtasks, reason about which robot has which skill, and negotiate conflicts in a medium people can read. RoCo had robot arms, each driven by a language model, discuss and refine a joint plan in dialogue, with a motion planner checking each proposal for feasibility [63]. SMART-LLM decomposed instructions into tasks, formed coalitions and allocated them to robots with different skills [64]. Chen and colleagues compared centralized, decentralized and hybrid language-model planners and found that the choice of architecture affects success rate and token cost, with hybrid designs often the best compromise [65]. CoELA built cooperative embodied agents from language-model modules for perception, memory, communication and planning, and found that agents that communicate in natural language could also cooperate with people [192]. These systems inherit the strengths and weaknesses of language-model planning described in Task Planning, Section 3 and Foundation Models for Physical AI: broad common sense, but no guarantees, so they are best used to propose plans that classical allocation, scheduling and motion planning then verify [63,193].

9.4 Fleets as learning systems

A fleet is also a data-collection system. Levine and colleagues used up to fourteen robot arms in parallel to collect more than 800,000 grasp attempts for learning hand–eye coordination [194]; cloud robotics shares computation, data and learned models across robots [195]; and the Open X-Embodiment collaboration pooled data from 22 robot embodiments to train cross-embodiment policies [66]. AutoRT used foundation models to propose tasks and orchestrate data collection across a fleet of more than 20 robots [67], and Fleet-DAgger studied how a small number of human supervisors should allocate their attention across a large robot fleet during interactive learning [196]. Federated learning, which trains a shared model from private local data without centralizing it, is a natural fit when fleets cannot share raw sensor data [197]. These are multi-agent systems in which the shared resource is knowledge rather than space.

9.5 Other open directions

  • Scaling with guarantees. Collision avoidance for thousands of agents is solved for simple dynamics, but combining scale with dynamic feasibility, communication limits and formal safety for heterogeneous agents remains open [80,198,199].
  • Interaction-aware autonomy at scale. Game-theoretic planners handle a few agents; dense traffic and crowds require scalable approximations, equilibrium selection that matches human conventions, and validation against real interactive data [133,137,139].
  • Learning with and from teams. Credit assignment, partner generalization and safe exploration all become harder with more agents; zero-shot coordination with people is still far from human-level teamwork [119,131,182].
  • Swarm engineering. Designing local rules that provably produce a desired collective behaviour, rather than discovering them by trial and error, remains a central open problem in swarm robotics [19,200].

10 Practice, evaluation and open problems

10.1 Building a multi-robot system

Practical systems combine the layers of this article with those of the rest of the series. A typical warehouse or logistics fleet has a central fleet manager that allocates tasks (Task Planning), plans or reserves paths (Motion Planning), and monitors progress, while each robot runs its own localization, local planning and reactive safety layer (Reactive Behavior) so that it stays safe if the manager or the network fails [1,47]. Field teams that cannot rely on a network push more autonomy to each robot and share maps opportunistically [3,97]. Road vehicles combine on-board autonomy with optional cooperation through V2X, designed so that the vehicle is safe when communication is absent [2,50].

Several tools support this work. ROS 2 provides publish–subscribe middleware with configurable quality of service [114]; Gazebo, ARGoS and VMAS simulate robot teams at different fidelities and scales [130,201,202]; SUMO and Waymax simulate traffic [151,153]; and the Robotarium gives researchers remote access to a physical swarm of small robots, with barrier-function safety filters that prevent collisions regardless of the submitted code [203,204].

10.2 Evaluating multi-agent systems

Evaluation must look beyond single-robot metrics. Useful measures include team task completion, makespan and throughput; scalability (how performance and computation change with the number of agents); communication load; robustness to agent failure, message loss and adversaries; safety margins between agents and around people; and, for human–robot teams, fluency, workload and trust [23,158,175]. Benchmarks exist for several sub-problems: MAPF benchmark maps [11], the StarCraft multi-agent challenge for cooperative MARL [205], Overcooked for human–AI coordination [181], the Waymo Open Motion Dataset for interactive prediction [133], OPV2V and DAIR-V2X for cooperative perception [99,100], and Habitat 3.0 for robots collaborating with simulated humans in homes [206]. For deployed automated vehicles, crash rates compared with human benchmarks over millions of miles are becoming the ultimate field metric [207]. Results should be reported over many random seeds, team sizes and partner types, because multi-agent outcomes are often bimodal: the same algorithm can coordinate perfectly in one run and deadlock in the next.

10.4 Open problems

  • Coordination under real communication. Most guarantees assume connectivity or bounded delays; theory and practice for teams that are disconnected most of the time are immature [23,108].
  • Consistent shared world models. Merging maps, object tracks and semantic knowledge across robots without double counting or corrupting each other's beliefs, at scale and with outliers, remains difficult [96,97].
  • Humans in the loop at fleet scale. How many robots one person can supervise, how to keep their trust calibrated, and how to share authority are open human-factors questions [162,163].
  • Security. Resilient consensus protects specific algorithms; securing perception sharing, learned policies and language-model coordinators against malicious agents is largely open [184,186].
  • Accountability. When a collision results from an interaction between several autonomous agents, deciding which agent behaved wrongly requires formal models of responsibility such as RSS and careful logging [142,207].

Conclusion

Multi-agent physical intelligence is what turns individually capable robots into fleets, swarms and teams. Its foundations are graph-theoretic: consensus converges at a rate set by algebraic connectivity, and formations hold together only while the graph stays connected. Shared perception must avoid counting the same information twice, which covariance intersection and consensus filters achieve at a modest cost in accuracy. Communication is a resource to plan, not a given. Multi-agent learning must overcome non-stationarity and miscoordination, which centralized training with decentralized execution addresses. On the road, other agents respond to the robot, so planning becomes a game in which equilibrium selection matters as much as computation, and explicit cooperation through V2V turns string-unstable traffic into stable platoons. With people, the robot must infer intent, make its own intent legible and keep trust calibrated. At the frontier lie resilient algorithms that tolerate faulty and malicious agents, heterogeneous teams, foundation models that negotiate plans in language, and fleets that learn collectively. The examples in this article show the recurring lessons in small form: connectivity sets speed; feedforward and feedback each have their role; consistency beats false confidence; averaging over a partner's exploration hides the best joint action; prediction without interaction freezes or deadlocks; communication delay limits how closely vehicles can follow; and confidence thresholds trade speed against coordination errors.
