1 What task planning is, and where it sits in a robot

A robot asked to "clear the table and put the clean cups away" has to answer several questions before a single motor moves. Which objects does the request refer to? What does "away" mean in this house? Which cups are clean? In what order should things happen, given that the robot has one gripper, that the dishwasher door must be opened before anything goes in, and that the cupboard is blocked by a chair? And what should it do when a cup slips out of its hand halfway through? Task planning is the part of a robot's intelligence that answers these questions: it chooses and orders discrete, meaningful actions (navigate, pick, open, place, wait, ask) to achieve a goal, and supervises their execution [1,2].

Formally, automated planning is the problem of finding a sequence or structure of actions that transforms an initial state into one satisfying a goal, given models of what each action requires and what it changes [3,4]. Task planning in robotics differs from the textbook problem in three ways. First, the symbols a planner manipulates (on(cup, shelf), door_open) must be grounded in perception and in continuous motion: a symbolic action is only useful if some controller can carry it out, and a symbolic state is only correct if perception supports it [5,6]. Second, robots act in an open, partially observable and stochastic world, so plans fail and must be monitored and repaired [7,8]. Third, the goal itself usually arrives in an imprecise form, often as natural language, and must be turned into something a planner can check [9].

Most deployed robots therefore organize decision making in layers. A widely cited formulation is the three-layer architecture: a deliberative layer that plans over long horizons with symbolic models, a sequencing or executive layer that selects and monitors behaviours (often a state machine or behavior tree), and a reactive skill layer of feedback controllers that run at high rates [10–12]. Figure 1 shows the components discussed in this article and where they sit. The motion and control layers are covered elsewhere in the series: collision-free paths and trajectories in motion planning, feedback controllers in classical and modern control, kinematics and dynamics in physical modeling, learned skills in reinforcement learning and imitation learning, and model-predictive control with learned dynamics in world models. This article concentrates on the levels above them.

Three tensions run through the whole field and recur in every section. Deliberation versus reaction: planning ahead finds better and more coherent behaviour, but the world changes faster than a planner can think, which is why Brooks argued in the 1980s for behaviour-based robots with no central planner at all [13,14] and why hybrid architectures emerged as the compromise [10,15]. Expressiveness versus tractability: even propositional STRIPS planning is PSPACE-complete [16], and adding time, resources, continuous geometry or uncertainty makes things harder still, so practical systems choose representations that are as weak as the problem allows. Engineered versus learned knowledge: classical planners need hand-written action models, while large language and vision-language models bring broad common sense but no guarantees of correctness or executability [17,18]. Much current research is about combining the two.

2 A short history: from Shakey to language-model planners

2.1 Planning as reasoning about action (1950s–1970s)

Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver introduced means–ends analysis: find a difference between the current state and the goal, and apply an operator relevant to reducing it, recursively setting up the operator's preconditions as subgoals [19]. Green showed that plans could be extracted from proofs in first-order logic [20], and McCarthy and Hayes formalized change in the situation calculus, raising the frame problem of saying compactly what an action does not change [21].

The decisive step for robotics came at SRI with Shakey, a mobile robot built between 1966 and 1972 that navigated rooms, pushed boxes and reasoned about its actions [22]. Shakey's planner, STRIPS, sidestepped the frame problem with a simple convention: each operator lists its preconditions, the facts it adds and the facts it deletes, and every other fact persists [23]. The same project produced the A* search algorithm [24] and, with PLANEX, one of the first systems for executing plans under monitoring: "triangle tables" recorded which preconditions each remaining step relied on, so the executor could skip steps that were already achieved or re-run steps whose effects had been undone [25]. These three ideas, operator models, heuristic search and execution monitoring, remain the core of task planning half a century later.

The 1970s added structure. ABSTRIPS planned first in an abstract space that ignored less critical preconditions and refined the plan afterwards [26]. NOAH represented plans as partially ordered networks of actions that could be refined hierarchically, committing to an order only when interactions forced one [27], and NONLIN extended the approach with goal-structure bookkeeping [28]. These systems are the ancestors of hierarchical task network (HTN) planning (Section 5).

2.2 Reaction, architectures and the executive (1980s–1990s)

By the mid-1980s, plan-then-act robots were widely seen as too slow and brittle for dynamic environments. Brooks's subsumption architecture built behaviour from layers of simple reactive modules with no symbolic world model [13,14]. In parallel, reactive planning systems such as Firby's reactive action packages and Georgeff and Lansky's Procedural Reasoning System selected pre-written procedures at run time according to the current situation [29,30]. The synthesis was the three-layer architecture, exemplified by 3T and by the LAAS architecture, in which a planner, an executive and reactive skills each work at their own time scale [10–12]. NASA's Remote Agent, built to control the Deep Space 1 spacecraft, combined a temporal planner/scheduler, an executive and model-based fault diagnosis and recovery, and became an influential example of autonomy in a high-stakes physical system [31].

2.3 Fast planners and a common language (1990s–2010s)

Graphplan (1995, journal version 1997) and SATPLAN produced orders-of-magnitude speedups by compiling planning into planning graphs and Boolean satisfiability [32,33]. The Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL), created for the first International Planning Competition in 1998, gave researchers a shared input format [34,35]; later versions added durative actions and numeric fluents (PDDL2.1) and preferences and trajectory constraints (PDDL3) [36,37]. Heuristic search planners then took over: HSP and FF derived heuristics by solving a relaxed problem that ignores delete lists [38,39], Fast Downward introduced multi-valued state variables and the causal-graph heuristic [40], and LAMA, built on Fast Downward with landmark heuristics, won the satisficing track of the 2008 competition [41]. HTN planners such as SHOP and SHOP2 matured in the same period [42,43].

In robotics, planning re-entered real systems through middleware. ROSPlan connected PDDL planners to the Robot Operating System [44], PlanSys2 did the same for ROS 2 [45,46], and integrated task-and-motion planners began to reason about geometry and symbols together [47–49]. Meanwhile the video-game industry's behavior trees were adopted for robot control because they are modular and reactive [50,51], and today they run inside the ROS 2 navigation stack [52].

2.4 Language models enter the loop (2022–)

Large language models (LLMs) changed the interface to task planning. In 2022 Huang and colleagues showed that LLMs can decompose household tasks into plausible step-by-step plans without task-specific training [53], SayCan grounded those plans in the robot's skill affordances [54], and Inner Monologue closed the loop with feedback from success detectors and scene descriptions [55]. Code as Policies and ProgPrompt had LLMs write executable robot programs [56,57]. Systematic evaluations soon showed that LLMs alone are unreliable planners on problems that require tracking state and preconditions [17,18], which motivated hybrid systems that use LLMs to translate goals into PDDL or temporal logic for classical solvers [58–60]. The foundation-model side of this story, including vision-language-action models that fold high-level reasoning into a single network, is told in the foundation models article; here we treat LLMs as one component of a task-planning system.

3 Natural-language instruction interpretation

Natural language is the most convenient way for people to tell robots what to do, and one of the hardest inputs for a planner. An instruction is underspecified ("tidy up"), context-dependent ("put it back"), ambiguous ("the cup" when there are three) and frequently mixes goals, constraints and procedures ("don't use the top shelf; do the glasses first"). Interpreting it requires mapping words to the robot's own categories, objects, places and actions: the symbol grounding problem in its robotic form [5,9].

3.1 From semantic parsing to probabilistic grounding

Winograd's SHRDLU (1972) understood typed commands about a simulated block world by combining syntax, semantics and a model of the world, and could ask questions when a reference was unclear [73]. It worked because its world was tiny and fully known. Statistical approaches later learned the mapping from data. MacMahon and colleagues and Chen and Mooney interpreted route instructions by parsing them into action plans grounded in a map [74,75]; Kollar and colleagues extracted spatial description clauses (figure, verb, landmark, spatial relation) and grounded them probabilistically [76]. Tellex and colleagues' Generalized Grounding Graphs factor the meaning of a command according to its parse tree and infer the most probable groundings of each phrase to objects, places and paths in the robot's world model, which allowed a robotic forklift and other platforms to follow commands such as "put the pallet on the truck" [77]. Matuszek and colleagues trained a semantic parser to translate commands into programs in a robot control language [78].

Large benchmarks pushed this work toward learning end to end. Room-to-Room asks an agent to follow navigation instructions in photorealistic buildings [79], and ALFRED pairs household instructions with long sequences of navigation and manipulation actions in simulation [80]; both showed that following long, compositional instructions remains difficult for learned agents. A complementary line keeps the robot honest about what it does not understand. Thomason and colleagues' system improved both its parser and its perceptual concepts through clarification dialogues with users [81], and KnowNo uses conformal prediction over an LLM planner's candidate actions to decide, with a statistical guarantee on task success, when the robot should stop and ask for help [82].

3.2 Language models as interpreters

LLMs replace hand-built lexicons with broad knowledge of how people talk about tasks. Used directly as planners, they generate fluent action sequences that are often not executable because they ignore what the robot can do and what the world currently contains [53]. Three families of methods address this, and the foundation models article covers them in more detail:

  • Grounding by scoring. SayCan multiplies the LLM's preference for each skill by a learned value function estimating whether the skill can succeed now [54]. SayPlan grounds LLM planning in large buildings by letting the model search a 3D scene graph and checking its plans with a simulator [83,84].
  • Grounding by feedback. Inner Monologue and ReAct interleave reasoning with actions and feed observations, success detections and human corrections back into the prompt [55,85]. LLM-Planner re-plans when an embodied agent gets stuck, using what it has observed [86].
  • Grounding by program. Code as Policies and ProgPrompt prompt the LLM to write programs over a robot API, so that loops, conditionals and perception calls make the plan explicit and inspectable [56,57].

A fourth approach, increasingly preferred where correctness matters, uses the LLM only as a translator from language into a formal goal and lets a sound solver do the planning. LLM+P translates a problem description into PDDL and calls a classical planner [58]; Guan and colleagues have the LLM draft a PDDL domain model that people then correct [87]; Lang2LTL grounds commands with temporal structure ("go to the kitchen, but only after visiting the hall") into linear temporal logic [59]; and AutoTAMP translates instructions into signal temporal logic for a task-and-motion planner, with automatic syntax and semantic checks that send errors back to the LLM [60]. Kambhampati and colleagues argue for this "LLM-modulo" pattern in general: LLMs generate candidates and knowledge, external verifiers check them [17]. The trade-off is that translation errors become goal errors, so interfaces that show the formal goal to the user, or ask when uncertain, matter as much as translation accuracy [9,82]. Language interfaces also open a new attack surface: crafted prompts can make LLM-controlled robots carry out harmful actions, so the formal layer is a useful place to enforce constraints that no instruction can override [88].

4.1 Classical planning: states, actions and search

In the STRIPS formulation, a state is a set of ground atoms, an action a is applicable in state s if its preconditions pre(a) ⊆ s, and applying it yields (s ∖ del(a)) ∪ add(a); a plan is a sequence of actions from the initial state to any state containing the goal atoms [3,23]. PDDL writes the same model in a Lisp-like syntax with typed, parameterized action schemas, and modern planners ground the schemas into actions automatically [34,35,40]. Deciding whether a plan exists is PSPACE-complete in general [16], yet heuristic search solves many practical problems quickly because good heuristics can be computed automatically from the model.

The most influential idea is delete relaxation: estimate the distance to the goal by solving a simplified problem in which actions never delete facts. Bonet and Geffner's hmax takes the cost of a set of facts to be the cost of the most expensive one, which never overestimates and is therefore admissible for A*; hadd sums the costs, which is more informative but can overestimate because it ignores shared subplans [38]. FF's heuristic extracts an explicit relaxed plan and counts its actions, and FF combined it with an aggressive enforced hill-climbing search [39]. Landmarks, facts that every plan must achieve at some point, give further heuristics [41]. 4.2 Temporal goals

Many robot tasks are not "reach a state" but "behave in a certain way over time": patrol these rooms forever, visit the charging station whenever the battery is low, never enter the operating theatre while it is occupied. Linear temporal logic (LTL), introduced into computer science by Pnueli in 1977, expresses such properties with operators for "always" (□), "eventually" (◇), "next" and "until" [95,96]. Every LTL formula can be translated into an automaton that accepts exactly the behaviours that satisfy it; planning then becomes a graph search in the product of the robot's transition system and that automaton, and the same machinery used to verify software synthesizes provably correct controllers [89,96]. Kress-Gazit, Fainekos and Pappas used reactive synthesis from LTL to generate controllers for robots that must respond to environment events, building on the GR(1) fragment, for which synthesis takes polynomial time rather than the doubly exponential time of full LTL [92,93]. Variants interpreted over finite traces suit tasks that end [97]; signal temporal logic adds real-valued time bounds and quantitative robustness for continuous signals [98]; and reward machines encode temporally extended goals as automata that a reinforcement learner can exploit [99].

4.3 Goals as rewards, and goal-conditioned skills

The decision-theoretic alternative states the task as a reward function and optimizes expected return in a Markov decision process [90,91]; this handles stochastic outcomes and trade-offs naturally, and is treated in depth in the reinforcement learning article. Rewards are less transparent than logical goals: it is hard to read a reward function and know which behaviours it permits, which is one reason for translating between them [99]. Goal-conditioned policies, which take the desired goal as an input, connect the two views: Kaelbling formulated learning to achieve arbitrary goals in 1993 [100], and hindsight experience replay made goal-conditioned learning practical with sparse rewards by relabelling failed attempts with the goals they did achieve [101]. A library of goal-conditioned skills is exactly what a task planner needs at its lowest level.

4.4 Task decomposition

Decomposition breaks a task into subtasks that can be solved, ordered and executed separately. A planner can find a decomposition implicitly (every plan decomposes the goal into steps), or be given one: an HTN encodes recipes for decomposing tasks (Section 5), a behavior tree encodes it as a reactive control structure (Section 6), and an LLM can propose one from common sense [53,54]. Good decompositions have subgoals that are serializable, meaning they can be achieved one after another without undoing each other, and much of the difficulty of planning comes from goals that interact, such as needing the gripper for two things at once [3,4]. Decomposition also determines what can be learned: skill-learning methods discover reusable subtasks from demonstrations [102], and Konidaris, Kaelbling and Lozano-Pérez showed how to construct symbolic abstractions whose preconditions and effects are exactly what is needed to plan with a given set of learned skills [6].

5 Hierarchical planning

People do not plan a dinner party one muscle twitch at a time, and robots should not either. Hierarchy reduces search by planning first with abstract actions and refining them only as needed, and it lets designers encode procedural knowledge about how tasks are usually done [1,94].

5.1 Hierarchical task networks

An HTN planning problem consists of an initial state, an initial task network (a partially ordered set of tasks to accomplish), primitive operators as in STRIPS, and methods, each of which says how a compound task may be decomposed into subtasks when a precondition holds [3,63]. Planning means repeatedly replacing compound tasks with the subtasks of an applicable method until only primitive actions remain and their preconditions are satisfied. Unlike classical planning, the goal is to perform tasks rather than reach a state, which lets a designer rule out undesirable ways of achieving an effect simply by not writing methods for them. Erol, Hendler and Nau showed that HTN planning is strictly more expressive than STRIPS planning, and undecidable in its general form; restrictions such as total ordering restore decidability [63].

SHOP and its successor SHOP2 made HTN planning practical by decomposing tasks in the same order they will be executed, so the planner always knows the complete current state and method preconditions can call arbitrary code, including numeric computations and calls to external reasoners [42,43]. SHOP2 was one of the award winners of the 2002 International Planning Competition and has been used in applications such as evacuation planning and manufacturability analysis [43]. HTN-style decomposition is also the backbone of many robot executives and of cognitive robot frameworks such as CRAM, in which plans are programs with explicit failure handling [7,103]. The survey by Bercher, Alford and Höller organizes the many HTN variants [94].

5.2 Abstraction with guarantees

Abstraction is only useful if abstract plans can be refined. ABSTRIPS assumed that ignoring low-criticality preconditions would rarely produce unrefinable plans [26]. Marthi, Russell and Wolfe made the idea precise with angelic semantics: each high-level action is described by optimistic and pessimistic descriptions of the states reachable by some refinement, so a planner can prove that an abstract plan definitely works, or definitely cannot work, before refining it [104]. Kaelbling and Lozano-Pérez's Hierarchical Planning in the Now (HPN) takes a pragmatic line suited to robots: it plans abstractly, commits to and executes the first abstract step, refines it into lower-level plans only when it is about to be executed, and replans when execution produces surprises [48]. Interleaving planning and acting this way keeps plans short and fresh in uncertain environments, and HPN was extended to plan in belief space, where actions such as looking are chosen to reduce uncertainty [105].

5.3 Temporal abstraction in learning

Reinforcement learning developed its own hierarchy. The options framework models temporally extended actions, each with an initiation set, an internal policy and a termination condition, and shows that an MDP with options is a semi-Markov decision process to which standard planning and learning methods still apply [106]. MAXQ decomposes a task's value function along a programmer-specified task hierarchy [107]. Options are the learning counterpart of HTN methods and primitive skills, and current robot systems often combine them: a symbolic or language-based planner sequences learned options, and each option is a policy trained by reinforcement or imitation learning [6,54]. Hierarchical vision-language-action models follow the same pattern inside one system, with a high-level model that turns open-ended prompts and feedback into short language subtasks and a low-level policy that executes them [71,72].

6 State machines and behavior trees

Plans must be executed by something, and most robots in service today are not driven by a planner at all but by a hand-designed executive that encodes the task directly. The two dominant formalisms are finite-state machines and behavior trees [51,108].

6.1 Finite-state machines and statecharts

A finite-state machine (FSM) has a set of states, each associated with a behaviour, and transitions triggered by events or conditions: in Navigate, on arrived go to Grasp; on grasp failed go to Relocalize object. FSMs are easy to understand for small tasks and map naturally onto robot software. Their problem is scale: the number of transitions grows quickly with the number of states, and adding one behaviour, such as "go and recharge whenever the battery is low", requires transitions from almost every state. Harel's statecharts addressed this with hierarchy (states containing sub-machines), orthogonal concurrent regions and broadcast communication [61], and hierarchical state machines are the basis of widely used robot executives. SMACH, a Python library for hierarchical state machines, was developed at Willow Garage for the PR2 and used in tasks such as fetching drinks from a refrigerator [65,109]. FlexBE, developed for rescue robotics, adds a graphical editor and lets an operator adjust the robot's autonomy level while a behaviour is running [110].

6.2 Behavior trees

Behavior trees (BTs) originated in the computer-game industry as a way to build modular, reactive non-player characters, and were introduced to robotics around 2012 [50,51,111]. A BT is a tree whose root sends a tick signal down to its children at a fixed rate. Every node, when ticked, returns Success, Failure or Running [51]:

  • Leaves are actions, which start or continue a behaviour and report its status, and conditions, which test the world and return success or failure immediately.
  • A Sequence ticks its children from left to right and returns as soon as one of them does not succeed: it is a logical AND that remembers nothing between ticks.
  • A Fallback (also called Selector) ticks children from left to right until one does not fail: a logical OR that tries alternatives in priority order.
  • Decorators modify a single child: retry up to n times, invert the result, apply a timeout. Parallel nodes tick several children at once.

Because the tree is re-ticked from the root on every cycle, a BT keeps re-checking the conditions that its current action depends on and switches behaviour as soon as one changes, which is what makes it reactive. The standard design pattern puts the goal condition as the first child of a Fallback and the actions that achieve it as later children ("postcondition–precondition–action"), so that a tree degrades gracefully: if something undoes an achieved subgoal, the tree notices on the next tick and re-achieves it [51,112]. Colledanchise and Ögren showed that BTs generalize sequential behaviour compositions, the subsumption architecture and decision trees, and analysed their modularity, safety and robustness properties [67]. Every BT can be expressed as an FSM and vice versa, but the BT's tree structure localizes changes: adding the recharge behaviour is one new branch at the top of the tree [51,108].

6.3 Behavior trees in practice, and their limits

BTs are now common in robot software. The ROS 2 navigation stack, Nav2, configures its navigation logic, including recovery behaviours such as clearing costmaps, spinning and backing up, as behavior trees executed by the BehaviorTree.CPP library [52], and a study of open-source robotics projects found BTs used for a wide variety of tasks, often alongside state machines [113]. Their weaknesses mirror their strengths. A BT encodes one designer's plan for anticipated situations; it does not reason about goals it was not built for. Large trees accumulate implicit dependencies through the shared "blackboard" of variables that nodes read and write, and the semantics of memory, parallelism and halting differ between libraries [108,113]. For these reasons BTs are increasingly generated rather than hand-written: from planners, which expand goal conditions into postcondition–precondition–action subtrees at run time [112]; by learning; and, more recently, by language models [108].

7 Scheduling and resource allocation

Planning decides what to do; scheduling decides when, and with which resources. The distinction is blurred in practice, because temporal and resource constraints change which plans are possible, but the two fields grew up separately: planning in AI, scheduling in operations research [3,114]. Robots need both. A hospital delivery robot has time windows, a battery that must be recharged, and lifts it shares with people; a warehouse runs hundreds of robots that must be assigned to orders and kept out of each other's way [115].

7.1 Time: temporal planning and temporal networks

PDDL2.1 introduced durative actions with conditions and effects at their start, end and over their duration, so that a planner can schedule actions concurrently [36]. Temporal planners such as POPF build plans forward in time while keeping their actions partially ordered and checking temporal consistency with a constraint network [116]. The standard network is the simple temporal network (STN) of Dechter, Meiri and Pearl: variables are time points, and each constraint bounds the difference between two of them, lo ≤ tj − ti ≤ hi [62]. Each constraint becomes two weighted edges in a distance graph; the network is consistent if and only if that graph has no negative cycle, and the all-pairs shortest-path distances give the tightest bounds implied by all the constraints together. Floyd–Warshall computes them in O(n3) time [62,117].

Executing a temporal plan means choosing actual times as events happen. Muscettola, Morris and Tsamardinos showed how to reformulate an STN into a dispatchable form in which an executive can commit to each event's time using only local propagation, which is what makes temporal plans executable in real time [118]. Durations that nature, not the robot, controls (how long navigation takes) require simple temporal networks with uncertainty and the stronger notion of dynamic controllability: a strategy that reacts to observed durations and never violates a constraint [119]. These ideas were developed for spacecraft and rovers: Remote Agent's planner and executive used flexible temporal plans [31], and MAPGEN supported daily activity planning for NASA's Mars Exploration Rovers [120].

7.2 Resources and classical scheduling

Resource constraints such as one gripper, two charging docks or a single lift turn scheduling into a combinatorial problem. Job-shop scheduling, in which jobs consist of operations that need particular machines in a given order, is NP-hard already for two machines [121], and robot cells, multi-arm assembly and fleet charging have the same structure [114]. Three solution technologies dominate: mixed-integer linear programming, constraint programming with specialized propagation for resources (such as edge finding for disjunctive machines), and heuristic or local search [114,122]. Constraint-based scheduling is attractive for robots because the same constraint network can be updated incrementally when the world changes.

7.3 Multi-robot task allocation

When several robots share a mission, someone must decide who does what. Gerkey and Matarić's taxonomy classifies multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) by whether robots can do one task or several at a time (ST/MT), whether tasks need one robot or several (SR/MR), and whether assignment is instantaneous or extended over time with future tasks considered (IA/TA) [64]. The simplest class, ST-SR-IA, is the linear assignment problem, solvable in polynomial time by Kuhn's Hungarian method [123,124]; most other classes are NP-hard, including ST-SR-TA, which requires scheduling a sequence of tasks for each robot [64]. Later taxonomies add interdependent utilities and temporal and ordering constraints between tasks [125,126].

Market-based methods are popular because they are decentralized and robust to communication loss: robots bid on tasks according to their own estimated costs and an auction awards each task to the best bidder [127]. MURDOCH demonstrated auction-based allocation on physical robots [128]; sequential single-item auctions, which award one task per round, have performance guarantees for some team objectives [129]; and the consensus-based bundle algorithm lets robots build bundles of tasks and resolve conflicts through local communication, converging to a conflict-free assignment [130]. Bertsekas's auction algorithm solves the assignment problem itself optimally through a sequence of bids with price increments [131]. Once tasks are allocated, the robots' paths must not collide; conflict-based search finds optimal collision-free paths for many agents by planning for each agent separately and resolving conflicts by branching on constraints [132].

8 Task-and-motion planning

A symbolic plan such as pick(cup); place(cup, shelf) hides geometric questions that decide whether it can be executed: is there a grasp of the cup that is reachable and collision-free, is there room on the shelf, and can the arm move between these configurations? Classical task planners assume the answers are yes; motion planners answer them only for a given sequence of actions. Integrated task and motion planning (TAMP) searches over both together, and is needed whenever geometry affects which symbolic plan is right: when an obstacle must be moved first, when a placement must leave room for later objects, or when the grasp chosen now determines whether a later insertion is possible [49,133].

8.1 Motion planning in brief

Motion planning finds a collision-free path for a robot in its configuration space, the space of joint angles or poses [134,135]. Exact methods are impractical beyond a few dimensions, so robotics relies on sampling-based planners: probabilistic roadmaps (PRM) for repeated queries in the same environment [136], rapidly-exploring random trees and RRT-Connect for single queries [137], and RRT* and PRM* for asymptotically optimal paths [138]. Trajectory optimizers such as CHOMP and TrajOpt instead improve an initial trajectory by minimizing cost subject to collision constraints [139,140]. Libraries such as OMPL package these algorithms [141]. The motion planning article treats sampling-based and optimization-based planners in depth; this section only needs the fact that a motion planner can answer "is there a feasible path from here to there?", usually at considerable computational cost.

8.2 Why integration is hard

Manipulation planning already has a hybrid structure: the robot alternates between transit motions with an empty hand and transfer motions holding an object, and the configuration space is a union of lower-dimensional manifolds, one for each grasp and placement, connected where they intersect [142,143]. Lozano-Pérez's Handey system (1987) already combined object recognition, grasp planning and motion planning for pick-and-place [144]. Adding a symbolic layer makes the space larger still. A TAMP planner must choose a discrete plan skeleton and continuous parameters (grasps, placements, base poses, trajectories) for each action, and the two interact in both directions: a skeleton may have no feasible parameters, and good parameters for one step may make a later step impossible. Navigation among movable obstacles is a classic example in which the robot must decide which objects to push aside to reach its goal [145].

8.3 Families of TAMP algorithms

Garrett and colleagues' review groups TAMP methods by how they interleave discrete search and continuous constraint satisfaction [49]:

  • Sequencing first. A task planner proposes skeletons and a geometric module tries to refine them, reporting failures back. Srivastava and colleagues' planner-independent interface layer lets an off-the-shelf PDDL planner work with a motion planner: when refinement fails because an object is in the way, the interface adds the obstruction as a fact and asks the task planner for a new plan [146]. Dantam and colleagues encode the task layer incrementally as satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) and add constraints learned from motion-planning failures [147].
  • Satisfaction first. Samplers generate candidate continuous values (grasps, poses, paths) that make facts true, and the task planner reasons over the sampled values. PDDLStream extends PDDL with streams, declarations of black-box samplers and their certified outputs, so that a classical planner can request new samples optimistically and use them in plans [68]. aSyMov combined a symbolic planner with roadmaps for both the robots and the objects [47].
  • Optimization. Logic-geometric programming formulates TAMP as a nonlinear program over a trajectory whose structure is selected by a symbolic search over action sequences, and finds plans for tool use and complex manipulation by optimizing physics-based objectives [66,148].
  • Hierarchical and online. HPN refines abstract actions only when they are about to be executed, which suits partially known environments, and plans in belief space when information gathering is needed [48,105].

8.4 Learning to plan faster, and language-driven TAMP

Because the search is expensive, much recent work learns to guide it. Driess, Ha and Toussaint trained a network to predict promising action sequences directly from a scene image, so that the geometric solver is called on few skeletons [149]; Wang and colleagues learned the conditions under which skills succeed, giving TAMP systems samplers and preconditions for new skills [150]; and PLOI uses a graph neural network to predict which objects matter, so that planning starts on a small subproblem and grows it only if needed [151]. Language enters at the top: Text2Motion uses an LLM to propose skill sequences and checks their geometric feasibility with learned value functions and dynamics before execution [152], and AutoTAMP translates instructions into temporal-logic specifications for a TAMP solver [60]. Representations from the representations article, such as object-centric models and 3D scene graphs, supply the symbols these planners reason about [84].

9 Multi-step execution and error recovery

A plan is a prediction, and in the physical world predictions fail. Grasps slip, doors turn out to be locked, people move objects, localization drifts and actions take longer than expected. Over a long task these small probabilities compound: a ten-step plan whose steps each succeed 95% of the time succeeds end to end only about 60% of the time. Reliable robots are therefore distinguished less by the plans they make than by how they execute them: how they detect that something has gone wrong, decide what it means, and recover [7,8].

9.1 Monitoring: knowing that something went wrong

Execution monitoring compares what the robot observes with what its plan expects [8]. The oldest form is PLANEX's triangle table, which records, for every suffix of the plan, the conditions that must hold for the rest of the plan to work; at each step the executive finds the latest suffix whose conditions are currently true and continues from there, skipping steps that serendipity has already achieved and repeating steps whose effects have been undone [25]. Modern executives check three kinds of expectations. Action outcome monitoring checks each action's expected effects (is the object in the gripper?); plan invariant monitoring checks the preconditions of all remaining steps, so that a problem is noticed before the robot reaches it; and model-based diagnosis infers which hidden fault best explains a pattern of symptoms, as Remote Agent did for spacecraft hardware [31,153]. Expectations need not be stated explicitly in the plan: Bouguerra, Karlsson and Saffiotti used semantic knowledge, such as that a kitchen should contain a sink, to detect that a robot had arrived in the wrong room [154]. In learned systems, success detection is often itself a learned classifier or a vision-language model asked whether the step succeeded [55,155].

Some failures cannot be detected by checking discrete states, because they are a matter of continuous error accumulating. Lozano-Pérez, Mason and Taylor's preimage framework for fine-motion planning chose compliant motions that are guaranteed to reach a goal despite bounded uncertainty in position and control [156], and Donald extended it to plans that either succeed or reliably recognize that they have failed, error detection and recovery as part of the plan itself [157]. Belief-space planning, covered in the reinforcement learning article, generalizes this by choosing actions that keep uncertainty low enough for later steps to succeed [105,158].

9.2 Recovery: retry, repair or replan

Once a discrepancy is detected, there are four broad responses, in increasing order of cost:

  1. Retry the failed action, perhaps with different parameters (a new grasp, a slower approach). This is the right answer for stochastic failures with a stable cause, and is built into behavior trees as a decorator (Section 6) [51].
  2. Run a recovery behaviour designed for a known failure mode, such as clearing the costmap and rotating in place when navigation is stuck, as in Nav2 [52].
  3. Repair the plan by local modification, keeping as much of it as possible. Repair produces plans that are more stable, that is, closer to the original, which matters when other agents or people have already acted on the plan [159]. In the worst case repair is no easier than planning from scratch [160].
  4. Replan from the observed state. Fast planners make this attractive: FF-Replan, which simply determinizes a probabilistic domain, plans with FF, and replans whenever an unexpected outcome occurs, won the first International Probabilistic Planning Competition, to the surprise of many in the field [161].

Replanning works well when failures are recoverable and the planner is fast, and badly when an unexpected outcome is a dead end or when the plan must anticipate likely failures rather than react to them; then probabilistic planning in an MDP or POMDP is the better model [90,158,161]. Frameworks such as ROSPlan and PlanSys2 package the planning–dispatch–monitor–replan loop for ROS-based robots [44,45].

The lesson generalizes: retries are cheap and handle noise; replanning handles surprises; neither handles failures the model cannot represent, which is where the next approaches come in.

9.3 Explaining failures and learning from them

A robot that cannot explain why it failed cannot ask for the right help or avoid the failure next time. Language and vision-language models now provide a general-purpose way to describe what happened: REFLECT summarizes multisensory robot experience hierarchically and asks an LLM to explain failures and propose corrections [155], Inner Monologue feeds success detection and scene descriptions back into planning [55], and KnowNo asks a person when the planner's uncertainty is too high to act safely [82]. Embodied chain-of-thought reasoning trains vision-language-action policies to reason about plans, subtasks and objects before acting, which makes their intermediate decisions inspectable and correctable [162]. These capabilities are powerful but unverified: a fluent explanation is not necessarily the true cause, and evaluation protocols for long-horizon robot behaviour are still being established [163].

Getting the interfaces right is the main engineering task: symbols must be grounded in perception with calibrated confidence, skills must report success and failure honestly, and the executive must know when to escalate from retrying to replanning to asking a person [7,82].

10.2 Evaluation

Classical planners are compared on the International Planning Competition's benchmark domains, which have driven progress in planner speed and coverage since 1998 [37,164]. Robot task planning is harder to evaluate because success depends on perception and control as much as on planning. Simulation benchmarks such as ALFRED and BEHAVIOR-1K define long-horizon household tasks with formal goal conditions [80,165], language-conditioned manipulation benchmarks such as CALVIN test chains of instructions [166], and PlanBench measures LLMs' ability to plan and to reason about change on classical domains [18]. Results on these benchmarks show the gap between fluent plans and correct ones: on PlanBench's Blocksworld plan-generation task, the best LLM tested in 2023 solved roughly a third of the problems, far below a classical planner, and did much worse when the domain's names were obfuscated [18]. Reasoning models, trained with reinforcement learning to produce long chains of thought before answering [69], have narrowed the gap on standard Blocksworld problems, but a 2024 evaluation found that they were still far from saturating the benchmark, performed much worse on obfuscated and larger problems, and raised open questions about efficiency, cost and guarantees of correctness [70]. For physical robots, rigorous evaluation means many trials, reported failure modes and statistical comparisons, not single demonstrations [163].

10.3 Open problems

  • Where do the models come from? Planners need action models and symbolic abstractions; writing them by hand does not scale. Learning symbols from skills, PDDL from language and demonstrations, and preconditions from experience are active research areas [6,87,150].
  • Verification of learned components. LLM and VLA planners bring generality without guarantees. Combining them with formal goal languages, verifiers and runtime monitors, in the LLM-modulo spirit, is a promising direction, but the guarantees are only as good as the grounding [17,60,88].
  • Generalized plans. A robot should not plan from scratch for every new number of dishes. Generalized planning seeks programs that solve whole families of problems; recent work has LLMs write such programs and checks them automatically [167].
  • Long-horizon reliability. Errors compound with plan length. Better failure detection, explanation and recovery, and planning that anticipates likely failures, are needed before robots can carry out hour-long tasks unattended [155,163].
  • Multi-agent and human-aware planning. Real deployments share space and resources with people and other robots; allocation, scheduling and planning must account for others' goals and for changes of plan during execution [125,130].
  • Unifying reasoning across levels. Hierarchical vision-language-action models and embodied reasoning models blur the boundary between task planning and control [71,72,168]; how much explicit structure (symbols, constraints, search) such systems need to be reliable is an open question.

Conclusion

Task planning is how a robot connects what people want to what its controllers can do. Instructions must be grounded in the robot's perception and turned into goals a planner can check, whether as goal states, temporal-logic formulas, rewards or task networks. Heuristic search makes classical planning fast in practice despite its worst-case complexity; hierarchy adds expert knowledge and abstraction; state machines and behavior trees execute plans reactively; temporal networks and allocation algorithms decide when and by whom things happen; task-and-motion planning makes plans geometrically feasible; and monitoring, retry, repair and replanning keep long tasks on track when the world does not cooperate. The examples in this article show each idea in miniature: ambiguity is measurable and should trigger questions; informed heuristics shrink search by orders of magnitude; automata make temporal goals checkable; methods encode know-how but must backtrack; behavior trees recover by construction; temporal constraints expose slack and conflicts; greedy allocation can be far from optimal; geometric failures should become symbolic knowledge; and replanning recovers from surprises that retries cannot. Language models have made robots far easier to instruct, and the most robust current systems pair them with the planning, scheduling and execution machinery described here rather than replacing it.
