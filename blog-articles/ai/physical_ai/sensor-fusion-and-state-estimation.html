1 State estimation: the belief behind every action

A robot never observes its own pose, the velocity of the car ahead or the position of the cup it is reaching for. It observes pixels, laser returns, Doppler shifts, wheel ticks and specific forces, each corrupted by noise, bias, latency and occasional gross errors. State estimation is the process of inferring the quantities a controller or policy needs (the state) from those indirect measurements together with a model of how the state evolves. Sensor fusion is the part of that process that combines several sensors so that the result is more accurate, more complete or more robust than any single source [1,2]. The companion article in this series, Sensors and Sensory Modalities for Physical AI, describes the transducers themselves; this article is about what happens downstream of them.

The modern view, set out in Probabilistic Robotics, is that a robot should represent its knowledge as a probability distribution, a belief, rather than as a single best guess, because only a distribution says how far the guess can be trusted [3]. Planning under that belief lets a robot slow down when it is unsure, look again before grasping, or reject a measurement that contradicts everything else it knows. This holds whether the downstream consumer is a classical planner or a learned policy: an end-to-end network still benefits from inputs whose errors are bounded and whose failures are detected.

1.1 A short history

The mathematics is older than robotics. Gauss published the method of least squares in 1809 to determine planetary orbits from imperfect observations [4,5]. Wiener's 1949 theory of optimal filtering of stationary signals, developed for wartime fire control, framed estimation in the frequency domain [6]. In 1960 Kalman recast the problem in state-space form and gave a recursive solution that processes one measurement at a time and applies to non-stationary systems [7]; the continuous-time version followed with Bucy in 1961 [8]. Stanley Schmidt's group at NASA Ames almost immediately linearized the filter about the current estimate (the extended Kalman filter) to navigate the Apollo circumlunar missions, and Kalman filtering has been central to aerospace navigation ever since [9–11]. Ho and Lee showed in 1964 that the filter is a special case of recursive Bayesian inference [12], and Rauch, Tung and Striebel derived the corresponding fixed-interval smoother in 1965 [13].

Robotics adopted these tools in the 1980s, when Smith and Cheeseman showed how to propagate spatial uncertainty through chains of coordinate transforms [14,15] and Durrant-Whyte cast multisensor integration as a problem of combining uncertain geometric observations [16]. Sequential Monte Carlo methods, foreshadowed in 1969 [17], became practical with the bootstrap particle filter of Gordon, Salmond and Smith in 1993 [18] and transformed mobile robot localization by the end of that decade [19]. In 1997 Julier and Uhlmann introduced the unscented transform as a better way to propagate Gaussians through nonlinear functions [20]. Since the mid-2000s, smoothing over factor graphs with sparse nonlinear least squares has become the dominant back end for SLAM and visual-inertial odometry [21–23], and since the late 2010s learned components have increasingly been placed inside these probabilistic structures rather than replacing them [24,25].

2 Foundations: state-space models and the Bayes filter

2.1 State, process model and measurement model

An estimator needs two models. The process (motion) model describes how the state xk evolves given a control or proprioceptive input uk, and the measurement model describes what each sensor would report for a given state [3,42]:

xk = f(xk−1, uk) + wk,   wk ~ N(0, Qk)      zk = h(xk) + vk,   vk ~ N(0, Rk)

The choice of state is a design decision. A drone's navigation filter might carry position, velocity, attitude and IMU biases; a tracker carries each object's position, velocity and extent; a SLAM system adds landmark positions or past poses. Anything that changes slowly and corrupts the measurements, such as a gyro bias, a camera–IMU time offset or a wheel radius, is often best added to the state so that it is estimated rather than ignored [43,44]. The state must also be observable: the measurements over time must constrain it. Yaw is unobservable from an accelerometer alone, absolute position is unobservable from visual-inertial odometry, and an estimator that is not designed around these facts can report confidence it has not earned [45,46].

2.2 The recursive Bayes filter

Under the Markov assumption (the current state summarizes the past, and each measurement depends only on the current state), the posterior belief can be computed recursively in two steps [3,47]:

Predict:  p(xk | z1:k−1) = ∫ p(xk | xk−1, uk) p(xk−1 | z1:k−1) dxk−1
Update:  p(xk | z1:k) = η · p(zk | xk) · p(xk | z1:k−1)

Every filter in this article is an approximation of these two equations. The Kalman filter solves them exactly when the models are linear and the noise Gaussian; the extended and unscented filters approximate the nonlinear case with a Gaussian; grid and particle filters represent arbitrary distributions numerically [3,47,48]. Fusion of several sensors falls out naturally: if their noises are independent given the state, the likelihood is the product of the individual likelihoods, so each sensor's update can be applied in turn, whenever its measurement arrives [16,42].

2.3 Filtering, smoothing and optimization

A filter estimates the current state from past measurements; a smoother also uses later measurements to refine past states [13,47]. Writing the joint posterior over a whole trajectory as a product of factors (a prior, one factor per motion step and one per measurement) gives a factor graph. With Gaussian noise, maximizing that posterior is a sparse nonlinear least-squares problem, which Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt methods solve efficiently by exploiting sparsity [21,22]. Fixed-lag smoothers, which optimize over a sliding window and marginalize older states, sit between the two and are the norm in visual-inertial and lidar-inertial odometry [38,49,50]. A filter can be seen as a smoother that marginalizes every past state immediately and never revisits its linearization point; that is why smoothing is usually more accurate for the same model, at higher computational cost [22,51].

3 Kalman filters and their nonlinear extensions

3.1 The linear Kalman filter

For a linear system xk = F xk−1 + B uk + wk, zk = H xk + vk with Gaussian noise, the belief stays Gaussian and is fully described by a mean x̂ and covariance P. The Kalman filter propagates them as follows [7,42]:

x̂− = F x̂ + B u,   P− = F P FT + Q
y = z − H x̂−,   S = H P− HT + R,   K = P− HT S−1
x̂ = x̂− + K y,   P = (I − K H) P− (I − K H)T + K R KT

The innovation y is the part of the measurement that the prediction did not explain, and S is its predicted covariance. The gain K weighs prediction against measurement in proportion to their uncertainties. For linear Gaussian systems the filter is the minimum mean-square-error estimator, and among linear estimators it is optimal even when the noise is not Gaussian [7,52]. The last line is the Joseph form of the covariance update; it is algebraically equal to the shorter (I − KH)P− but stays symmetric and positive semi-definite under rounding error. Square-root and UD-factorized implementations, developed for the limited arithmetic of 1960s and 1970s flight computers, go further and remain the safest choice for poorly conditioned problems [11,53]. The information filter propagates P−1 and P−1x̂ instead, which turns the update into a sum and makes it attractive for decentralized fusion across many sensor nodes [42,54].

The filter is only as good as its models. Q encodes how much the motion model is trusted and R how much each sensor is trusted. A standard sanity check is statistical consistency: if the filter is right about its own uncertainty, the normalized estimation error squared (NEES) eTP−1e follows a χ² distribution with as many degrees of freedom as the state, and the normalized innovation squared (NIS) yTS−1y follows a χ² distribution with the measurement dimension. NEES needs ground truth and is used in simulation; NIS needs only the filter's own innovations and can be monitored on a running robot [42]. The same structure appears in the GNSS/INS example of the sensors article, where accelerometer data drive the prediction and GNSS fixes the update.

3.2 The extended Kalman filter and its variants

Real motion and measurement models are nonlinear: a camera projects through a division by depth, a range-bearing sensor measures √(x² + y²) and atan2(y, x), and orientation lives on a curved manifold. The extended Kalman filter (EKF) linearizes f and h about the current estimate and applies the Kalman equations with the Jacobians F = ∂f/∂x and H = ∂h/∂x [9,42]. It is cheap and remains the workhorse of navigation systems, including the generic EKF/UKF nodes in the ROS robot_localization package [43,55]. Its weakness is that linearization errors are not reflected in the covariance, so a strongly nonlinear problem or a poor initial estimate can make it inconsistent or divergent; EKF-SLAM, for example, becomes overconfident because Jacobians evaluated at different estimates of the same landmark give the filter information it does not actually have [45,46].

Several variants address this:

  • Iterated EKF (IEKF). Re-linearizes the measurement model about the updated estimate and repeats the update, which is a Gauss–Newton iteration on the measurement likelihood; ROVIO and FAST-LIO both use iterated updates [42,56,57].
  • Error-state (indirect) EKF. Propagates a nominal state with the full nonlinear model and lets the filter estimate only the small error, which stays close to zero where linearization is accurate. For orientation, the error is a three-parameter rotation vector rather than a quaternion, avoiding singular covariances. The error-state formulation is standard in inertial navigation and visual-inertial odometry [34,43,58].
  • Invariant EKF. For systems with suitable symmetries, defining the error on a Lie group makes its dynamics independent of the state estimate, giving convergence guarantees and better consistency than the standard EKF [37]. Contact-aided invariant EKFs are used for legged robot state estimation [59].

3.3 The unscented and cubature Kalman filters

Instead of linearizing the function, the unscented transform picks a small set of deterministic sigma points that match the mean and covariance of the current Gaussian, passes each through the nonlinear function, and recomputes a mean and covariance from the results [20,60]. For an n-dimensional state there are 2n + 1 points:

χ0 = x̂,   χ±i = x̂ ± [√((n + λ) P)]i,   λ = α²(n + κ) − n

The unscented Kalman filter (UKF) captures the posterior mean and covariance accurately to second order for any nonlinearity, needs no Jacobians and costs about the same as an EKF [60,61]. The cubature Kalman filter uses a third-degree spherical-radial cubature rule that yields a similar point set with positive weights, improving numerical stability in higher dimensions [62]. Stanley, the robot that won the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge, used a UKF to fuse GPS, inertial and wheel-odometry data for its pose estimate [63].

4 Bayesian filtering beyond Gaussians: particle filters

4.1 When a Gaussian is the wrong shape

A robot switched on in a building with several identical corridors does not have a single uncertain position; it has several distinct hypotheses. A tracker that loses a pedestrian behind a parked van, a manipulator unsure which of two handles it is touching, and a vehicle whose GNSS fix may be a multipath reflection all face posteriors that are multimodal or heavy-tailed. A Gaussian filter must pick one mode and can lock onto the wrong one [3]. Grid (histogram) filters discretize the state and apply the Bayes filter exactly on the grid, which is feasible for low-dimensional states such as a planar pose on a coarse map [3]. For larger or continuous states, sampling methods scale better.

4.2 Sequential importance resampling

Monte Carlo methods, named and popularized by Metropolis and Ulam in 1949, approximate distributions with random samples [65]. Applied recursively to filtering, they had been proposed by 1969 [17], but the weights of the samples degenerated until only one carried any mass. Gordon, Salmond and Smith's 1993 bootstrap filter fixed this by resampling [18]. A particle filter represents the belief with N weighted samples {x(i), w(i)} and repeats three steps [48,66]:

  1. Predict: draw each particle forward from the motion model, xk(i) ~ p(xk | xk−1(i), uk).
  2. Weight: multiply each weight by the measurement likelihood p(zk | xk(i)) and normalize.
  3. Resample: when the effective sample size Neff = 1 / Σ(w(i))² drops below a threshold, draw a new set of equally weighted particles in proportion to the weights.

The effective sample size criterion comes from Kong, Liu and Wong's analysis of sequential imputation [67]. Of the common resampling schemes, systematic and stratified resampling add less Monte Carlo variance than plain multinomial resampling and cost O(N) [68]. Isard and Blake's CONDENSATION algorithm brought particle filtering to visual contour tracking in 1998 [69], and Dellaert, Fox, Burgard and Thrun introduced Monte Carlo localization (MCL) for mobile robots in 1999 [19].

4.3 Practical particle filtering in robotics

  • Robust MCL. Mixing a few particles drawn from the measurement model, rather than the motion model, lets MCL recover from the "kidnapped robot" problem in which the robot is moved without being told [70].
  • Adaptive sample size. KLD-sampling chooses the number of particles so that the approximation error stays bounded, using many particles during global localization and few once the belief is concentrated [71].
  • Rao–Blackwellization. When part of the state is conditionally linear-Gaussian, it can be marginalized analytically. FastSLAM samples the robot path with particles and attaches an independent small EKF to each landmark [31]; GMapping applies the same idea to occupancy-grid mapping with an improved proposal distribution and adaptive resampling [72].
  • The curse of dimensionality. The number of particles needed to avoid weight collapse grows exponentially with the effective dimension of the problem, so plain particle filters are unsuitable for high-dimensional states such as full SLAM maps or dense fields [73]. That is why particle filters in robotics usually handle a low-dimensional core (a pose, a hypothesis index) and leave the rest to Gaussian or optimization-based estimators.

5 Camera–lidar–IMU fusion

Cameras, lidar and inertial sensors are the core of most physical AI perception stacks because their strengths and failure modes complement each other. An IMU measures angular velocity and specific force at hundreds of hertz, unaffected by lighting or scene texture, but its integrated position drifts within seconds. Cameras provide dense appearance and bearing information at low cost but no direct metric scale, and degrade in darkness, glare, motion blur and texture-poor scenes. Lidar provides direct, accurate geometry largely independent of lighting, but its returns are sparse, its scans are distorted by motion during the sweep, and it struggles in long corridors and open fields where geometry is degenerate [23,74,75]. Both optical sensors degrade in fog, rain and snow, which motivates adding radar [76,77]. The vision article in this series covers visual odometry and SLAM from the camera's point of view; this section concentrates on how the three modalities are combined.

5.1 Prerequisites: calibration and synchronization

Fusion requires the rigid transforms between sensor frames (extrinsics) and the offsets between their clocks. Camera–lidar extrinsics are classically estimated from a checkerboard observed by both sensors [78], or without targets by maximizing the mutual information between lidar reflectivity and image intensity [79]. Camera–IMU extrinsics and time offset can be estimated jointly with a continuous-time B-spline trajectory, as in the Kalibr toolbox [44]. Hardware triggering or IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol timestamps reduce the clock problem at the source [80]. Because errors here masquerade as sensor noise, many estimators also refine extrinsics and time offsets online as additional states [50,81].

5.2 Architectures: loose versus tight coupling

In a loosely coupled system each sensor pipeline produces its own estimate (for example, a lidar scan-matching pose or a visual odometry pose) and a fusion filter combines those estimates. Loose coupling is modular and robust to the failure of one pipeline, but it discards the raw measurements' correlations and cannot use a sensor whose information alone is insufficient. A tightly coupled system feeds raw or low-level measurements (feature reprojections, point-to-plane residuals, inertial readings) into one estimator, which uses partial information, such as a few tracked features, that a separate pipeline would reject [43,82]. The same distinction exists in GNSS/INS integration, where tight coupling uses individual satellite pseudoranges instead of a position fix [43].

5.3 The inertial backbone

In most camera–lidar–IMU systems the IMU drives the prediction step: its measurements are integrated through the strapdown navigation equations to propagate attitude, velocity and position between exteroceptive updates, and its biases are carried as slowly varying states [43,74]. Lightweight attitude-only estimators, such as the complementary filters in the sensors article, apply the same principle on the rotation group [83]. The IMU also de-skews lidar sweeps by supplying the sensor's motion during the scan [38,84]. Optimization-based estimators cannot afford to re-integrate hundreds of IMU samples whenever a linearization point changes. Lupton and Sukkarieh proposed preintegrating the inertial measurements between two keyframes into a single relative-motion constraint that is independent of the initial state [35], and Forster and colleagues reformulated preintegration on the rotation manifold with analytic bias-correction Jacobians, the form used in most current systems [36].

5.4 Visual-inertial odometry

Visual-inertial odometry (VIO) recovers metric scale, gravity direction and IMU biases from the combination of a camera and an IMU. The multi-state constraint Kalman filter (MSCKF) keeps a sliding window of camera poses in an error-state EKF and uses each tracked feature to constrain the poses that observed it, without adding the feature to the state, so its cost is linear in the number of features [34]. Hesch and colleagues showed that a standard EKF-based VIO gains spurious information about global yaw, a direction that is in fact unobservable, and proposed observability-constrained updates to fix it [45]. ROVIO uses an iterated EKF with photometric error terms directly on image patches [56]. Optimization-based systems such as OKVIS, VINS-Mono and ORB-SLAM3 minimize reprojection and preintegrated-IMU residuals over a sliding window of keyframes with marginalization, and add loop closure for global consistency [49,50,85]. Reviews and open platforms such as OpenVINS, and benchmarks such as the EuRoC micro aerial vehicle datasets, make these methods comparable [81,82,86].

5.5 Lidar-inertial and lidar-visual-inertial odometry

LOAM established the pattern of extracting edge and planar features from lidar scans and registering them at two rates, odometry and mapping [84]. LIO-SAM places lidar odometry, preintegrated IMU factors, GNSS and loop closures in a factor graph solved incrementally with iSAM2 [33,38]. FAST-LIO takes the filtering route: a tightly coupled iterated error-state Kalman filter with a reformulated gain whose cost depends on the state dimension rather than the number of lidar points [57]. FAST-LIO2 registers raw points directly to the map and maintains it in an incremental k-d tree, reaching up to 100 Hz odometry and mapping on both Intel and ARM-based processors [39]. LVI-SAM and R3LIVE add cameras to lidar-inertial systems, so that either the visual or the lidar subsystem can carry the estimate when the other degenerates, and R3LIVE also builds a coloured dense map [40,87].

Dead reckoning on the biased IMU alone is useless after a minute: the accelerometer bias, integrated twice, puts it tens of metres off. Either exteroceptive sensor makes the IMU biases observable and recovers them to within a few per cent, after which the IMU bridges the gaps between updates. The two exteroceptive sensors are complementary even here: the lidar pose gives the best position, the camera bearings the best heading, and the combination is better than either. During the lidar outage the camera keeps the error bounded to about a third of its lidar-only value. Real systems face the harder versions of these problems, including degenerate lidar geometry, feature-poor images and unknown landmark positions, which is what the tightly coupled systems above address [40,75,87].

5.7 Learned feature-level fusion for perception

For object detection and scene understanding, camera–lidar fusion increasingly happens inside neural networks. MV3D fused region features from a lidar bird's-eye view, a lidar front view and the camera image [88]. PointPainting decorates each lidar point with the semantic-segmentation scores of the pixel it projects to, then runs a standard lidar detector [89]. TransFusion uses a transformer decoder whose object queries are initialized from lidar and refined with soft attention over image features, which makes it robust to poor image conditions and small calibration errors [90]. BEVFusion transforms both camera and lidar features into a shared bird's-eye-view grid and outperformed earlier fusion methods on nuScenes [41,91]. Bijelic and colleagues showed that adaptive, entropy-steered fusion of camera, lidar, radar and gated imaging generalizes to adverse weather not seen in training [76]. These detectors produce per-frame detections; turning them into tracks with velocities and identities is the job of the estimators in the next section.

6 Localization, mapping and tracking

Three estimation problems recur in every mobile physical AI system: where am I relative to a map (localization), what does the environment look like (mapping), and where are the other moving things and where are they going (tracking). Doing the first two at once is simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) [23,92].

6.1 Localization

Leonard and Durrant-Whyte's 1991 beacon tracker formulated mobile robot localization as an EKF that matches predicted to observed geometric features [93]. Monte Carlo localization later made global localization and recovery from failure routine on 2-D lidar maps [19,70]. Scan-matching methods such as iterative closest point (ICP) and the normal distributions transform (NDT) register a scan against a map and supply pose measurements to a filter [94,95]; Levinson, Montemerlo and Thrun localized autonomous cars to within about 10 cm against prior maps of lidar ground reflectivity with a particle filter [96]. Outdoors, GNSS supplies absolute position and inertial navigation bridges outages and multipath, usually in an error-state Kalman filter [43]. Map-based localization turns the problem into a fusion of map matching, odometry and GNSS whose weights must reflect how reliable each source currently is.

6.2 SLAM: from the stochastic map to factor graphs

Smith, Self and Cheeseman's stochastic map placed the robot pose and all landmark positions in one state vector with a full covariance, so that observing one landmark also corrects the others through their correlations [15]. Dissanayake and colleagues proved that this EKF-SLAM solution converges, with landmark uncertainty decreasing monotonically to a lower bound set by the initial uncertainty [97]. EKF-SLAM's cost grows quadratically with the number of landmarks, and its linearization makes it inconsistent over long runs [46], which led first to particle-filter methods such as FastSLAM [31] and then to graph-based formulations.

Lu and Milios formulated globally consistent scan alignment as optimization over a network of pose-to-pose constraints in 1997 [30], the idea behind modern pose-graph SLAM. Each node is a robot pose; each edge is a relative-pose measurement from odometry or from registering two scans, with an information matrix expressing its certainty. Loop-closure edges, created when the robot recognizes a previously visited place, connect distant parts of the trajectory and correct accumulated drift. Dellaert and Kaess showed that the information matrix of such problems is sparse and that a sparse factorization solves the full smoothing problem efficiently (√SAM) [21]; iSAM and iSAM2 update the solution incrementally as new measurements arrive [32,33]. General solvers such as g2o and GTSAM implement these methods, and Cartographer combined local submaps with real-time loop closure for 2-D and 3-D lidar [22,98,99]. Strasdat, Montiel and Davison showed for visual SLAM that keyframe optimization gives more accuracy per unit of computation than filtering, which is a large part of why optimization replaced filtering in most SLAM back ends [51]. Grisetti and colleagues give a tutorial derivation of the Gauss–Newton pose-graph solver used in the example [100].

Loop closures reduce the absolute trajectory error (ATE) more than tenfold in a handful of iterations. A single wrong loop closure, given the same confidence as the correct ones, drags the whole map out of shape, because least squares lets one large residual dominate. Replacing the quadratic cost with a Cauchy robust kernel, solved by iteratively reweighted least squares, downweights the inconsistent edge and recovers the correct solution. Robust back ends built on this idea include switchable constraints, dynamic covariance scaling and graduated non-convexity [101–103]; they are discussed further in Section 8.

6.3 Mapping

Moravec and Elfes introduced the occupancy grid in 1985 to build maps from wide-angle sonar: the environment is divided into cells, each holding the probability that it is occupied, updated with an inverse sensor model as each range reading arrives [104,105]. Working in log-odds turns the Bayesian update into an addition per cell [3]. OctoMap extends the idea to 3-D with an octree that represents free, occupied and unknown space compactly at multiple resolutions [106]. Dense surface reconstruction, semantic maps and learned scene representations build on the same pose estimates; the vision article in this series covers them.

6.4 Multi-object tracking

Tracking other agents adds a problem single-target filtering does not have: data association. A detector produces an unordered set of detections per frame, some of them false alarms, with some targets missed, and the tracker must decide which detection belongs to which track, when to start a track and when to end one [42]. Classical solutions, developed for radar and sonar, form a hierarchy:

  • Gating discards detections whose Mahalanobis distance to a track's predicted measurement exceeds a χ² threshold [42].
  • Global nearest neighbour (GNN) assigns detections to tracks one-to-one by solving a linear assignment problem, classically with Kuhn's Hungarian method [107].
  • Probabilistic data association (PDA) and its joint multi-target version (JPDA) update each track with a weighted mixture of all gated detections instead of committing to one [26,108].
  • Multiple hypothesis tracking (MHT) keeps a tree of association hypotheses over several frames and defers decisions until later data resolve them [27].
  • Random finite set filters such as the probability hypothesis density (PHD) filter propagate the first moment of the multi-target distribution, avoiding explicit association; the Gaussian-mixture PHD filter gives a closed-form implementation [109,110].
  • Interacting multiple model (IMM) filters run several motion models (for example, constant velocity and coordinated turn) in parallel and mix them according to Markov mode-switching probabilities, handling manoeuvring targets [28,64].

In robotics and autonomous driving these ideas meet deep detectors. SORT showed that a Kalman filter per track with Hungarian assignment on bounding-box overlap, fed by a strong detector, was competitive with far more complex trackers while running in real time [111]; DeepSORT added an appearance embedding to the association cost to reduce identity switches through occlusions [112]. AB3DMOT applied the same recipe to 3-D lidar detections as a baseline [113], and CenterPoint associates objects across frames using velocities predicted by the detector itself [114]. Performance is measured with the CLEAR MOT metrics (MOTA, MOTP) or set-based metrics such as OSPA that penalize both localization and cardinality errors [115,116].

7 Motion and velocity estimation

Controllers and planners need velocities as much as positions: a legged robot's balance controller needs its body velocity, a manipulator's impedance controller needs joint velocities, and a planner needs the velocities of other road users to predict their paths. Velocity is rarely measured directly, so it is estimated, either by differentiating position measurements, by integrating accelerations, or from sensors whose physics are sensitive to motion, such as Doppler radar and optical flow.

7.1 Odometry and encoder-based velocity

Wheel odometry integrates encoder increments through the robot's kinematic model (differential drive, Ackermann steering or omnidirectional) [117]. Its errors are partly systematic (unequal wheel diameters, uncertain wheelbase), which Borenstein and Feng's UMBmark procedure calibrates by driving a square path in both directions, and partly non-systematic (slip, uneven floors), which only fusion with other sensors can bound [118]. Velocity from an encoder is classically computed by counting ticks over a fixed interval or timing the interval between ticks; both trade quantization against delay. Bélanger showed that a Kalman filter with a kinematic model gives better velocity and acceleration estimates from shaft encoders than finite differences [119]. The quadrature decoding example in the sensors article produces exactly the count stream such a filter consumes.

7.2 Inertial and kinematic fusion on legged robots

Legged robots cannot use wheel odometry, but whenever a foot is in stationary contact with the ground, leg kinematics give the body's velocity relative to that foot. Bloesch and colleagues fused leg kinematics and IMU measurements in an EKF that includes the foot positions in the state, and analysed which states remain observable, obtaining a consistent estimator that needs no assumptions about the terrain [120]. Hartley and colleagues developed a contact-aided invariant EKF for the same problem whose error dynamics are independent of the state estimate, improving convergence and consistency [59]. Learned inertial odometry is a related trend: TLIO trains a network to regress displacement and its uncertainty from a window of IMU data and feeds both into an EKF as a measurement [121].

7.3 Doppler radar ego-motion

A Doppler radar measures the radial velocity of every reflection. For a stationary reflector at azimuth θ, the measured radial velocity depends only on the sensor's own velocity (vx, vy):

vr = −(vx cos θ + vy sin θ)

so a single scan containing two or more stationary reflectors determines the sensor velocity. Moving objects violate this model and must be rejected. Kellner and colleagues estimated the sensor velocity with RANSAC [122] followed by least squares, and then recovered the vehicle's speed and yaw rate from the sensor's mounting position under the assumption that the rear axle does not slip sideways [123]. Doer and Trommer combined such radar velocity measurements with an IMU in an EKF for radar-inertial odometry, which works in darkness, smoke and fog where cameras and lidar fail [124].

7.4 Optical flow and scene motion

Optical flow is the apparent motion of brightness patterns between images. Horn and Schunck estimated a dense flow field with a global smoothness constraint, and Lucas and Kanade estimated it locally by least squares over a window, both in 1981 [125,126]; deep networks such as RAFT now set the accuracy standard [127]. Combined with depth from stereo or lidar and with the camera's ego-motion, flow separates the motion of the robot from the motion of objects in the scene. Inside VIO, tracked feature flow is the measurement that constrains velocity between keyframes [34,50].

7.5 Motion models for other agents

Estimating another agent's velocity and predicting its path relies on a motion model. Li and Jilkov's survey catalogues the standard choices: white-noise acceleration (constant velocity), Wiener-process acceleration (constant acceleration), the Singer model with correlated acceleration, and coordinated-turn models [64]. The constant-velocity and CTRV models used in Sections 3 and 6 are the most common in robotics. When a target switches between models, an IMM filter weights them online [28]; when its behaviour depends on intent and context, as for pedestrians and drivers, learned predictors take over beyond the short horizon that kinematic models handle well.

8 Uncertainty estimation and sensor fault detection

An estimate without a trustworthy uncertainty is dangerous in a physical system, because downstream decisions (braking distance, grasp approach, whether to hand control to a person) depend on how wrong the estimate might be. Safety standards for road vehicles require hazards from faulty sensors and processing to be identified and controlled [128], and in practice that means monitoring whether the estimator's own uncertainty is credible and whether each sensor is behaving as modelled.

8.1 Where the covariance comes from, and how it goes wrong

A Kalman filter's covariance is correct only if Q, R and the models are correct. In practice, filters become overconfident for four recurring reasons: measurement noise that is correlated in time or between sensors but modelled as independent; linearization error (Section 3.2); unmodelled biases and delays; and fusing the same information twice, for example when two estimates that share a common source are combined as if independent [29,42,46]. The covariance of a scan-matching or visual-odometry front end is itself an estimate: Censi derived a closed-form ICP covariance that is far more realistic than heuristic values [129], and Zhang, Kaess and Singh showed how to detect degenerate directions in optimization-based estimators from the eigenvalues of the problem's Hessian, so that poorly constrained directions are not updated with false confidence [75].

8.2 Checking and tuning consistency

The NEES and NIS tests of Section 3.1 are the basic tools [42]. NEES is evaluated in simulation or against a motion-capture or survey-grade reference; NIS runs online. Mehra showed in 1970 that the innovation sequence of an optimal filter is white and used its sample autocorrelation to estimate unknown noise covariances, the basis of adaptive Kalman filtering [130]. Covariance tuning is therefore not guesswork: sensor noise is identified from static data (for inertial sensors, with the Allan variance shown in the sensors article), process noise is set so that NIS sits inside its χ² band on representative data, and the result is verified with NEES where ground truth exists [42,130].

8.3 Fusion with unknown correlations

Decentralized systems, such as several robots sharing map estimates or a vehicle fusing tracks from independent radar and camera trackers, often cannot know how correlated their inputs are. Treating them as independent double-counts shared information. Covariance intersection fuses two estimates as a convex combination of their information matrices, C−1 = ω A−1 + (1 − ω) B−1, with ω chosen to minimize the fused uncertainty, and is guaranteed consistent for any cross-correlation [29]. 8.4 Detecting and isolating faulty sensors

Sensor faults include abrupt steps (a GNSS multipath jump, a camera knocked out of alignment), slow drifts (a clock or bias ramp), stuck or frozen outputs, increased noise and complete dropouts. Fault detection and isolation (FDI) is a mature discipline: Willsky's 1976 survey already classified model-based methods [131], and Isermann's textbook covers residual generation, change detection and fault-tolerant design [132]. For estimators, the innovation is the natural residual. Mehra and Peschon proposed testing the innovation sequence for whiteness, zero mean and the correct covariance to detect faults in 1971 [133]. A χ² test on each NIS value catches abrupt faults immediately. A slow drift may never exceed that threshold, because the filter partly follows it, so sequential change detectors are used: Page's cumulative sum (CUSUM) test accumulates small deviations of a statistic from its expected value and alarms when the sum exceeds a threshold [134,135]. In satellite navigation, receiver autonomous integrity monitoring (RAIM) applies the same principle to redundant pseudoranges, using the least-squares residual to detect and exclude a faulty satellite [136].

The χ² gate rejects every fix during the step fault from its first epoch. The price is a handful of rejected good fixes, the usual trade between detection delay and false alarms that the CUSUM allowance and threshold control [135]. Detection is easier the more accurate the independent reference is; with poorer odometry the same ramp would go unnoticed for longer, which is why integrity monitoring needs redundancy, not just a test. In the correlated-fusion experiment, naive Kalman fusion produces a mean NEES above its consistent value of 2, while covariance intersection stays below it, slightly conservative as its guarantee implies [29].

8.5 Robust estimation

Detection with hard thresholds is one strategy; downweighting outliers continuously is another. Huber's M-estimators replace the quadratic cost with one that grows more slowly for large residuals [137], and in estimators they are applied by iteratively reweighted least squares, as in the pose-graph example of Section 6. Switchable constraints add a variable to each loop closure that the optimizer can use to turn it off [101]; dynamic covariance scaling achieves a similar effect in closed form [102]; and graduated non-convexity starts from a convex surrogate and gradually restores the robust cost, tolerating very high outlier rates without an initial guess [103]. In tracking, gating and PDA serve the same purpose [26,42].

8.6 Uncertainty from learned components

Networks increasingly provide the measurements that estimators fuse: detections, depth, learned inertial displacements. To be fused correctly, they must report uncertainty too. Kendall and Gal distinguished aleatoric uncertainty (noise inherent in the observation, which a network can learn to predict per input) from epistemic uncertainty (the model's ignorance, which grows on unfamiliar inputs) and showed how to estimate both [138]. Deep ensembles estimate predictive uncertainty by training several networks and measuring their disagreement [139]. TLIO illustrates the payoff: its network outputs a covariance with each displacement, and the EKF uses that covariance as R, so the filter trusts the network less when the network is unsure [121]. Even so, a learned uncertainty should be checked with the same NIS tests as any other sensor.

9 Learning, tooling and engineering practice

9.1 Learning inside the estimator

Rather than replacing filters, much current research makes them differentiable and learns the parts that are hardest to model by hand. Backprop KF trains a convolutional network to produce measurements and their noise for a Kalman filter by backpropagating through the filter itself [24]. Differentiable particle filters do the same for the motion and measurement models of a particle filter, encoding the Bayes-filter structure as an algorithmic prior [140]. KalmanNet keeps the known parts of the state-space model and replaces the Kalman gain computation with a recurrent network, which helps when noise statistics are unknown or the model is only partially known [25]. For physical AI systems built around large learned policies, these hybrids offer a way to keep the interpretability, uncertainty and fault-detection machinery of classical estimation while learning from data.

9.2 Tooling

Most robots do not need a custom estimator. ROS 2 provides standard message types, coordinate-frame trees and timestamping for multi-sensor systems [141], and the robot_localization package offers configurable EKF and UKF nodes that fuse odometry, IMU, GNSS and pose sources [55]. For smoothing and SLAM, g2o and GTSAM provide factor-graph optimization with robust kernels, IMU preintegration and incremental solvers [22,98]. Open-source VIO and LIO systems such as OpenVINS, VINS-Mono, ORB-SLAM3, LIO-SAM and FAST-LIO2 are common starting points, evaluated on public datasets such as EuRoC, KITTI and nuScenes [38,39,50,81,85,86,91,142].

Conclusion

Sensor fusion and state estimation are where a physical AI system's raw measurements become something it can act on. The core ideas are more than half a century old: Kalman's recursive filter, its Bayesian interpretation, the Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother and the innovation tests for consistency and faults. What has changed is scale and structure. Particle filters made multimodal beliefs practical, factor graphs and sparse solvers made it possible to smooth over entire trajectories in real time, IMU preintegration and iterated error-state filters made tightly coupled camera–lidar–IMU odometry routine, and learned networks now supply measurements, uncertainties and even gains inside these estimators. The engineering discipline stays the same throughout: model every sensor honestly, check that the estimator's confidence is earned, and design for the moment one input is wrong.
