1 What a world representation is for

Every robot that acts on more than its current sensor reading keeps some description of the world in memory. David Marr defined a representation as "a formal system for making explicit certain entities or types of information, together with a specification of how the system does this", and stressed that the choice of representation decides which questions are easy to answer and which are hard [1]. That trade-off is the subject of this article. A list of 3-D points makes it easy to align two scans but hard to ask whether a corridor is wide enough for the robot; a voxel grid answers the corridor question in constant time but says nothing about which voxels belong to the same chair; a scene graph says that the mug is on the table in the kitchen but cannot be used directly to plan a collision-free arm motion [2–4].

The world model of the previous article in this series predicts how the world will change, especially in response to the agent's actions (World models, section 1). A world model needs a state to predict, and this article is about that state: the data structures, learned or hand-designed, in which geometry, semantics and physical properties are held, fused over time and queried. Davison calls the combined system that maintains such a representation in real time "spatial AI" and argues that its design is dominated by the structure of the representation, its memory layout and the cost of keeping it consistent as new data arrive [5].

1.1 Requirements

Surveys of robotic mapping and semantic perception converge on a common list of properties by which representations can be compared [2,3,5,6]:

  • Metric fidelity: how accurately geometry is stored, and at what resolution.
  • Uncertainty: whether the representation distinguishes known-free, known-occupied and unknown space, and whether it carries probabilities or covariances [7].
  • Incremental update: whether new measurements can be fused locally, and whether the map can be deformed when the pose estimate changes after a loop closure [2,8].
  • Query cost: nearest-neighbour search, ray casting, collision and distance queries, and semantic queries such as "where are the cups?".
  • Memory: how storage scales with the size of the environment and the resolution.
  • Semantics and structure: whether objects, rooms and relations are explicit [9–11].
  • Dynamics and physics: whether moving objects, mass, friction and articulation can be represented and simulated.
  • Differentiability: whether gradients of a rendering or physics loss can flow back into the representation, which learning-based systems increasingly require [12].

No single representation is best on all of these, which is why practical systems layer several of them. Table 1 previews the families covered in sections 3 to 8.

1.2 Frames, poses and uncertainty

Whatever its content, a representation is expressed in coordinate frames, and robotics represents rigid transforms between frames as elements of the special Euclidean group SE(3): a rotation in SO(3) and a translation [30,31]. Pose estimates are uncertain, so mapping systems either attach covariances to poses and landmarks, as in the stochastic map of Smith, Self and Cheeseman [32,33], or keep the poses as variables in a factor graph and re-optimize them as evidence accumulates [34,35]. Perturbations and covariances are most conveniently handled in the tangent space of the group, which is why Lie-group machinery appears throughout modern estimators [31,36]. A practical consequence for every representation below is that maps should be anchored to poses that may later move: surfel maps deform, submaps are re-anchored, and object nodes in a scene graph carry their own pose relative to a parent [4,8]. The estimation side of this story is told in the sensor-fusion article (Sensor fusion, localization and mapping).

2 A short history: from blocks worlds to neural fields

2.1 Cognitive maps and the first machine representations

The idea that animals navigate with an internal map predates robotics. Tolman's rat experiments in the 1940s led him to propose "cognitive maps" in place of chains of stimulus-response associations [37]. In 1971 O'Keefe and Dostrovsky found hippocampal neurons that fire when a rat is in a particular place [38], and in 2005 Hafting and colleagues found entorhinal "grid cells" whose firing fields tile the environment in a regular triangular grid, a metric code for position [39]. Psychologist Kenneth Craik had already argued in 1943 that an organism carrying "a small-scale model of external reality" could try out actions before taking them [40].

The first machine representations were geometric. Roberts' 1963 thesis recovered polyhedral "blocks world" models from line drawings [41]; Binford proposed generalized cylinders as volumetric shape primitives [42]; Baumgart's winged-edge polyhedra became a standard boundary representation [43]; and Requicha's 1980 survey organized solid modelling into boundary representations, constructive solid geometry and spatial-occupancy enumerations, categories that still describe CAD and simulation assets today [44]. SRI's Shakey robot (1966-72) combined a grid model of free space with logical assertions about rooms, doors and boxes that its STRIPS planner manipulated [45,46]. Marr's influential framework then described vision as building successively richer representations: the primal sketch, the viewer-centred "2½-D sketch" of depth and surface orientation, and object-centred 3-D models [1].

2.2 Probabilistic mapping

Mobile robotics in the 1980s introduced uncertainty explicitly. Chatila and Laumond built consistent world models from uncertain observations [47]; Smith and Cheeseman's stochastic map represented landmarks and robot pose jointly with a covariance matrix, the origin of EKF-SLAM [32,33]; and Moravec and Elfes introduced the occupancy grid, a tessellation of space into cells each holding the probability of being occupied, built from wide-angle sonar [48,49]. Kuipers' spatial semantic hierarchy layered control, causal, topological and metric levels of representation, arguing that robust navigation needs the qualitative layers as well as metric ones [9,50]. In 1991 Brooks argued the opposite extreme: that the world itself could serve as the model and that behaviour-based robots could dispense with central representations altogether [51]; the field's subsequent history can be read as a search for representations that are cheap enough to maintain and rich enough to be worth it. By the 2000s probabilistic grid and feature maps were standard, and SLAM had become a mature estimation problem with graph-based back ends [2,7,52].

2.3 Dense, semantic and learned representations

Three developments reshaped the field after 2010. First, commodity depth cameras and GPUs made dense volumetric reconstruction real-time: KinectFusion (2011) fused depth images into a truncated signed distance field (TSDF) on a GPU, building on Curless and Levoy's 1996 volumetric method [16,53], and voxel hashing and octrees made such maps scale [15,54]. Second, deep networks made semantics cheap, leading to semantic and object-level maps and then to hierarchical 3-D scene graphs that organize buildings into floors, rooms, places and objects [10,11,18,19,55]. Third, the representation itself became learned: point-set networks such as PointNet operated directly on raw points [21], implicit networks such as DeepSDF and occupancy networks stored shapes as continuous functions [24,56], neural radiance fields (NeRF) represented whole scenes as networks rendered by volume rendering [25], and 3D Gaussian splatting (2023) replaced the network with millions of explicit, differentiable Gaussian primitives [26]. In parallel, the aerospace and manufacturing communities developed the digital twin, a virtual representation of a specific physical asset kept synchronized with it through data [57–59], which today merges with robot simulation and world foundation models [60,61].

3 Geometric maps and point clouds

The oldest and still most widely used representations store geometry explicitly, as samples of surfaces or as a sparse set of distinctive landmarks, anchored in one or more coordinate frames. They differ in density, in what they make explicit (points, normals, connectivity) and in how they are indexed for search.

3.1 Landmark and feature maps

A feature map stores a sparse set of landmarks, each with a position and, in visual SLAM, a descriptor for recognizing it again. EKF-SLAM keeps landmark positions and the robot pose in one Gaussian state [32,62]; graph-based systems keep them as variables in a factor graph and refine them by bundle adjustment [34,63]. ORB-SLAM's map, for example, consists of keyframes, 3-D map points with ORB descriptors and a covisibility graph linking keyframes that observe common points [64,65]. Such maps are compact and excellent for localization, but they say almost nothing about free space: a sparse map cannot tell a robot whether it can drive between two landmarks [2]. The construction of these maps is covered in the vision article (Visual odometry and SLAM).

3.2 Topological and hybrid maps

A topological map is a graph whose nodes are distinctive places and whose edges are traversable routes; it records connectivity rather than coordinates. Kuipers and Byun built such maps from distinctive places found by hill-climbing a distinctiveness measure [50], Choset and Nagatani used the generalized Voronoi graph, the set of points equidistant from the two nearest obstacles, as a topological skeleton of free space [66], and appearance-based methods such as FAB-MAP recognize places from visual words without metric geometry [67]. Topological maps scale to large environments and support fast route planning, which is why the "places" layer of modern 3-D scene graphs is essentially a topological map attached to metric geometry (section 5) [11,19]. Hybrid metric-topological maps keep local metric submaps connected by a graph, so that loop closures re-anchor submaps instead of rewriting a global grid [6,9].

3.3 Point clouds

A point cloud is an unordered set of 3-D points, optionally with intensity, colour, normal or time stamp per point, as produced directly by lidar, depth cameras and stereo (Sensors, range sensing) [68,69]. Point clouds make no assumption about topology and so represent thin structures, clutter and vegetation that defeat surface models. Their weaknesses are equally direct: density varies with range and incidence angle, there is no notion of free space or of which points belong to the same surface, and storage grows with every scan.

Three operations dominate point-cloud processing. Downsampling replaces all points within a voxel by their centroid; implemented with a spatial hash of integer voxel coordinates, it needs memory only for occupied voxels [54,70]. Neighbour search underlies normal estimation, clustering and registration, and is served by k-d trees, which Bentley introduced in 1975 and which answer k-nearest-neighbour queries in roughly logarithmic time for low-dimensional data [71], or by octrees [72]. Registration aligns clouds: the iterative closest point (ICP) algorithm alternates nearest-neighbour correspondence with a closed-form rigid alignment [13,73], with many variants for speed and robustness [74]; the normal distributions transform (NDT) instead models each voxel's points as a Gaussian and aligns scans to that piecewise-smooth density [75]; and LOAM extracts edge and planar feature points to make lidar odometry real-time [14]. For storage and transmission, MPEG has standardized geometry-based and video-based point cloud compression (G-PCC and V-PCC) [76].

3.4 Normals, surfels and meshes

Points become surfaces once local structure is estimated. Hoppe and colleagues estimated a tangent plane at each point by principal component analysis of its neighbours: the eigenvector of the neighbourhood covariance with the smallest eigenvalue is the normal, and the ratio of that eigenvalue to the sum of all three ("surface variation") measures local curvature or noise [77,78]. Normals have a sign ambiguity that must be resolved, for example by orienting them towards the sensor [68,77]. A surfel (surface element) stores position, normal, radius and often colour and confidence; surfels were introduced as rendering primitives [79] and became a map representation in ElasticFusion, whose surfel map is deformed non-rigidly by a deformation graph when a loop closure corrects the trajectory [8]. Triangle meshes add explicit connectivity, enabling rendering, physics collision and geodesic computation; they are usually extracted from volumetric fields by marching cubes [80] or fitted to oriented points by Poisson reconstruction [81], and processed with half-edge data structures [82].

3.5 Semantic geometric maps for driving

Automated driving uses a specialized geometric representation, the high-definition (HD) map, which stores lane boundaries, lane connectivity, traffic rules and landmarks with centimetre accuracy. Lanelet2, for instance, represents roads as "lanelets" (atomic lane sections bounded by left and right polylines) with regulatory elements attached, and layers physical, relational and topological information so that the same map supports localization, routing and behaviour planning [84]. HD maps illustrate a general pattern: geometry alone is not enough, and the useful representation couples geometry with semantics and connectivity, the theme of section 5.

4 Occupancy grids and volumetric representations

Volumetric representations partition space itself, not just surfaces, and store a quantity for every cell: an occupancy probability, a signed distance to the nearest surface, a height, or a semantic label. Because free and unknown space are represented explicitly, they are the natural input to collision checking and motion planning.

4.1 Occupancy grids

An occupancy grid stores, for each cell mi, the posterior probability that it is occupied given all measurements, assuming that cells are independent [48,49]. Under that assumption each cell runs a binary Bayes filter, most conveniently in log-odds form

lt,i = lt−1,i + log [ p(mi | zt, xt) / (1 − p(mi | zt, xt)) ] − l0

where p(mi | zt, xt) is the inverse sensor model, high near a range return and low along the beam before it, and l0 the prior log-odds [7]. Cells along each beam are enumerated with a voxel traversal algorithm such as that of Amanatides and Woo [85]. The independence assumption ignores correlations between neighbouring cells and the per-cell update can be overconfident, so practical systems clamp log-odds to bounds, which also lets the map forget stale evidence and keeps it compressible [15]. A worked 2-D example is in the vision article (Scene understanding, bird's-eye-view and occupancy representations); here we focus on how volumetric maps scale and what else they can store.

4.2 Octrees, hashing and 2.5-D maps

A dense 3-D grid at 5 cm resolution needs 8,000 cells per cubic metre, and most of them are either empty or never observed. Octrees, introduced for solid modelling by Meagher [72], recursively subdivide a cube into eight children only where needed, and OctoMap made them the standard probabilistic 3-D map in robotics: inner nodes summarize their children, homogeneous subtrees are pruned to a single node, and queries can be answered at any resolution [15]. Voxel hashing takes a different route, allocating small dense blocks (for example 8×8×8 voxels) only near observed surfaces and finding them through a hash table, which gives constant-time access and is well suited to GPUs [54]; OpenVDB's hierarchical B+-tree-like structure plays the same role in film and simulation [86].

Outdoors and on legged robots, the world is often stored as a 2.5-D elevation map, a grid of heights with variances. Fankhauser and colleagues propagate the robot's pose uncertainty into the elevation map so that terrain near the robot stays sharp while distant cells grow uncertain [87], and multi-level surface maps store several height intervals per cell so that bridges and overhangs can be represented [88].

4.3 Signed distance fields

Occupancy says whether a cell is full; a signed distance field (SDF) says how far each point is from the nearest surface, positive outside and negative inside, so the surface is the zero level set. Curless and Levoy fused range images into a truncated SDF by keeping, in each voxel near a surface, a weighted running average of the distance to the measured surface along the sensor ray, truncated to a narrow band [53]:

Dt(v) = [Wt−1(v) Dt−1(v) + wt(v) dt(v)] / [Wt−1(v) + wt(v)],    Wt(v) = Wt−1(v) + wt(v)

Averaging many noisy observations of the same surface reduces noise, and interpolating the zero crossing between voxels locates the surface to a fraction of a voxel. KinectFusion implemented this on a GPU with ray casting for tracking [16], and marching cubes extracts a mesh from the zero level set [80]. For planning, however, the TSDF's "projective" distances along camera rays are not true Euclidean distances, and they are only defined within the truncation band. A Euclidean signed distance field (ESDF) stores the true distance to the nearest obstacle everywhere, so a collision check for a spherical robot body reduces to one lookup, and the ESDF gradient points away from obstacles, which trajectory optimizers such as CHOMP use directly [89]. Voxblox builds an ESDF incrementally from a TSDF [17], FIESTA builds it directly from occupancy [90], and nvblox does both on a GPU [91]; batch ESDFs can be computed in linear time with exact distance transforms [92].

Systems therefore inflate robot radii by at least the voxel size or interpolate distances more carefully [17,90].

4.4 Continuous, semantic and predicted volumes

Grids discretize space at a fixed resolution and treat cells independently. Gaussian process occupancy maps instead model occupancy as a continuous function with spatial correlation, filling gaps between beams and providing calibrated variances, at a computational cost that grows quickly with data [93]. Hilbert maps make continuous occupancy scalable by projecting points into a feature space and training a logistic-regression classifier online by stochastic gradient descent [94], a direct precursor of the feature encodings and neural fields of sections 6 and 7.

Voxels can also hold semantics. Metric-semantic systems such as SemanticFusion and Kimera fuse per-pixel class probabilities from a segmentation network into a volumetric map or mesh by Bayesian updates [55,95]. Learned volumetric prediction goes further and fills in what was never observed: semantic scene completion predicts occupancy and class for the whole view frustum from a single depth image [96], and in autonomous driving, camera-based networks predict dense 3-D semantic occupancy around the vehicle, now evaluated on benchmarks such as Occ3D [97–99]. These predicted grids are no longer measurements but beliefs, and they inherit the calibration problems of the networks that produce them [100].

5 Scene graphs and object-centric representations

Geometric and volumetric maps describe where matter is. Tasks are phrased in terms of things: fetch the mug, open the drawer, avoid the person. Object-centric representations make those things explicit as entities with identity, pose, shape, category and properties, and scene graphs add the relations among them and the larger structures (places, rooms, floors) that contain them [3,10,11]. How 2-D scene graphs and pairwise spatial predicates are computed from detections is covered in the vision article (Scene understanding); this section is about the representation itself and how it is maintained over time.

5.1 Object-level maps

SLAM++ (2013) was an early demonstration that a map can consist of objects instead of points: it recognized known furniture models in depth data, inserted each as a node with a 6-DoF pose in a pose graph, and gained compression, loop-closure robustness and the ability to predict unseen geometry from the models [18]. Later systems relaxed the need for a model database. Fusion++ keeps a separate small TSDF per object instance, discovered by instance segmentation, so objects can be re-localized and later moved [101]; QuadricSLAM represents each object by a dual quadric, an ellipsoid estimated directly from 2-D bounding boxes in several views [102]; and CubeSLAM uses 3-D cuboids from single images as landmarks jointly optimized with camera poses [103]. Panoptic Multi-TSDFs combine a volumetric background with per-object submaps that can be added, removed and replaced as the scene changes over long periods [104]. The common idea is a two-level representation: a coarse object-level state (identity, pose, extent, class, existence probability) that planners and language interfaces use, and attached detailed geometry used for grasping and collision checking.

5.2 Hierarchical 3-D scene graphs

A 3-D scene graph is a layered, directed graph. Armeni and colleagues proposed layers for building, rooms, objects and cameras, with attributes on nodes and relations on edges [10]; Kim and colleagues built 3-D scene graphs from RGB-D for agents [105]; and Wald and colleagues learned to predict semantic scene graphs directly from 3-D reconstructions [106]. Rosinol and colleagues' "3D dynamic scene graphs" made the idea actionable for robots by stacking five layers, a metric-semantic mesh, objects and agents (including people with time-varying pose), places (a topological map of free space), rooms, and buildings, and by building them from sensor data with Kimera [4,11]. Hydra constructs such graphs incrementally in real time, detecting rooms from the places layer and correcting the whole hierarchy after loop closures [19]. Open-vocabulary variants attach vision-language features to nodes so that objects can be found by free-form text: ConceptGraphs merges per-view 2-D segments into 3-D object nodes with CLIP and language-model captions [20,107], and HOV-SG builds floor, room and object layers with open-vocabulary features for language-grounded navigation [108].

The graph is useful because it matches the structure of tasks. Language models can plan over a compact textual rendering of a scene graph where a dense map would not fit in a prompt, as in SayPlan, which searches a 3-D scene graph by expanding and collapsing rooms [109]. Classical task planners need a symbolic state of predicates such as on(mug, table), the representation used since STRIPS and standardized in PDDL [46,110], and task-and-motion planning connects such symbols to geometry [111]. Knowledge-processing frameworks such as KnowRob go further and link scene objects to ontologies of object classes, affordances and actions [112].

5.3 Keeping an object map current

The world changes, and an object representation must decide what to believe about objects it is not currently seeing. Infants already expect objects to persist when occluded [113,114], and robots need the same discipline: absence of a detection is evidence of absence only if the object should have been visible. A standard approach borrowed from multi-target tracking keeps an existence probability per object and updates it by Bayes' rule with the detector's detection and false-alarm rates, but only for objects inside the current field of view and not occluded [115,116]. Long-term systems also model the rhythm of change; FreMEn, for example, represents the probability that a door is open or a place is occupied as a sum of periodic components learned from observations [117].

Two properties of the representation do the work. First, the hierarchy keeps queries cheap: the whole three-room scene is 16 nodes, compared with over a million voxels for the same space at 5 cm, and route planning runs on seven places rather than on a grid. Second, the existence update is visibility-aware: three failed looks at the table drive the old mug's probability from 0.90 to 0.01, while the laptop, which was never in view, keeps its prior of 0.85. Without that distinction a robot either forgets everything it cannot see or never notices that things have moved [104,117].

5.4 Learned object-centric representations

A parallel line of machine-learning research asks networks to discover objects without labels. The motivation is the "binding problem": distributed neural representations do not naturally separate, bind and reuse the features of distinct entities [119]. MONet and IODINE decomposed images into per-object latent vectors with attention or iterative variational inference [120,121]; Slot Attention introduced a simple iterative attention module that maps a set of input features to a fixed number of exchangeable "slots" [122]; and SAVi extended slots to video, conditioning them on cues such as an initial bounding box and propagating them over time [123]. Object-centric latents are attractive as world-model states because dynamics can then be learned per object and per interaction, as in interaction networks and contrastive structured world models (World models, section 4) [124,125]. The gap between these learned slots and the explicit object maps of robotics is narrowing as foundation-model features make unsupervised segmentation more reliable [20,126].

6 3-D spatial embeddings

An embedding maps geometry, a point, a point set, a voxel, a view or a whole place, to a vector in which useful similarity is easy to compute. In physical AI, embeddings serve three purposes: encoding position so that networks can represent detailed functions of space; encoding sets of points or volumes so that networks can recognize and segment them; and attaching semantic features to locations so that space can be queried by meaning. A thread running through all three is invariance and equivariance to the rigid motions of SE(3) [127].

6.1 Encoding position

Feeding raw coordinates to a multilayer perceptron gives poor results for detailed shapes because such networks are biased towards low-frequency functions, the "spectral bias" analysed by Rahaman and colleagues [128]. Two remedies dominate. Fourier features map a coordinate x to [sin(2πBx), cos(2πBx)] with a random or fixed frequency matrix B; Tancik and colleagues showed through the neural tangent kernel that the scale of B sets the bandwidth of what the network can learn, too small giving blur and too large giving noisy interpolation [129]. The construction is related to the random features that approximate shift-invariant kernels [130] and to the sinusoidal positional encodings of transformers [131], and NeRF's positional encoding is a deterministic version of it [25]. Learned feature grids instead store trainable vectors at the vertices of one or more grids and interpolate them: Plenoxels showed that a sparse voxel grid of coefficients can replace the network entirely [132], convolutional occupancy networks use 2-D planes or 3-D volumes of features [133], EG3D introduced axis-aligned "triplanes" [134], and Instant-NGP's multiresolution hash encoding stores features for many grid resolutions in hash tables, reducing training times for neural graphics primitives to seconds or minutes [135].

Polynomials cannot represent the teeth; Fourier features at bandwidth σ = 1 are too smooth, σ = 6 is about right, and σ = 24 fails completely because 4,000 samples cannot constrain such high frequencies between samples, the behaviour Tancik and colleagues predicted [129]. Multiresolution grids match the best Fourier features without any bandwidth tuning, because each level adds detail only where the data demand it, which is the rationale for hash-grid encodings [135]. In practice the read-out is a small MLP rather than a linear map, but the encoding decides most of what the model can represent.

6.2 Encoding point sets and volumes

Point clouds are unordered, so a network on raw points must be invariant to permutation. PointNet achieved this by applying a shared MLP to each point and aggregating with a symmetric max-pooling function [21]; PointNet++ added hierarchical grouping to capture local structure [136]; DGCNN builds dynamic k-nearest-neighbour graphs in feature space [137]; and Point Transformer applies self-attention within local neighbourhoods [138]. For large scenes, sparse convolutions on voxelized points compute only at occupied sites: submanifold sparse convolutions keep the active set fixed through the network [139], and the Minkowski engine generalizes sparse convolution to 4-D space-time [140]. Detection backbones such as VoxelNet and PointPillars, and bird's-eye-view (BEV) feature maps lifted from cameras, are embeddings of this kind optimized for driving (Vision, section 9.4) [98,99,141,142].

Rigid motion is a nuisance for recognition and a signal for manipulation, so it should be handled explicitly. Rotation-invariant embeddings ignore it; rotation-equivariant embeddings transform predictably with it, so that a rotated input yields a correspondingly rotated output. Tensor field networks and SE(3)-transformers build equivariance into layers using spherical harmonics [143,144], and Vector Neurons lift each neuron to a 3-D vector so that standard layers become SO(3)-equivariant [145]. Neural descriptor fields use such equivariant features to represent object-relative poses, so that a grasp demonstrated on one mug transfers to a new mug in a new pose [146]. When orientation itself must be regressed, continuous parameterizations such as the 6-D form built from two columns of the rotation matrix behave better than quaternions or Euler angles, which are discontinuous representations of rotation for a network [147].

6.3 Embedding places

Global place descriptors compress an entire scan or image into one vector so that a robot can recognize where it is among thousands of stored places, the front end of loop closure. NetVLAD aggregates local image features into a trainable descriptor [148] and PointNetVLAD does the same for point clouds [149]. Scan Context is a hand-designed alternative for lidar: it bins points into rings and sectors around the sensor and records the maximum height in each bin; a "ring key" summarizing each ring is invariant to heading and is used for fast candidate retrieval, and comparing the full descriptor under circular column shifts both verifies the match and estimates the relative yaw [22].

6.4 Semantic feature fields and implicit scene embeddings

Vision-language models such as CLIP embed images and text in a shared space [107]. Lifting their features into 3-D gives maps that can be queried with arbitrary language. VLMaps fuses pixel-aligned visual-language features into a top-down grid for navigation [150]; OpenScene co-embeds 3-D points with text and image pixels in CLIP feature space [151]; distilled feature fields and LERF add a feature output to a radiance field and train it to reproduce 2-D foundation-model features from every view [23,152]; and F3RM uses such fields for few-shot, language-guided grasping [153]. Large multimodal models are also being given 3-D inputs directly, as in 3D-LLM and SpatialVLM [154,155].

A more radical idea is to embed the whole scene in a latent vector or set and to let a network answer queries about it. DeepMind's Generative Query Network encoded several views of a scene into a single representation from which a decoder rendered unseen views [156], and the Scene Representation Transformer encodes images into a set of latent tokens without explicit 3-D geometry [157]. Notably, agents trained to navigate develop grid-cell-like units resembling those found in the entorhinal cortex, suggesting that metric spatial embeddings can emerge when they are useful [39,158].

7 Neural representations of geometry and dynamics

A neural field is a field, a quantity defined at every point of space and possibly time, that is parameterized fully or partly by a neural network [12]. Instead of storing values on a grid, the scene is the function fθ(x) itself, queried at any resolution, compact when the network is small, and differentiable with respect to both the input coordinates and the parameters. Xie and colleagues' survey traces the idea through graphics, vision and robotics and identifies its common components: the coordinate encoding (section 6.1), the network or primitive representation, a differentiable "forward map" such as rendering that connects the field to measurements, and priors or regularizers [12].

7.1 Implicit surfaces

In 2019 three groups independently represented shapes as networks that classify or measure points in space. Occupancy networks predict the probability that a point lies inside an object [56]; IM-NET predicts inside/outside for generative shape modelling [159]; and DeepSDF predicts the signed distance and conditions the network on a per-shape latent code, so that one network represents a whole category and can complete partial scans by optimizing the code [24]. Surfaces are extracted at the zero level set with marching cubes, and normals are the network's input gradient [24,80].

Gropp and colleagues' implicit geometric regularization (IGR) showed that a network can learn a signed distance function from a raw point cloud with no distance supervision at all: the loss asks for f = 0 on the samples (and optionally ∇f = n where normals are known) and adds an eikonal term (‖∇f‖ − 1)2 at random points, because a true distance function has unit-norm gradient almost everywhere [160]. SIREN replaced ReLUs with sine activations so that derivatives of the field are themselves well represented, which matters when losses involve gradients [161]. The example trains a small network with Fourier-feature inputs on 300 noisy oriented surface points of a 2-D shape, with the gradients of the eikonal and normal terms derived by hand.

Robotics uses the same ingredients online: iSDF trains a neural SDF in real time from a depth stream and queries it for collision costs and gradients [162].

7.2 Radiance fields and volume rendering

Mildenhall and colleagues' neural radiance fields (NeRF) represent a scene as a network that maps a 3-D position and viewing direction to a volume density σ and an emitted colour c, and render a pixel by the classical emission-absorption volume rendering integral [25,163,164], approximated by quadrature over samples ti along the ray:

Ĉ = Σi Ti (1 − e−σiδi) ci,    Ti = exp(−Σj<i σjδj)

Because every step is differentiable, the field is trained only from posed photographs by minimizing photometric error, and expected depth follows by replacing ci with ti [25]. Density, however, is not a surface; geometry extracted from it is often noisy. NeuS and VolSDF therefore parameterize density as a function of a signed distance field, for example VolSDF's σ(x) = α Ψβ(−d(x)) with Ψβ the cumulative distribution function of a zero-mean Laplace distribution with scale β, so that the surface is well defined as the SDF's zero level set while training still uses volume rendering [165,166].

7.3 Gaussian splatting

3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) keeps the differentiable rendering but discards the network. The scene is a set of anisotropic 3-D Gaussians, each with position, covariance, opacity and view-dependent colour (spherical-harmonic coefficients); for each frame they are projected to 2-D Gaussians, sorted by depth in screen tiles and alpha-composited front to back with the same transmittance formula as NeRF [26]. The projection follows the elliptical weighted average (EWA) splatting of Zwicker and colleagues [167]. Because rasterizing sorted primitives is far cheaper than querying a network hundreds of times per ray, 3DGS reached real-time rendering at 1080p with quality competitive with the best NeRF variants, and its explicit primitives can be edited, moved and attached to physics [26,168]. 2D Gaussian splatting flattens the primitives into oriented disks, essentially differentiable surfels, to obtain more accurate geometry [79,169].

The example renders depth for a 1-D pinhole camera in a 2-D scene, first by volume rendering an SDF-derived density at several sharpnesses β and sample counts, then by alpha-compositing 4,000 depth-sorted Gaussian splats placed on the surfaces.

The table shows the central tension of volume rendering. A soft density (β = 0.1) is easy to sample but biased, because the rendered depth averages over a thick shell; a sharp density (β = 0.01) is accurate to about 3 mm but only with hundreds of samples per ray, since coarse samples step over the thin surface. NeRF addresses this with hierarchical importance sampling and Instant-NGP with occupancy grids that skip empty space [25,135]. The splat renderer reaches millimetre-level depth with one sort per frame and no per-ray search. Both representations produce mixed depths at silhouettes, where a pixel's weights straddle foreground and background; such "flying pixels" must be filtered before rendered depth is used for collision checking.

7.4 Neural fields in robotics

Neural fields have moved quickly into mapping and manipulation. iMAP showed that a single MLP could serve as the only map in a real-time RGB-D SLAM system, trained continually on keyframes [170]; NICE-SLAM replaced the single network with hierarchical feature grids for scalability [171]; and SplaTAM and similar systems build Gaussian-splat maps online, tracking the camera by rendering [172]. For planning, Adamkiewicz and colleagues navigated a robot through a pre-trained NeRF by treating its density as a collision cost [173]; Dex-NeRF exploited NeRF's view-dependent density to grasp transparent objects that depth cameras miss [174]; and 3-D neural scene representations have served as state encoders for visuomotor control [175]. Feed-forward models such as DUSt3R now predict dense 3-D point maps from uncalibrated image pairs without per-scene optimization, blurring the line between reconstruction and representation learning (Vision, 3-D reconstruction) [176].

7.5 Representing dynamics

Static fields describe a frozen world. Several extensions represent change. Deformation fields map each observation time to a canonical space: D-NeRF learns a time-conditioned displacement field for dynamic scenes [177] and Nerfies an optimization-regularized deformation for casually captured, non-rigidly moving subjects [178]. Persistent primitives track the same Gaussians through time, as in Dynamic 3D Gaussians, which yields dense 6-DoF tracking of every point as a by-product of view synthesis [179]. Compositional object fields give each object its own field and learn their interactions: Driess and colleagues combined per-object NeRF encodings with a graph neural network dynamics model to plan multi-object manipulation from images [180,181].

The most physically grounded approach couples the representation to a simulator. PAC-NeRF represents an object as a continuum, renders it with a radiance field and simulates it with a differentiable material point method, so that material parameters such as Young's modulus or viscosity can be identified from video [182]; PhysGaussian likewise treats Gaussian kernels as material-point particles carrying mass, velocity and stress, so the same primitives are rendered and simulated [168]. Learned simulators on particles and meshes (Physical modelling, deformables) and physics-informed networks [181,183,184] are the dynamics counterpart of neural fields, and together they turn a reconstruction into something that can be pushed, poured or bent in simulation, which brings us to digital twins.

8 Digital twins and physics-based models

The representations so far describe how the world looks and where things are. A physics-based model also says how the world responds: masses, inertias, joint limits, friction coefficients, stiffness, actuator models. A digital twin is such a model of a specific physical asset or environment, kept synchronized with its real counterpart by data, and used to monitor, predict and decide [61,185].

8.1 What makes a model a twin

Grieves introduced the concept in the context of product lifecycle management in the early 2000s, describing a physical product, its virtual counterpart and the data connections between them [57,186]. NASA's 2010-2012 technology roadmaps and Glaessgen and Stargel's 2012 paper defined the digital twin of a flight vehicle as "an integrated multiphysics, multiscale, probabilistic simulation of an as-built vehicle or system that uses the best available physical models, sensor updates, fleet history, etc., to mirror the life of its corresponding flying twin" [58,187]. Kritzinger and colleagues distinguished three levels by the direction of automated data flow: a digital model has no automatic data exchange with the physical object; a digital shadow is updated automatically from the physical object; and a digital twin exchanges data automatically in both directions, so that its results can change the physical system [59]. The 2024 report of the US National Academies emphasizes that the bidirectional loop, verification and validation, and quantified uncertainty are what distinguish a twin from an ordinary simulation [61], and Kapteyn and colleagues formalized a twin as a probabilistic graphical model coupling the physical state, the digital state, observations and control decisions over time [188]. For manufacturing, ISO 23247 standardizes a reference architecture [189], and industrial surveys catalogue applications from design to maintenance [190].

8.2 Describing robots and scenes for simulation

A robot twin starts with a machine-readable description. The Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) of ROS describes a robot as a tree of links connected by joints, with visual and collision geometry and inertial parameters per link; an analysis of hundreds of public URDF files found frequent modelling errors and ambiguities, a reminder that a twin is only as good as its description [191,192]. SDFormat extends this to whole worlds with multiple models, sensors, lights and closed kinematic loops [193]; MuJoCo's MJCF adds actuators, tendons and solver parameters [27,194]; and Pixar's Universal Scene Description (OpenUSD), originally a film-production format for composing large scenes from layered files, has become the scene description used by NVIDIA's Omniverse and Isaac simulators [29,195,196]. Robot simulators such as Gazebo [28], MuJoCo [27], Drake [197], Isaac-based frameworks [29,196] and Habitat for photorealistic embodied navigation in scanned buildings [198,199] consume these descriptions and step the dynamics; the physics inside them is the subject of the physical modelling article (Physical modelling, simulation).

8.3 Building twins from reality

Every representation in this article can feed a twin. Scans become collision meshes and visual assets (section 4, section 7); scene graphs supply object identities, articulation and support relations (section 5); and system identification supplies physical parameters (Physical modelling, system identification) [200]. "Real-to-sim-to-real" pipelines make this concrete: RialTo reconstructs a target scene from a phone scan into an articulated simulation, trains a policy there with reinforcement learning and transfers it back to the real robot [201]. Where parameters cannot be measured precisely, domain randomization trains over distributions of them [202,203], and adaptive approaches close the loop by adjusting the simulation's parameter distribution to match real rollouts [204]. Neural reconstructions with attached physics (section 7.5) [168,182] and video world foundation models [60] are extending the twin idea to environments that are hard to describe by hand.

8.4 Keeping a twin synchronized

Synchronization is state and parameter estimation. Fast-changing states (poses, velocities) are tracked by filters or smoothers; slowly drifting parameters (friction, wear, payload) by recursive identification with forgetting, which discounts old data so the estimate can follow change [200,205]. Residuals between measurements and the twin's predictions then drive fault detection and diagnosis [206], and the synchronized model is used for prognosis and for what-if questions that would be expensive or dangerous to ask the real asset [61,188].

The example is a twin of a conveyor drive whose viscous friction starts to grow at t = 1,200 s as a bearing wears. A few rules of thumb follow from the examples and the literature. Store raw measurements (points, images) when you may need to reprocess them, but plan on derived representations. Use occupancy octrees when unknown space matters, as in exploration and safety-critical navigation [15]; use TSDF and ESDF maps when you need accurate surfaces and smooth distance gradients for manipulation and trajectory optimization [17,89]; use object and scene-graph layers for anything a person will ask about in words [19,109]; and use neural fields or splats when photometric fidelity, compactness or differentiability is worth the extra training cost and the weaker guarantees [12,26].

9.2 Layered systems

Modern spatial perception systems therefore stack representations and keep them consistent. Kimera and Hydra build, from the same sensor stream, a pose graph, a metric-semantic mesh, a places graph, objects and rooms, and propagate loop-closure corrections through every layer [4,19]. Driving stacks combine prior HD maps, online occupancy and object tracks [84,97]. Davison's FutureMapping vision argues that such systems should be designed around a graph of representations with local, message-passing updates so that they map efficiently onto future parallel hardware [5]. Kuipers' insight from the 1990s still holds: different layers serve different reasoning, and the layers must be linked [9].

9.3 Evaluation

Representations are evaluated against the use they serve. Geometric accuracy is measured with Chamfer distance, precision, recall and F-score of reconstructed surfaces against ground-truth scans, for example on Tanks and Temples [207]; trajectory accuracy with absolute and relative pose error on benchmarks such as TUM RGB-D and EuRoC [208,209]; volumetric and semantic quality with intersection over union on datasets such as ScanNet, Replica and Occ3D [97,199,210]; and rendering quality with image metrics for novel views [25,26]. Task-level evaluation, whether a robot using the representation succeeds more often, is the most meaningful and the least standardized.

9.4 Open problems

  • Change over long time spans. Most maps assume a static world with transient dynamic objects. Representations that model object permanence, periodic change and gradual drift remain an active area [4,104,117].
  • Uncertainty in learned representations. Occupancy grids and factor graphs carry probabilities; neural fields, feature fields and predicted occupancy usually do not, or do so poorly calibrated [100,211].
  • Physical properties from perception. Geometry and appearance can be captured from video; mass, friction and stiffness still need interaction or careful identification, though physics-coupled fields are beginning to close the gap [168,182].
  • Common interfaces. Simulation formats (URDF, SDFormat, MJCF, USD), mapping formats and learned representations do not interoperate well, and twins built by hand are error-prone [191,195].
  • From representation to world model. World foundation models learn implicit representations from video at scale, but whether their latent states support reliable metric reasoning and physical prediction is an open question (World models, section 9) [60,212].

Conclusion

A robot's representation of the physical world determines what it can notice, remember, ask and predict. Sixty years of work have produced a rich toolbox: landmark and point-cloud maps for registration and localization; occupancy grids, octrees and signed distance fields for free space and collision; scene graphs and object maps for entities, relations and tasks; spatial embeddings for recognition and open-vocabulary queries; neural fields and Gaussian splats for compact, differentiable, photorealistic geometry; and digital twins that add physics and stay synchronized with the real system. The examples in this article show the recurring trade-offs in miniature: indexing and hashing turn infeasible searches into cheap ones; averaging in a signed distance field beats any single measurement; hierarchy and visibility-aware updates keep object maps small and current; the encoding of coordinates and the choice of invariances decide what a learned representation can express; differentiable rendering trades sampling cost against sharpness; and a physics model is only as useful as its synchronization with reality. The trend is towards layered representations that combine explicit geometry, objects and physics with learned features, and towards world models that reason over them.
