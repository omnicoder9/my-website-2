1 What motion planning is, and where it sits in physical AI

Motion planning is the problem of computing a motion that takes a robot from a start state to a goal state while satisfying constraints: it must not collide with obstacles or with itself, it must respect joint limits and the robot's kinematics and dynamics, and it should be good by some measure such as length, time, energy or smoothness [1–3]. The classic statement is the "piano mover's problem": given a rigid object, a known set of obstacles and two placements of the object, find a continuous collision-free motion between them, or report that none exists [1,4]. Modern robots add moving obstacles, uncertainty about the world and about their own state, dynamics that limit how quickly they can change velocity, and other robots and people who are planning at the same time [5,6].

In a physical AI system, motion planning sits between deciding what to do and actually doing it. A task planner, a language-conditioned policy or a human operator chooses goals such as "pick up the cup" or "drive to dock 4" (the companion article on task planning covers this layer, and how it is integrated with motion planning in task-and-motion planning [7]). The motion planner turns each goal into a geometric path or a time-parameterized trajectory, and a feedback controller tracks that trajectory using state estimates from the perception and fusion stack (see sensor fusion and state estimation) [8,9]. In practice the layers are nested: a global planner computes a route through a map at a low rate, a local planner or model predictive controller refines it at tens of hertz against fresh sensor data, and a safety layer can override both [5,10,11]. Figure 1 shows this structure.

Three distinctions recur throughout the field and organize this article:

  • Path versus trajectory. A path is a geometric curve through the robot's configuration space; a trajectory is a path together with timing, so it specifies velocities and accelerations and can be checked against actuator limits [1,8]. Many systems plan a path first and then time-parameterize it (Section 4).
  • Completeness and optimality. A planner is complete if it finds a solution whenever one exists and reports failure otherwise; resolution complete if this holds at a given discretization; and probabilistically complete if the probability of finding an existing solution tends to one as computation grows [1,3]. Analogous notions of (asymptotic) optimality describe the quality of the path found [13].
  • Deliberative versus reactive. Deliberative planners reason about the whole route in advance; reactive methods such as potential fields and the dynamic window approach choose the next command from local information only. Reactive methods are fast and handle surprises, but can get stuck; deliberative methods are globally informed but need replanning when the world changes [14–16].

Motion planning is also where "reasoning" in physical AI becomes concrete. Deciding whether a narrow doorway is passable, whether to wait for a pedestrian or overtake, or in which order robots should cross an intersection are reasoning problems about geometry, time and other agents, and the algorithms in this article make that reasoning explicit and checkable [6,17]. Their reach goes beyond robots: the same algorithms plan animated characters, check that parts can be assembled, and have been used to study protein folding pathways [1,18].

2 A short history: from Shakey to GPU planners

Motion planning as a computational problem began with the first mobile robots. SRI's Shakey (1966–1972) planned routes among obstacles using a visibility-graph-like representation and, to search it efficiently, Hart, Nilsson and Raphael introduced the A* algorithm in 1968, which extends Dijkstra's 1959 shortest-path algorithm with an admissible heuristic estimate of the remaining cost [19–22]. Lozano-Pérez and Wesley showed in 1979 that shortest paths for a polygonal robot translating among polyhedral obstacles run along the edges of a visibility graph, and Lozano-Pérez's 1983 "configuration space approach" gave the field its central abstraction: represent the robot as a single point in the space of its configurations, and the obstacles as the regions of that space where the robot would collide [23–25].

The early 1980s brought complexity theory. Reif proved in 1979 that a generalized version of the mover's problem is PSPACE-hard; Schwartz and Sharir gave a complete algorithm for the general piano mover's problem via cylindrical algebraic decomposition, polynomial in the number of obstacles but doubly exponential in the number of degrees of freedom; Hopcroft, Schwartz and Sharir showed that coordinating many independent objects (the "warehouseman's problem") is PSPACE-hard; and Canny's roadmap algorithm reduced the complexity to singly exponential in the dimension [4,26–28]. Even finding shortest paths for a point among polyhedra in three dimensions is NP-hard [29]. These results explained why exact methods did not scale to arms with six or more joints, and motivated approximate, heuristic and randomized methods.

In parallel, Khatib's 1986 artificial potential fields made real-time obstacle avoidance practical for manipulators and mobile robots by treating the goal as an attractor and obstacles as repulsors, at the cost of local minima that were soon analysed in detail [14,30]. Rimon and Koditschek constructed navigation functions, potentials with a single minimum at the goal, for certain classes of environments [31]. Barraquand and Latombe's randomized path planner (1991) escaped local minima with random walks and solved problems with many degrees of freedom, foreshadowing the sampling revolution [2,32].

That revolution came in the mid-1990s. The probabilistic roadmap (PRM) of Kavraki, Švestka, Latombe and Overmars (1996) samples random collision-free configurations and connects nearby ones, building a graph that captures the connectivity of free space without ever constructing it explicitly [33]. LaValle's rapidly-exploring random tree (RRT, 1998) grows a tree from the start toward random samples, which biases it to explore unexplored space and extends naturally to systems with dynamics [34,35]; the bidirectional RRT-Connect (2000) became a default for manipulators [36]. In 2011 Karaman and Frazzoli proved that RRT almost surely does not converge to an optimal path and introduced RRT* and PRM*, which do [13].

The 2000s took planning onto real vehicles at scale. In the DARPA Grand Challenge (2005) and Urban Challenge (2007), autonomous cars combined lattice and search-based planners such as hybrid A* with local trajectory generation and continuous replanning among other vehicles [37–40]. Kiva Systems (later Amazon Robotics) coordinated hundreds of warehouse robots, making multi-agent path finding an industrial problem [41]. Trajectory optimization re-entered manipulation planning with CHOMP (2009), STOMP and TrajOpt [42–44], and open-source software, notably OMPL, MoveIt and the ROS navigation stack, put these algorithms in the hands of most robotics labs [10,45,46]. The current decade has added convex-optimization planners with global guarantees, GPU-parallel planners that solve manipulator problems in milliseconds, and learned components from sampling distributions to diffusion models that generate whole trajectories [47–51].

3 Configuration-space planning

3.1 The configuration space

A robot's configuration is a minimal set of parameters that specifies the position of every point of the robot; the set of all configurations is the configuration space, C-space or 𝒞 [1,8,24]. A mobile robot moving in the plane has configuration (x, y, θ), so 𝒞 = ℝ2 × S1; a free-flying rigid body has 𝒞 = ℝ3 × SO(3); a six-joint arm with revolute joints has 𝒞 = T6, a six-dimensional torus, because each joint angle wraps around [1,3]. The dimension of 𝒞 is the number of degrees of freedom. The forward kinematics that map a configuration to the positions of the robot's links, and the constraints that restrict feasible velocities, are covered in kinematics and robot motion constraints.

Each workspace obstacle O induces a C-obstacle 𝒞obs = {q ∈ 𝒞 : A(q) ∩ O ≠ ∅}, where A(q) is the region occupied by the robot at configuration q; the free space is 𝒞free = 𝒞 \ 𝒞obs [2,24]. Motion planning then becomes: find a continuous path τ : [0,1] → 𝒞free with τ(0) = qstart and τ(1) = qgoal. For a translating convex polygon among convex polygonal obstacles, C-obstacles are Minkowski sums O ⊕ (−A), which can be computed exactly in time linear in the number of vertices [24,62]. Rotation makes C-obstacles curved, and for articulated arms they are complex, high-dimensional sets that are never computed explicitly in practice. Instead, planners query a collision checker, which answers whether a given configuration (or short motion) is free, and treat 𝒞free as a black box that can only be probed (Section 5) [1,63].

3.2 Complete methods: roadmaps and cell decompositions

For low-dimensional problems with explicit geometry, there are exact planners that find a path or prove none exists [2,3]:

  • Visibility graphs connect mutually visible vertices of polygonal C-obstacles; shortest paths in the plane lie on this graph, though they graze obstacles [20,23].
  • Generalized Voronoi diagrams (retraction methods) keep the robot as far as possible from obstacles and give maximum-clearance roadmaps [64,65].
  • Cell decompositions partition 𝒞free into simple cells (for example trapezoids) and search the adjacency graph of cells; Schwartz and Sharir's and Canny's general algorithms are of this kind and apply to any semi-algebraic problem, but their cost grows exponentially with dimension [4,28,62].

Because the general problem is PSPACE-hard, no algorithm can be both complete and efficient in high dimensions unless widely believed complexity conjectures fail [26,28]. Practical planners therefore trade completeness for speed in one of three ways: discretize 𝒞 (search-based planning), sample it (Section 6) or optimize a trajectory locally (Section 7) [1,66].

3.3 Search on grids and lattices

Discretizing 𝒞 into a grid of cells turns planning into graph search. Dijkstra's algorithm expands states in order of cost-to-come g; A* expands in order of f = g + h, where the heuristic h estimates cost-to-go; if h never overestimates (it is admissible), A* returns an optimal path, and if it is also consistent, A* expands no more nodes than any other optimal algorithm using the same heuristic [1,21,22]. Weighted A* inflates the heuristic by w > 1 and returns paths at most w times the optimal cost, usually with far fewer expansions [67]; anytime variants such as ARA* start with a large w and decrease it while time remains [68]. For robots that discover the map as they move, D* and D* Lite repair the previous search instead of starting again, which made them standard on planetary and field robots [57,58]. The grid itself is usually an occupancy grid or a costmap built from sensor data, with obstacles inflated by the robot radius so that the robot can be treated as a point [16,69,70]; the article on representations of the physical world describes these maps.

Grid paths have another flaw visible in practice: they are restricted to grid directions, which any-angle variants such as Theta* and post-smoothing remove [71].

Grids ignore the robot's kinematics. For car-like robots, state lattices connect discrete states (x, y, θ, sometimes curvature or velocity) with precomputed, dynamically feasible motion primitives, so every path in the graph is drivable [72]. Hybrid A*, used by Stanford's Junior in the DARPA Urban Challenge, searches continuous states with discrete steering inputs and prunes with a coarse grid; it was followed by numerical smoothing of the resulting path [39,40]. Analytic curves give exact shortest paths for simple car models: Dubins showed that for a car that only drives forward with a minimum turning radius, the shortest path consists of at most three segments that are arcs of the minimum radius or straight lines, and Reeds and Shepp extended this to cars that can reverse [52,53]. Planners use these curves as heuristics and as the final "analytic expansion" to the goal [1,40].

4 Path planning and trajectory generation

4.1 From path to trajectory

A path planner returns a geometric curve q(s), s ∈ [0,1]; a trajectory is a function of time q(t) whose derivatives must satisfy the robot's limits on velocity, acceleration, jerk and torque [8,9]. A common pipeline is decoupled: plan a path, smooth it, then choose a time scaling s(t) so that q(t) = q(s(t)). Shortcutting, which repeatedly replaces a section of the path by a straight segment when that segment is collision-free, removes most of the detours of sampling-based paths; Hauser and Ng-Thow-Hing combined it with bounded-acceleration timing to produce fast, smooth manipulator motions [63,73]. Kant and Zucker's path–velocity decomposition applies the same separation to moving obstacles: plan a path among static obstacles, then plan the velocity along it to avoid moving ones [74]. Decoupling is fast but can fail where geometry and timing interact, for example when a fast car must take a wider line through a curve; kinodynamic planners search directly over states including velocity and over control inputs [35,75].

4.2 Polynomials, splines and minimum-derivative trajectories

Between two configurations at rest, the simplest smooth time scaling is a polynomial. A cubic s(τ) = 3τ2 − 2τ3 with τ = t/T matches positions and zero velocities at both ends; a quintic s(τ) = 10τ3 − 15τ4 + 6τ5 also sets the boundary accelerations to zero [8,9]. Flash and Hogan showed that the quintic is the trajectory minimizing integrated squared jerk, and that it predicts the smooth, bell-shaped velocity profiles of human point-to-point arm movements [76]. For quadrotors, whose control inputs relate to the fourth derivative of position, Mellinger and Kumar minimized integrated squared snap through a sequence of waypoints, solving a quadratic program over piecewise polynomial coefficients; Richter and colleagues reformulated it so that the polynomial segments can be solved for in closed form, enabling fast aggressive flight through cluttered indoor spaces [77,78]. Mueller and colleagues derived closed-form minimum-jerk motion primitives for quadrocopters that are cheap enough to generate and check for feasibility in very large numbers, so a planner can choose among many candidates in real time [79]. For road vehicles, Werling and colleagues generated quintic and quartic polynomials in a Frenet frame along the lane centreline, separately in lateral and longitudinal directions, and chose the lowest-cost candidate that is collision-free [80].

4.3 Time-optimal and limit-respecting time scaling

Industrial controllers often use trapezoidal velocity profiles: accelerate at the limit, cruise at the speed limit, decelerate at the limit; if the move is too short to reach full speed, the profile is triangular [8]. Jerk-limited (S-curve) profiles smooth the corners; Ruckig computes jerk-limited, time-optimal profiles online from arbitrary initial and target states within a control cycle [81]. For a given path through a multi-joint robot, the fastest timing subject to torque limits is the time-optimal path parameterization (TOPP) problem: because the dynamics projected onto the path become constraints on the path acceleration s̈ as a function of s and ṡ, Bobrow, Dubowsky and Gibson and Shin and McKay showed in 1985 that the optimum is bang-bang in s̈ and can be found by integrating forward and backward in the (s, ṡ) phase plane [54,55]. Verscheure and colleagues showed that the problem becomes convex in the variables b = ṡ2 and a = s̈, and TOPP-RA solves it robustly with a sequence of small linear programs based on reachability analysis [82,83].

5 Collision and obstacle avoidance

5.1 Collision detection and distance queries

Planners spend most of their time asking whether configurations and motions are collision-free; in sampling-based planners collision checking typically dominates the run time [1,63,84]. A collision checker places the robot's link geometry at a configuration using forward kinematics and tests it against the environment. Efficient checkers use a two-stage pipeline [84,85]:

  • Broad phase. Bounding volumes (spheres, axis-aligned boxes, oriented boxes) arranged in hierarchies quickly discard pairs of objects that are far apart. The OBBTree of Gottschalk, Lin and Manocha showed that tight oriented boxes give fast interference tests for large polygonal models [86].
  • Narrow phase. Exact tests on the remaining pairs. For convex shapes the Gilbert–Johnson–Keerthi (GJK) algorithm computes the distance between two convex sets by iteratively searching their Minkowski difference for the point nearest the origin, and Lin and Canny's algorithm tracks closest features incrementally as objects move [56,87]. Non-convex meshes are decomposed into convex pieces or handled with triangle-level tests.

The FCL library implements these queries (collision, distance, continuous collision for a swept motion) for the shapes used in robotics and is the checker behind MoveIt [46,85]. Two further representations are common. Signed distance fields store, for every voxel, the distance to the nearest obstacle surface (negative inside); a robot approximated by spheres is then checked with one lookup per sphere, and the field's gradient points away from obstacles, which optimization-based planners exploit (Section 7) [88,89]. Occupancy maps such as octrees store free, occupied and unknown space probabilistically and are queried by planners on mobile robots and drones [69,90]. Motions between configurations must also be checked: planners either sample the segment at a resolution fine enough that no obstacle can fit between samples, or use continuous collision checks of the swept volume [1,85].

5.2 Potential fields and navigation functions

Khatib's artificial potential field approach defines a function U(q) that is low at the goal and high near obstacles, and moves the robot along −∇U [14]. A common form is an attractive term ½ katt ‖q − qgoal‖2 plus a repulsive term ½ krep (1/d − 1/d0)2 active when the clearance d is below a range d0 [3,14]. Potential fields are cheap and react instantly to new obstacles, which is why they remain the basis of many reactive controllers. Koren and Borenstein catalogued their inherent problems: local minima where forces cancel, no passage between closely spaced obstacles, and oscillations in narrow corridors [30]. Navigation functions avoid local minima by construction in sphere and star-shaped worlds [31], and a grid wavefront (distance-to-goal computed by breadth-first search or the fast marching method) is a numerical navigation function for any discretized map [2,32,91].

5.3 Reactive local planners

Mobile robots have refined reactive avoidance into a family of local planners. The vector field histogram builds a polar histogram of obstacle density around the robot and steers through the most suitable gap [92]. The dynamic window approach (DWA) of Fox, Burgard and Thrun searches directly in the space of translational and rotational velocities, restricted to those reachable within one control period under acceleration limits and those that allow the robot to stop before hitting an obstacle, and picks the velocity that best trades progress toward the goal, clearance and speed [15]. Elastic bands deform a global path as if it were an elastic strip pushed away by obstacles, and timed elastic bands add timing and kinematic constraints and optimize the deformed trajectory [93,94]. These planners, along with model predictive controllers (Section 7.4), are the local planners of the ROS 2 navigation stack [10,11].

5.4 Safety: guaranteeing avoidance

Avoidance is ultimately a safety property, and several tools state it formally. An inevitable collision state is a state from which every possible future trajectory collides; a robot must avoid entering such states, not just colliding states, which matters for fast vehicles that cannot stop instantly [95]. Hamilton–Jacobi reachability computes the set of states from which an adversarial or uncertain environment can force a collision, giving a provably safe set and a least-restrictive safety controller, at a cost exponential in the state dimension [96,97]. Control barrier functions (CBFs) encode the safe set as {x : h(x) ≥ 0} and add the condition ḣ ≥ −α(h) as a linear constraint in a quadratic program that minimally modifies a nominal command; this safety filter pattern lets any planner or learned policy run on top of a guaranteed collision-avoidance layer [12,98] (see also safe reinforcement learning). Responsibility-sensitive safety formalizes safe following distances and right-of-way for road vehicles [99]. When contact with people is allowed, as for collaborative robots, standards such as ISO/TS 15066 bound speeds, forces and pressures instead of forbidding contact, and robots detect and react to collisions after they occur [100,101].

6 Sampling-based planning: PRM, RRT and their descendants

6.1 Probabilistic roadmaps

Sampling-based planners avoid constructing 𝒞free explicitly. They draw random configurations, keep those the collision checker accepts, and connect them with a simple local planner (usually a straight line in 𝒞 checked at fine resolution) [1,63,102]. The probabilistic roadmap (PRM) does this in two phases: a learning (construction) phase samples N free configurations and tries to connect each to its k nearest neighbours or to all neighbours within a radius, producing a graph; a query phase connects the start and goal to the graph and searches it with A* or Dijkstra [33]. Because the roadmap is reused, PRM suits multi-query settings such as a manipulator working repeatedly in a fixed cell. Kavraki, Kolountzakis and Latombe showed that the probability of failing to find an existing path decays exponentially with the number of samples, at a rate that depends on how much clearance the path has [103]. Narrow passages, where the free space is thin, are the method's Achilles heel: uniform samples rarely land in them. Sampling strategies that concentrate near obstacle boundaries (Gaussian sampling), the analysis of "expansive" spaces, and lazy evaluation that defers edge collision checks until an edge is on a candidate shortest path all address this [104–106].

6.2 Rapidly-exploring random trees

The RRT grows a tree rooted at the start. At each iteration it samples a random configuration qrand, finds the nearest tree node qnear, and extends from qnear a fixed step toward qrand, adding the new node if the extension is collision-free [34,35]. Because the probability of a node being selected for extension is proportional to the volume of its Voronoi region, RRTs are pulled toward unexplored space, the "Voronoi bias" [1,35]. A small goal bias (sampling the goal itself a few percent of the time) speeds convergence. RRT-Connect grows trees from start and goal and repeatedly tries to connect them greedily, which is very effective for single-query manipulator problems [36]. The extension step needs only a way to propagate the system forward under a control, so RRTs handle differential constraints and dynamics (kinodynamic planning) naturally; forward-propagating RRT variants were later shown to be probabilistically complete under mild conditions [35,107]. For tasks with constraints such as keeping a cup upright or a door handle grasped, sampling must stay on a lower-dimensional constraint manifold, for which projection, continuation and atlas-based techniques exist [108].

With 400 uniformly sampled nodes, PRM fails on three of 20 seeds: too few samples land in the narrow gap to connect the two sides. Its collision-check count is high because it builds the whole roadmap, but that roadmap would answer further queries almost for free. RRT solves every instance with far fewer checks, and RRT-Connect, growing from both ends and connecting greedily, needs only about a third as many again. Raw sampling-based paths are jagged (RRT's path is 30% longer than the straight-line distance in this instance), and a few shortcutting passes bring all three planners to nearly the same length, which is why shortcutting is applied routinely after planning [45,73].

6.3 Asymptotic optimality: RRT*, PRM* and informed sampling

RRT finds a path, but Karaman and Frazzoli proved that the probability of it converging to an optimal path is zero; once a solution is found, later samples rarely improve it [13]. Their RRT* adds two steps. When a new node is added, it is connected to the neighbour within a radius r(n) ∝ (log n / n)1/d that gives the lowest cost-to-come, not to the nearest node; then neighbours are rewired through the new node if that lowers their cost. With the radius shrinking at this rate, the expected number of neighbours grows logarithmically, the cost per iteration stays within a constant factor of RRT's, and the solution converges almost surely to the optimum; PRM* applies the same connection radius to roadmaps [13].

Several descendants accelerate convergence. Informed RRT* samples, once a solution of cost c exists, only from the ellipsoid of points whose straight-line detour through them costs less than c, the only region that can improve the solution [109]. FMT* performs a lazy dynamic-programming "fast march" over a batch of samples [110]. BIT* processes batches of samples as an implicit random geometric graph and searches it in order of heuristic cost, unifying graph search and sampling [111]. For systems with dynamics, where connecting two states exactly requires solving a boundary-value problem, SST achieves asymptotic near-optimality using only forward propagation and a sparse set of witness nodes [112].

6.4 Practice of sampling-based planning

Sampling-based planners are the default for manipulators because they scale to seven or more degrees of freedom and need only a collision checker [63,66]. The Open Motion Planning Library implements dozens of variants behind a common interface and is the planning back end of MoveIt [45,46], with a benchmarking infrastructure for comparing them [113]. Their weaknesses are equally well known: they offer no proof of infeasibility, so a failure after a time limit is ambiguous; their raw paths need smoothing and time scaling; and their run times are random, with heavy tails in narrow passages [66,102]. Recent work attacks speed directly: vectorizing collision checks with CPU SIMD instructions brought RRT-Connect planning for a 7-joint arm to the microsecond-to-millisecond range [49], and GPU implementations run many planners or optimizers in parallel [48].

7 Optimization-based planning

7.1 Trajectory optimization

Optimization-based planners treat the trajectory itself as the decision variable and solve

minimizeξ  J(ξ) = ∫ ℓ(q(t), q̇(t), u(t)) dt   subject to  dynamics,  q(0) = qstart,  q(T) ∈ 𝒢,  limits,  d(q(t)) ≥ dsafe,

where ℓ penalizes effort, jerk or time and d is the signed distance to obstacles [1,114,115]. The theory goes back to the calculus of variations, Pontryagin's maximum principle and dynamic programming [116,117]. Numerical methods divide into indirect methods, which solve the optimality conditions, and direct methods, which discretize the trajectory and hand the resulting finite-dimensional problem to a nonlinear programming solver [114,118]; when the subproblems are convex, as in the sequential convex methods below, convex optimization theory gives efficient and reliable solvers [119]. Direct shooting optimizes only the controls and simulates the states; direct collocation optimizes states and controls at knot points and imposes the dynamics as equality constraints between them, which is better conditioned for long horizons and allows infeasible initial guesses [115,120]. Differential dynamic programming and its Gauss–Newton variant iLQR exploit the problem's temporal structure with backward Riccati-like passes and are widely used for online control of humanoids and legged robots [121–123]. General-purpose tools include interior-point solvers such as Ipopt, quadratic programming solvers such as OSQP, and modelling frameworks such as CasADi and Drake [124–127]. Trajectory optimization through contact, where the robot deliberately touches its environment, is covered in contact mechanics [128].

7.2 CHOMP, STOMP and TrajOpt

Three methods brought trajectory optimization into everyday manipulation planning. CHOMP (covariant Hamiltonian optimization for motion planning) represents a trajectory as a sequence of waypoints and minimizes a smoothness cost plus an obstacle cost computed from a signed distance field, integrated along the arc length of every body point [42,89]. Its key idea is the covariant gradient step ξ ← ξ − η−1 A−1 ∇F, where A is the matrix of the smoothness metric: premultiplying by A−1 spreads each local obstacle push smoothly along the whole trajectory, so a straight-line initialization that passes through obstacles can be pushed out of collision in a few hundred cheap iterations [89]. STOMP replaces gradients with stochastic sampling of noisy trajectories and a probability-weighted update, so it can optimize non-differentiable costs such as torque limits or task constraints [43]. TrajOpt formulates collision avoidance as a constraint and solves a sequence of convex subproblems: at each iteration it linearizes the signed distance between convex shapes around the current trajectory, including swept volumes between waypoints, and solves a quadratic program within a trust region with ℓ1 penalties for constraint violation [44]. GPMP2 casts planning as probabilistic inference with a Gaussian process prior over continuous-time trajectories, solved with factor graphs [129].

Optimization is local: it finds the best trajectory in the homotopy class of the initial guess, not the best overall, and it can get stuck in infeasible local minima when obstacles are thin or the guess is poor [44,89]. In practice systems restart from several seeds, initialize from a sampling-based path, or run many optimizations in parallel; cuRobo, for instance, runs many seeds of a particle-based and a gradient-based optimizer on a GPU and uses a graph planner as fallback [44,48].

7.3 Convex formulations with global guarantees

Non-convexity comes from the obstacles: free space is not convex. One remedy decomposes free space into convex regions and solves a mixed-integer or convex problem over them. IRIS computes large obstacle-free convex polytopes by alternating between separating hyperplanes and ellipsoid growth via semidefinite programming [130]. Marcucci and colleagues' graphs of convex sets (GCS) then formulate planning through a graph of such regions as a mixed-integer program whose convex relaxation is tight enough in practice that rounding recovers near-optimal smooth trajectories, combining the global view of graph search with the continuous optimization of trajectory optimization [47]. For vehicles, Zhang, Liniger and Borrelli reformulated the distance between polytopic vehicles and obstacles through duality as smooth constraints, enabling exact collision avoidance in tight parking manoeuvres [131].

7.4 Receding horizons and model predictive control

When the world changes or the model is imperfect, the optimization is repeated: model predictive control (MPC) solves a finite-horizon trajectory optimization from the current state, applies the first control, and re-solves at the next step [132,133]. MPC is the workhorse of local planning for cars, drones and legged robots; for example, contouring MPC drove 1:43-scale race cars at the limit of handling with solve times of milliseconds [134], and EGO-Planner re-optimizes quadrotor trajectories online against obstacles without building a full distance field [135]. Sampling-based MPC such as model predictive path integral control (MPPI) evaluates thousands of noisy control sequences in parallel and averages them by cost, handling non-smooth costs and dynamics [136]. Planning with learned dynamics models inside MPC is covered in world models. Time-optimal planning for quadrotors, which must decide how to allocate time across waypoints with the full nonlinear dynamics, illustrates how far optimization can push performance: Foehn and colleagues' time-optimal trajectories outpaced expert human pilots in experiments [137].

7.5 Feedback motion planning

A trajectory is a single motion; a real robot is perturbed off it. Feedback motion planning plans with controllers rather than trajectories: Burridge, Rizzi and Koditschek composed controllers whose basins of attraction funnel into one another [138]; LQR-trees grow a tree of stabilized trajectories whose regions of attraction, verified with sums-of-squares programming, cover the state space [139]; and funnel libraries precompute verified tubes around motion primitives and sequence them online among obstacles [140,141]. Riemannian motion policies take a related reactive route for manipulators, combining many local acceleration policies (avoid this obstacle, reach this target, respect this joint limit) into one coherent policy through a geometric weighting [142].

8 Motion planning in dynamic environments

8.1 Adding time

A moving obstacle occupies different regions at different times, so collision-freedom depends on when the robot is where. Planning in state-time or configuration-time space, with time as an extra, irreversible dimension, makes moving obstacles static again [1,143]; the path–velocity decomposition handles them in two steps (Section 4.1) [74]. Safe interval path planning compresses time into intervals during which a grid cell is collision-free, so that A* can search space-time with few more states than space alone [144]. Planning among moving obstacles is harder than static planning even in simple cases: Canny and Reif showed that planning a velocity-bounded point robot among moving polygonal obstacles is NP-hard even in the plane, and replanning with D* Lite-style incremental search is one way practical systems cope [1,29,58]. When obstacles are not just moving but uncertain, the robot must avoid inevitable collision states with respect to their possible futures, not only their predicted ones [95,97].

8.2 Velocity obstacles

Fiorini and Shiller's velocity obstacle (VO) is the set of robot velocities that, if held constant, would lead to collision with a moving obstacle within some time horizon; choosing a velocity outside the union of VOs avoids all obstacles to first order [59]. When obstacles are themselves robots or people who also avoid, VO is too pessimistic and causes oscillations, because each agent assumes the other will not react. Reciprocal velocity obstacles (RVO) assume each agent takes half of the avoidance responsibility [145], and optimal reciprocal collision avoidance (ORCA) turns this into half-plane constraints on velocity so that each agent solves a small linear program, scaling to thousands of agents with guarantees of collision-free motion under its assumptions [60]. Extensions handle non-holonomic robots and robot teams [146]. Section 9 shows reciprocity at work in a multi-robot example.

8.3 Prediction and interaction

Velocity obstacles extrapolate constant velocity, but people and vehicles turn, stop and react. Planners therefore increasingly consume probabilistic predictions of other agents' trajectories. Pedestrian models range from Helbing and Molnár's social force model, in which pedestrians are driven by attractive and repulsive "social forces" [147], to learned predictors such as Social LSTM and Trajectron++, which output multimodal distributions over future trajectories conditioned on neighbours and maps [148,149] (see also predicting future states in the world models article). Using predictions naively can cause the freezing robot problem: when the predicted futures of a crowd cover all of the space ahead, every plan looks unsafe and the robot stops, although people would make way; Trautman and Krause showed that modelling the joint, cooperative behaviour of robot and crowd resolves it [150]. More generally, the robot's actions influence others: Sadigh and colleagues planned for an autonomous car that models human drivers as optimizing their own reward in response to it, producing behaviours such as nudging forward to signal intent at an intersection [151]. Human-aware and social navigation adds comfort, legibility and social conventions to the cost [152,153], and reinforcement learning has been used to learn decentralized collision-avoidance policies for crowds [154] (see deep reinforcement learning).

The following example shows why prediction matters even for a reactive planner. A unicycle robot uses the dynamic window approach to cross a corridor in which six pedestrians walk across its path. "Static" DWA checks its candidate rollouts against pedestrians frozen at their current positions; "predictive" DWA checks the robot's position at each future time against each pedestrian's constant-velocity prediction at the same time.

Constant-velocity prediction is the crudest possible model; the gains from better, interaction-aware prediction come on top of this [149,150].

8.4 Uncertainty, speed and safety

Uncertainty about the robot's own state and the obstacles' positions turns hard constraints into probabilistic ones. Chance-constrained planners bound the probability of collision, for example by allocating a risk budget across obstacles and time steps [155]; belief-space planners such as the belief roadmap and LQG-MP choose paths along which the estimator and controller will keep uncertainty small [156,157] (see uncertainty in state estimation). At high speed, perception latency becomes the limiting factor: Falanga and colleagues used event cameras to let a quadrotor dodge objects thrown at it with a total latency of a few milliseconds [158], and Loquercio and colleagues trained an end-to-end policy in simulation that flew a quadrotor at high speed through forests and other unknown environments, mapping sensor data directly to trajectories [159]. In autonomous driving, the motion planner sits inside a layered stack of route planning, behaviour decisions, motion planning and control, and must handle both dynamic agents and traffic rules [5,6,160].

9 Coordinated planning for multiple robots

9.1 Coupled, decoupled and prioritized planning

Planning for k robots at once is planning in the product of their configuration spaces, whose dimension is the sum of theirs. Centralized (coupled) planners search this joint space and can be complete and optimal, but their cost grows exponentially with k; Hopcroft, Schwartz and Sharir's PSPACE-hardness result for the warehouseman's problem shows that this is intrinsic [1,27]. Decoupled planners plan each robot separately and then resolve interactions. Erdmann and Lozano-Pérez introduced prioritized planning: robots are planned one at a time in priority order, each treating the already-planned robots as moving obstacles in configuration-time space [161]. In path coordination, paths are fixed and only the timing along them is planned in a coordination diagram whose axes are the robots' path parameters [162,163]. Decoupled methods are fast but incomplete: a high-priority robot can block the only route of a lower-priority one. Švestka and Overmars built composite roadmaps from single-robot PRMs, and discrete RRT (dRRT) explores the implicit product of single-robot roadmaps without constructing it [164,165].

9.2 Multi-agent path finding

Warehouses, airports and games abstract the problem to multi-agent path finding (MAPF) on a graph: agents occupy vertices, move or wait in discrete time steps, and must not share a vertex at the same time or swap along an edge; solutions are measured by the sum of agents' path costs or by the makespan [17]. Optimal MAPF is NP-hard [166]. Silver's cooperative A* is a prioritized planner that reserves cells in a space-time table [167]. Conflict-based search (CBS) is the best-known optimal method: a high-level search over a constraint tree starts from individually optimal paths, finds the first conflict between two agents, and creates two children, each forbidding one of the two agents from being at the conflict location at that time; the low level replans only that agent with space-time A* [61]. M* instead plans in the joint space only where robots interact, expanding the search's dimension locally [168]. Bounded-suboptimal ECBS and fast rule-based solvers such as PIBT and LaCAM scale to hundreds or thousands of agents [169–171], and lifelong MAPF continuously assigns new goals, as in warehouse fulfilment, where Kiva-style systems coordinate hundreds of drive units carrying shelves [41,172,173].

Exploiting such symmetries, and trading optimality for speed, is the focus of much MAPF research [17,169].

9.3 Continuous coordination: reciprocal avoidance, assignment and swarms

Robots in continuous spaces coordinate with the tools of Section 8 applied to each other. With reciprocal velocity obstacles and ORCA, each robot needs only its neighbours' positions and velocities, so the computation is decentralized and scales to large teams [60,145]. Buffered Voronoi cells give each robot a region it can move in that is guaranteed not to overlap its neighbours' [174], and control barrier functions give minimally invasive safety certificates for robot teams [175]. Distributed model predictive control lets each drone optimize its own trajectory while sharing predicted trajectories with others [176]. When robots are interchangeable, assignment and planning can be solved together: CAPT computes collision-free trajectories for interchangeable robots by choosing the assignment of robots to goals that minimizes the sum of squared distances [177]. Quadrotor swarms combine discrete MAPF on a roadmap with continuous trajectory optimization, as in the Crazyswarm platform and its trajectory planner [178,179], and fully decentralized swarms of micro aerial vehicles have flown through dense forests [180]. Flocking rules, from Reynolds' boids to Olfati-Saber's analysis, and self-assembly in a thousand-robot Kilobot swarm show the extreme of decentralized coordination by local rules [181–183]. Multi-arm manipulation, where several arms share one workcell, is a coupled problem of the same kind and has been tackled with learned decentralized planners [184]. Surveys cover the wider landscape [185].

The difference is solely in the assumed behaviour of the others, which is why modelling other agents is the heart of multi-robot and dynamic-environment planning [60,145].

10 Learning and motion planning

Classical planners are general but slow in high dimensions and blind to experience; learned policies are fast but carry no guarantees. Much current work combines them [66,186]:

  • Learned sampling and heuristics. Ichter, Harrison and Pavone trained a conditional variational autoencoder on solutions to bias the samples of sampling-based planners toward regions where solutions lie, such as narrow passages, while keeping a fraction of uniform samples to preserve probabilistic completeness [50].
  • Neural planners. Motion planning networks (MPNet) encode the environment and generate paths node by node, falling back to a classical planner to repair infeasible segments [186]; motion policy networks learn a reactive policy from millions of expert trajectories generated in simulation and run at control rates in unknown environments from point clouds [187].
  • Generative trajectory models. Diffusion models generate whole trajectories by iterative denoising and can be guided by collision and goal costs at sampling time, as in Diffuser and motion planning diffusion [51,188]; diffusion policies generate action sequences directly from observations [189]. These are covered in diffusion-based action generation.
  • Reinforcement learning. Locomotion and agile flight increasingly use policies trained with reinforcement learning in simulation, which perform perception-aware avoidance and planning implicitly: perceptive quadrupeds traverse rough terrain, and a learned drone-racing policy beat human world champions [190–192] (see sim-to-real transfer).
  • Language and task-level reasoning. Large models increasingly supply the goals and costs a motion planner optimizes; VoxPoser, for example, composes 3-D value maps from language and passes them to a motion planner [193]. Integrating symbolic task decisions with geometric feasibility checks is the subject of task-and-motion planning [7,194], covered in the task planning article; language-conditioned planning is covered in foundation models for physical AI.

A common design keeps a classical layer as the final arbiter: learned components propose, and a collision checker, optimizer or safety filter verifies, so that the system inherits the speed of learning and the guarantees of planning [12,50,186].

11 Systems, evaluation and open problems

11.1 Software

Most robots run motion planning through a small set of open-source frameworks. For manipulators, MoveIt connects kinematics, collision checking (FCL), planners (OMPL, CHOMP, STOMP and others) and time parameterization in ROS [45,46,85,195]; cuRobo and VAMP provide GPU and CPU-vectorized planning with millisecond run times [48,49]; Drake provides trajectory optimization, GCS and verification tools [47,127]. For mobile robots, the ROS 2 navigation stack Nav2 combines layered costmaps, global planners such as A* and hybrid A*, and local controllers such as DWA, timed elastic bands and MPPI [10,11,70]. Autonomous-driving stacks such as Autoware provide open implementations of route, behaviour and motion planning for vehicles [196]. 11.2 Evaluation

Because sampling-based planners are randomized and optimization depends on initialization, planners must be compared over many runs and problems, reporting success rate within a time limit, distributions of planning time and solution cost, and not just means [66,113]. OMPL's benchmarking infrastructure and MotionBenchMaker's procedurally generated manipulation datasets support such comparisons [113,197]; MAPF has standard grid benchmarks and definitions [17]. For deployed systems, the relevant metrics also include safety margins, comfort (acceleration and jerk), and in social settings the impact on people's paths and comfort [5,153].

11.3 Open problems

  • Guarantees in the open world. Planners guarantee collision-freedom relative to a map and a model; perception errors, prediction errors and latency break those assumptions. Combining reachability, safety filters and calibrated uncertainty remains active research [12,97,155].
  • Interaction. Crowds, traffic and multi-robot fleets are games, not obstacles; planning that is safe, efficient and legible among adaptive agents is unsolved in general [150,151,153].
  • Contact and manipulation. Most motion planners treat contact as forbidden, but manipulation requires it; planning through contact is combinatorial and numerically hard [7,128] (see contact mechanics).
  • Infeasibility and completeness. Practical planners rarely prove that no solution exists, which matters for task planners that must decide when to try something else [7,66].
  • Learning with guarantees. Learned samplers, policies and generative trajectory models are fast and perceptive, but integrating them with verification so that the combined system remains safe is an open question [12,50,51].
  • Scale. Lifelong coordination of thousands of robots in warehouses and the planning of high-degree-of-freedom humanoids push both multi-agent search and high-dimensional planning to their limits [48,171,173].

Conclusion

Motion planning turns goals into motions that are collision-free, feasible and good. The configuration space reduces every robot to a point and every obstacle to a forbidden region; complexity results show why exact planning in that space is hopeless in high dimensions, and the field's main families are the responses: search on grids and lattices, sampling with PRM and RRT and their optimal descendants, and trajectory optimization from CHOMP to convex graphs of sets. Time parameterization turns paths into trajectories that respect actuator limits, collision checkers and distance fields make the geometry queryable, and reactive methods and safety filters keep the robot safe when the plan meets reality. Moving obstacles add time and, above all, the need to predict and model others, and many robots turn planning into coordination, where reciprocity and conflict resolution replace simple avoidance. The examples in this article show the recurring trade-offs in miniature: heuristics and weights trade optimality for speed; C-space can be disconnected in ways the workspace hides; reactive fields have local minima that global information removes; narrow passages starve uniform sampling; rewiring buys optimality; optimization finds the best path only in the homotopy class it starts in; prediction prevents collisions that reactive planning walks into; and assumptions about other agents decide whether a team collides. The strongest systems layer these methods, global and local, classical and learned, with a verifiable safety layer at the bottom.
