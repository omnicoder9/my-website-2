1 What a foundation model is, and what changes for physical AI

In 2021 a group at Stanford introduced the term foundation model for "any model that is trained on broad data (generally using self-supervision at scale) that can be adapted (e.g., fine-tuned) to a wide range of downstream tasks", naming BERT, GPT-3 and CLIP as examples [1–4]. Two properties define the class. The first is scale: model size, data and compute are increased together, and test loss falls predictably as a power law in each [5,6]. The second is homogenization: one pretrained network, usually a transformer [7], becomes the common starting point for many tasks that previously had their own bespoke models [1]. Sutton's "bitter lesson", that general methods which exploit growing computation eventually beat methods built on hand-coded domain knowledge, is the usual argument for why this happens [8].

Physical AI, the robots, vehicles and other embodied systems that sense and act in the world, has been slower to benefit, for three reasons that recur throughout this article. First, data: there is no internet of robot actions. Text and images exist in the billions [9], whereas the largest pooled robot datasets hold on the order of a million trajectories collected at great expense [10,11]. Second, grounding: a language model can say that a sponge is used for wiping spills, but nothing in its training ties the word "sponge" to pixels, to grasp poses or to the joint torques that move a particular arm [12,13]. Third, embodiment: robots differ in kinematics, sensors, control rates and action spaces, so data from one robot is not directly usable by another [10,14]. Moravec observed decades ago that perception and mobility, effortless for people, are much harder for machines than abstract reasoning [15]; foundation models have made the abstract part cheaper without removing the physical part.

Three strategies have emerged for bringing foundation models into robots, and they organize this article (Figure 1):

  • Use pretrained models as components. Frozen or fine-tuned vision and vision-language encoders provide features, open-vocabulary detections and spatial reasoning to an otherwise conventional robot stack (Sections 3 and 4) [16–18].
  • Use language models as planners. A large language model (LLM) or vision-language model (VLM) decomposes an instruction into steps or writes code, and separate low-level skills execute them (Section 5) [13,19,20].
  • Train the policy itself as a foundation model. A vision-language-action model (VLA) maps images and an instruction directly to motor commands, starting from a pretrained VLM and fine-tuning on robot data, often pooled across many robots (Sections 6–8) [10,21–23].

This article belongs to the Learning and AI Models part of the series. It builds on the articles on computer vision, where open-vocabulary detection and segmentation were introduced, and on world models, which covers video world foundation models such as Genie and Cosmos [24,25] that predict rather than act. The companion articles on imitation learning and reinforcement learning cover how demonstration data are collected and how policies are trained from demonstrations and rewards; here those methods appear only where foundation models change them. Each section introduces concepts and history, then the main algorithms and systems, then a short, runnable Python program whose real output is shown.

2 A short history: from SHRDLU to vision-language-action models

2.1 Symbolic language and planning (1960s–1990s)

The ambition to instruct robots in natural language is as old as AI robotics. Winograd's SHRDLU (1968–1970) conversed about and manipulated a simulated blocks world, parsing commands such as "pick up a big red block" into procedures that acted on a symbolic world model [45]. SRI's Shakey, built in the same period, combined vision, a symbolic world model and the STRIPS planner, whose representation of actions by preconditions and effects survives today in the Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL) [46–48]. These systems worked only in worlds whose objects and relations had been specified in advance. Harnad named the underlying difficulty the symbol grounding problem: symbols manipulated by a program have no meaning to the program unless they are connected to sensing and action [12]. Brooks argued that robots should therefore be built from the bottom up, from tight sensor-to-action loops, without central symbolic representations [49].

2.2 Statistical grounding and deep visuomotor learning (2000s–2010s)

Probabilistic approaches learned the mapping from words to objects, places and actions from annotated examples; Tellex and colleagues, for example, grounded navigation and manipulation commands with a learned graphical model over language and the robot's world [50]. Deep learning then supplied the perception side: convolutional networks trained on ImageNet transformed computer vision [51–54], and Levine and colleagues showed that a single network could be trained end to end from camera pixels to motor torques [55]. Large self-supervised data collection followed: 50,000 grasp attempts over 700 robot hours [56], roughly 800,000 grasp attempts across a fleet of arms [57], and QT-Opt's reinforcement learning on 580,000 real grasps [58]. RoboNet pooled 15 million video frames from seven robot platforms to study transfer between robots [59]. These systems learned one skill, or one family of skills, at a time.

2.3 Language, vision and the transformer (2017–2021)

The transformer [7] made it practical to train very large sequence models, and BERT and GPT-3 established pretraining followed by fine-tuning or in-context prompting as the dominant recipe for language [2,3]. The Vision Transformer applied the same architecture to image patches [60], and CLIP and ALIGN learned a joint image–text embedding from hundreds of millions to billions of web image–caption pairs, enabling zero-shot recognition of categories never labeled during training [4,61]. Robotics began to borrow these components: CLIPort used CLIP features inside a manipulation policy [62], and language-conditioned imitation learning trained policies that followed free-form instructions [63,64].

2.4 Foundation models enter robotics (2022–2023)

2022 saw three distinct uses of foundation models in robots. LLMs were used as zero-shot planners that decompose high-level tasks into steps [65]; SayCan grounded such plans by weighting each candidate skill with a learned estimate of whether the robot could currently perform it [13]; and Code as Policies had the LLM write executable robot programs [19]. At the same time, robotics transformers were trained on large multi-task datasets: Gato, a single 1.2-billion-parameter transformer, played games, captioned images and controlled a real robot arm [66], and RT-1 learned more than 700 tasks from 130,000 demonstrations collected with 13 robots over 17 months [67]. In 2023 PaLM-E injected images and robot state into a 562-billion-parameter language model [36], and RT-2 fine-tuned large VLMs to output robot actions as text tokens, coining the term vision-language-action model [21]. The Open X-Embodiment collaboration pooled data from 22 robot embodiments and showed that a single policy trained on all of it outperformed policies trained on each robot's data alone [10].

2.5 Open and generalist VLAs (2024–2026)

From 2024 the emphasis shifted to open models, continuous action generation and cross-embodiment scale. Octo and OpenVLA released open-source generalist policies trained on Open X-Embodiment data [22,39]. Physical Intelligence's π0 paired a pretrained VLM with a flow-matching "action expert" to produce high-frequency continuous actions for dexterous tasks such as laundry folding [23], and π0.5 co-trained on heterogeneous data to clean up homes it had never seen [68]. NVIDIA's GR00T N1 targeted humanoids with a dual-system design [38], and Google DeepMind's Gemini Robotics built a VLA and an embodied-reasoning model on Gemini 2.0 [37]. Smaller open models such as SmolVLA made VLAs trainable on consumer hardware using community-collected datasets [69,70]. Table 2 summarizes the milestones.

3 Multimodal encoders and shared representations

Every foundation model for robotics begins with an encoder that turns raw sensor data into vectors. What distinguishes the foundation-model era is that these encoders are pretrained once, on data far larger and more diverse than any robot dataset, and then reused. Two families dominate: contrastive encoders that place images and text in a shared embedding space, and self-supervised visual encoders trained on images or video alone.

3.1 Contrastive image–text pretraining

Contrastive learning trains an encoder so that matched pairs of inputs map to nearby vectors and mismatched pairs to distant ones. Van den Oord and colleagues framed it as the InfoNCE objective, a classification of the true partner among a set of negatives [71], and SimCLR showed that it produces strong image features when the pairs are two augmentations of the same image [72]. CLIP applied the idea across modalities. Given a batch of N image–caption pairs, an image encoder f and a text encoder g produce unit-norm embeddings ui and vj, and the symmetric loss is

L = −(1/2N) ∑i [ log softmaxj(ui·vj / τ)i + log softmaxj(vi·uj / τ)i ]

with a learned temperature τ [4]. Trained on 400 million web image–text pairs, CLIP could classify images into categories it had never been trained on by embedding prompts such as "a photo of a dog" and choosing the nearest; its zero-shot ImageNet accuracy matched a fully supervised ResNet-50 [4]. ALIGN reached similar results with over a billion noisier pairs [61], and LAION-5B made a dataset of 5.85 billion image–text pairs openly available [9]. SigLIP replaced the batch-wide softmax with an independent sigmoid loss on every image–text pair, which removes the need for a global normalization across the batch and works well at both small and large batch sizes [26]; SigLIP encoders are now the vision backbone of several VLAs [22,23,35].

3.2 Self-supervised and robot-oriented visual features

Image–text training captures object identity and attributes well, but robots also need geometry, part structure and fine spatial detail, which captions rarely describe. Self-supervised encoders learn these from images alone. Masked autoencoders (MAE) hide a large fraction of image patches, 75% in the original work, and train a vision transformer to reconstruct them [73]; DINOv2 uses self-distillation on a curated set of 142 million images and produces features that work for depth estimation and segmentation without fine-tuning [27].

A line of work asked whether encoders pretrained on human video would serve robots better than ImageNet or CLIP features. R3M trained a ResNet on the Ego4D egocentric video corpus [74] with a time-contrastive objective, video–language alignment and a sparsity penalty, and improved imitation-learning success in simulation by more than 20% over training from scratch and by more than 10% over CLIP and MoCo features [16]. MVP pretrained vision transformers with MAE on millions of images from egocentric video and the web and used them, frozen, for real-robot control [75]; VIP learned a representation whose distances act as a goal-reaching value function [76]; and Voltron conditioned masked reconstruction on language narrations of the video [77]. The most systematic comparison, CortexBench, evaluated many such encoders across 17 embodied tasks and found that no single pretrained representation was best everywhere, although adapting the encoder to the task narrowed the gaps [78]. The practical lesson for VLAs has been to combine encoders with complementary strengths: OpenVLA, for example, concatenates DINOv2 and SigLIP features [22,79].

3.3 Binding more modalities

Robots sense more than images. ImageBind showed that a single embedding space could bind six modalities (images, text, audio, depth, thermal and inertial data) using only image-paired data for each, so that, for example, audio and text become aligned through their common link to images [28]. The Perceiver architecture handles arbitrary input modalities by cross-attending from a fixed set of latent vectors to any number of input tokens [80], an idea later used to resample image features into a fixed number of tokens for VLMs [31]. In generalist policies, proprioception, force and joint state are usually tokenized by small modality-specific networks and fed to the shared transformer alongside image and language tokens [39,41]. The sensors article describes these modalities; the design principle in a foundation model is to map each into the shared token space through a lightweight, trainable adapter while keeping the large backbone common.

3.4 What shared representations buy a robot

A shared vision–language space gives a robot open-vocabulary perception: detectors such as OWL-ViT and Grounding DINO localize objects named by arbitrary text, and promptable segmenters such as SAM outline them, so that the set of objects a robot can talk about is no longer fixed at training time [17,81,82]. These are described in the computer vision article. For policies, pretrained features improve sample efficiency and robustness to visual changes such as new backgrounds and lighting [16,75,78], and a common embedding for images and instructions lets the policy treat a goal image and a sentence describing the same goal interchangeably [39,77].

4 Vision-language models

A vision-language model takes one or more images together with text and produces text. Early systems were task specific: an image captioner pairing a convolutional encoder with a recurrent decoder [83], or a classifier over answers for visual question answering (VQA) [84]. Modern VLMs are general: they reuse a pretrained LLM as the decoder and answer arbitrary questions, follow instructions, read text in images and reason over several images at once.

4.1 Architecture: encoder, connector, language model

Most open VLMs have three parts (Figure 2): a pretrained vision encoder, usually a ViT trained with CLIP or SigLIP [4,26,60]; a connector that converts image features into a sequence of vectors in the LLM's input embedding space; and a pretrained decoder-only LLM that attends jointly to the visual tokens and the text tokens and is trained with the ordinary next-token objective

L = −∑t log pθ(wt | w<t, image tokens)

on the text that follows the image [32,33]. Designs differ mainly in the connector and in which parts are trained:

  • Flamingo (80 billion parameters) froze both the vision encoder and the LLM, resampled image features into a fixed number of tokens with a Perceiver-style resampler, and inserted new gated cross-attention layers into the frozen LLM. Trained on interleaved web documents of images and text, it performed new tasks from a handful of examples given in the prompt [31].
  • BLIP-2 kept both the image encoder and the LLM frozen and trained only a lightweight Querying Transformer (Q-Former) whose learned queries extract the visual information most useful to the LLM [32].
  • LLaVA connected a CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder to the Vicuna LLM with a single linear projection, trained first to align features on image–caption pairs and then end to end on 158,000 instruction-following examples that text-only GPT-4 had generated from image captions and bounding boxes [33].
  • PaLI and PaLI-X scaled vision and language components jointly to 17 and 55 billion parameters [34,85]; PaliGemma combined a SigLIP encoder with the 2-billion-parameter Gemma LLM into a 3-billion-parameter model intended for fine-tuning, and it became the backbone of the π0 VLA [23,35].
  • Prismatic VLMs systematically varied the encoder, connector, LLM and training recipe and found that fused DINOv2 and SigLIP features improved spatial and localization tasks, the design OpenVLA inherited [22,79].

Proprietary frontier models such as GPT-4 and Gemini accept images natively and perform strongly on multimodal benchmarks, but their architectures are only partly documented [86,87].

4.3 What VLMs contribute to robots, and where they fall short

For a robot, a VLM is a general-purpose scene interpreter. It can answer questions about the workspace, decide whether a subtask succeeded, describe what changed after an action, and propose the next step, functions that previously required separate, task-specific classifiers [20,36]. PaLM-E showed that training a single model on a mixture of internet vision-language tasks and embodied tasks (planning, VQA on robot scenes) produced positive transfer: the embodied tasks benefited from the internet data, and the largest 562-billion-parameter model retained most of its language ability [36].

The weakness is metric and physical reasoning. VLMs trained on captions are often unreliable at judging distances, sizes and relative positions, information that captions seldom state. SpatialVLM addressed this by generating 2 billion spatial question–answer pairs from 10 million real images using depth estimation and object detection, and fine-tuning on them improved quantitative spatial answers enough to serve as dense rewards for robot learning [18]. Gemini Robotics-ER extends a frontier VLM with embodied reasoning: pointing to objects, predicting grasps and 3D bounding boxes, and estimating trajectories, which it can pass to a low-level controller [37]. Even so, a VLM outputs text or coordinates, not motor commands. Turning its knowledge into action is the subject of the next two sections: either let it plan with existing skills, or train it to act.

5 Language-conditioned planning

The quickest way to give a robot the common-sense knowledge in a language model is to let the model plan and leave execution to skills the robot already has. The instruction "I spilled my drink, can you help?" requires knowing that spills are cleaned with sponges, that a sponge must be found and picked up before it can be used, and that the table is where the spill is; LLMs encode a great deal of such procedural knowledge [13,65]. The difficulty is that the LLM knows nothing about this robot, this room or this moment.

5.1 LLMs as zero-shot planners

Huang and colleagues showed that large LLMs prompted with a household task produce plausible step-by-step plans without any additional training, but that the raw plans are often not executable: steps are phrased in ways that match no available action, or refer to objects that are absent. They mapped each generated step to the most similar admissible action using a sentence-embedding model and fed the result back into the prompt, trading some correctness of meaning for executability [65]. Chain-of-thought prompting, which asks the model to produce intermediate reasoning before its answer, improves multi-step reasoning in large models and is now routinely used in planners [89].

5.2 Grounding plans in affordances: SayCan

SayCan grounds the LLM by combining two probabilities for each skill π in a fixed library, given instruction i and current state s [13]:

π* = argmaxπ   p(ℓπ | i, history)  ×  p(success | s, ℓπ)

The first factor ("say") is the LLM's probability of the skill's text description ℓπ as the next step of the plan. The second ("can") is an affordance value function, trained with reinforcement learning for each skill, that estimates whether the skill would succeed from the current camera image. The chosen skill is executed, its description is appended to the prompt and the process repeats until the LLM selects "done". With the 540-billion-parameter PaLM, SayCan planned correctly for 84% of 101 instructions in a real kitchen and executed 74% of them successfully [13,29]. 5.3 Closing the loop with feedback

Open-loop plans fail when the world does not behave as expected. Inner Monologue fed textual feedback back into the LLM's prompt at each step: whether the last action succeeded (from a learned success detector), a description of objects in the scene, and answers from a human. The LLM could then retry failed steps, re-plan when objects moved and ask clarifying questions, raising success on long-horizon tabletop and kitchen tasks over open-loop planning [20]. LM-Nav applied the same compositional idea to navigation, using GPT-3 to extract landmarks from directions, CLIP to locate them in images and a learned navigation model to drive between them, without fine-tuning any of the three [4,90].

5.4 Plans as code

Natural-language steps are ambiguous about quantities, loops and conditions. Code as Policies prompted a code-writing LLM with the signatures of perception and control functions and a few examples, and had it write Python programs that call those functions, including arithmetic on positions, loops over detected objects and newly defined helper functions [19]. ProgPrompt similarly expressed plans as Python-like programs whose assertions checked preconditions in the current state [91]. VoxPoser went a step further and had the LLM write code that queries a VLM for object locations and composes 3D value maps of where the gripper should and should not go, which a motion planner then follows; this produced zero-shot trajectories for everyday manipulation tasks without any skill library [92].

5.5 How well do language models plan?

Benchmarks of classical planning problems suggest caution. On PlanBench, a suite built from International Planning Competition domains such as Blocksworld, even GPT-4 produced valid plans for only about a third of Blocksworld problems (206 of 600), and performance collapsed when object and action names were obfuscated, which suggests retrieval of familiar patterns rather than search [93]. Kambhampati and colleagues argue that LLMs should be used as idea generators and translators inside "LLM-Modulo" frameworks in which external verifiers check every candidate plan [94]. LLM+P follows this route: the LLM translates the natural-language problem into PDDL, a classical planner solves it optimally, and the LLM translates the plan back [48,95]. For physical tasks, the verifier is often the world itself, through success detection and re-planning as in Inner Monologue [20].

5.6 From pipelines to hierarchical VLAs

The planners above call separate low-level skills. Recent systems fold planning and acting into one model or a tightly coupled pair, an arrangement often described by analogy with Kahneman's fast, intuitive "System 1" and slow, deliberate "System 2" [96]. Embodied chain-of-thought reasoning trains a VLA to write out a plan, the current subtask, the gripper position and relevant object boxes before emitting each action; this raised OpenVLA's absolute success rate on difficult generalization tasks by 28% [22,97]. Hi Robot uses a high-level VLM to interpret open-ended prompts and corrections and to issue simple language commands to a low-level VLA [98], and π0.5 predicts a semantic subtask in text before generating the actions for it, within a single model [68]. GR00T N1 and Gemini Robotics separate a slower reasoning VLM from a faster action module explicitly [37,38]. In each case language has become an internal interface inside the policy rather than an external API.

6 Vision-language-action models

A vision-language-action model is a policy πθ(at:t+H | ot, ℓ) that maps the current observation ot (camera images and usually proprioception) and a language instruction ℓ to the next action or chunk of H actions, and whose parameters are initialized from a pretrained VLM [21,22,44]. The hope is that the VLM's knowledge of objects, attributes and language transfers to control: that a robot trained to pick up cans will also pick up "the extinct animal" when shown a toy dinosaur, because the VLM knows what a dinosaur is [21].

6.1 Precursor: RT-1 and action tokens

RT-1, the Robotics Transformer, established the template without a VLM. It encoded six camera frames with an ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet whose layers were modulated by the instruction's embedding through FiLM, compressed the resulting feature maps with TokenLearner, and passed the tokens to a transformer that output actions [67,99,100]. Each of 11 action dimensions (arm pose, gripper, base motion and a mode switch) was discretized into 256 bins and predicted as a categorical token. Trained on 130,000 teleoperated episodes covering more than 700 instructions, RT-1 succeeded on 97% of training instructions and generalized better to new instructions, distractors and backgrounds than the baselines it was compared with [67]. 6.2 RT-2: actions as language

RT-2 took the decisive step of fine-tuning large pretrained VLMs, PaLI-X and PaLM-E, to output actions [21,36,85]. It reused RT-1's 256-bin discretization and wrote each action as a string of integers, for example "1 128 91 241 5 101 127", so that actions became ordinary text tokens and no new output layer was needed. Crucially, it co-fine-tuned on a mixture of robot trajectories and the original web-scale vision-language data, which preserved the VLM's knowledge. In about 6,000 evaluation trials, RT-2 matched RT-1 on seen tasks and roughly doubled its success on unseen objects, backgrounds and environments, and it showed semantic generalization absent from the robot data, such as placing an object on a picture of a particular celebrity or picking up an improvised hammer [21]. Because RT-2's largest model has 55 billion parameters, it ran in the cloud at a few hertz [21].

6.3 OpenVLA and open models

OpenVLA made the recipe reproducible. It starts from a 7-billion-parameter Prismatic VLM (a Llama 2 language model with fused DINOv2 and SigLIP visual features) and fine-tunes it on 970,000 trajectories from Open X-Embodiment, again predicting 256-bin action tokens [10,22,30,79]. Across 29 tasks on several robots it achieved 16.5% higher absolute success than the 55-billion-parameter RT-2-X while using seven times fewer parameters, and it could be adapted to new robots by low-rank adaptation on a single consumer GPU [22,101]. A later study of fine-tuning recipes found that replacing autoregressive token-by-token decoding with parallel decoding of continuous action chunks, trained with a simple L1 regression loss, raised average success on the LIBERO simulation benchmark from 76.5% to 97.1% and generated actions about 26 times faster [102,103].

6.4 Representing actions: tokens

The choice of action representation turned out to matter as much as the backbone. Per-dimension binning is simple and lets a VLA reuse its language vocabulary, but it scales poorly for high-frequency, dexterous control: a 50 Hz, 7-dimensional arm produces 350 tokens per second, and consecutive tokens are so strongly correlated that next-token training provides little learning signal [104]. FAST (Frequency-space Action Sequence Tokenization) instead applies the discrete cosine transform (DCT) to each action dimension over a chunk, scales and rounds the coefficients so that most high-frequency ones become zero, and compresses the result with byte-pair encoding (BPE), the same subword method used for text [104–106]. Autoregressive VLAs trained with FAST tokens handled dexterous high-frequency tasks on which binning failed, and matched a diffusion-based π0 while training up to five times faster [104]. 6.5 Representing actions: continuous generative heads

The alternative is to keep actions continuous and attach a generative model as the output head. Regression with a mean-squared-error loss is inadequate when demonstrations are multimodal, when the same observation is followed by different, equally valid actions, because the minimizer of squared error is their average, which may be invalid. Diffusion Policy addressed this by generating action sequences with a denoising diffusion model conditioned on the observation, improving average success over the prior state of the art by 46.9% across 12 tasks from four benchmarks [107,108]; the imitation learning article covers it in detail.

π0 brought this idea into a VLA. A PaliGemma backbone processes images and the instruction; a separate, smaller "action expert" transformer of about 300 million parameters, which attends to the backbone's internal representations, generates a chunk of 50 future actions with flow matching [23,35,109]. Flow matching learns a velocity field vθ that transports Gaussian noise ε to data along straight paths [109,110]:

Aτ = τA + (1 − τ)ε,     L(θ) = Eτ, ε, A ‖ vθ(Aτ, o, τ) − (A − ε) ‖2

At inference, a chunk is sampled by integrating dA/dτ = vθ from noise at τ = 0 to τ = 1 in about ten Euler steps, fast enough to control robots at up to 50 Hz. π0 was pretrained on a mixture of its own dexterous data from seven robot configurations and 68 tasks plus the Open X-Embodiment data, and could then be fine-tuned for long tasks such as folding laundry from a hamper, assembling boxes and bussing tables [23]. The regression head steers into the obstacle every time; the flow-matching head samples one side or the other in proportion to the demonstrations and tracks how the two modes shift with the observation.

Attaching a freshly initialized action head can damage the pretrained backbone: early in training, large gradients from the untrained head overwrite the VLM's knowledge. "Knowledge insulation" trains the backbone on discretized FAST tokens while stopping gradients from the continuous action expert, which kept language following and generalization while preserving fast continuous inference [104,111].

6.6 Speed: chunks, parallel decoding and two systems

Large models are slow, while physical control needs fast, smooth commands. Three techniques reconcile them. Action chunking predicts a sequence of future actions per inference and executes several of them before re-planning, which also reduces compounding error and jitter; it was popularized by the Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) policy for bimanual manipulation [112]. Parallel decoding outputs a whole chunk in one forward pass instead of one token at a time [102]. Asynchronous, dual-rate execution runs a slower reasoning model and a faster action model concurrently: GR00T N1 couples a VLM running at about 10 Hz with a diffusion-transformer action module producing 120 Hz motor commands, and SmolVLA overlaps inference of the next chunk with execution of the current one [38,69].

7 Robotics foundation models and general-purpose action policies

A general-purpose action policy is a single learned controller meant to perform many tasks, in many scenes, ideally on many robots, from instructions or goals given at run time. VLAs are one way to build one; others have no language backbone at all. What makes such a policy a foundation model is the same two-stage pattern as in language: broad pretraining on heterogeneous data, followed by adaptation to a particular robot, task or deployment [1,39,42].

7.1 Generalist agents

Gato was the first prominent demonstration that one set of weights could act across very different domains. A 1.2-billion-parameter transformer was trained on 604 tasks, from Atari games and image captioning to stacking blocks with a real robot arm, by serializing every modality into a flat sequence of tokens; which task to perform was inferred from a prompt of example tokens [66]. VIMA showed that a transformer conditioned on multimodal prompts, sentences interleaved with images of objects or goal scenes, could specify and solve a wide range of tabletop tasks in one model [114]. RoboCat, built on the Gato architecture, added a self-improvement loop: after fine-tuning on 100 to 1,000 demonstrations of a new task or robot, the agent practised the task, and the data it generated were added to the training set of the next, more capable generalist [88].

7.2 Data for robot foundation models

Because no web-scale corpus of robot actions exists, building one has become a central research activity. Table 5 lists the main open datasets. The Open X-Embodiment dataset assembled over a million real-robot trajectories from 22 embodiments, contributed by 21 institutions, in a common format [10]. DROID collected 76,000 trajectories, about 350 hours of interaction, with one standardized robot platform in 564 scenes and 86 tasks across many buildings and institutions, specifically to increase scene diversity [11]; BridgeData V2 provided 60,096 trajectories with a low-cost arm across 24 environments [115]. How such data are collected (teleoperation, kinesthetic teaching, handheld devices) is the subject of the imitation learning article; two developments are specific to foundation models. First, foundation models now help collect data: AutoRT used a VLM to describe scenes and an LLM to propose tasks, filtered by a "robot constitution" of safety rules, to direct a fleet of robots collecting diverse episodes with less human supervision [116]. Second, models are increasingly pretrained on data without robot actions. Latent Action Pretraining (LAPA) learns a discrete latent action between consecutive video frames, pretrains a VLA to predict these latent actions from human and robot videos, and then maps them to real actions with a small amount of labeled robot data [117]; GR00T N1 organizes its training data as a "pyramid" of plentiful web data and human video at the base, synthetic and simulated data in the middle and scarce real-robot data at the top [38]. Handheld collection devices such as the Universal Manipulation Interface record demonstrations with a wrist-mounted camera on a gripper that a person carries, so that data can be gathered in the wild without a robot present [118].

7.3 Architectures for generalist policies

Octo illustrates the design choices for a policy without a large language backbone. Images from any number of cameras, a language instruction or goal image, and optional proprioception are tokenized by modality-specific encoders and processed by a transformer; learned "readout" tokens summarize the sequence and feed a diffusion head that outputs action chunks. New observation types and action spaces are added by attaching new tokenizers and heads and fine-tuning, and the models were trained on 800,000 Open X-Embodiment trajectories in two sizes, 27 and 93 million parameters [39]. At the other end of the scale, RDT-1B is a 1.2-billion-parameter diffusion transformer pretrained on 46 datasets with over a million episodes and fine-tuned for bimanual manipulation [113]. VLAs (Section 6) add a language-model backbone, trading inference speed for semantic knowledge and instruction following.

Evidence that broad pretraining helps came first from Open X-Embodiment. RT-1-X, trained on the pooled data, improved success by about 50% on average over models trained on each laboratory's own data for robots with small datasets, and RT-2-X roughly tripled RT-2's success on "emergent skill" evaluations that required knowledge contributed by other robots' data [10]. Diversity appears to matter as much as volume: policies co-trained with DROID's scene-diverse data were more robust to new scenes than those trained on less diverse datasets [11], and π0.5's ability to work in unseen homes improved as the number of distinct training environments grew [68].

7.4 The training recipe: pretrain, co-train, post-train

Current robot foundation models are trained in stages that mirror LLM practice [23,38,68]:

  1. VLM pretraining on web-scale image–text data, usually inherited from an existing model [35,79].
  2. Robot pretraining on a broad mixture of robot trajectories from many tasks and embodiments, often co-trained with continuing vision-language data so that general knowledge is not forgotten [21,68].
  3. Post-training on smaller, high-quality data for a target robot or task family, the analogue of instruction tuning. π0 reports that fine-tuning on curated data for complex tasks such as laundry folding benefits from the broad pretraining even though the tasks are far from the pretraining distribution [23].
  4. Deployment-time adaptation, for example parameter-efficient fine-tuning on tens to hundreds of demonstrations (Section 8.4) [22,102], or reinforcement learning from real-world practice, which is covered in the reinforcement learning article.

8 Transferring skills across robots and tasks

A foundation model earns its name only if what it learns on some robots and tasks transfers to others. Transfer across tasks on the same robot has been the more successful so far, because pretrained vision-language knowledge maps directly onto new objects and instructions [21,22]. Transfer across embodiments is harder, because robots differ in what they sense, how their actions are parameterized and how they move [10,41].

8.1 The embodiment gap

Three kinds of difference separate robots. Observation differences include camera number, placement and intrinsics, and which proprioceptive and force signals exist. Action-space differences include joint versus end-effector commands, absolute versus delta targets, control frequency, gripper type and the number of arms or legs. Dynamics differences include link lengths, masses, actuator limits and controller gains. A policy that outputs joint velocities for a six-joint arm cannot be run, unchanged, on a seven-joint arm or a quadruped [14,40].

8.2 Shared action spaces

The simplest bridge is to choose an action representation that is meaningful on every robot. Open X-Embodiment converted most of its datasets to a seven-dimensional action of end-effector position and orientation deltas plus gripper opening, leaving each robot's own controller to convert them into joint commands [10]. RDT-1B defined a larger "physically interpretable" unified action space with slots for joint positions, end-effector poses and base velocities, filling only the slots each robot has [113]. In the language of modular networks, each robot then needs only its own stem (which turns its sensors into the shared representation) and head (which turns shared actions into its motor commands), while the policy in between is shared [14,41]. The shared end-effector policy reaches most goals on the new arm without a single sample from it, whereas a joint-space policy copied from a robot with the same number of joints but different link lengths fails, as does a joint-space policy trained from scratch on 300 samples from the new arm. The gap to the analytic expert comes from the learned policy's approximation error near the goal. The catch, in real systems, is that a shared Cartesian action space hides real differences in reachability, speed and contact behaviour, which is why cross-embodiment pretraining is still followed by per-robot fine-tuning [10,39].

8.3 Modular and morphology-aware architectures

Devin and colleagues first decomposed policies into task-specific and robot-specific neural network modules that could be recombined, so that a task learned on one robot could be executed by another robot whose module had been trained on different tasks [14]. MetaMorph treated a robot's morphology as a sequence of limb tokens processed by a transformer, and learned a single locomotion controller for many simulated body shapes that transferred zero-shot to unseen morphologies [120]. In navigation, the General Navigation Model trained one policy on 60 hours of data from six different robots, with a normalized action space of waypoints, and showed that it could drive robots not seen during training [121].

Heterogeneous Pre-trained Transformers (HPT) scale this pattern: per-embodiment stems tokenize proprioception and vision into a fixed number of tokens, a shared transformer trunk with up to more than a billion parameters processes them, and per-task heads decode actions; pretraining the trunk on 52 datasets spanning real robots, simulation and human video improved fine-tuned performance on unseen tasks by more than 20% [41]. CrossFormer trained a single transformer policy on 900,000 trajectories from 20 embodiments, spanning single- and dual-arm manipulation, wheeled navigation, quadruped locomotion and quadcopter flight, using separate readout tokens and action heads per action type, and matched specialist policies tailored to each embodiment [40].

8.4 Parameter-efficient adaptation

When a new robot or task does need data, the size of a VLA makes full fine-tuning expensive. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) freezes each pretrained weight matrix W0 and learns a low-rank update, W = W0 + BA, with B ∈ ℝd×r, A ∈ ℝr×k and rank r much smaller than d and k [101]. OpenVLA reported that LoRA matched full fine-tuning on new robot setups while training only a small fraction of the parameters [22]. 8.5 Transfer across tasks, and its limits

Task transfer in foundation-model policies comes in degrees. Semantic generalization, to new objects, attributes and phrasings, transfers well from VLM pretraining: RT-2 and OpenVLA handle objects and instructions absent from their robot data [21,22]. Compositional generalization, recombining known skills and objects in new ways, works when the architecture shares structure across them (Section 6.1) [64,67]. Behavioural generalization, performing a motion skill never demonstrated on any robot, remains rare; when it appears it usually reflects data from other embodiments or from human video that contained the skill [10,117]. Transfer can also be negative: adding data from a mismatched embodiment or a low-quality source can lower performance on the target, so data mixtures are weighted and curated [10,39]. Finally, transfer from simulation remains attractive because simulated data are cheap; domain randomization of visual and physical parameters, introduced to close the visual reality gap for object localization [122], is now combined with real-data co-training in generalist models [38]. The physical modeling article discusses simulators and the reality gap.

9 Evaluation, safety and open problems

9.1 Benchmarks and real-world evaluation

Evaluating a generalist policy is hard because its whole point is to work in situations that no fixed test set anticipates. Simulation benchmarks give reproducible numbers: CALVIN measures how many of five chained language instructions a policy completes in a row [123], and LIBERO measures knowledge transfer across 130 manipulation tasks grouped into suites that vary objects, layouts and goals [103]. Because simulated scenes look and behave differently from real ones, SIMPLER built simulated counterparts of real evaluation setups and showed that their success rates rank real-world policies consistently with physical trials [124]. Ultimately, though, claims about generalist robots rest on physical trials, which are slow and expensive, so most papers report tens of trials per task [21–23].

That makes statistics important. A success rate estimated from n trials is a binomial proportion, and its uncertainty is best summarized with an interval that behaves well for small n and for rates near 0 or 1, such as the Wilson score interval [125]. Differences of a few points between VLAs evaluated on small real-world suites should therefore be read with caution, and reports should include trial counts and intervals.

9.2 Failure modes

The characteristic failures of robot foundation models follow from how they are built. Because a VLA imitates its data, it inherits the data's mistakes and its gaps: it can be confidently wrong in states unlike any it was trained on, and small errors compound over a long task [44,67]. Pretrained knowledge can mislead as well as help, as when a language prior overrides what the camera shows [13,20]. VLMs remain weak at precise metric and physical reasoning (Section 4.3) [18]. Large models are slow, which limits closed-loop reactivity unless the architecture separates fast control from slow reasoning (Section 6.6) [38,102]. And most VLAs have little memory beyond a short history of frames, which limits tasks that require remembering what was done or seen earlier [42,68].

9.3 Safety

Robots act on the physical world, so the consequences of a model error are physical. Two kinds of risk are specific to foundation models. The first is semantic: a model may follow an instruction that is harmful in context, or fail to recognise that an action is unsafe. Robey and colleagues showed that jailbreaking techniques developed for chatbots, adapted to LLM-controlled robots, induced harmful actions in all of their attempts on three different robotic systems, from a simulated self-driving model to a commercial quadruped [126]. The ASIMOV benchmark and "robot constitutions" built from real-world injury scenarios and from models' own reasoning aim to measure and improve semantic safety [116,127]. The second risk is physical: even a correct high-level decision can be executed with excessive force or speed, or in collision. For this reason, foundation-model components are usually placed above, not instead of, conventional safety layers: rate and force limits in the controller, collision checking, protective stops and the functional-safety requirements of standards such as ISO 10218 for industrial robots [128]. Regulation is also arriving: the European Union's AI Act classifies AI systems that are safety components of machinery among the high-risk systems subject to conformity requirements [129].

9.4 Open problems

  • Data. Robot datasets remain several orders of magnitude smaller than text and image corpora. Whether scaling human video, simulation and autonomous data collection can close the gap, and in what mixture, is the central open question [10,38,116,117].
  • Action representation and speed. Tokens, diffusion and flow heads each trade expressiveness, training efficiency and inference speed differently, and the best choice may depend on the robot and task [23,102,104,111].
  • Physical understanding. VLAs learn physics only implicitly from demonstrations. Coupling them with predictive world models, which can evaluate an action's consequences before it is taken, is an active direction; see the world models article [24,25,130].
  • Learning from deployment. Imitation alone caps a policy at the quality of its data. Reinforcement learning from real-world practice, with rewards from VLMs or success detectors, can improve beyond it but raises questions of safety and sample cost [18,76,88].
  • Evaluation and reproducibility. Real-world results are hard to compare across laboratories; shared hardware, open models and datasets, and simulation proxies are partial answers [11,39,70,124].

9.5 Summary

Foundation models have changed physical AI in three steps. Pretrained multimodal encoders gave robots open-vocabulary perception and a shared space in which images, language and other sensors meet. Language models and VLMs made common-sense task knowledge available to robots, first as planners grounded by affordances, feedback and code, and then as the backbone of vision-language-action models that produce motor commands directly. Pooling robot data across laboratories and embodiments, and designing shared action spaces and modular stems and heads, made generalist policies that transfer across tasks and, increasingly, across robots. The examples in this article show the core mechanisms in miniature: contrastive alignment that generalizes to unseen combinations; a trained connector that lets a frozen language model describe images; affordance-weighted planning; guarded execution of generated code; compact action tokenization; generative action heads that preserve multimodal behaviour; shared action spaces that transfer a skill to a new arm; low-rank adaptation; and the statistics needed to tell whether any of it worked. The systems that succeed in practice combine these foundation models with the sensing, state estimation, modelling and safety engineering described elsewhere in this series.
