1 What a world model is, and why physical AI needs one

In 1943 the psychologist Kenneth Craik proposed that if an organism "carries a 'small-scale model' of external reality and of its own possible actions within its head, it is able to try out various alternatives, conclude which is the best of them, react to future situations before they arise" [1]. Eight decades later this is close to a working definition of a world model in robotics and machine learning: a model, usually learned from data, that predicts how the state of the environment will evolve, in particular in response to the agent's own actions, so that the agent can evaluate behaviour before committing to it [2–4].

Formally, a world model approximates the transition and observation structure of a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) [5]. With hidden state st, action at, observation ot and reward rt, it supplies some or all of

st+1 ~ p(st+1 | st, at)   (dynamics)     ot ~ p(ot | st)   (observation)     rt ~ p(rt | st, at)   (reward)

together with an inference procedure (a filter or encoder) that turns the history of observations into a belief about the current state [5,6]. Model-based reinforcement learning uses such a model either to plan online or to generate simulated experience for learning a policy, which is the key distinction from model-free methods that learn values or policies directly from interaction [4,7].

Physical AI systems, the robots, vehicles and other embodied agents that perceive and act in the real world, need world models for three practical reasons. First, real interaction is slow, expensive and sometimes dangerous, so a model that lets the agent rehearse in imagination can reduce the amount of real experience needed by orders of magnitude [8–10]. Second, a model separates knowledge of how the world works from the task, so the same model can serve many goals by changing only the cost function, as in model-predictive control [11,12]. Third, explicit predictions with uncertainty can be inspected, checked against safety constraints and used to ask "what if?" before acting [13,14].

This article sits in the World Models and Physical Understanding part of the series alongside the article on physical modeling, which covers analytic mechanics, contact, simulators and system identification. Here the focus is on learned and predictive world models and on how agents plan with them. The article follows the six capabilities in Figure 1: predicting future states, modeling objects and their interactions, estimating the consequences of actions, representing uncertainty, evaluating hypothetical scenarios and planning. Each section introduces the concepts and history, the main algorithms and systems, and a short runnable Python program whose real output is shown.

2 A short history: from mental models to world foundation models

2.1 Internal models in psychology and neuroscience

Craik's "small-scale model" anticipated a large literature on mental simulation [1,13]. In motor neuroscience, forward models that predict the sensory consequences of motor commands, and inverse models that compute the commands needed to reach a desired outcome, became a central explanation for how the brain compensates for delays and noise; Wolpert, Ghahramani and Jordan showed that human estimates of hand position during movement were consistent with a Kalman-filter-like combination of a forward model and sensory feedback [18,19]. Developmental psychology found that infants a few months old already expect objects to persist and move continuously, suggesting some physical expectations are learned very early or are innate [20,21], and Battaglia, Hamrick and Tenenbaum modeled adult physical judgments as approximate probabilistic simulation, an "intuitive physics engine" [22]. The physical modeling article discusses intuitive physics in more depth.

2.2 Control and estimation: models as the core of the loop

Engineering had used explicit predictive models since the start of modern control. Kalman's filter (1960) propagated a linear-Gaussian state-space model forward in time and corrected it with measurements [23]; Bellman's dynamic programming formalized optimal sequential decisions given a model [24]. In process control, Richalet and colleagues reported in 1978 on industrial applications of "model predictive heuristic control", and by the late 1980s model predictive control (MPC), repeatedly optimizing actions over a receding horizon with a model of the plant, was established in industry [11,25,26]. MPC remains the template for planning with learned models today (Section 8).

2.3 Learning models for reinforcement learning

Around 1990 two lines of work introduced learned world models into neural reinforcement learning. Schmidhuber trained a recurrent network to model the environment and used it for planning by gradient descent through the model [27], and Jordan and Rumelhart showed how a learned forward model can act as a "distal teacher" that converts errors in outcome space into errors in action space [28]. Sutton's Dyna architecture interleaved learning from real experience with learning from simulated experience generated by a learned model [29,30]. In robotics, Atkeson and Santamaría found model-based learning far more data-efficient than model-free learning on a pendulum swing-up task [31], and PILCO (2011) combined Gaussian-process dynamics models with analytic policy gradients to learn a real cart-pole swing-up from roughly 20 s of interaction [8,32].

2.4 Deep world models

Deep learning made it possible to learn models directly from images. Action-conditional video prediction produced plausible Atari frames many steps ahead [33], and Finn, Goodfellow and Levine trained video predictors on tens of thousands of real robot pushing interactions, which led to "visual foresight" planning for robot manipulation [34–36]. Ha and Schmidhuber's 2018 "World Models" popularized the term: a variational autoencoder compressed frames, a recurrent mixture-density network predicted the next latent, and a small controller was trained largely or wholly inside the "dream" [2,37]. PlaNet introduced the recurrent state-space model (RSSM) for planning from pixels [15]; the Dreamer agents learned policies by imagination in that latent space, culminating in DreamerV3, which used a single configuration across more than 150 tasks and was the first algorithm to collect diamonds in Minecraft from scratch without human data [16,38,39]. In parallel, probabilistic ensembles (PETS) and model-based policy optimization (MBPO) made learned models competitive on continuous control [9,40], MuZero learned a model only of what matters for planning (rewards, values and policies) and matched AlphaZero at board games while setting a new state of the art on Atari [17,41], and TD-MPC2 scaled latent-space MPC to a single agent trained on dozens of tasks [12,42].

2.5 World foundation models

Since 2023 the term has also been applied to large generative models trained on internet-scale video. GAIA-1 generated driving video conditioned on actions and text [43]; OpenAI described its Sora video model as a step toward "world simulators" [44]; Genie learned a playable environment, including a latent action space, from unlabeled gameplay video [45], and Genie 3 generates navigable environments in real time [46]; UniSim learned an action-conditioned simulator of real-world interaction from diverse datasets [47]; NVIDIA's Cosmos platform released pretrained "world foundation models" for robotics and driving [48]. A competing view, argued by LeCun, holds that a world model should predict in a learned abstract representation rather than in pixels, the joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) [3,49,50]; V-JEPA 2 combined such a video model with a small action-conditioned predictor and used it for zero-shot robot planning [51]. Whether these models capture physics or mostly reproduce the statistics of their training videos is an active question (Section 9).

3 Predicting future states of the environment

3.1 State, latent state and the prediction problem

The most basic job of a world model is prediction: given what has been observed and what the agent will do, what happens next? When the state is low-dimensional and known, joint angles and velocities for example, the model is a learned transition function ŝt+1 = fθ(st, at), often parameterized as a residual st + Δθ(st, at) and fitted by regression on logged transitions [4,9,53]. Classical system identification fits models of this kind with structure from physics; the physical modeling article covers that approach and the trade-off between physical structure and flexible function approximators. Learned world models usually sit at the flexible end: neural networks, Gaussian processes or, in the examples below, random Fourier features with ridge regression, a cheap approximation to a kernel method [54,55].

When the agent observes images or other high-dimensional signals, predicting directly in observation space is expensive and wasteful, because most pixels are irrelevant to control. Modern world models therefore learn an encoder zt = eφ(o≤t) and predict in the latent space [2,3,15]. Three choices distinguish the main families:

  • What is predicted. Generative models reconstruct observations (pixels, point clouds) from the latent state, so the latent must keep everything needed to redraw the scene [2,15,43]. Joint-embedding predictive (JEPA) models predict the representation of future observations and never reconstruct pixels, so unpredictable details such as leaf textures can be ignored [3,49,50]. Value-equivalent models such as MuZero predict only rewards, values and policies, the quantities planning needs [17].
  • How stochasticity is handled. Deterministic models predict one future; stochastic models represent a distribution, for example with variational latent variables [56,57], mixture-density outputs [2], discrete categorical latents [39] or diffusion [58,59].
  • The sequence architecture. Recurrent networks [15,60], transformers over tokenized frames [43,45,61] and video diffusion models [48,58] all appear in current systems.

3.2 The recurrent state-space model

PlaNet's recurrent state-space model (RSSM), reused in all Dreamer agents, splits the latent state into a deterministic recurrent part ht and a stochastic part zt [15,38]:

ht = fθ(ht−1, zt−1, at−1)    prior ẑt ~ pθ(ẑt | ht)    posterior zt ~ qθ(zt | ht, ot)    ôt ~ pθ(ot | ht, zt)    r̂t ~ pθ(rt | ht, zt)

Training maximizes a variational evidence lower bound: reconstruct observations and rewards from the posterior while keeping the prior, which is all that is available when imagining the future, close to the posterior in Kullback–Leibler divergence [15,56]. The deterministic path carries information reliably over many steps, while the stochastic path lets the model represent several possible futures; PlaNet reported that both components were needed for successful planning [15]. DreamerV2 replaced Gaussian latents with vectors of categorical variables [39], and DreamerV3 added normalization and transformation tricks (such as symlog predictions) so that one set of hyperparameters works across domains [16].

3.3 Compounding error and multi-step training

A model trained to predict one step ahead is used to predict many steps ahead by feeding its own predictions back in. Small one-step errors then compound, and the rolled-out trajectory drifts into states the model never saw in training, where its errors are larger still [62,63]. Remedies include training on multi-step rollouts [15,62], latent "overshooting" objectives [15], keeping imagined rollouts short and branching them from real states [40], and planning over horizons only as long as the model is trustworthy [9,42]. With identical dynamics and decoder learners, the choice of representation decides success. 3.5 Large-scale video prediction

At the other end of the scale, generative video models predict thousands of pixels per frame for long horizons. Robot video predictors trained on real pushing data were used for planning by visual foresight [34,36]; stochastic video models added latent variables to represent multiple futures [57]. Transformers that tokenize frames and predict tokens autoregressively, as in IRIS for Atari [61] and GAIA-1 for driving [43], and diffusion models such as DIAMOND [58] and the Cosmos models [48] are now common. Genie showed that a controllable model can be learned from video alone by inferring discrete latent actions between frames [45]. These models produce compelling imagery, but visual quality and physical correctness are different things (Section 9) [66].

4 Modeling object movement and interactions

4.1 Objects, relations and intuitive physics

Physical scenes are made of objects that persist, move continuously and interact mainly through contact and nearby forces. Human infants appear to expect these regularities from a young age [20,21], and benchmarks such as IntPhys and Physion test whether machine models share them, by measuring "surprise" at impossible events or by asking models to predict outcomes such as whether two objects will touch [67,68]. Physion found that vision-based models fell well short of human accuracy, while models given object- or particle-level scene information came closer [68], and a model with object-centred representations learned intuitive-physics expectations more readily than models without them [69].

A world model that represents objects explicitly gains two properties that flat models lack: compositionality (the same learned interaction rule applies to any pair of objects) and generalization to different numbers of objects [70]. Interaction networks represent a scene as a graph whose nodes are objects and whose edges are relations; a shared edge network computes an "effect" for each interacting pair, the effects are summed at each receiving node, and a node network predicts the object's next state [52]. Graph network simulators extend this encode–process–decode scheme to fluids, sand and deformables with thousands of particles [71,72]; the physical modeling article discusses their use as surrogate simulators.

4.2 Discovering objects from pixels

Explicit object states are not available from raw sensors, so object-centric world models must also learn to segment. Slot Attention binds image features to a fixed number of "slots" through iterative competitive attention, producing per-object latent vectors without supervision [73]. Contrastively trained structured world models (C-SWM) learn object-factored latent states and a graph neural network transition model by contrasting true next states with negative samples rather than reconstructing pixels [65]. CLEVRER, a video reasoning benchmark of colliding objects, showed that answering explanatory, predictive and counterfactual questions about collisions benefits from combining object-level dynamics models with symbolic reasoning [74]. For object detection, tracking and scene graphs from perception, see the computer vision article.

The relational model recovers the node terms and the pairwise force law almost exactly, and because the same edge function is applied to every pair it predicts systems of 12 particles, twice as many as it was trained on, with little loss of accuracy. This is the inductive bias argument made for graph networks [52,70,71]: building the structure of the world (objects, symmetric pairwise interactions, permutation invariance) into the model buys data efficiency and generalization.

5 Estimating the consequences of candidate actions

5.1 Action-conditioned forward models

Passive prediction tells an agent what will happen; to decide what to do, it must predict what will happen if it takes each candidate action. An action-conditioned forward model ŝt+1 = fθ(st, at) does exactly this, and is the model used by MPC, Dyna and PETS [9,11,30]. In pixel-based models the action enters the dynamics network, for example by multiplicative interactions between action and hidden state in action-conditional video prediction [33] or as an input to the RSSM transition [15]. Collecting data that covers the consequences of many different actions is essential; robots therefore often gather experience with random or exploratory actions before, or while, planning with the model [10,34,53].

Action consequences are rarely judged by the next state alone. Practical world models add heads that predict reward or task cost, episode termination (for example a fall or collision) and, in value-based hybrids, the long-term value of the predicted state [16,17,42]. MuZero takes this to its conclusion: its model is trained only so that the predicted rewards, values and policies along imagined action sequences match those observed, without any requirement that the latent state resemble the true state [17]. Such value-equivalent models are compact, but they cannot be reused for a new task without retraining because they encode nothing irrelevant to the old one [4].

5.2 Forward versus inverse models

The alternative to searching over actions with a forward model is to learn an inverse model a = g(s, sgoal) that outputs the action directly. Jordan and Rumelhart pointed out a fundamental problem: when several actions lead to the same outcome, the inverse mapping is one-to-many, and a regression model trained on such data averages the valid actions, which may itself be invalid [28]. Their remedy was to train the controller through a learned forward model, the "distal teacher" [28]; the same reasoning motivates the forward-plus-inverse internal models of motor neuroscience [18,19].

The forward model's predicted outcomes match the true outcomes to within a few centimetres, and evaluating many candidates reveals two qualitatively different solutions: a direct slide and a faster shot banked off the wall. The inverse model, trained on data containing both, outputs a speed between them that matches neither, and misses by about 10 cm, several times the error of the forward-model search. Evaluating candidates with a forward model is more expensive than a single inverse-model query, but it handles multimodal solutions and arbitrary new cost functions without retraining [4,28].

5.3 Visual foresight and goal-conditioned prediction

Visual foresight applied this pattern to real robots: an action-conditioned video prediction model, trained on autonomously collected interactions, predicts where designated pixels (for example on an object to be pushed) will move under candidate action sequences, and a sampling-based planner chooses the sequence that brings them closest to the goal [35,36]. Because the model is task-agnostic, the same model served different pushing and rearrangement goals [36]. Recent large models extend the idea to language-specified actions, for example UniSim, which conditions video prediction on text instructions and low-level robot controls and was used to train policies that were then deployed on real robots [47].

6 Representing uncertainty and incomplete information

6.1 Two kinds of uncertainty

Every world model is wrong somewhere, and an agent that plans with it needs to know where. It is useful to distinguish aleatoric uncertainty, inherent randomness that more data cannot remove (sensor noise, a pedestrian's unknown intention), from epistemic uncertainty, the model's ignorance due to limited data, which more data would reduce [75,76]. The distinction matters for planning: aleatoric uncertainty should be planned around, while epistemic uncertainty indicates where the model cannot be trusted, where a planner may be exploiting model errors, and where exploration would be informative [9,63].

Common representations are:

  • Probabilistic outputs, such as a Gaussian mean and variance per prediction, trained by maximum likelihood; these capture aleatoric uncertainty [9,76].
  • Gaussian processes, whose posterior variance grows away from the data; PILCO propagated this uncertainty through multi-step predictions by moment matching and showed that ignoring model uncertainty caused learning to fail [8,32,55].
  • Ensembles of independently initialized or bootstrapped networks, whose disagreement approximates epistemic uncertainty [77]; PETS combined probabilistic networks in an ensemble and propagated particles through randomly chosen members ("trajectory sampling") during planning [9]. Monte Carlo dropout is a cheaper approximation [78].
  • Stochastic latent variables, as in the RSSM and stochastic video models, which represent multimodal futures [15,39,57].

Inside the training range the ensemble is accurate, the noise estimate grows with angle as the true noise does, and the 95% predictive interval covers about 93% of new observations. Outside the training range the epistemic term grows by almost two orders of magnitude, correctly flagging that the predictions are not to be trusted. It still understates the actual error (about 19 against an epistemic spread of about 10 at 3 rad): ensembles indicate where a model is ignorant better than how much [76,77]. Algorithms therefore keep imagined rollouts short so they stay near real data, or penalize ensemble disagreement in the reward [40,79].

6.2 Partial observability and belief states

Uncertainty also arises because the agent cannot observe the full state: a hidden object, an unknown mass or friction coefficient, another agent's intention. In a POMDP the optimal decision depends on the belief, the posterior distribution over states given all past observations and actions, which can itself be propagated forward in time [5,6]. Bayes filters, Kalman filters and particle filters are the classical tools for maintaining beliefs; the sensor fusion article covers them in detail [23,80]. Learned world models usually fold the filter into the network: the RSSM posterior q(zt | ht, ot) is a learned filter, and the recurrent state summarizes history [15,38].

A belief-space world model predicts not one future but the distribution of futures implied by what the agent currently knows. The listing below maintains a particle-filter belief over the position, velocity and unknown friction coefficient of a block being pushed, from noisy position measurements, and at several moments asks what the belief predicts for one second later.

Estimating hidden physical parameters online and propagating their uncertainty into predictions is the same idea as adaptive and domain-randomized control in simulation-trained robots [32,81], applied inside a world model.

6.3 Using uncertainty when acting

Uncertainty estimates feed back into behaviour in three main ways. Risk-aware planning optimizes the expected cost under the predicted distribution, or a pessimistic statistic of it, rather than the cost of a single most likely future [8,9]. Pessimism in offline settings penalizes actions whose outcomes the ensemble disagrees about, so that a policy learned from a fixed dataset does not exploit model errors [79]. Active information gathering chooses actions that reduce uncertainty, for example probing an object to learn its mass before lifting it, which is optimal behaviour in a POMDP when the information pays for itself [5].

7 Evaluating hypothetical scenarios

7.1 Imagination, counterfactuals and mental simulation

A world model lets an agent ask questions about situations it is not in: what would happen if the pedestrian stepped out now, if the road were wet, if the box were heavier than it looks. Cognitive scientists treat such mental simulation as a core human ability, used for prediction, explanation and planning [13,22]. Gerstenberg and colleagues found that people's judgments of whether one object caused another to go through a gate were predicted by a model that simulates what would have happened had the first object been absent, a counterfactual simulation [82].

Pearl's causal hierarchy distinguishes three rungs of reasoning: association (what is likely given what I see), intervention (what will happen if I do something) and counterfactuals (what would have happened had something been different, given what actually happened) [83]. An action-conditioned world model answers interventional queries by construction. Counterfactual queries additionally require holding fixed the unobserved factors that explain what actually happened, which in simulation corresponds to reusing the same random seeds, or the same inferred latent noise, while changing the intervention [83]. CLEVRER's counterfactual questions ("what would happen if the cylinder were removed?") test exactly this capability in learned video models [74].

7.2 Imagination for learning and decision making

Imagined experience can be used to make decisions directly or to train policies. Imagination-augmented agents (I2A) learned to interpret imagined rollouts from an imperfect model as additional input to a policy, and learned to ignore the model where it was unreliable [84]. Dyna and Dreamer train value functions and policies on imagined trajectories [30,38]; in Ha and Schmidhuber's VizDoom experiment the controller was trained entirely inside the learned model and then transferred to the real environment, but only after the model's temperature (stochasticity) was raised, because a deterministic dream was too easy to exploit [37].

7.3 Scenario simulation for safety

For safety-critical systems, evaluating hypothetical scenarios is a validation method. Autonomous-driving developers build data-driven simulators in which recorded traffic is replayed and agents are controlled by learned behaviour models, so a policy can be tested against many plausible variations of real situations: TrafficSim learned multi-agent behaviour models for this purpose [85], and Waymax provides a hardware-accelerated simulator built on recorded driving data [14]. Generative world models extend this to sensor-level simulation, producing video of rare situations conditioned on actions or text [43,48]. Because the outcome of a scenario is random, the result of each "what if" is an estimated probability with a confidence interval, and comparisons between alternatives are more precise when they share random numbers (the same sampled pedestrians, the same noise), the counterfactual idea from Section 7.1 in statistical form [83].

On a dry road, reactive braking roughly halves the collision probability relative to keeping speed, and slowing near the crosswalk in advance reduces it further at a cost of about a second. The wet-road hypothetical reveals something the dry-road tests hide: the reactive policy was tuned assuming dry-road braking, so on a wet road it gains almost nothing over doing nothing, while the precautionary policy still helps. Asking such questions in a model, rather than discovering the answers on the road, is the main reason safety-critical robotics invests in world models and simulation [14,85]. The value of the answer depends on the fidelity of the model, here a few lines of code and in practice a validated simulator or learned model whose own errors must be quantified (Section 9) [66].

8 Planning with predicted trajectories and simulated outcomes

8.1 Model-predictive control with a learned model

The most direct way to use a world model is model-predictive control: at every time step, optimize a sequence of actions at:t+H−1 over a horizon H to minimize the predicted cost, execute only the first action, observe the new state and re-plan [11,26]:

a*t:t+H−1 = argmina 𝔼 [ Σk=0H−1 c(ŝt+k, at+k) + V(ŝt+H) ],    ŝt+k+1 ~ fθ(ŝt+k, at+k),    ŝt = st

Re-planning at every step makes MPC robust to model error, because mistakes in the prediction are corrected by fresh observations [26]. The optional terminal value V estimates the cost beyond the horizon; TD-MPC learns it by temporal-difference learning so that short-horizon planning in a learned latent space can solve long-horizon tasks [12,42].

Learned models are typically non-differentiable in practice (or have unreliable gradients), so sampling-based optimizers dominate [9,15,42]:

  • Random shooting samples many action sequences and keeps the best; it is simple but scales poorly with horizon and action dimension [53].
  • The cross-entropy method (CEM), originally developed for rare-event simulation and combinatorial optimization, iteratively refits a Gaussian sampling distribution to the best ("elite") samples [86,87]. It is the planner in PETS and PlaNet [9,15].
  • Model-predictive path integral control (MPPI) derives the update from information theory: it averages sampled perturbations weighted by the exponentiated negative cost, and was demonstrated on aggressive autonomous driving [88]. TD-MPC uses a variant of this update [42].
  • Gradient-based trajectory optimization such as iLQR and differential dynamic programming linearize the model around a trajectory and solve a sequence of linear-quadratic problems; it is fast when the model is smooth and differentiable [89,90].

All three planners query only the learned model, yet CEM and MPPI swing the real pendulum up in under five seconds and balance it for the rest of the run. Random shooting with the same budget never succeeds: independent uniformly random torques rarely produce the coordinated pumping needed, and without a sampling distribution that improves over iterations or carries over between time steps it never finds such a sequence. Iterative refinement and warm starting are what make sampling-based planners practical [9,87,88].

8.2 Tree search with learned models

For discrete actions, and in games, Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) builds a search tree by repeatedly simulating from the root, selecting actions with an upper-confidence rule (UCT) that balances promising and under-explored branches [91,92]. AlphaZero combined MCTS with a learned policy and value network but used the known game rules as its model [41]; MuZero replaced the rules with a learned latent model trained to predict rewards, values and policies, and matched AlphaZero's performance in Go, chess and shogi while learning Atari games from pixels [17].

8.3 Learning policies in imagination

Planning at every step is computationally expensive; an alternative is to amortize planning into a policy trained on imagined experience. Dyna is the classic form: each real transition updates both the value function and the model, and the model then generates additional simulated transitions for further value updates [7,29]. Without planning, the agent needs hundreds of steps per episode for many episodes; with 50 simulated updates per real step it follows a near-shortest path (the optimum is 14 steps; exploration adds a few) from the third episode onward, because the reward found in the first episode is propagated back through the remembered transitions in imagination rather than by repeated real experience [7]. The first episode is shorter with planning too, because replaying zero-reward transitions erodes the small random initial action values for actions already tried, nudging the agent toward untried ones.

Dreamer scales the same idea to learned latent models and continuous control: an actor and a critic are trained entirely on imagined latent trajectories, with the actor optimized by backpropagating value estimates through the differentiable model [38]. DreamerV3 learns its world model, critic and actor concurrently from replayed experience with fixed hyperparameters across domains [16], and DayDreamer showed that this is data-efficient enough to learn on physical robots, including a quadruped that learned to walk within about an hour of real-world interaction [10]. MBPO interleaves short model rollouts branched from real states with model-free policy optimization, and gives a bound relating model error to policy performance that justifies keeping those rollouts short [40].

8.4 Planning in learned representation spaces

When the world model is not generative, planning happens entirely in latent space with a goal specified as a latent too. TD-MPC2 plans with MPPI in a learned latent space with a learned terminal value, and a single model trained on many continuous-control tasks across embodiments [12]. V-JEPA 2-AC, an action-conditioned predictor trained on top of a frozen self-supervised video encoder using a small amount of robot interaction video from the DROID dataset, plans robot arm motions by minimizing the distance between the predicted and goal image embeddings with CEM, and was used for zero-shot pick-and-place in new environments [51,93]. Planning against embeddings avoids generating pixels, but it depends on the embedding space making task-relevant differences measurable [3,51].

8.5 Pitfalls: model exploitation and objective mismatch

An optimizer searching against a learned model will find actions for which the model is wrong in its favour. This model exploitation is the main failure mode of planning with learned models; remedies are uncertainty-aware objectives that penalize or average over ensemble members [9,79], short horizons and re-planning [26,40], and continued data collection under the planner's own behaviour so that errors the planner exploits are corrected [10,53]. A related issue is objective mismatch: models trained for one-step likelihood are not necessarily the models that yield the best control, because likelihood weights all transitions equally while control depends on a small task-relevant subset [63]. Value-equivalent and task-aware models such as MuZero and TD-MPC respond to this by training the model with task-related losses [17,42].

9.2 Evaluating world models

Because world models are means rather than ends, they are evaluated in several complementary ways. Predictive accuracy measures error at the horizons that matter, including multi-step rollouts (Section 3.3) [62]. Downstream control performance measures what the agent achieves using the model, typically on standard suites such as the DeepMind Control Suite for continuous control [94] and the Atari 100k benchmark of 26 games with a budget of 100 000 interactions [58,61,95]; the gap between the two kinds of evaluation is the objective mismatch of Section 8.5 [63]. Physical understanding is probed with intuitive-physics benchmarks such as IntPhys and Physion [67,68] and, for generative video models, with tests that compare generated continuations with real physical outcomes: Physics-IQ found that physical understanding in current video models was severely limited and unrelated to visual realism [66], and Kang and colleagues found that video models trained on simulated physics generalized within their training distribution but failed outside it [96]. Calibration of predicted uncertainty is checked by comparing predicted intervals with observed outcome frequencies, as in Section 6.1 [77].

9.3 Open problems

  • Physical consistency. Large video models can render plausible scenes that violate conservation laws, object permanence or contact constraints [66,96]. Combining learned models with physical structure, as discussed in the physical modeling article, and with object-centric representations [70,73] is one direction.
  • Long horizons. Compounding error limits open-loop prediction; hierarchical models, learned terminal values and re-planning mitigate but do not remove the problem [40,42,62].
  • Grounding actions. Video learned from the internet rarely contains the robot's actions; latent action models and small amounts of action-labelled robot data, as in Genie and V-JEPA 2, are approaches to closing that gap [45,51,93].
  • What to predict. Pixel reconstruction, latent prediction and value equivalence trade generality against efficiency, and there is no consensus on the best objective for physical AI [3,16,17].
  • Trust and verification. Before a world model is used to certify safety or to train policies that run on real hardware, its error and its uncertainty estimates must themselves be validated; this is a measurement problem as much as a learning problem [14,77].

Conclusion

A world model is the part of an intelligent system that answers the question "what happens next, and what if I act?" The idea runs from Craik's small-scale model of reality through Kalman filtering and model predictive control to Dyna, Dreamer, MuZero and today's world foundation models. Across that history the same capabilities recur: predicting future states, capturing how objects move and interact, estimating the consequences of actions, representing what is uncertain or hidden, evaluating hypothetical scenarios and planning with simulated outcomes. The examples in this article show the core lessons in miniature: multi-step error, not one-step error, determines usefulness; the representation decides whether dynamics are easy to learn; relational structure generalizes; forward models handle multimodal solutions that inverse models average away; uncertainty must be represented and propagated; and planners need iterative, warm-started optimization and protection against exploiting model errors. Physical AI will increasingly rely on learned world models, and its reliability will depend on how well those models are grounded in physics and data and how honestly their uncertainty is reported.
