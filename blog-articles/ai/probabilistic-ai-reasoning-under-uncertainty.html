§1Why an intelligent system needs uncertainty

A logical agent asks whether a sentence is true or false. A real agent rarely has that luxury. Sensors are noisy, the world is only partly observable, models are approximations, and the future has not happened yet.

Russell and Norvig name three reasons why purely logical rules fail in domains like medical diagnosis: laziness (listing every exception is too much work), theoretical ignorance (science has no complete theory of the domain) and practical ignorance (even with a complete theory, we have not run every test on this patient).1 A rule such as "toothache implies cavity" is simply wrong for many patients; "toothache makes cavity more likely, by this much" is right.

Probability theory is the standard calculus for this kind of graded belief. Cox showed in 1946 that any system for reasoning with degrees of plausibility that satisfies a few qualitative consistency requirements must be isomorphic to probability theory,2 and Jaynes developed that result into a full account of probability as "the logic of science".3 Kolmogorov's axioms give the same calculus a measure-theoretic foundation.4 The practical upshot is that an AI system that wants consistent beliefs should either use probability or explain clearly why it does not.

Uncertainty also has more than one source, and the distinction matters later. Aleatoric uncertainty is noise inherent in the data, such as a sensor's jitter, and does not shrink with more data. Epistemic uncertainty is ignorance about the model itself, and does shrink as evidence accumulates.5 A well-designed system tracks both.

What this article covers

Each section introduces one family of tools, works a small example by hand, gives a runnable Python program (NumPy only) with its real output, and ends with exercises. The programs are short on purpose: they are meant to be read, not imported.

§3Probability theory and Bayesian inference

The axioms

A probability measure P assigns a number to every event A in a sample space Ω such that

P(A) ≥ 0, P(Ω) = 1, P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) if A ∩ B = ∅.(Kolmogorov)

Everything else follows from these three statements.4 A random variable is a function of the outcome; its distribution lists the probability of each value. For several variables the joint distribution P(X, Y) answers every question about them, because we can always marginalise:

P(X = x) = ∑y P(X = x, Y = y).(sum rule)

The trouble is size. A joint distribution over n binary variables has 2n − 1 free numbers, which is about 1030 for a hundred variables. Most of probabilistic AI is about representing and computing with joint distributions without ever writing them down, by exploiting independence: X and Y are independent when P(X, Y) = P(X) P(Y), and conditionally independent given Z when P(X, Y | Z) = P(X | Z) P(Y | Z).38

Two readings of probability

The frequentist reading treats a probability as a long-run frequency of repeatable events. The Bayesian reading treats it as a degree of belief held by an agent, which can apply to one-off propositions such as "this patient has appendicitis" or "the parameter is between 0.6 and 0.8".3,39 AI systems mostly need the second, because an agent has to act on beliefs about singular situations. The two readings share the same mathematics; they differ in what may be given a probability.

Bayesian inference in one line

Treat unknown quantities θ as random variables. Before seeing data, a prior p(θ) encodes what we believe. A likelihood p(D | θ) says how probable the data would be under each value. Bayes' theorem (§4) combines them into the posterior:

p(θ | D) = p(D | θ) p(θ)p(D)∝ likelihood × prior.

Predictions average over the posterior rather than committing to one parameter value. This posterior predictive distribution,

p(x̃ | D) = ∫p(x̃ | θ) p(θ | D) dθ,

is the source of the Bayesian approach's honest error bars: when data are scarce, the posterior is wide and predictions are correspondingly cautious.39,40

Worked example: is this coin biased?

Let θ be the probability of heads. A Beta(a, b) prior has density proportional to θa−1(1−θ)b−1, and the Binomial likelihood for h heads in n tosses is proportional to θh(1−θ)n−h. Multiplying them gives another Beta:

θ | D ~ Beta(a + h, b + n − h), E[θ | D] = a + ha + b + n

A prior that yields a posterior of the same family is called conjugate.39 The prior parameters behave like "pseudo-counts" of heads and tails seen before the experiment. The program below simulates a coin with θ = 0.7 and starts from a mild Beta(2, 2) belief in fairness.

When the integral is hard

Conjugacy is the exception. For most models p(D) = ∫ p(D | θ) p(θ) dθ has no closed form, and approximate inference takes over. The two great families are:

  • Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) builds a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior and averages over its samples. Metropolis's 1953 algorithm, Gibbs sampling and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo are members.12,23,31
  • Variational inference (VI) picks a tractable family q(θ) and minimises the KL divergence KL(q ‖ p(θ | D)), turning inference into optimisation. It is usually faster than MCMC and is usually biased.41

Both families reappear in §5 (Gibbs sampling) and §8 (probabilistic programming).

Exercises

  1. A uniform Beta(1, 1) prior is updated with 7 heads in 10 tosses. Give the posterior, its mean, and its mode (the MAP estimate).
    Solution

    Posterior Beta(1 + 7, 1 + 3) = Beta(8, 4). Mean 8 / 12 ≈ 0.667. Mode (a − 1)/(a + b − 2) = 7/10 = 0.7, which equals the maximum-likelihood estimate because a uniform prior adds nothing to the likelihood's shape. The mean is pulled toward 0.5 because it averages over the whole posterior.

  2. Prove that if A and B are independent and P(B) > 0, then P(A | B) = P(A).
    Solution

    By definition P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B). Independence means P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B), so P(A | B) = P(A) P(B) / P(B) = P(A). Learning B tells us nothing about A.

  3. How many free parameters does a full joint distribution over 20 binary variables need? How many if all 20 are mutually independent?
    Solution

    Joint: 220 − 1 = 1,048,575. Fully independent: one number per variable, so 20. Real models sit in between; graphical models (§5) let us choose exactly which independences to assume.

§4Bayes' theorem and conditional probability

Conditioning

The conditional probability of A given B is the fraction of B's probability in which A also holds:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)P(B), P(B) > 0.

Rearranged, this is the product rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) P(B). Applied repeatedly it gives the chain rule, which factorises any joint distribution:

P(X1, …, Xn) = ∏iP(Xi | X1, …, Xi−1).

Combining the product rule with the sum rule gives the law of total probability, P(B) = ∑i P(B | Ai) P(Ai) for any partition {Ai}.

The theorem

Because P(A ∩ B) can be written two ways, P(A | B) P(B) = P(B | A) P(A). Dividing gives Bayes' theorem:7,8

P(H | E) = P(E | H) P(H)P(E | H) P(H) + P(E | ¬H) P(¬H)

It inverts the direction of reasoning. Medical knowledge is naturally stored causally, as P(symptom | disease), and sensors are characterised as P(reading | state). Bayes' theorem turns that knowledge into the diagnostic direction an agent needs: P(disease | symptom).1

An equivalent and often clearer form uses odds. With prior odds O(H) = P(H)/P(¬H) and likelihood ratio Λ = P(E | H)/P(E | ¬H),

O(H | E) = Λ × O(H).(odds form)

Each independent piece of evidence multiplies the odds by its likelihood ratio, so in log-odds evidence simply adds.

Worked example: the base-rate fallacy

In 1978 Casscells, Schoenberger and Graboys asked 60 students and staff at Harvard Medical School this question: a disease has prevalence 1 in 1,000, and a test for it has a false-positive rate of 5%. What is the chance that a person with a positive result actually has the disease? Only 11 of the 60 gave the correct answer of about 2%; the most common answer was 95%.42 Assuming the test detects every true case, Bayes' theorem gives

P(D | +) = 1.00 × 0.0011.00 × 0.001 + 0.05 × 0.999≈ 0.0196.

Naive Bayes

If evidence variables E1, …, Ek are assumed conditionally independent given a class C, the posterior factorises as P(C | e1:k) ∝ P(C) ∏i P(ei | C). This naive Bayes model is the simplest Bayesian network (a class node with arrows to every feature). Its independence assumption is usually false, yet its decisions are often good, which is why it remains a strong baseline for text classification.1,40 Its probabilities, though, tend to be pushed toward 0 and 1, a calibration problem taken up in §10.43

Exercises

  1. A screening test has sensitivity 90% and false-positive rate 9% for a condition with prevalence 1%. What is P(condition | +)?
    Solution

    0.9 × 0.01 / (0.9 × 0.01 + 0.09 × 0.99) = 0.009 / 0.0981 ≈ 0.092. About 9%, so roughly 10 of every 11 positives are false alarms.

  2. Redo exercise 1 with the odds form.
    Solution

    Prior odds 1 : 99. Likelihood ratio 0.9 / 0.09 = 10. Posterior odds 10 : 99, so P = 10 / 109 ≈ 0.092. Same answer, one multiplication.

  3. Monty Hall. You pick door 1. The host, who knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you did not pick, opens door 3. Use Bayes' theorem to find the probability that the car is behind door 2.
    Solution

    Let Ci be "car behind door i", each with prior 1/3, and O3 the host opening door 3. P(O3 | C1) = 1/2 (he chooses between 2 and 3), P(O3 | C2) = 1 (forced), P(O3 | C3) = 0. Then P(C2 | O3) = (1 × 1/3) / (1/2 × 1/3 + 1 × 1/3 + 0) = 2/3. Switching wins two-thirds of the time.

  4. Show that in log-odds form, k conditionally independent pieces of evidence combine additively.
    Solution

    Conditional independence gives P(e1:k | H) = ∏ P(ei | H), and likewise for ¬H. So O(H | e1:k) = O(H) ∏ Λi, and taking logs, log O(H | e1:k) = log O(H) + ∑ log Λi. Each item contributes its "weight of evidence" log Λi.

§5Bayesian networks and Markov networks

A graphical model draws the independence structure of a distribution as a graph, then stores only the small local tables the graph says are needed.

Bayesian networks

A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) whose nodes are random variables, plus one conditional probability table (CPT) per node giving P(Xi | Parents(Xi)). It represents the joint distribution

P(x1, …, xn) = n∏i=1P(xi | parents(Xi)).

This is the chain rule with most conditioning variables deleted. The deletions are the modelling assumptions: each node is conditionally independent of its non-descendants given its parents.24,38 Pearl's 1988 book established the formalism and its message-passing inference; Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter's junction-tree method made exact inference practical on networks with loops.24,25

Example: the burglar alarm

Pearl's classic example: your house alarm can be set off by a burglary or an earthquake, and two neighbours, John and Mary, may call you when they hear it.24 The numbers below are the ones used by Russell and Norvig.1

Both neighbours call. How likely is a burglary? We want P(B | j, m), which means summing the hidden variables E and A out of the factorised joint and normalising:

P(B | j, m) = α ∑e∑aP(B) P(e) P(a | B, e) P(j | a) P(m | a).

Two calls raise the probability of a burglary from 0.1% to about 28%. It stays well below one half because false alarms (0.1% per day) and earthquakes are, together, still more common than burglaries.

Reading independence off the graph

Whether two variables are independent given evidence can be read from the graph with d-separation. Every path is made of three kinds of connection, and a path is blocked by the evidence as shown in Figure 4.24,38

The cost of exact inference

Variable elimination and the junction tree are exponential only in the network's treewidth, so sparse networks are cheap. In general, though, exact inference in Bayesian networks is NP-hard,44 which is why sampling and variational methods matter in practice.

Markov networks

Some dependencies have no natural direction: neighbouring pixels in an image, or adjacent words that tend to share a tag. A Markov network (Markov random field, MRF) uses an undirected graph and attaches a non-negative potential φc to each clique c:

P(x) = 1Z∏cφc(xc), Z = ∑x∏cφc(xc).

Potentials are not probabilities; they score how compatible a configuration is. The normalising constant Z, the partition function, sums over every joint state and is usually the hard part. Independence is simpler to read than in a DAG: a set of nodes separates two others if every path between them passes through it. The Hammersley–Clifford theorem says that for strictly positive distributions, factorising over cliques and satisfying these graph-separation independences are equivalent.20,38

The program computes exact answers by brute-force enumeration of all 16 states, then estimates the same conditional by Gibbs sampling, which repeatedly resamples one variable from its distribution given its neighbours. Geman and Geman introduced it for exactly this kind of model.23

outputZ = 21202.0
P(A=1) = 0.500   P(A=1 | C=1) = 0.962
Gibbs estimate of P(A=1 | C=1) = 0.960

Observing C = 1 pulls A toward 1 through both paths around the cycle. The Gibbs estimate matches the exact value to two decimal places.

When the potentials are conditioned on an observed input sequence, the result is a conditional random field, which became a standard model for sequence labelling.46

  1. Count the free parameters in the burglary network and compare with the full joint over the same five binary variables.
    Solution

    B: 1, E: 1, A: 4 (one per parent configuration), J: 2, M: 2. Total 10, against 25 − 1 = 31 for the full joint.

    Not marginally: the path J ← A → M is a fork through an unobserved node, so it is open, and a call from John raises the chance that Mary calls. Given A the fork is blocked, so J ⊥ M | A.

§6Hidden Markov models

Many problems unfold over time: speech, a patient's condition, a robot's position. A hidden Markov model (HMM) assumes a discrete hidden state Xt that evolves as a Markov chain and emits an observation Et at each step.26 It is specified by

  • a transition model P(Xt | Xt−1), a matrix T;
  • a sensor (emission) model P(Et | Xt), a matrix O;
  • an initial distribution P(X0).

The three problems

Rabiner's 1989 tutorial organises HMM practice around three questions.26

ProblemQuestionAlgorithmCost
Evaluation / filteringHow likely is this observation sequence? What is P(Xt | e1:t)?Forward algorithmO(T K2)
DecodingWhich hidden sequence best explains the observations?Viterbi algorithm17O(T K2)
LearningWhich T, O maximise P(e1:T)?Baum–Welch, an instance of EM18,22O(T K2) per iteration
 K is the number of hidden states and T the sequence length. Enumerating all hidden paths would cost O(KT).

The forward algorithm is a two-step recursion that the filters of §7 generalise:

P(Xt | e1:t) = α P(et | Xt)∑xt−1P(Xt | xt−1) P(xt−1 | e1:t−1).update · predict

Viterbi replaces the sum with a max and keeps back-pointers to recover the best path.

Worked example: the umbrella world

A security guard in an underground building cannot see the weather but sees whether the director carries an umbrella.1 Rain persists with probability 0.7; an umbrella appears on 90% of rainy days and 20% of dry days.

output[0.818 0.883 0.191 0.731 0.867]
log P(e) = -3.373
Viterbi: ['rain', 'rain', 'dry', 'rain', 'rain']

On day 1 the belief in rain is 0.818, and on day 2 it is 0.883, matching the hand calculation in Russell and Norvig.1 The umbrella-free day 3 drops it to 0.191, and the Viterbi path labels that day dry while keeping the rest rainy. Each step multiplies probabilities smaller than one, so long sequences underflow; normalising each step (as in forward) or working in log space (as in viterbi) avoids that.

HMMs were the dominant acoustic model in speech recognition from the 1970s through the 2000s,47 and are still used in bioinformatics, gesture recognition and anywhere a small, interpretable sequence model is needed.

Exercises

  1. Compute the filtered belief P(rain2 | u1, u2) for the umbrella world by hand, starting from P(rain0) = 0.5.
    Solution

    Day 1: predict ⟨0.5, 0.5⟩; update with ⟨0.9, 0.2⟩ gives ⟨0.45, 0.10⟩, normalised ⟨0.818, 0.182⟩. Day 2: predict 0.818 × 0.7 + 0.182 × 0.3 = 0.627, so ⟨0.627, 0.373⟩; update gives ⟨0.564, 0.075⟩, normalised ⟨0.883, 0.117⟩.

  2. Why is the forward algorithm O(T K2) rather than O(KT)?
    Solution

    The Markov property means the belief at time t summarises everything relevant about the past. Each step needs only the K-vector from the previous step and a K × K matrix product, so the work is K2 per step instead of one term per possible path.

  3. Extend hmm.py with the backward pass to compute smoothed estimates P(Xt | e1:T). Is the smoothed belief for day 3 higher or lower than the filtered one, and why?
    Solution

    Backward messages: bT = 1, bt = T (O[:, et+1] ⊙ bt+1); smoothed ∝ ft ⊙ bt. The smoothed belief in rain on day 3 is higher than 0.191 (about 0.31) because the umbrellas on days 4 and 5 suggest a rainy spell, and rain tends to persist. Smoothing uses future evidence that filtering cannot.

§7Kalman filters and particle filters

An HMM with a continuous state is a state-space model. Tracking that state is the Bayes filter: alternate a prediction through the dynamics with an update from the sensor.

The Kalman filter

Kalman's 1960 paper solved the filter in closed form for linear dynamics with Gaussian noise:14

xt = F xt−1 + wt, wt ~ 𝒩(0, Q);  zt = H xt + vt, vt ~ 𝒩(0, R).

If the belief starts Gaussian, it stays Gaussian, so a mean x̂ and covariance P carry everything:

StepEquationMeaning
Predict meanx̂⁻ = F x̂push the estimate through the dynamics
Predict covarianceP⁻ = F P F⊤ + Quncertainty grows by the process noise
Innovationy = z − H x̂⁻,  S = H P⁻ H⊤ + Rsurprise in the measurement, and its expected spread
Kalman gainK = P⁻ H⊤ S−1how much to trust the measurement over the prediction
Updatex̂ = x̂⁻ + K y,  P = (I − K H) P⁻move toward the measurement; shrink uncertainty

For linear-Gaussian systems the Kalman filter is the exact Bayesian posterior and hence the minimum mean-squared-error estimator; for non-Gaussian noise it is still the best linear estimator.14,48 Engineers at NASA Ames, led by Stanley Schmidt, adapted it for estimating position and velocity on circumlunar trajectories, the work that led to its use in Apollo navigation.15

The program tracks an object moving at roughly constant velocity from position-only measurements with a standard deviation of 2 m. Notice that it also estimates the velocity, which is never measured directly.

outputraw measurement RMSE: 1.25 m
Kalman estimate RMSE: 0.67 m
final velocity estimate: 0.75 m/s (true 0.92)

For nonlinear models the extended Kalman filter linearises the dynamics and sensor with Jacobians at the current estimate. The unscented Kalman filter propagates a small set of deterministically chosen "sigma points" through the nonlinearity instead, which captures the mean and covariance more accurately without derivatives.48,49

The particle filter

When the belief is multimodal (a robot that could be in front of any of several identical doors) no Gaussian can represent it. The particle filter, or sequential Monte Carlo, represents the belief by N samples. Gordon, Salmond and Smith's 1993 bootstrap filter is the template:27,50

  1. Propagate each particle through the motion model, adding noise.
  2. Weight each particle by the likelihood of the measurement, w(i) ∝ p(zt | xt(i)).
  3. Resample N particles with probability proportional to weight, so effort concentrates where the posterior mass is.

Without resampling the weights degenerate: after a few steps one particle carries almost all the weight. A common rule resamples only when the effective sample size 1 / ∑ (w(i))2 falls below N/2.50 In robotics this algorithm is known as Monte Carlo localisation.48

outputt= 0  true=  4.0  estimate= 31.0  particle sd= 28.7
t=10  true= 14.0  estimate= 27.1  particle sd= 20.1
t=25  true= 29.0  estimate= 29.1  particle sd=  0.8
t=39  true= 43.0  estimate= 43.1  particle sd=  1.4

At step 10 the printed "estimate" is meaningless: it averages over three separate clusters. This is a general lesson. A summary statistic of a multimodal posterior can describe a state the system is sure it is not in, so look at the whole distribution.

Exercises

  1. A scalar Kalman filter predicts x̂⁻ = 8 with P⁻ = 4; a sensor with R = 2 reads z = 12 (H = 1). Compute K, x̂ and P.
    Solution

    K = 4 / (4 + 2) = 2/3; x̂ = 8 + (2/3)(12 − 8) ≈ 10.67; P = (1 − 2/3) × 4 ≈ 1.33. Figure 8 plots this case.

  2. In kalman.py, what happens to the estimates if you set R to 0.01 while the real noise stays at 4? If you set Q to zero?
    Solution

    With R too small the filter trusts every measurement, so the gain approaches 1 and the estimate follows the noise; RMSE rises toward the raw measurement error. With Q = 0 the filter believes the motion is perfectly predictable, P shrinks toward zero, the gain decays, and the filter stops responding to real changes in velocity. Tuning Q and R is most of the practical work.

  3. Normalised weights are (0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1). What is the effective sample size, and should the filter resample?
    Solution

    1 / (0.49 + 0.01 + 0.01 + 0.01) = 1 / 0.52 ≈ 1.92. That is below N/2 = 2, so yes.

§8Probabilistic programming

A probabilistic programming language (PPL) lets you write a generative model as ordinary code and get inference for free.

The idea is to separate the model (how data could have been generated) from the inference (working backwards from data to causes). Two primitives are enough: sample draws a latent variable from a distribution, and observe conditions on a data value by multiplying its likelihood into the program's weight. An inference engine then runs or transforms the program to approximate the posterior over everything sampled.30,32

Exercises

  1. Using the Trace class, write a model for the coin of §3: a bias θ with a Beta(2, 2) prior and ten observed tosses. What do you need to add?
    Solution

    A Beta distribution class with draw (for example random.betavariate(a, b)) and a Bernoulli class whose log_prob(x) is log(θ) if x is 1 and log(1 − θ) otherwise. The model is θ = t.sample(Beta(2, 2)) followed by t.observe(Bernoulli(θ), x) for each toss. The weighted mean of θ should approach the conjugate answer (2 + h)/(4 + n).

  2. Why does the effective sample size of likelihood weighting collapse as you add data points?
    Solution

    Each observation multiplies in another likelihood factor, so the posterior becomes more concentrated relative to the prior. The fraction of prior draws that land in the high-posterior region shrinks roughly geometrically, and a few lucky draws take nearly all the weight. Methods that move toward high-probability regions, such as MCMC, or that fit a proposal, such as variational inference, avoid this.

  3. What extra power does a universal PPL such as Church have over BUGS?
    Solution

    It allows models whose structure is itself random: recursion, an unbounded or random number of objects, and control flow that depends on sampled values. BUGS requires a fixed, finite graph declared in advance.29,30

§9Causal inference and causal graphs

Probability tells us what to expect when we observe something. Causation tells us what will happen when we change it.

Pearl describes three rungs of causal reasoning: association (seeing: how does observing X change my belief in Y?), intervention (doing: what happens to Y if I set X?) and counterfactuals (imagining: would Y have happened had X been different?).51,52 A pure correlational learner, however large, stays on the first rung.

Structural causal models and the do-operator

A structural causal model assigns each variable a mechanism Xi := fi(parents, Ui) with independent noise Ui, drawn as a causal DAG. An intervention do(X = x) replaces X's mechanism with the constant x, which deletes every arrow into X. In general P(Y | do(X = x)) ≠ P(Y | X = x): conditioning selects the cases where X happened to be x, along with whatever caused it to be.28,51 Wright's 1921 path diagrams were an early version of the same idea.9

The back-door adjustment

A set of variables Z satisfies the back-door criterion relative to (X, Y) if no node in Z is a descendant of X, and Z blocks every path between X and Y that has an arrow into X. If it does, the interventional distribution can be computed from observational data:28,51

P(y | do(x)) = ∑zP(y | x, z) P(z).(back-door adjustment)

Compare the ordinary conditional, P(y | x) = ∑z P(y | x, z) P(z | x): the only difference is P(z) in place of P(z | x). Adjustment weights each stratum by how common it is in the whole population, not by how common it is among the treated. More general identification problems are handled by Pearl's do-calculus.28

Worked example: Simpson's paradox in kidney-stone treatment

Simpson described how an association can reverse when data are aggregated.53 A well-known real case comes from a 1986 comparison of kidney-stone treatments.54

outputA: naive P(S|T)=0.780   back-door P(S|do(T))=0.833

After adjustment, A has the higher success rate, consistent with the within-group comparison. The data alone cannot settle which table to trust; the causal graph does. If the third variable had instead been caused by the treatment (a mediator), adjusting for it would have been wrong.51,55

Seeing, adjusting and doing in simulation

A simulation lets us check the adjustment against ground truth. The treatment raises recovery by 20 percentage points; severity lowers it and makes treatment more likely.

outputnaive difference +0.048 | back-door adjusted +0.197 | RCT +0.202

The naive comparison sees only a 5-point benefit because treated patients are sicker. The back-door estimate recovers the true effect, as does the simulated randomised trial.

Two frameworks, one problem

Rubin's potential outcomes framework defines a unit's causal effect as Yi(1) − Yi(0), of which only one term is ever observed.19 Its "ignorability given Z" assumption plays the role of the back-door criterion, and the two frameworks agree on the estimands they share.51,55 Learning the graph itself from data, when the graph is unknown, is the subject of causal discovery.56

Exercises

  1. Verify the adjusted success rate for treatment A in the kidney-stone data by hand.
    Solution

    P(small) = (87 + 270)/700 = 0.51, P(large) = 0.49. Adjusted rate = 0.931 × 0.51 + 0.730 × 0.49 ≈ 0.475 + 0.358 = 0.833.

  2. In the DAG Z → X, Z → Y, X → M → Y, which of ∅, {Z}, {M}, {Z, M} satisfy the back-door criterion for the effect of X on Y?
    Solution

    Only {Z}. The empty set leaves the back-door path X ← Z → Y open. Any set containing M fails because M is a descendant of X, and conditioning on it would block part of the very effect we want to measure.

  3. A hospital studies only admitted patients. Admission happens if a patient has disease A or disease B. Explain why A and B may appear negatively correlated in the hospital even if they are independent in the population.
    Solution

    Admission is a collider, A → Admitted ← B. Restricting to admitted patients conditions on it, which opens the path: among the admitted, a patient without A must have B. This is Berkson's bias, the collider pattern of Figure 4, and it is why "adjust for everything" is bad advice.

§10Uncertainty estimation and calibration

A model that says "90% confident" should be right about 90% of the time. Many are not.

Calibration

A probabilistic classifier is calibrated if, among all inputs where it predicts class ŷ with confidence p, the prediction is correct a fraction p of the time:37,57

P(Ŷ = Y | P̂ = p) = p for all p ∈ [0, 1].

Calibration is checked with a reliability diagram: bin predictions by confidence and plot each bin's accuracy against its mean confidence. A calibrated model lies on the diagonal. The expected calibration error summarises the gaps, weighting each of M bins by its share of the n samples:37

ECE = M∑m=1|Bm|n| acc(Bm) − conf(Bm) |.

ECE depends on the binning and can be gamed; it is best reported alongside a strictly proper scoring rule, which is minimised in expectation only by the true probabilities. The Brier score (p − y)2 and the log score −log p(y) (negative log-likelihood) are the standard ones.11,58

Modern networks are overconfident

Guo and colleagues found in 2017 that modern deep networks such as ResNets, though more accurate than older networks, are markedly worse calibrated, typically overconfident. They linked this to greater depth and width, batch normalisation and reduced weight decay.37 Their remedy is temperature scaling: divide the logits z by a single scalar T > 0 fitted by minimising NLL on a validation set,

q̂ = softmax(z / T).

T > 1 softens the distribution. Because one positive scalar cannot change which logit is largest, accuracy is unchanged. It is a one-parameter version of Platt scaling; isotonic regression is a non-parametric alternative.37,43

Estimating epistemic uncertainty in deep learning

Calibration on in-distribution data is not the whole story. A model should also be uncertain on inputs unlike anything it was trained on. Practical approaches include:

  • Bayesian neural networks: put a distribution over weights and average predictions over it.59
  • Monte Carlo dropout: keep dropout on at test time and average several stochastic forward passes, which Gal and Ghahramani showed approximates Bayesian inference in a deep Gaussian process.35
  • Deep ensembles: train several networks from different random initialisations and average them. Simple, parallel, and in large benchmarks one of the most reliable methods.36,60
  • Separating the two kinds: let the network predict a noise variance for aleatoric uncertainty, and use sampling over weights for epistemic uncertainty.5

Under dataset shift, calibration from every method degrades as the shift grows, so a model calibrated on its test set should not be trusted blindly in deployment.60

Conformal prediction

Conformal prediction wraps any model and outputs a set of labels (or an interval) guaranteed to contain the truth with probability at least 1 − α, assuming only that calibration and test data are exchangeable.61,62 The split-conformal recipe is short: compute a nonconformity score si (for example 1 − p̂(yi)) on n held-out points, take q̂ as the ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉ / n empirical quantile, and return every label whose score is at most q̂. The guarantee is marginal (on average over inputs), not conditional on each input.62

Exercises

  1. A model makes four predictions with confidences 0.9, 0.9, 0.6, 0.6; the first is wrong and the rest are right. Using two bins, (0.5, 0.75] and (0.75, 1], compute the ECE.
    Solution

    High bin: accuracy 0.5, confidence 0.9, gap 0.4. Low bin: accuracy 1.0, confidence 0.6, gap 0.4. Each bin holds half the samples, so ECE = 0.5 × 0.4 + 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.4. Note that the model is overconfident in one bin and underconfident in the other.

  2. A forecaster says 80% chance of rain and it rains. What is the Brier score? What would it have been for a forecast of 100%? Of 50%?
    Solution

    (0.8 − 1)2 = 0.04; 0 for 100%; 0.25 for 50%. Lower is better. A single outcome rewards boldness, but averaged over many days only the true probabilities minimise the expected score.

  3. Why can temperature scaling fix ECE without changing accuracy, and what can it not fix?
    Solution

    Dividing all logits by the same T > 0 preserves their order, so the argmax and accuracy are unchanged while confidences shrink or grow. It cannot fix a model whose ranking of classes is wrong, a miscalibration that differs between classes or regions of input space, or overconfidence on shifted or out-of-distribution data.37,60

§11Decision-making under uncertainty

Beliefs matter because they drive actions. Decision theory combines probability with preferences.

Maximum expected utility

von Neumann and Morgenstern showed that preferences over lotteries satisfying a few axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence) can be represented by a utility function U, with lotteries ranked by expected utility.10 Savage extended this to subjective probabilities.63 A rational agent therefore chooses

a* = argmaxa EU(a | e) = argmaxa∑sP(s | a, e) U(s).

People do not always behave this way. Kahneman and Tversky documented systematic departures, such as weighting losses more than equal gains,64 which matters when an AI system models or advises humans. For designing an agent, though, maximum expected utility is the standard normative target.1

Decision networks and the value of information

A decision network (influence diagram) extends a Bayesian network with decision nodes (rectangles) and utility nodes (diamonds).1

The value of perfect information about a variable is how much expected utility would rise if we learned its value before acting:16

VPI(E) = ∑eP(e) maxa EU(a | e) − maxa EU(a).

It is never negative, and it is zero when no possible observation would change the decision.1 An agent should gather information when its value exceeds its cost.

Sequential decisions: Markov decision processes

When actions have lasting consequences, the agent faces a Markov decision process (MDP): states S, actions A, transition probabilities P(s′ | s, a), rewards R(s) and a discount γ. The optimal value function satisfies Bellman's equation:13

V*(s) = R(s) + γ maxa∑s′P(s′ | s, a) V*(s′).

Value iteration applies this equation as an update until it converges. The program solves the umbrella decision, then the 4×3 grid world of Russell and Norvig, where each move succeeds with probability 0.8 and slips sideways otherwise, and each non-terminal step costs 0.04.1

Exercises

  1. With the umbrella utilities of Figure 12 and P(rain) = 0.1, which action maximises expected utility? At what probability of rain are the two actions equally good?
    Solution

    EU(take) = 0.1 × 70 + 0.9 × 80 = 79; EU(leave) = 0.9 × 100 = 90, so leave it. Indifference: 70p + 80(1 − p) = 100(1 − p), so 90p = 20 and p = 2/9 ≈ 0.22.

  2. Explain why the value of perfect information can never be negative.
    Solution

    With the information, the agent can still choose the action that was best without it, in every case, so the expected utility with information is at least as high. Formally, ∑e P(e) maxa EU(a | e) ≥ maxa ∑e P(e) EU(a | e) = maxa EU(a), because a maximum of a sum is at most the sum of maxima.

  3. In decide.py, change the step reward to −2. How does the policy in the bottom-right cell change, and why?
    Solution

    Life is now so costly that the agent heads straight for any terminal state, even the −1 square, because ending the episode quickly is better than wandering. From the bottom-right cell it moves up into the −1 square. The reward function, not only the dynamics, determines the policy.1

§12Choosing a tool

The methods in this article share one engine (represent beliefs as distributions, update them with Bayes' rule, act to maximise expected utility) and differ in what they assume about the problem. The table is a starting point for choosing among them.
