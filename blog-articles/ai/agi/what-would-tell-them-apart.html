Part I · The question

01The disagreement is about diagnosis, not dates

Ask nine researchers when artificial general intelligence arrives and you will get nine dates. Ask them what is missing and you will get something far more useful: nine different diagnoses of the same patient. The scaling hypothesis, the algorithmic-breakthrough hypothesis, the agent hypothesis, the world-model hypothesis, the embodiment hypothesis, the neuro-symbolic hypothesis, the developmental hypothesis, the evolutionary hypothesis and the collective-intelligence hypothesis are not nine forecasts. They are nine claims about what the observed failures of current systems are evidence of.

The question itself is as old as the field. The founding proposal assumed that every aspect of learning and intelligence could be described precisely enough to be simulated, and set a summer to do it;1 the test that framed the discussion for fifty years replaced the question of thinking with a question about indistinguishable behaviour;2 and the first sustained hypothesis about mechanism — that a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action — was stated as an empirical claim, to be confirmed or refuted by construction.3 It has been contested continuously since, by formal limitation results,4 by arguments that symbol manipulation cannot constitute understanding,5 and by a research community that adopted the term ‘artificial general intelligence’ precisely to mark a return to the original question after decades of narrow success.6

That reframing matters because it changes what would count as progress in the argument. A forecast is settled by waiting. A diagnosis is settled by finding the observation that the rival diagnoses do not both predict. Platt called this strong inference and complained, in 1964, that most fields do not practise it: they accumulate results consistent with a favoured hypothesis rather than devising the experiment whose two possible outcomes send you down different branches of a tree.7 The literature on the path to AGI is an unusually pure case of the failure he described. Nearly every result published in the last three years is consistent with all nine positions, and is claimed by advocates of at least four.

The reason is not carelessness. It is that these hypotheses are of different logical types, and most of them are compatible. "Scale is sufficient" and "physical interaction is necessary" are genuine contradictories. "Agents are the route" and "world models are the route" are not: an agent with a world model satisfies both. A large part of what looks like disagreement is a dispute about the ordering of ingredients under a fixed budget, which is a question in the economics of research, not in the theory of mind. Sorting the real contradictions from the apparent ones is most of the work.

There is also a structural obstacle, and it is old. No hypothesis in this field is tested alone. Every test runs a hypothesis together with a benchmark, a training regime, an elicitation method and a scaffold, so a failure can always be assigned to the auxiliary assumptions rather than to the hypothesis under test.8 Lakatos's description of a research programme with a protected hard core and a disposable protective belt fits the field so exactly that one can predict, from any negative result, roughly which belt component will be blamed.9 Meehl's observation about soft psychology applies too: where the theories are weak and the measurements noisy, more data makes it easier, not harder, to find support for whatever you already believe.10

None of that makes the question unanswerable. The demarcation criterion still does work here even if it does not do all of it: a claim earns scientific standing by forbidding something, and a claim that forbids nothing is not thereby false but is certainly not being tested.11 It means the useful question is narrower than "which theory is right". It is: for each of these nine claims, what is the observation that would move it, and has anyone made that observation? This article works through the nine, states each as a falsifiable claim, computes what the claim implies where the implication is computable, and ends with a register of the observations that would actually discriminate. The stake is not only intellectual: safety programmes at frontier laboratories are now explicitly conditioned on assumptions about which route arrives and how continuous the approach is, which makes the diagnosis a planning input rather than a debating point.12

One prior worth having in view: the field's own practitioners do not believe the simplest answer. In the AAAI presidential panel's community survey of 475 researchers, 76 per cent judged it unlikely or very unlikely that scaling current approaches would yield AGI.13 That is a striking number, but note what it is: a vote, not an observation. Expert opinion in this field has a poor calibration record, and aggregate timeline estimates have moved by decades in single survey cycles.14 A show of hands is not a discriminating experiment. It belongs in the article as a fact about the community, not as evidence about the world.

02What is being claimed when generality is claimed

Before asking which route reaches the destination, it is worth checking that the parties agree on the destination. They do not, and several apparent disagreements about the path are disagreements about the goal wearing a disguise.

There are at least four operative definitions in play. The formal definition treats intelligence as expected performance across a weighted distribution of environments, with weights given by algorithmic simplicity; this is elegant, uncomputable, and silent about which environments matter.1516 The efficiency definition treats intelligence as skill-acquisition efficiency: not what a system can do, but how little experience and how few priors it needs to become able to do something new.17 The psychometric definition takes the most validated model of human cognitive structure and asks a system to cover it; the recent operationalisation grounded in Cattell-Horn-Carroll theory scores GPT-4 at 27 per cent and GPT-5 at 58 per cent of a well-educated adult's profile, with long-term memory storage sitting at zero.18 The economic definition, implicit in most industrial usage and explicit in the levels framework, indexes generality to the fraction of valuable tasks performed at a given percentile of human skill.19

These are not variants of one idea. They rank systems differently and they license different hypotheses. Under the economic definition, the agent hypothesis is nearly true by construction: whatever raises the fraction of tasks completed is the route, and memory, tools and feedback demonstrably raise it. Under the efficiency definition, the same systems look unimpressive, because their performance is bought with experience volumes no human requires. Under the psychometric definition, the interesting fact is not the average but the shape: a profile that is expert in some domains and absent in others is exactly what a checklist score conceals and what a person does not have.

Two consequences follow. First, when someone says a benchmark has been solved, ask which definition the benchmark operationalises. The abstraction-and-reasoning corpus was designed against the efficiency definition and explicitly priced its results in cost per task for that reason.20 Benchmarks built for the economic definition score the same systems very differently, and benchmarks in general have a long record of claiming more construct validity than they possess.21 Second, the widely circulated observation that GPT-4 showed "sparks" of generality22 and the equally widely circulated observation that current models are narrower than educated adults18 are both true, because they measure different things. Mitchell's survey of the definitional dispute is the best short treatment of why this recurs: the term carries a century of contested meaning from psychometrics into a field that mostly does not read that literature.23

This article uses the efficiency and psychometric definitions where the hypotheses make claims about mechanism, and the economic definition where they make claims about deployment. Where a hypothesis is only defensible under one of them, that is noted, because it is usually the most informative thing about it.

03The failures every theory is explaining

All nine hypotheses are responses to the same five observations. Stating them precisely is what makes the hypotheses comparable, because a diagnosis is only as good as the symptom it accounts for.

Competence is jagged. Current systems are simultaneously superhuman and subhuman within a single profile, and the boundary is not predictable from the outside. The psychometric operationalisation makes this quantitative: high scores on knowledge and mathematics beside a zero on long-term memory storage.18 Field studies of professionals using these systems find the same shape at the task level, with performance improving on one side of an invisible frontier and degrading on the other.24 The pattern has a mechanistic explanation: models trained to predict text carry systematic sensitivities to task probability and output probability that no amount of instruction removes.25

Generalisation off-distribution is brittle. Systematic recombination of known parts -- the thing a person does without noticing -- remains the reliable failure case.26 The abstraction-and-reasoning corpus was built specifically to make this measurable by generating tasks whose solutions cannot be memorised, and its second edition was calibrated so that every task is solved by at least two humans within two attempts while frontier systems, at release, scored in low single digits.20

Nothing is learned on the job. Weights are frozen at deployment. Worse, the underlying methods do not merely fail to remember; over long sequences of tasks they lose the capacity to learn at all, with binary classification accuracy in a controlled continual setting falling from 89 per cent on an early task to around 77 per cent -- roughly the level of a linear network -- by the two-thousandth.27 This sits on top of the older catastrophic-interference result, which describes forgetting rather than the loss of plasticity itself.2829

Reliability collapses with horizon. The length of task a frontier agent completes has risen exponentially for six years, but the number that rises is the length at which it succeeds half the time.3031 The gap between that and the length at which it succeeds eight times in ten is the whole of the deployment problem, and section 15 shows that gap is larger than any independent-failure model predicts.

Sample efficiency is off by orders of magnitude. A child hears on the order of tens of millions of words before school; frontier pretraining consumes tens of trillions of tokens.32 The BabyLM effort exists to ask what is achievable within a developmentally plausible budget, and the honest summary is that the gap narrows under careful curation but does not close.33 The most striking result in this area used headcam recordings from a single child to learn word-referent mappings from roughly 1 per cent of that child's waking experience, which sharpens the question rather than settling it.34

Every hypothesis in this article is an answer to the question of what these five have in common. That is why they are hard to tell apart: a single set of symptoms admits many consistent diagnoses.

04Necessity, sufficiency, and how to read a plate

The claims come in two logical shapes and the shapes have very different evidential requirements.

A sufficiency claim says that a named ingredient, pushed far enough, is enough. "Scale is sufficient" is the paradigm. Sufficiency claims are confirmed by arrival and refuted only by exhaustion, which means they are nearly unfalsifiable on any human schedule: a believer can always say the scale was not yet large enough. The only tractable falsification is the one Plate 1 names -- a capability that stays flat across enough orders of magnitude that the flatness itself becomes the datum, while some non-scale intervention moves it.

A necessity claim says that without a named ingredient, no amount of anything else suffices. "Physical interaction is necessary" is the paradigm. Necessity claims have the opposite profile: they are refuted by a single existence proof and confirmed only by continued absence. This asymmetry is why the necessity claims in this field turn over faster. The claim that systematic compositional generalisation requires built-in symbols was a reasonable reading of the evidence in 2018;26 a meta-learning regime that produced human-level systematicity in an ordinary sequence model in 2023 is the kind of single result that necessity claims cannot survive.35

Most positions in the debate are stated in the weak form and defended in the strong one. An advocate will assert that world models are necessary, retreat under pressure to the claim that they are helpful, and then treat evidence for helpfulness as evidence for necessity. Each plate below therefore states the claim in the strongest form its advocates actually defend, and names the observation that would refute that form.

Each plate carries a five-cell gauge marking which resources the claim requires: S for scale, A for a new algorithm or architecture, X for experience in the sense of interaction with consequences, R for explicit representational structure, and O for organisation -- plurality, staging, or population. A plate with one filled cell is a bottleneck claim and is easy to test. A plate with four is a research programme and is nearly impossible to test. The closing ledger totals the cells across all twenty-two plates, and the total is itself informative.

One further piece of apparatus. The plates are grouped into five bands by what they say the missing ingredient is: A scale, B method, C the loop between a system and its environment, D the process that produces the system, and E the organisation of the system into parts. The nine named hypotheses distribute unevenly across these bands, and the unevenness is the first substantive finding of the article: three of the nine are claims about the loop, and they are much more closely related to each other than their advocates usually admit.

Part II · Scale

05The scaling hypothesis

The scaling hypothesis holds that the present recipe -- large parameterised models, gradient descent, next-token prediction on broad corpora -- reaches general intelligence given enough compute and data, and that effort spent on conceptual invention is better spent on capacity.

Its canonical statement is not a paper but an essay: the observation that across seven decades of the field, the methods that eventually won were the ones that leveraged computation, and that researchers repeatedly wasted effort building in human knowledge that the learned system later discovered better on its own.36 The empirical backbone arrived separately, in the finding that generalisation error falls as a power law in model size, dataset size and compute across many orders of magnitude,3738 refined by the compute-optimal allocation result that corrected the earlier recommendation and roughly equalised the growth rates of parameters and tokens.39 The capability evidence arrived alongside: a model trained only to predict text performing tasks it was never trained on, from a description and a few examples.40

Two things should be said in the hypothesis's favour before its problems. First, it has been right about the direction of nearly every argument it has had. The architectures that scaled displaced the ones that encoded structure,41 and the components that mattered -- gradient learning through composed layers,42 attention as a general mixing primitive41 -- are notable for how little they assume. The record is also broader than language: the same recipe of large models, large data and search produced superhuman Go from human games and then without them,4344 protein structure prediction at experimental accuracy,45 and a language model whose professional-examination performance was the proximate cause of the current wave of AGI discussion.46 Second, it is the only hypothesis in this article with a quantitative predictive instrument. Its rivals offer arguments; it offers a curve that lets an organisation commit a nine-figure budget to a prediction about a number it has not yet measured.

The problems are equally real, and they are of three kinds. There is a question about what the curve promises, which is arithmetic and is settled in the next section. There is a question about whether the inputs the curve consumes exist in the required quantity, which is section 7. And there is a question about the relation between the quantity the curve predicts -- cross-entropy on a corpus -- and the quantity anyone cares about, which is the oldest complaint against the position and the one least amenable to computation. Sceptics hold that some capabilities appear discontinuously at scale, which would break planning;47 the reply is that the discontinuities are artefacts of discontinuous metrics and vanish when the metric is made continuous.48 That exchange is worth studying as a model of the field's evidential difficulties: both sides are analysing the same runs.

06The arithmetic of a scaling curve

Take the compute-optimal fit at face value and ask what it promises. Under the published parametric form -- an irreducible term of 1.69 nats per token plus terms falling as a power of parameters and of tokens39 -- allocating compute optimally at each budget produces the frontier in the figure below.

Three numbers matter. The first is that each order of magnitude of compute removes a constant 29.8 per cent of the remaining reducible loss. This is what a power law looks like from the inside: not diminishing returns in the loose sense, but returns that are exactly proportional to what is left. It is a good deal or a bad one depending entirely on how much of what remains you need.

The second is that there is not much left. At 1028 FLOP -- two orders of magnitude beyond the current frontier -- the irreducible term accounts for 96.9 per cent of total loss. The entire reducible remainder is 0.054 nats. Everything the scaling hypothesis promises past that point must be extracted from a residue smaller than 3 per cent of the quantity being measured.

The third is the exchange rate. Halving whatever reducible loss remains costs 1.96 orders of magnitude of compute -- a factor of 91. At the 4- to 5-fold annual growth in frontier training compute that has held since 2010,4950 that is 3.3 years at 4x and 2.8 years at 5x. Per halving. The scaling hypothesis is not refuted by this arithmetic; it is priced by it. A believer should be comfortable saying that two or three further halvings of the reducible loss produce general intelligence, and should notice that this is a decade of compute growth under assumptions that Epoch's own analysis of power, capital and fabrication treats as the optimistic case.51

The deeper issue is what the quantity being halved measures. Cross-entropy on web text is the average surprise of a corpus, and the corpus is a sample of what people have written down. The scaling hypothesis's strongest form requires that the residual 3 per cent contains the structure whose absence produces jagged profiles, brittle recombination and horizon collapse. Nothing in the curve says so. It is compatible with the residual containing exactly that, and equally compatible with the residual being the irreducible noise of human text production -- typos, arbitrary word choices, the coin flips of composition -- in which case the curve is asymptoting to the entropy of a channel and telling us nothing about generality at all.

This is the single most important gap in the scaling literature, and it is not closed by pointing at benchmarks, because the benchmark-versus-loss relation is precisely the disputed mapping. Plate 2 states the claim in the form its advocates defend and names what would break it.

07The data wall and what lies past it

The curve consumes two inputs. Compute is manufactured; text is not. Epoch's estimate of the effective stock of public human-generated text, adjusted for quality and repetition, is about 300 trillion tokens, with the projection that current development trends fully utilise it somewhere between 2026 and 2032 and a median around 2028.52

Running that stock against the compute-optimal token demand computed above gives a specific crossing point.

A compute-optimal run exhausts the stock at 1027.4 FLOP -- 2.3 years from a 1026 frontier at 4x annual growth, 2.0 years at 5x. That is the generous case. Nobody trains compute-optimally any more, because inference cost dominates deployment economics and the rational allocation over a model's lifetime is to overtrain a smaller model on more tokens.53 Five-fold overtraining moves the crossing to 1026.1 FLOP, which is to say: approximately now.

What follows is less dramatic than the phrase "data wall" suggests, and more interesting. The wall does not stop the curve; it changes which term is binding. Past it, additional compute must go into parameters against a fixed token budget, and the token term stops falling. The arithmetic says the loss at the compute-optimal wall is 1.756, and the whole remaining prize between there and 1030 FLOP is 0.040 nats -- 2.26 per cent of the loss at the wall.

Three exits are on offer and each has an evidential status worth stating precisely. Repetition works, but with measured decay: multiple epochs recover much of the value of fresh data for a few passes and then stop.54 Synthetic data is the most-invoked exit and the least-established; the demonstrated failure mode, where models trained on recursively generated output progressively lose the tails of the distribution, is real but was demonstrated under conditions -- full replacement of real data, no filtering -- that no serious lab operates under.55 The exit that has actually delivered is the third: post-training on verifiable rewards, where the data is generated by the model and scored by something that is not the model.5657 That is not more text. It is a different kind of signal, and its arrival inside the scaling programme is the most substantive concession the programme has made to its critics -- because the thing supplying the signal is a checker, an environment, or a person, which is to say: not scale.

08The second axis, and what fell in eighteen months

Between 2024 and 2026 the field acquired a second scaling axis. Models trained to produce long deliberation traces before answering, optimised against verifiable rewards, converted inference compute into accuracy at rates that pretraining could not match.5856 The underlying observations were simple and had been available: chains of intermediate steps improve reasoning performance,59 repeated sampling raises the chance that at least one sample is correct,60 and a fixed inference budget is often better spent on search than on a larger model.61

The consequence for this article is a single striking datum. The abstraction-and-reasoning corpus was designed to resist exactly the scaling hypothesis: tasks generated so that memorisation cannot help, calibrated against human performance, with a second edition released in 2025 specifically because the first had begun to yield.176220 At release in May 2025, frontier reasoning systems scored around 3 per cent on the second edition, and its authors noted that scores below 5 per cent are not meaningfully distinguishable from noise.20 By early 2026 the leading verified score was in the mid-fifties; by February 2026 a deliberation-heavy system was above 84 per cent, and code-evolution scaffolds pushed a strong model from 88.1 to 95.1 per cent.63 Public leaderboard tracking through mid-2026 places several frontier systems above 90 per cent -- on a task set every item of which was solved by at least two humans within two attempts.20

Read carelessly, that is a total victory for scale. Read carefully, it is not, and the reasons are instructive. The benchmark fell to test-time adaptation and search, not to a larger pretraining run -- the same conclusion its authors drew from the first edition, where they observed that log-linear scaling alone was insufficient and that new test-time methods were required.20 The efficiency metric the benchmark introduced precisely to prevent this reading shows systems of similar accuracy separated by orders of magnitude in cost per task. And the response of the benchmark's authors was to build a third edition that is not a static puzzle set at all, but an interactive environment in which an agent must acquire goals on the fly, build a model of an unfamiliar world, and learn continuously across a session.64

That move is the most honest thing in the recent literature. It concedes that the previous target was passable by means its designers did not intend, and it relocates the target onto the axis where the disagreement now lives: not whether a system can solve a hard problem given a description, but whether it can work out what the problem is by acting.

09The verifier ceiling

Test-time compute converts a generation problem into a selection problem. That conversion has a cost, and it is computable.

Two results. The first is that accuracy has an interior maximum. With a false-accept rate of 5 per cent the optimum is at k = 89 and beyond that more samples make the system worse, because the pool of plausible-but-wrong candidates grows faster than the pool of right ones. With a 20 per cent false-accept rate the optimum falls to k = 69 and the achievable accuracy is 0.682, against an oracle pass@128 of 1.000. The gap between those two numbers is the entire content of the phrase "we have a verifier problem".

The second result is that the ceiling has a closed form. As k grows, the probability the selected answer is correct converges to

p / (p + (1 − p) f)

which is the verifier's precision at the generator's base rate. The lab confirms this to four decimal places against the exact finite-k computation. It is Bayes's theorem wearing an engineering hat, and it says something the pass@k literature routinely obscures: inference compute does not move the ceiling at all. The ceiling moves only when the verifier improves. Everything spent past the peak is spent making the selection problem harder.

This has been observed empirically -- resampling against imperfect verifiers produces gains that flatten and then reverse65 -- and it explains the shape of the field's successes. Where the checker is exact, the method is transformative: formal proof assistants, unit tests, competitive-programming judges, the abstraction corpus's exact-match grid. Where the checker is a learned reward model or another instance of the same model, gains saturate early, which is why process supervision -- scoring the steps rather than the answer -- was such a productive line.6667

The strategic implication is uncomfortable for the scaling position and for the agent position alike. The economy is mostly made of tasks with no cheap checker. For those tasks the second scaling axis delivers the precision of whatever judge you can build, and no more.

Part III · Method

10The algorithmic-breakthrough hypothesis

The algorithmic-breakthrough hypothesis holds that the current recipe is not merely expensive but structurally incapable, and that some architecture or learning rule not yet invented is required.

Its strongest form comes from an unexpected direction. The author of the bitter lesson has spent the past year arguing that large language models are a dead end -- not because scale is the wrong lever, but because the field scaled the wrong object.68 The argument is precise: a model trained to predict what a person would say is doing imitation, supervised on a fixed corpus, and there is no definition within that framework of what makes an action good. Learning happens in a special phase and then stops, while the vast majority of the system's lifetime compute is spent in deployment contributing nothing to the weights. The positive programme, set out with Silver, divides the field's history into an era of simulation, an era of human data, and an era of experience, and argues that the transition to the third is forced by the exhaustion of the second.69 It sits on a long-standing position that reward maximisation in a rich environment is enough to produce the full range of intelligent behaviour.7071

The other strong form comes from LeCun, whose objection is representational rather than motivational: autoregressive generation over discrete tokens is the wrong substrate for prediction under uncertainty, and the alternative is a joint-embedding architecture that predicts in a learned abstract space and discards what is unpredictable.72 That position has now been capitalised: LeCun left Meta in November 2025 after twelve years and raised over a billion dollars for a company built explicitly around world models rather than language models.73

The hypothesis has an obvious methodological weakness. In the weak form -- "something new will be needed" -- it forbids nothing and is compatible with every possible future. It becomes testable only in its specific versions, and its specific versions carry the ordinary risks: continual learning may turn out to be achievable within the current substrate by better optimisation rather than by a new rule, and joint-embedding prediction may turn out to be an efficiency improvement rather than a change of kind.

It also has an underrated strength, visible in the historical record. The advances that changed the exponent rather than the constant were nearly all methodological: gradient learning through deep composition,42 attention,41 preference-based post-training,74 and verifiable-reward post-training.56 Each was cheap to demonstrate at small scale and each unlocked scale that was previously unproductive. That is the pattern the hypothesis predicts, and it is not the pattern the strong scaling position predicts.

11What has actually counted as a breakthrough

It is worth being disciplined about what the two positions are actually disputing, because the historical evidence is usually deployed loosely by both.

The scaling reading of history is that every apparent breakthrough was a scaling enabler: convolution, attention and residual connections mattered because they made larger models trainable, not because they encoded knowledge. On this reading, methods are the plumbing and compute is the water. The breakthrough reading is that this concedes the point -- if the plumbing determines whether the water does anything, then the plumbing is the binding constraint, and the fact that it has been solved four or five times in seventy years is a reason to expect it to bind again.

Both readings fit the record. What distinguishes them is a prediction about the form of the next advance, and this is genuinely discriminating. The scaling position predicts that the next capability jump arrives with a training run and is announced as a model. The breakthrough position predicts that it arrives with a method, is reproducible at small scale before it is reproduced at large scale, and is legible as an idea. The 2024-2026 reasoning-model transition is a test case that neither side can claim cleanly: reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards is a method, it was demonstrated to work at modest scale and reproduced openly within months,5657 and it required frontier-scale base models to be worth doing.

Note also that the interesting disputes about current model capability are unresolved in a way that should induce caution about strong claims in either direction. A widely discussed 2025 result reported that reasoning models collapse beyond a complexity threshold on controlled puzzle families;75 the rebuttal argued that several of the collapses were artefacts of output-token limits and of including provably unsolvable instances.76 Whatever the resolution, the episode is a clean instance of Duhem's problem: the same runs support opposite conclusions about the hypothesis under test depending on assumptions about the harness.

One structural point deserves more weight than it usually gets. There is no free lunch: averaged over all possible problems, no search or learning procedure beats any other.77 Any claim that a particular architecture is generally superior is therefore implicitly a claim about the distribution of problems this universe presents. The scaling hypothesis and the breakthrough hypothesis are both, at bottom, claims about that distribution -- one holding that it is well matched by capacity and compression, the other that it is not. Neither side usually states it that way, and the disagreement would be clearer if they did.

12The neuro-symbolic hypothesis

The neuro-symbolic hypothesis holds that learned representations must be combined with explicit structured reasoning -- variables, bindings, rules, constraints -- because certain properties of thought are not obtainable by function approximation over vectors.

The classical argument is systematicity: cognitive capacities come in families closed under recombination, so a system that understands one arrangement of parts thereby understands its rearrangements, and a representational scheme without constituent structure has to learn each arrangement separately.78 The modern restatements are the demand for robust variable binding and world knowledge alongside learning,79 the argument that human-like learning requires compositional, causal and intuitive-physical structure,80 and the substantial engineering literature on hybrid architectures.818283 Empirical support arrived through recombination benchmarks that sequence models failed and humans passed immediately.26 The position also has unusual survey support: in the AAAI panel's community survey, more than 60 per cent of respondents held that a system approaching human-like reasoning would need to be at least half symbolic.13

Then came the result that necessity claims cannot survive. Training an ordinary sequence model with a meta-learning regime over a stream of compositional tasks produced systematic generalisation matching or exceeding human performance on the same tests that had grounded the original argument.35 The symbols were not built in; the training distribution supplied the pressure and the network supplied the competence. This does not refute the hybrid engineering programme, which was never only about systematicity. It does refute the strong form of Plate 6, and it should be treated as a live demonstration of how the strong forms in this debate tend to fail: not by accumulated contrary evidence, but by one construction that the argument had not considered.

A second necessity claim in this family is stronger and has survived. Where an answer must be certified rather than estimated -- a proof, a plan that provably respects constraints, a configuration that provably satisfies a specification -- something outside the network must do the checking, because a network's confidence is not a guarantee.8485 That claim is about epistemic status rather than capability, and it does not dissolve when models improve.

13What constraints buy, and what they inherit

The engineering case for symbolic structure is usually made qualitatively. It can be made exactly, on a problem small enough to enumerate.

Placing eight non-attacking queens has 16,777,216 candidate placements with one queen per column, of which 92 are legal. A blind sampler therefore needs 182,361 draws in expectation to find one legal board. A backtracking search that consults the constraints while building enumerates all 92 in 2,057 node expansions. And checking whether a given board is legal costs 28 pairwise comparisons.

Those three numbers are the whole neuro-symbolic argument in miniature. Generation is 6,513 times harder than verification. Constrained search is 89 times cheaper per solution than sampling, and the ratio grows with problem size: 305-fold at six queens, 281,381-fold at ten. A system that can propose but not prune pays the generation cost; a system that can prune pays the search cost; a system that can only check pays neither and solves nothing. Hybrids exist to sit in the middle.

The counter-argument is equally computable, and it is the part the hybrid literature under-reports. Run the same search with an incomplete formalisation -- diagonals omitted from the constraint model -- and it expands 109,601 nodes and returns 40,320 "solutions", of which 92 are legal: 0.23 per cent.

That second search is exhaustive, sound with respect to its own axioms, fully interpretable, and wrong. The symbolic layer does not manufacture correctness; it inherits correctness from a formalisation that someone had to get right. Moving the burden is often an excellent trade -- formalisations are reviewable and networks are not -- but it is a move, not a discharge.

The recent history of automated mathematics is the best available test, and it cuts both ways in a way that should be stated carefully. In 2024 a hybrid system solved four of six International Mathematical Olympiad problems for 28 of 42 points, one point below the gold threshold, with every solution machine-checked in a formal proof assistant -- and with the non-geometry problems manually formalised into Lean by human experts after the competition released them.8687 In 2025 a general-purpose model with extended deliberation reached the gold-medal standard reading the problems in natural language, within the competition's own time limits.88

The scaling reading is that the symbolic apparatus was scaffolding and was discarded within a year. The neuro-symbolic reading is that the two systems produced different kinds of output: one produced proofs a machine certified, the other produced arguments humans graded. Both readings are correct, and they are answers to different questions. The first is about capability; the second is about warrant. Confusing them is the most common error in this corner of the debate, in both directions.

14The agent hypothesis

The agent hypothesis holds that generality is a property of a system rather than of a network: a foundation model supplies world knowledge and language, and memory, tools, planning and feedback supply what the weights lack.

It is the hypothesis with the most engineering behind it and the least evidential discipline. The components are well studied individually -- interleaving reasoning with actions,89 verbal self-critique across attempts,90 learned tool invocation,91 persistent skill libraries in open-ended environments,92 and long-lived memory streams in simulated societies.93 Benchmarks exist that specifically require the composition -- tasks that are conceptually simple for a person but need browsing, file handling and multi-step execution, on which humans score far above unassisted models.94

The case in its favour is straightforward: the same weights, given a better scaffold, complete substantially longer tasks, and the improvement is measurable on the same instrument that measures model improvement.95 If generality is indexed economically, this is close to decisive.

The case against is methodological, and it is Plate 8's tell. The hypothesis is compatible with every observation. If a scaffolded system improves, that confirms it. If a bare model improves, the scaffold is said to have been internalised. If a scaffold fails, some component was missing. A hypothesis with no forbidden observation is a description of engineering practice, not a theory of what is missing. It becomes a real claim only when it says which component is doing the work and how much -- and the honest answer, from the measurement literature, is that memory and feedback contribute much less than tool access and that the contributions shrink as base models improve.

There is one version of the agent hypothesis that is sharp, and it is worth separating out. If the missing ingredient is memory in the sense of storage that changes what the system can do, then the psychometric finding that current systems score zero on long-term memory storage while scoring highly elsewhere is a precise statement of the gap.18 That claim forbids something: it says a retrieval index bolted to a frozen model will not close the gap, because retrieval changes what is in the context and not what the network has learned to do. That is Plate 9, and unlike Plate 8 it can be wrong.

15Horizon is reliability, compounded

The agent literature reports horizons. It should report reliability, because under any model in which a long task presents independent opportunities to fail, the two are the same quantity and the second is the one that can be improved.

Take a task of human-length T presenting one failure opportunity per human-minute, each survived with probability p. The length completed with probability r is log r / log p steps. The consequence is immediate and is plotted below.

The ratio between the 80 per cent horizon and the 50 per cent horizon is log 0.8 / log 0.5 = 0.3219, for every value of p. It does not depend on model quality, scale, architecture or training. Under proportional compounding, a system that does four-hour tasks half the time does 77-minute tasks eight times in ten, and no amount of scaling changes the fraction.

Measured horizons do not match. Reported figures put the 80 per cent horizon at roughly a tenth of the 50 per cent horizon for one early-2026 frontier model and around a fifth for a mid-2026 one, against the 0.32 that independence predicts.95 Fitting n(T) = cTk to those ratios gives k = 0.487 and 0.691 respectively. Sub-linear k means the shortfall at high reliability is worse than proportional compounding predicts: the failure modes on long tasks are not a longer sequence of the same small failures, but a different and heavier-tailed population of them. That is a substantive finding about what long-horizon work actually demands, and it is exactly what the agent hypothesis's strong form needs to explain.

The extrapolation is worth doing carefully because it is done carelessly so often.

Taking the published post-2023 doubling time of 131 days and a February 2026 anchor, a 50 per cent horizon of one working month (167 hours) arrives 498 days out; the 80 per cent horizon of the same length arrives 938 days out.31 At the all-time 196-day doubling the figures are 745 and 1,404 days. Two caveats belong with those numbers and are usually dropped. First, the measuring organisation states plainly that its own measurements above 16 hours are unreliable with the current task suite, which puts the extrapolation's starting point close to the edge of the instrument.95 Second, the task distribution is software, machine learning and cybersecurity, and horizons vary by orders of magnitude across domains.96

The reliability requirement is the number that should sober everyone. For 80 per cent success on a task of one working month at one failure opportunity per minute, per-step reliability must reach 0.99997773 -- one failure per 44,904 steps. That is not a scaling target or a scaffolding target. It is a specification, and no current system is within two orders of magnitude of it.

16The world-model hypothesis

The world-model hypothesis holds that intelligence depends on an internal predictive model of the environment: a system that can simulate how the world evolves and how its own actions change it, and can therefore evaluate options it has not tried.

The idea is older than the field. Craik's proposal that the nervous system carries a small-scale model of external reality is from 1943;97 Tolman's cognitive maps, demonstrated by latent learning in rats that had never been rewarded, are from 1948.98 The modern computational line runs from compressed latent dynamics models used for policy learning,99 through planning in a learned latent space that reached state of the art across Atari, Go, chess and shogi without being given the rules,100 to general model-based agents trained across many domains with fixed hyperparameters.101

The strong contemporary version is LeCun's: autoregressive prediction over tokens is the wrong substrate, and prediction should happen in a learned representation space where unpredictable detail can be discarded rather than modelled.72 The joint-embedding programme has produced image and video systems that learn representations by predicting in latent space,102103 and the position is now backed by a research organisation founded to pursue it.73

Running alongside is a generative line that treats a world model as something you can enter: interactive environments generated from unlabelled video,104 diffusion models operating as real-time game engines,105 and general-purpose world models that render navigable environments from a text prompt at 24 frames per second and 720p, holding consistency for a few minutes.106 The stated ambition is explicit -- unlimited curricula of rich simulated environments in which agents can be trained -- which makes this line a supplier to the agent, developmental and evolutionary hypotheses simultaneously.

Two questions decide whether the hypothesis is doing work. The first is whether accurate prediction implies a usable model, which is empirical and is section 17. The second is whether the model needs to be explicit at all: a sufficiently good policy behaves as though it had a model, and the claim that the model must be a separable component is exactly the kind of structural commitment the scaling position has repeatedly won against.

17Rollout error, and why simulated worlds are short

A model used for planning is used autoregressively: its own output becomes its next input. Errors therefore compound, and the compounding has a shape.

Let per-step error be eps and let each step amplify existing error by a factor lambda. Accumulated error after H steps is eps(lambdaH − 1)/(lambda − 1), which is linear when lambda = 1 and exponential otherwise.

At a per-step error of 10−4, a non-amplifying model stays usable for 1,000 steps. At lambda = 1.01 that falls to 241; at lambda = 1.05, to 81. The important feature is what happens when the model gets better: with 5 per cent amplification, driving per-step error from 10−2 to 10−6 -- four orders of magnitude of model improvement -- moves the usable horizon from 9 steps to 175. Under amplification, horizon grows logarithmically in model quality. This is the quantitative form of the well-known result that model-based methods must limit rollout length, and that short rollouts from many start states beat long rollouts from few.107

Apply it to interactive generation. Two minutes at 24 frames per second is 2,880 autoregressive steps. Holding accumulated drift below 0.1 requires per-frame error under 3.5 × 10−5 with no amplification, and under 10−12 with 1 per cent amplification. That is why the state of the art in generated interactive worlds is measured in minutes rather than hours, and why its own documentation lists limited action space, multi-agent interaction and long-horizon consistency as the open problems.106 The limitation is not a missing engineering trick; it is the arithmetic of autoregression.

The second problem is deeper. Prediction accuracy does not entail a model that generalises. The sharpest demonstration adapts a transformer trained on orbital trajectories to predict forces: the model predicts trajectories excellently, and the force law recovered by symbolic regression from its predictions is nonsense.108 The same group's earlier work found that a model producing near-perfect turn-by-turn directions had an implicit map containing streets that do not exist.109 The inductive-bias probe is the right instrument precisely because it sidesteps the interpretation problem with linear probes: it asks how the model learns when adapted, not what can be decoded from it.

Against this sits genuine positive evidence: a sequence model trained only on legal Othello moves develops a decodable and causally manipulable board representation.110 The two literatures are not in contradiction. They say that next-token training sometimes recovers the generating structure and sometimes recovers a bundle of heuristics that mimics it, and that from the outside these are indistinguishable by accuracy alone. That is a strong argument for the world-model hypothesis as a research programme and a weak one for it as a claim about what current systems already have.

18The embodiment hypothesis

The embodiment hypothesis holds that physical interaction with the world is necessary for intelligence of sufficient generality: that representation without sensorimotor contact is ungrounded, and that the competences we find hardest to build are precisely the ones bodies deliver.

The classical statements are Brooks's -- that intelligence is grounded in situated activity rather than in symbolic reasoning about it, and that the abstraction of perception and action out of the problem removes the hard part.111112 The developmental version holds that concepts are assembled out of action, and that the infant's body is what makes the learning problem tractable.113114 The philosophical version is the symbol grounding problem: a system whose symbols are defined only by other symbols has no way to fix reference.115 Its sharpest empirical anchor remains the paired-kitten experiment, in which one kitten moved and the other was carried through identical visual experience, and only the active one developed normal visually guided behaviour.116

There is also an economic argument, older than any of these: the hard problems are the ones evolution spent the most time on. Perception and mobility consume the vast bulk of biological optimisation and are the parts machines find hardest, while the reasoning we consider difficult is recent and cheap.117 Progress since 2023 has not overturned this. Frontier language models pass professional examinations while general-purpose manipulation remains a research problem, pursued with vision-language-action models trained on cross-embodiment corpora,118119120 all of which report open-world generalisation as the objective rather than the achievement.

The engineering line the hypothesis inspired is real and has advanced: end-to-end training of perception and control as one policy replaced the hand-partitioned pipeline a decade ago.121 The problem for the hypothesis is data, and it is severe: robot experience is collected in real time, on hardware, under supervision, and cannot be scraped.122 Simulation with randomised dynamics is the standard mitigation123 and is itself an argument that what is needed is interaction, not flesh.

The contemporary form of the linguistic dispute is worth stating precisely because it is often caricatured. The claim that a system trained on form alone cannot acquire meaning is an argument about what the training signal makes available, not a claim that current systems are useless.124 The reply is that reference is not the only route to meaning, and that conceptual role within a sufficiently rich distribution may suffice.125 Neither side has produced the discriminating experiment, which would have to hold distributional coverage fixed and vary grounding.

19What intervention buys that observation cannot

Most of the arguments in this article are inductions from research history. One is a theorem, and it deserves to be separated from the rest.

Consider three-variable systems. The chain X → Y → Z, the reversed chain X ← Y ← Z, and the fork X ← Y → Z imply exactly the same conditional independence: X and Z are dependent, and independent given Y. No amount of passive data distinguishes them, because there is nothing left in the observational distribution to look at.126127128

Exhaustive enumeration puts numbers on how common this is. Of the 25 labelled directed acyclic graphs on three variables, only 11 distinct observational signatures exist; 21 of the 25 graphs (84.0 per cent) share their signature with at least one other, and the largest observationally identical class contains 6 graphs. On four variables it is worse: 543 graphs, 185 signatures, 484 graphs (89.1 per cent) observationally ambiguous, and a largest class of 24. There are 1,212 pairs of four-variable graphs that no observational dataset can separate.

Every one of those 1,212 pairs is separated by a single intervention on one variable. Not two, not a designed sequence -- one, in every case, at both problem sizes tested. Interventional data is not incrementally more informative than observational data; it answers a question observation cannot pose.129130

This is the strongest formal result in the entire debate, and it is routinely misapplied. It is standardly deployed as an argument for robots. It is not one. What the theorem requires is intervention -- setting a variable and observing the consequence -- and bodies are one supplier among many. An A/B test is an intervention. Executing code and reading the error is an intervention. Calling a tool and receiving a result is an intervention. Training against a verifiable reward is a structured programme of interventions, which is a substantial part of why that method worked.56 Conversely, a robot operating from a fixed teleoperated demonstration corpus is doing imitation learning and gets no interventional benefit at all.

The correct reading is therefore that the theorem supports the experience family -- the shared core of the agent, world-model and embodiment hypotheses -- and is silent between its members. It also explains why so much text data is worth less than its volume suggests: descriptions of interventions others performed carry the results but not the counterfactual contrast, and are only as trustworthy as the reporting.

What the theorem does not establish is how much this binds in practice. Human scientific knowledge is largely transmitted rather than personally verified, and a corpus recording the outcomes of a few centuries of deliberate experimentation is a genuinely interventional record, filtered through prose. Quantifying that transfer is the open empirical question, and nobody has done it.

20The developmental hypothesis

The developmental hypothesis holds that general intelligence must be grown rather than trained: acquired progressively, in an order, with each stage building on representations the previous one fixed.

The computational evidence is old and specific. A recurrent network that failed to learn a complex grammar when given the full corpus learned it when its working memory was restricted early and relaxed over training -- the limitation was the enabling condition, not a handicap.131 The generalised version, presenting easier examples first, was shown to speed convergence and improve final quality on a range of tasks.132 Developmental psychology contributes the observation that the infant's own changing body and attention generate the ordering, so the curriculum is not imposed but produced by the learner's trajectory through its environment.133134

The programme's most disciplined instantiation asks what can be learned within a developmentally plausible data budget -- roughly the tens of millions of words a child hears before school, rather than the tens of trillions of tokens a frontier model consumes.3233 The most striking single results come from headcam recordings of one child's experience, which suffice to learn word-referent mappings and reasonable high-level visual representations without strong architectural priors.34135 That last qualification matters: the child-perspective results are frequently cited as support for core knowledge, and they are closer to evidence against it, since general architectures on child-scale data did much of the work.

The hypothesis divides into two claims that are usually run together. One is about ordering: that the sequence carries information. The other is about priors: that development starts from structured core systems for objects, agents, number and space.136 These are separable, they have different failure modes, and the evidence for them is very different in quality. The next section takes the ordering claim apart, because it is the one that can be computed exactly.

21Ordering as a claim about the target

Consider identifying a conjunctive concept over d binary features from positive examples. A tidy curriculum presents one feature at a time and needs d examples. Random experience needs the expected maximum of d geometric variables.

Random wins, and the margin grows. At d = 4 random needs 3.5 examples against the curriculum's 4; at d = 12 it needs 4.98 against 12. The reason is that an unordered example resolves several features at once, and the tidy schedule throws that incidental information away. Ordering is not free: on a decomposable target with a learner that can use everything it sees, structure in the presentation is a cost.

Now impose the condition Elman's experiment actually created -- a learner whose window is smaller than the target. A monolithic learner over d features searches 3d conjunctions; a staged learner fixing w = 4 features per stage searches (d/w) × 3w. At d = 20 that is 3,486,784,401 against 405: a reduction of 8.6 million-fold. Staging converts a product into a sum.

The conditional is the finding. Staging wins if and only if the target decomposes along the staging axis. If it does not -- if the target is, say, a parity over all d features -- then no stage can express it, the staged learner searches d/w subspaces and finds it in none of them, and the reduction is a reduction to zero. The developmental hypothesis, stated precisely, is therefore not a claim about learners at all. It is a claim about the structure of the thing being learned: that general competence is hierarchically decomposable in a way that permits staged acquisition. Everyone in the debate has an opinion about that, and nobody has evidence about it independent of the learning results themselves.

There is one asymmetry that favours the developmental camp and it comes from an unexpected direction. Whatever the merits of ordering, the capacity to keep learning is not currently available. Standard methods lose plasticity over long task sequences, with binary classification accuracy in a controlled setting falling from 89 per cent to roughly 77 per cent -- about the level of a linear network -- by the two-thousandth task, and specific interventions restoring it.27 A hypothesis that says competence must be grown over a long trajectory is, at minimum, pointing at a real defect in the tooling. That is Plate 17, and it is the most concrete thing in this band.

22The evolutionary hypothesis

The evolutionary hypothesis holds that general intelligence is the product of a search process operating over long timescales with no fixed objective, and that the productive route is to run such a process rather than to design the artefact.

Its technical lineage is substantial: genetic algorithms,137 genetic programming,138 the evolution of network topologies alongside weights,139 and the demonstration that abandoning the objective in favour of novelty search solves deceptive problems that objective-driven search cannot.140 The modern statement is Clune's: rather than hand-designing the pieces of an intelligent system, build an algorithm that generates architectures, learning algorithms and the learning environments themselves, and let the three co-evolve.141 The related argument holds that open-endedness -- a process that keeps producing novel, learnable challenges -- is a precondition for artificial superhuman intelligence rather than a curiosity.142

The instrumental results are real. Regularised evolution produced competitive image architectures;143 AutoML-Zero rediscovered backpropagation-like learning algorithms from arithmetic primitives;144 evolution strategies proved a scalable, parallelisable alternative to policy-gradient reinforcement learning.145

Two things are worth noticing about how this hypothesis relates to its rivals. First, it is the most aggressive scaling hypothesis of all: it proposes to spend compute on an outer loop whose every evaluation is an inner training run. Second, its recent successes have quietly abandoned blind variation. The system that improved on Strassen's 1969 algorithm found a way to multiply two 4×4 complex matrices in 48 scalar multiplications -- the first improvement in that setting in 56 years -- and it did so by using a large language model as the mutation operator over programs, with automated evaluators as the selection pressure.146 Applied to over fifty open mathematical problems it rediscovered the state of the art in roughly 75 per cent of cases and improved on it in about 20 per cent.146 Its predecessor did the same with a narrower scope.147

That is evolution with an extremely informed proposal distribution -- which is to say, evolution that has conceded the scaling and algorithmic premises and is using them as its variation operator. It is a powerful method. It is not an argument that blind search over learners is the road.

23Evolution priced in FLOP

The evolutionary hypothesis makes a compute claim whether or not its advocates state it as one. It is worth computing.

Start with the information rate. A backward pass yields all d partial derivatives for about twice the cost of a forward pass. A zeroth-order or finite-difference estimate yields one directional derivative per evaluation, so recovering equivalent information at 109 parameters costs on the order of 5 × 108 times as much.148 Evolution strategies mitigate this with parallelism and low-dimensional effective search, and their own headline result was wall-clock scalability rather than sample efficiency.145 The gradient's advantage over blind variation is not a matter of taste; it is a dimension-scaling factor.

Now price the outer loop, which is where the hypothesis actually lives -- searching over learners, not weights. A population of 100 evaluated over 1,000 generations, each evaluation costing 1020 FLOP (a small proxy training run, six orders of magnitude below the current frontier), totals 1025 FLOP. That is a frontier-scale budget spent entirely on the outer loop, with every inner run too small to be informative about frontier behaviour. This is the structural difficulty with architecture search at the frontier and the reason it is not done there.

Finally, the anchor. Order-of-magnitude estimates put the compute of animal evolution at around 1041 FLOP.149 From a 1026 frontier, closing fifteen orders of magnitude takes 24.9 years at 4x annual growth, 21.5 at 5x, and 15.0 at an implausible 10x -- and 4- to 5-fold growth is already the optimistic reading, bounded well before then by power, capital and fabrication.51

Two honest qualifications. The anchor covers the whole search including every organism's lifetime of experience, so as a target for an outer loop alone it is an upper bound on the brute-force route and says nothing about a targeted one. And evolution was not optimising for intelligence, which makes it a spectacularly inefficient reference process. The correct conclusion is not that the evolutionary hypothesis is refuted. It is that its unguided form is priced out of this century's compute, and its guided form -- an intelligent proposal distribution under automated selection -- is already in use and is not really the hypothesis its advocates set out to defend.

Part VI · Organisation

24The collective-intelligence hypothesis

The collective-intelligence hypothesis holds that AGI may consist of many interacting specialised agents rather than one monolithic model -- that generality is a property of an organisation, and that no participant need be general.

The oldest statement is Minsky's: mind as a society of agents, each individually unintelligent, whose interactions produce what we call thinking.150 The formal ancestor is Condorcet's jury theorem, which shows that majority verdicts of independent voters each better than chance converge on the truth as the population grows.151 The modern social-science version holds that diversity of problem representations can outperform individual ability.152 The multi-agent systems literature supplies the engineering vocabulary.153

The hypothesis has two very different instantiations and conflating them is the standard error. Within-model collectivism routes tokens to sparse experts inside one trained network, delivering large capacity at fixed inference cost.154155 This works, it is now standard at the frontier, and it is not a claim about many minds -- it is a claim about parameter efficiency inside one. Between-model collectivism runs separately prompted agents that talk to each other: debate to improve factuality,156 sampled reasoning paths aggregated by agreement,157 role-differentiated frameworks for software development.158159

The second kind is the interesting hypothesis and the weaker evidence. The systematic study of why it fails analysed over 1,600 annotated execution traces across seven popular frameworks and produced a taxonomy of 14 failure modes in three categories -- specification issues, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification -- with high inter-annotator agreement, and reported that improvements in base model capability will be insufficient to address the full taxonomy.160 That is the crucial finding: the failures are organisational, so a better model does not fix them.

There is a version of the hypothesis that is not really a hypothesis, and it deserves naming. If any sufficiently capable arrangement of software counts as the general system, the thesis is trivially satisfied and predicts nothing. It becomes substantive only in the form Plate 22 states: system-level capability continuing to rise while per-component capability is flat. That is measurable, and nobody has measured it.

25Correlated error, routers, and the coordination tax

Three quantities bound the collective hypothesis, and all three can be computed.

Correlated error. Take members each 70 per cent accurate, and let a fraction rho of cases be ones where all members err together. Majority accuracy converges not to 1 but to 1 − rho(1 − a).

At rho = 0.30 the ceiling is 0.91, and headcount cannot cross it: the 31st member is worth 0.0118, and going to 101 members buys 0.0067 more. This is the classical ensemble-diversity result in its starkest form -- ensemble accuracy is bounded by member correlation, not by member count.161 The implication for language-model committees is direct and unflattering. Agents instantiated from the same weights, trained on the same corpus, prompted from the same template are drawn from close to the maximally correlated case. Persona prompts do not decorrelate the errors that matter; they decorrelate style.

Routing. A specialist that is 95 per cent accurate in its domain and 40 per cent outside it, behind a router that is 90 per cent accurate, yields a system that is 89.5 per cent accurate -- worse than the specialist alone in its own domain. A perfect router is required merely to match one specialist. Every point of routing error is paid at the full difference between in-domain and out-of-domain competence. Routing is a ceiling, not a multiplier, and this is why the within-model version works: the router is trained jointly with the experts against the same loss, and the "domains" are learned rather than declared.

Coordination. If each handoff preserves the working context with probability c, then after k handoffs the surviving fraction is ck.

At c = 0.95, eight handoffs discard 33.7 per cent of the work; at sixteen, 56 per cent. Decomposition must buy more than that curve costs, and the empirical failure rates reported across multi-agent frameworks -- between 41 and 87 per cent depending on framework and task -- are consistent with it frequently not doing so.160

None of this refutes the collective hypothesis. It prices it, and the price tells you where it is a good bet: when the subtasks are genuinely separable so that handoffs are few, when the specialists are genuinely differently trained so that errors are genuinely uncorrelated, and when the routing decision is easier than the task. Those conditions describe an organisation, not a conversation. The single most useful thing the collective literature could do is stop reporting that a committee beat a single model on a benchmark, and start reporting rho.

Part VII · Adjudication

26What would tell them apart

Nine hypotheses, twenty-two claims. The question this article set out to answer is which observations would move them, and the answer is best given as a matrix.

Read the rows. A row with one filled cell is a discriminating test. A row filled across is a fact that everybody's theory already predicts, and settles nothing no matter how impressive the underlying result. Most of the results the field celebrates are in the second category, which is why the debate has not moved.

Four conclusions follow from the work above.

Only two genuine contradictions exist. The strong scaling claim -- nothing else is needed -- contradicts every necessity claim in the article, and is the only position that does. Among the rest, the strong neuro-symbolic necessity claim about systematicity contradicts the meta-learning result that produced human-level systematic generalisation without built-in symbols,35 and lost. Everything else is compatible. An embodied, developmentally staged, world-model-equipped agent with a symbolic verifier, discovered by an outer loop and deployed as a collective, satisfies eight of the nine hypotheses at once. The field argues as though it were choosing; mostly it is ordering.

One necessity claim is a theorem and the rest are inductions. Causal structure that observation underdetermines is identified by intervention -- 484 of 543 four-variable graphs are observationally ambiguous, and every ambiguous pair is separated by a single intervention. That result does not depend on anyone's research programme. But it establishes the necessity of interventional data, not of bodies, and its correct beneficiary is the experience family as a whole rather than the embodiment hypothesis in particular. Anyone using it to argue for robots specifically is over-claiming a result that would be equally satisfied by executing code and reading the error.

Three quantities are binding and under-reported. The verifier's precision bounds everything test-time compute can buy, in closed form: p / (p + (1 − p)f), and it does not move with sample count. Per-step reliability bounds horizon, and the 80 per cent horizon that deployment actually needs sits far below the fixed 0.32 fraction that independent compounding would give -- one failure per 44,904 steps is what a one-month task at 80 per cent demands. Error correlation bounds collectives at 1 − rho(1 − a) regardless of headcount. A field that reported these three numbers alongside its benchmark scores would be a field having a different argument.

The gauge totals say something about the state of play.

Across twenty-two plates drawn from the full range of the debate, the most-loaded commitment by a factor of more than two is A, a change of algorithm or architecture: 11 of 22 claims cannot be satisfied without one. Organisation follows at 8. Scale is loaded by 5, tied for last with experience and with explicit representation. That distribution is not evidence that the scaling hypothesis is unpopular -- it is the best-funded position in the history of the field. It is evidence that almost every claim now in circulation, including many made by the people who build the largest models, is a claim about what else is needed. The disagreement in 2026 is not whether something is missing. It is which missing thing to buy first.

A closing methodological note. The pattern of the last three years is that each benchmark has fallen to whichever ingredient was cheapest that year, and the ingredient has differed every time: pretraining scale, then post-training on verifiable rewards, then test-time search, then code-evolution scaffolds.63 That is informative about the economics of research and almost uninformative about the nature of intelligence. It is also why the most useful thing anyone has done recently is to move the target onto an axis where the cheap ingredient does not apply: an interactive benchmark in which an agent must acquire the goal by acting, build a model of an unfamiliar environment, and learn within the session.64 Whatever solves that will have settled more of this argument than another leaderboard could.

Until then, the honest position is the one Platt recommended and the field mostly avoids: for each claim you hold, write down in advance the observation that would make you drop it.7 Twenty-two of them are written down above. Very few of the results published this year bear on any of them.
