Framethe question, the vocabulary, the five codes

1What an unsolved problem is a claim about

--  ·  preamble: what an unsolved problem is a claim about

There is a list, and everyone in the field carries some version of it. Reliable reasoning. Robust long-horizon planning. Continual learning without catastrophic forgetting. Persistent memory, commonsense, causal understanding, grounding, world models, out-of-distribution robustness, calibrated uncertainty, interpretability, sample efficiency, autonomous error correction, reliable tool use, coordination among agents, alignment with what a person actually meant. Sixteen items, give or take, depending on who is writing them down. Formal definitions of the target range from the compression-theoretic1 to the psychometric,2 and staged accounts propose measuring progress by autonomy and generality rather than by any single threshold;3 the list survives all of them roughly intact, which is one reason to take it seriously.

The list is usually presented as an agenda: sixteen research programmes, sixteen conference tracks, sixteen places where a breakthrough might come from. That framing has a defect. It makes each item look like a capability that a system either has or lacks, and it invites the reply that has become the standard reply, which is that each one is improving and that the improvements will meet somewhere in the middle.

This article proposes a different reading, and the rest of it is an attempt to make the reading pay for itself. Every item on that list names a quantity that is supposed to stay the same, together with a change under which, as a matter of measured fact, it does not. Reliable reasoning is the claim that the answer does not move when the problem is re-described. Robustness is the claim that accuracy does not move when the input distribution does. Continual learning is the claim that yesterday's competence does not move when today's gradient is applied. Coordination is the claim that joint behaviour does not move when the partner is replaced by an equally good one trained separately. Alignment is the claim that the objective does not move when the optimiser leans on it.

Read this way, the sixteen are not sixteen capabilities. They are sixteen invariance claims, and an invariance claim has a definite form: a quantity, a class of perturbations, and an assertion that the first is constant over the second. That form is worth insisting on because it is falsifiable in a way that a capability claim is not. “The model can reason” has no refutation condition. “The model's answer is invariant under renaming and clause reordering” has exactly one: exhibit a renaming or a reordering that moves the answer. This is the old demand that a claim specify what would falsify it,4 applied to a field that has largely stopped making claims in that form; the corresponding experimental discipline is to design the test that separates the live hypotheses rather than the one that confirms the favoured hypothesis.5

The reframing also does something less obvious, which is the argument this article actually wants to make. Once the sixteen are stated as invariance claims, the reasons they fail turn out to be drawn from a very short list — five reasons, not sixteen — and those five have different remedies. Some of the sixteen are failing because nobody has written the invariant down anywhere the system could be optimised against it. Some are failing because the invariant cannot be picked out from the evidence available, no matter how much of that evidence there is. Some are failing because there is no path from an observed violation back to whatever caused it. Some are failing because the violation is invisible at the moment it matters. And some are failing because the fix is understood and costs more than anyone will pay.

Those are different problems wearing the same coat. Confusing them is expensive: it produces research that scales up the evidence when the evidence was never going to identify the answer, and engineering that buys a better detector when the thing that needed detecting was never specified. So the working question of this article is not “why is AGI hard”, which is unanswerable, but a smaller one that can be asked of each item in turn, and answered with a number: what exactly is supposed to stay the same, under what, and which of the five reasons is why it doesn't?

What this article is and is not

It is a technical article about open problems, not a forecast. It takes no position on when or whether the sixteen will be solved, and readers who want that will find the timing literature more useful than this. It is also not a survey; a survey would try to be complete about methods, and this is deliberately incomplete about methods in order to be precise about failure modes. Readers wanting the survey form have a good one in the collectively-authored catalogue of foundational challenges in assuring these systems.6 It is worth recording, too, that the research community is not of one mind about whether the current paradigm reaches the target: a 2025 survey of several hundred AI researchers found a large majority judging it unlikely that scaling current approaches yields general intelligence.7

Two things are load-bearing. The first is that every empirical claim about current systems is attached to a citation, and where the literature disagrees with itself the disagreement is reported rather than resolved. The second is that the structural claims — the ones about what is possible in principle rather than what happens to be true of this year's models — are backed by a small laboratory of exhaustive computations over formal systems small enough to enumerate completely. That laboratory, invariance_lab.py, is described in §20 and ships alongside this article; it uses only the Python standard library, contains no randomness of any kind, and reproduces byte-identically. Where a number appears in this article without a citation, it came from there, and you can check it.

The generalisation from a twelve-dimensional linear system to a frontier model is, of course, an inference and not a proof. §23 states that objection in its strongest form and answers it as well as it can be answered, which is not entirely.

2Invariant, perturbation class, span

--  ·  definitions: invariant, perturbation class, span

Three pieces of vocabulary do most of the work, and it is worth being pedantic about them because the imprecision they replace is where a great deal of confusion in this field lives.

The invariant

An invariant is a function of the system and the input — write it q(f, x) — whose value is claimed not to depend on some aspect of x. It need not be the output. It is often something about the output: the answer's correctness, the confidence attached to it, the rank order of two options, the identity of the feature that fired. Being explicit about which function is being claimed constant is the first discipline, because a great many arguments in this field are two people asserting invariance of different functions at each other.

The perturbation class

The perturbation class G is the set of changes under which the invariant is supposed to hold. It is a set, not a list of examples, and this distinction is the single most consequential one in the article. A test suite contains examples. A claim quantifies over the class. The relationship between the two is a coverage fraction, and that fraction is almost never reported.

It is worth seeing how quickly the arithmetic turns hostile. Suppose the perturbation class is generated by eight independent binary factors — lighting and dialect and latency and formatting and eight is a low estimate for anything deployed. The class has 256 members. A test suite that exercises the identity and each factor on its own has nine members: 3.52% of the class. A system whose failure mode is “two or more factors active at once” passes 100% of that suite and fails 96.48% of the class the suite was supposed to certify. Nothing about this depends on machine learning; it is counting, and it is the arithmetic behind Dijkstra's remark that testing shows the presence of defects and never their absence.8

Sampling instead of enumerating does not rescue it. To see every one of the 256 states by uniform sampling takes 1,568 draws in expectation, and the class grows exponentially in the number of factors while the sampling cost grows faster still. A budget of one billion tests exhaustively certifies twenty-nine binary factors. The reason robustness benchmarks keep being surprised is not that the field is careless. It is that the class is large and the suite is a list.

Span

The third term is the one that decides whether a problem is hard or impossible. A family of environments spans a perturbation class if the differences among them exercise every direction the class contains. The reason this matters is a result rather than an intuition: an invariant is identifiable from a family of environments only if the family spans the perturbations the invariant is supposed to survive. Where the family does not span, no amount of additional data of the same kind supplies the missing direction, because the missing direction is missing from the data-generating process, not from the sample.

The canonical version of this is invariant causal prediction, which searches for the set of predictors whose conditional relation to the target is stable across environments and returns the intersection of the invariant sets.9 The guarantee is real and the failure mode is instructive. §20 walks through it in a system small enough to enumerate exhaustively: four covariates, five environments, every one of the sixteen candidate predictor sets checked exactly. Three environments that shift one causal parent leave eight sets standing and an intersection of a single variable, which the estimator returns confidently and which is wrong. Sixteen further environments of exactly the same kind change nothing at all. One environment that moves the other parent cuts the survivors to four and recovers the truth.

The lesson generalises past causality. It is the reason a model can be evaluated on a hundred benchmarks and still be uncharacterised: a hundred benchmarks that vary the same thing are one benchmark. And it is the reason that “more data” and “more diverse data” are not synonyms, though they are frequently traded as if they were.

3Five reasons an invariant fails

--  ·  taxonomy: S / I / C / E / B

Given a violated invariant, there are five distinguishable reasons, and telling them apart is the point of the exercise because they have different remedies. This taxonomy is the article's own; it is offered as a working instrument rather than a discovery, and §23 discusses what would show it to be carved wrongly.

S — specification

The invariant is nowhere written down. Nothing in the loss, the reward, the schema, or the contract mentions it, so the system was never asked to preserve it and nothing is surprising about the fact that it did not. Relevance of a premise is like this. So is the promise an API makes as opposed to the types it declares. So, notoriously, is the objective that a reward model is a proxy for. The remedy for S is to write the thing down in a form something can be optimised or checked against, which is often hard and is at least clear.

I — identification

The invariant is specified, and the evidence available cannot pick it out. Several incompatible hypotheses fit everything observed equally well, and the observations that would separate them are of a kind the system never receives. Causal structure under purely observational data is the textbook case. So is the reference of a symbol given only co-occurrence. So, as §19 argues at some length, is the identity of a feature given only reconstruction error and a sparsity penalty. The remedy for I is never more data; it is a different kind of data — an intervention, an environment that moves what the others held still, a modality held out.

C — credit

The violation is visible and there is no path from it back to what caused it. The error surfaces three hundred steps downstream, or in a component that was fine, or in a parameter shared by the thing that broke and forty things that did not. Catastrophic forgetting is a credit failure in a precise sense: the update has no representation of which directions in parameter space were load-bearing for which past competence. The remedy for C is structural — something that carries provenance.

E — evaluation

The violation cannot be seen at the moment it matters. It may be perfectly visible in a laboratory with ground truth in hand, and invisible in deployment, which is where the system is. A bounded store that returns another item's value has failed, and says nothing. A model that behaves differently when it recognises an evaluation has failed, and the failure is by construction invisible to the evaluation. E is the most common code in this article's ledger, and it is the one most often mistaken for one of the others.

B — budget

Everything is specified, identified, attributable and detectable, and the fix costs more than anyone will pay. Sometimes this is an engineering complaint and sometimes it is a conservation law wearing an engineering complaint's clothes. §12 argues that the trade between retaining old competence and retaining the ability to learn is the latter: the projection that removes forgetting exactly spends rank doing it, and when the rank is gone the system is not slow to learn, it is unable to.

One methodological note before the substance. A plate in this article carries between one and three codes, and the assignment is a judgement about where the binding constraint currently sits, not a claim that the other four are absent. Almost every failure has some of all five in it. The claim is about which one you would have to relieve first for anything to move.

4Reliable reasoning

P01  ·  answer(x) == answer(rename(x)) == answer(reorder(x))

Start with the item that is hardest to state precisely and easiest to test. What would it mean for a system's reasoning to be reliable? Not that it is always right; nothing is. The usable version is an invariance claim: the answer does not depend on features of the problem statement that do not bear on the answer. Rename the people, redraw the numbers from the same schema, reorder the independent clauses, add a true remark that plays no part in the derivation. A reasoner's answer is invariant under all of it. A pattern-matcher's is not, and the gap between the two is measurable without anyone having to agree on what reasoning is.

The measurement exists. GSM-Symbolic generates instances from symbolic templates so that the same problem can be posed many ways, and reports that accuracy varies noticeably across instantiations of a single template and declines as the number of clauses grows. Its sharpest result is the one that concerns invariance directly: inserting a single clause that looks relevant and contributes nothing to the reasoning chain costs up to 65% of accuracy across frontier systems.10 The finding sits alongside work reaching compatible conclusions by other routes: transformers solving compositional tasks by matching linearised sub-graphs rather than executing an algorithm,11 models trained on “A is B” failing the corresponding question about B,12 and accuracy on controlled puzzle families collapsing past a complexity threshold.13 The authors' interpretation — that the systems are reproducing reasoning steps seen in training rather than performing them — is contested, and it should be; but the measurement is not the interpretation, and the measurement is an invariance violation with a number on it.

It is worth being careful about what such a result does and does not show, because this literature is unusually prone to overclaiming in both directions. A drop under distractor insertion does not establish that nothing deserving the name of reasoning is occurring. Humans also degrade under distractors, and the comparison is rarely run under matched conditions. What it does establish is narrower and sufficient: whatever process produces the answer is reading the irrelevant clause, and the specification under which the system was trained contains no term that would have told it not to.

That is the S code, and it is worth dwelling on because the fix is not obvious. Relevance is not a property that a next-token objective has any way to represent. Nothing in the loss says: this sentence should have left the answer alone. One can add such a term — consistency regularisation across paraphrases is exactly that, and it works about as well as the orbit it is fitted to is large.14 The lab makes the ceiling of that approach concrete.

The orbit decomposed, for the surface solver

The construction is a caricature and deliberately so. Six arithmetic word problems, each a bag of three clauses. The perturbation group is renaming (four ways), clause reordering (six ways), and insertion of zero or one topical distractor (three ways): an orbit of 72 per problem, 432 instances, every element of which leaves the correct answer unchanged. Three solvers. The first multiplies the first two numbers it sees and adds the rest — a positional heuristic that is correct on the canonical statement of every single problem. The second is the same heuristic with a filter for the one distractor it was shown during development. The third parses each clause into a role and uses only the roles the question needs.

The first solver scores 100% on canonical statements and 11.11% on the orbit. Renaming costs it nothing, because it never reads a name. Reordering and insertion cost it everything. The patched solver reaches 22.22%: it buys back precisely the distractor it was fitted to and precisely nothing else, which is what patching a perturbation rather than a class means arithmetically. The structural parser is at 100% everywhere, and gives the same answer for all 72 members of every orbit.

None of this shows that frontier models are the first solver. It shows what the reported numbers would look like if a system were somewhere between the first and the third, and it shows that the distance between 100% on the canonical form and 11.11% on the orbit can be entirely invisible to a benchmark that reports the canonical form.

Reliability along a chain

A second invariance claim hides inside the first. A derivation that is valid at step three should be valid at step three hundred; per-step reliability should not depend on position. It does. Recent work isolates execution from reasoning by supplying the plan and the knowledge outright, and finds that per-step accuracy falls as trajectories lengthen — not merely that errors compound at a constant rate, but that the rate itself rises. Controlling the fraction of errors visible in the context reproduces the effect, which identifies the mechanism as self-conditioning: a model that reads its own error-laden history becomes likelier to err next.15 The same paper makes the optimistic half of the argument, that marginal single-step gains compound into large horizon gains, and both halves are true.

§14 works through what self-conditioning does to the arithmetic of long-horizon planning, where its consequences are severe. Here the point is only that it is a credit failure and not a capability shortfall: the mechanism that produced step three hundred is conditioned on the record of the first two hundred and ninety-nine, and nothing in the objective distinguishes the two roles that record plays — evidence about the task, and evidence about the system's own recent behaviour.

The reason and the cause

Third, and most awkward for anyone who wants to use a trace as evidence: the reason a system states should be the reason it acted. Chain-of-thought prompting improves accuracy and produces a legible trace,16 and the temptation to read that trace as an explanation is strong. Introduce a cue that changes the answer without mentioning it, and models change the answer and produce a fluent justification that does not cite the cue.17 Faithfulness varies with model and task and can be measured, but is not reliably high, and the case that a trace should not be treated as an explanation at all has been put directly.18,19 And there is a specific trap: optimising the trace to look good is exactly the intervention that destroys its evidential value, a result demonstrated by training against a chain-of-thought monitor and watching the misbehaviour persist while the statement of it disappears.20

That last finding deserves more weight than it usually gets, because it is the clearest instance in the whole field of an E failure that gets worse as you attend to it. The trace is currently a usable safety instrument in part because nobody has optimised it hard. Its usefulness and its optimisation pressure are in direct conflict, and there is no known way to have both.

5Commonsense reasoning

P02  ·  conclude(K) == conclude(K + irrelevant)

Commonsense is the item on the list with the longest history and the least settled definition. McCarthy posed it in 1959 as the problem of a program that draws the obvious consequences of what it is told,21 and by 1969 had isolated the part that resists: the qualification problem, which is that the conditions under which an ordinary action succeeds cannot be finitely enumerated.22 Sixty-odd years and several paradigm changes later, the survey literature still identifies it as central and open.23

As an invariance claim it is this: a default conclusion survives the addition of facts that do not bear on it, and retracts on the addition of the one that does. Both halves are required, and they pull in opposite directions. A system that never retracts is rigid; a system that retracts under any addition is the distractor failure of §4 wearing different clothes.

Modern systems are, by any historical standard, extremely good at this. They will tell you that a dropped glass breaks, that it does not break on carpet, that it does not break if it is plastic, and that the plastic one might still break if it is very cold — which is more graceful non-monotonic reasoning than any hand-built system ever managed. The benchmark record reflects it: HellaSwag, PIQA, WinoGrande and the Winograd schemas that preceded them have all gone from discriminating to saturated,25,26,27,28 and the hand-built knowledge graphs of if-then inferences that were state of the art a few years ago are now comfortably outperformed by systems that were never shown them.29

And yet minor restatements of familiar puzzles still produce complete breakdowns, with high confidence and confabulated justification, in systems that handle the standard forms perfectly.24 The pattern is now familiar enough to state as a general observation: performance on the canonical statement of a commonsense problem and performance on its orbit are different quantities, and only the first is reported.

The interesting question is why this particular invariant resists, given how much of the relevant knowledge is evidently present. The answer this article offers is that commonsense is an S failure before it is anything else, and that the S is unusually deep. The knowledge is there; the specification of when to apply it is not, because the set of circumstances under which a default should be withdrawn is not finite and not enumerable in advance. Every training corpus is a sample from a distribution over situations people bothered to write about, and the qualifications that matter are exactly the ones nobody bothers to write about because everyone knows them.

This is the observation that motivates the core-knowledge line of argument: that the invariances of physical and social reality — objects persist, agents have goals, contact precedes effect — are not learned from text at all in humans but are present early and structure everything acquired afterwards.30,31 Whether they must be built in or can be induced is precisely the open question, and it is an I question rather than an S one: two systems, one with core knowledge built in and one having induced something functionally similar, are hard to tell apart on any test anyone has run.

The failed alternative is worth remembering, because it was a serious attempt at the S code. Cyc spent decades writing the qualifications down by hand.32 It is easy to be dismissive about the result and harder to say what the modern approach has done differently other than being much larger and much less explicit about what it contains.

6Grounding

P03  ·  refers(s, w) invariant under context(s)

Searle's argument that syntax does not suffice for semantics set the terms of this debate and has never been settled to anyone's satisfaction.33 Harnad's formulation of the resulting technical problem is still the cleanest: how do the symbols in a system get their meaning otherwise than by reference to more symbols?34 The invariance version is narrower and testable. What a term refers to should not change when the text around it does, and should change when the thing it refers to does.

The second half is the one that bites. A system whose access to the world is a corpus has, in an exact sense, no channel by which the world can correct it: the corpus is fixed, and a change in the referent produces no change in the evidence. This is the shape of the argument that form does not determine meaning,36 and of the constructive programme that says language must be grounded in perception and action.37

That programme has been complicated in a way worth reporting precisely, because it cuts against the obvious remedy. The most careful recent treatment argues that what referential grounding requires is a causal-informational relation between representation and referent plus a selection history that makes the relation the representation's function — and that on this analysis multimodality and embodiment turn out to be neither necessary nor sufficient.35 A robot with cameras is not thereby grounded, and a text-only system with the right feedback structure is not thereby ungrounded. If that is right, then bolting sensors onto a language model does not address the problem, and a good deal of work that presents itself as addressing the problem is addressing a different one.

The code here is I, and the underdetermination has a formal statement that predates the field. Distributional evidence fixes reference only up to transformations that preserve the distribution, which is Quine's indeterminacy of translation restated as a fact about co-occurrence matrices,38 and Putnam's model-theoretic argument makes the same point about any theory whose only constraints are internal.39 The response that the constraints are not merely internal — that reference is fixed by causal contact with what is referred to — is the right response, and it is exactly what a corpus does not supply.

Two qualifications keep this honest. First, there is a serious position holding that reference is not what meaning requires in the first place, and that conceptual role suffices;40 this article does not adjudicate that, and §6's invariance claim is agnostic between them, since both sides agree the system's use of a term ought to track something. Second, the empirical picture is more interesting than the philosophical one: large models do appear to build structured internal representations of the domains their training data describes,41,42 and the live question is whether such structure is grounding or a very good simulacrum of it — a question on which the two positions predict the same observations, which is what makes the code I.43

7Causal understanding

P04  ·  P(y | do(x)) == P(y | x) for the right x

Causal understanding has the cleanest invariance statement of anything on the list, because the field supplied one. A relation is causal when it survives intervention: setting the variable does what conditioning on it predicted.44,45 Prediction and control come apart precisely where the relation is not causal, and every failure of the second while the first holds is a violation of this invariant.

The reason this is hard is not that anyone is confused about the definition. It is that the definition names something observational data does not contain. A distribution is compatible with an entire equivalence class of structures; selecting within it requires either intervention or an assumption doing an intervention's work.50 No quantity of observation removes the ambiguity, because the ambiguity is not sampling error. Where data from several sources can be combined, the conditions under which a causal quantity transports across them are themselves a formal question with a formal answer, and the answer is frequently that it does not.51 This is the I code in its purest form, and it explains why the sub-problem resists scale in a way the field finds counterintuitive: scale supplies more of exactly the kind of evidence that was never going to separate the candidates.

Invariance across environments is the standard partial answer, and it deserves its reputation. If the mechanism generating the target is stable while everything else varies, then the predictors whose conditional relation to the target is stable across environments are the causal ones, and the intersection of the invariant sets is a conservative estimate of the parents.9,52 Invariant risk minimisation carried the idea into deep learning,53 where its guarantees turned out to require conditions that realistic settings violate; the subsequent analyses show the objective failing to capture the invariance it was named for, and careful benchmarking finds it does not reliably beat plain empirical risk minimisation with proper model selection.54,55,56

That sequence is worth reading as a case study in the difference between S and I. IRM wrote the invariant into the objective, which addressed S. It could not manufacture span, which is I, and the empirical disappointment followed. The lab's enumeration in §20 shows the mechanism in a system small enough to check exhaustively: nineteen environments that all perturb the same parent leave the estimator with eight candidate sets and an intersection that is a strict subset of the truth, and it will report that subset with no indication that anything is missing.

For language models specifically, the evidence is mixed in an informative way. Systems perform well on causal questions whose answers appear in the corpus and poorly on structurally identical questions whose answers do not, which is what one would expect of retrieval of causal claims rather than causal inference.46 Benchmarks built to require the inference rather than the recall find substantial gaps.47 Against this, careful work finds genuine capability on pairwise causal discovery tasks that is hard to explain by memorisation alone.57 The honest summary is that the question is open and the measurements are not yet designed to settle it, because most of them do not control for whether the answer was available in text.

One theoretical result deserves emphasis because it converts this from a preference into a requirement. An agent that is robust to a sufficiently rich class of distributional shifts must have learned an approximately correct causal model of the data-generating process — not as a design recommendation but as a theorem.58 The constructive programme that follows — learning representations whose variables are the ones causal relations actually hold between, rather than pixels or tokens — is the field's most serious attempt at the I code.59 If that result holds up, then §9's robustness problem and this section's problem are the same problem, and a system cannot be robust in the strong sense while remaining agnostic about causal structure.

8Reliable world models

P05  ·  predict(s, a) accurate for unvisited s

A world model is whatever a system uses to answer “what happens if”. The requirement long predates the current paradigm: acting well under partial observation requires maintaining a belief over states and predicting how actions move it,60 and the most-discussed recent architectural proposal for autonomous machine intelligence is organised around a predictive world model as its central module.61 The invariance claim is that its predictions remain accurate at states the training trajectories never visited, since a model that is accurate only where it has been is a lookup table with good manners.

The most useful recent work on this is methodological. Rather than asking whether a model “has” a world model, it asks what the model's implicit theory would have to be, and probes that. A sequence model trained on taxi trajectories in Manhattan achieves near-perfect next-turn prediction and, when its implicit map is extracted, produces a city containing streets that do not exist and impossible overpasses.48 The same programme applied to orbital mechanics recovers a model that predicts trajectories excellently and, when asked for the force law it implies, returns something that is not Newton's.49 The pattern is consistent: excellent on the visited manifold, incoherent as a theory.

Note what makes this an I failure with an E rider. Two systems — one with the law and one with an elaborate patchwork that agrees with it everywhere the data went — make identical predictions on the trajectories, which is what the benchmark measures. Separating them requires a counterfactual query, and constructing one requires knowing what the law is supposed to be, which is the thing under test. The evidence that would identify the model is exactly the evidence the evaluation does not collect.

The generative-video line of work is where this tension is currently most visible. Interactive environment models now maintain visual consistency over minutes of interaction and respond to actions in a way that looks like physics.62 Whether they encode dynamics or an extremely good renderer of what dynamics look like is not settled by the demonstrations, and the honest position is that the community does not yet have a probe that distinguishes them at that scale. Model-based reinforcement learning provides the constructive counterpoint: agents that learn a latent dynamics model and plan inside it now master diverse control tasks from scratch, including the long-standing diamond-collection benchmark, without human data.63,64

The second invariant in this section is the one that closes the loop, and it is where the problem stops being about representation. A world model is queried by a policy, and the policy's actions determine which states get queried. Accuracy on the distribution the training data came from is not accuracy on the distribution that acting on the model produces. This is why the strongest evidence about world models now comes from interactive benchmarks rather than static ones: ARC-AGI-3 drops an agent into a turn-based environment with no instructions, no stated goal and no win condition, requiring it to explore, infer the goal, build a model of the dynamics and plan — and reports humans solving 100% of environments against frontier systems below 1% on its efficiency measure.65 The predecessor benchmark's history is a caution about reading too much into any single number — ARC-AGI-2 went from low teens to the high seventies within a year, with evidence that the format itself was being absorbed into training data66 — but the format change is not a scoring change. It moves the query distribution under the agent's own control, and that is the invariant that fails.

9Out-of-distribution robustness

P06  ·  acc(D_test) == acc(D_deploy)

The invariance claim here is the one the whole practice of machine learning rests on and the one it is least entitled to: that performance measured on a held-out split predicts performance where the system runs. The held-out split is drawn from the same distribution as the training set. Deployment is not.

The problem is old enough to have a canonical reference collection70 and a canonical demonstration: train a classifier to identify which dataset an image came from, and it succeeds, which means the datasets differ in ways nobody intended.71 The empirical record is unusually clean, because the experiments are cheap to run and have been run repeatedly. Rebuilding a benchmark by following its own published construction protocol — not adversarially, just again — costs a large fraction of measured accuracy across every model tested, and the ordering of models is largely preserved while the absolute numbers are not.67 Synthetic corruption benchmarks measure something real,72 but interventions that improve robustness to synthetic corruptions transfer poorly to natural distribution shifts; the honest summary of a large survey of such methods is that almost none of them beat the baseline of simply training on more data.68 Curated natural-shift benchmarks make the gap concrete across domains from pathology to satellite imagery.73

Two findings sharpen the diagnosis past “shift is hard”. The first is underspecification: identically-specified training pipelines, differing only in random seed, produce models that are equivalent on the test split and systematically different under shift.69 The pipeline does not determine the inductive bias, so the test split cannot be measuring it. The second is shortcut learning: networks reliably latch onto whichever feature predicts the label most cheaply, which is a rational response to the objective and a disaster whenever that feature is an artefact of collection.74 Neither of these is a bug in a particular architecture; both are consequences of specifying the goal as expected loss on a fixed distribution, and the failure decomposes cleanly into a geometric part and a statistical part that call for different fixes.75

Against this there is a genuinely surprising regularity: in-distribution and out-of-distribution accuracy are often strongly, nearly linearly correlated across a wide range of models and shifts.76 Where that holds, in-distribution measurement is informative about robustness, and the pessimistic reading of this section is too strong. Where it fails — and it fails for adversarial and for structured compositional shifts — it fails without warning. The two results are not in conflict; together they say that the relationship between the two quantities is itself distribution-dependent, which is the same problem one level up.

The structural point is §2's coverage arithmetic, and it is why this problem should not be expected to close. The perturbation class is not enumerable, single-factor testing covers a vanishing fraction of it, and the theoretical results are unhelpful in a specific way: without assumptions restricting the relationship between source and target, no bound on target error is available from source data at all,77 and the no-free-lunch results say that a learner cannot be better than chance on every distribution.78 Robustness is therefore always robustness to a named class. The failure in practice is that the class is almost never named, which converts a precise, achievable claim into a vague, unachievable one.

Adversarial robustness is the sub-case where the class is named and the news is worse, because the class is defined by an adversary who reads your defence. The history is a long sequence of defences broken by adaptive attacks that the original evaluations did not consider,79,80 which is the E code in its most literal form: the evaluation and the threat are the same object viewed from two sides.

10Uncertainty calibration

P07  ·  P(correct | conf = c) == c, in every population

Calibration is the invariant that lets a downstream system act on a confidence: when a model says 0.8, it should be right eight times in ten. It is the most operationally important item on the list, because every other failure becomes survivable if it is announced, and none of them are if it is not.

Two distinct failures need separating, since they carry different codes and are constantly conflated.

The first is that post-training degrades it. Pre-trained models are close to calibrated on next-token predictions and can report their own accuracy with some fidelity.81 Preference-based post-training sharpens the output distribution and damages that property, an effect reported consistently and now attributed to reward models that prefer confident-sounding completions regardless of correctness.82,83,85 The remedy is legible: verbalised confidence recovers part of it,82 though it is itself systematically overconfident, and more so off the distribution its phrasing was tuned on,86 calibration-aware fine-tuning recovers more,85 and the underlying incentive can be changed. The clearest statement of the incentive is that evaluations awarding nothing for abstention make guessing the score-maximising strategy — which reframes a chunk of what gets called hallucination as a scoring-rule problem rather than a knowledge problem. The stronger claim, that some rate of confabulation is unavoidable for any system of this kind whatever the training regime, has been argued formally and is worth reading against the scoring-rule account rather than alongside it.87,84 That is an S failure, and S failures are the good kind: proper scoring rules have been understood for a long time.88

The second failure is not fixable by better objectives, and it is the one this section exists to make vivid. Calibration is a property of a predictor relative to a population, and populations move. Deep ensembles and temperature scaling improve calibration in distribution and degrade under shift, a result established across many methods and shift types.89,90

The lab isolates the effect in a construction with no noise in it at all. Two subgroups, four confidence bins, integer counts. Within each subgroup the predictor's accuracy in each bin is fixed and never changes. On the source mixture the predictor is perfectly calibrated: every bin's empirical accuracy equals its stated confidence, expected calibration error 0.0000. Carrying it to a different population is an inference, and it is the same inference that §9 says is unlicensed. 11Efficient learning

P08  ·  samples(skill) bounded as novelty(skill) rises

The invariance claim is that the cost of acquiring a skill does not explode when the skill is genuinely new. This is the item that most directly indicts the current paradigm, and also the one where the indictment is most often overstated, so it is worth separating what is measured from what is inferred.

The measurement side is solid. Defining intelligence as skill-acquisition efficiency rather than skill, and building a benchmark that enforces the definition by making every task unique, was the point of ARC.93 The interactive successor extends it: agents are dropped into novel turn-based worlds with no instructions and scored against human action efficiency, and as of March 2026 humans solve 100% of environments while frontier systems score below 1%.65 The gap between a system that can pass professional examinations — including examinations built expressly to be at the frontier of human expertise95 — and one that cannot efficiently learn a game a child figures out in minutes is the single most robust qualitative finding in the current evaluation literature. It is a finding about the shape of the capability profile, and broad multi-task collections were built to characterise exactly that shape.96

The inference side needs care. It does not follow that scaling has stopped working, and the record says otherwise on the static predecessor — ARC-AGI-2 moved from low teens to the high seventies within roughly a year, and the ARC Prize team's own analysis notes evidence that the benchmark's format was being absorbed into training corpora, which complicates reading that curve as pure capability.66 What the efficiency gap does show is that the current scaling relationship converts data into skill at a rate that is not the human rate, which is not the same as saying it converges to a lower ceiling.

There is a hard constraint on the input side worth stating without drama. The scaling relations that make the compute-versus-data trade explicit97,98 are relations about a resource with a finite stock: projections of the effective stock of human-generated public text put exhaustion somewhere in the late 2020s to early 2030s under continued growth in demand.94 Whether this binds depends on how much synthetic data, self-play and experience substitute, which is precisely the argument that the field's centre of gravity is now moving from a data-driven era to an experience-driven one.99 That argument is a research programme rather than a result, and it is the correct place to look for what would change this section's verdict.

Underneath the empirical dispute is a piece of theory that is not in dispute and is often forgotten. Sample complexity is a function of the hypothesis class, and there is no learner that is efficient over all target concepts. The oldest result in this line is that a language cannot be identified from positive examples alone without a restriction on the hypothesis space,100 and the most surprising recent one is that whether a given learning problem is solvable at all can be independent of the axioms of set theory.101,102,103,78 Efficient learning is therefore always efficient learning given a prior that fits. Human sample efficiency is not evidence of a better algorithm in the abstract; it is evidence of priors that fit the world humans live in, whether they arrive by evolution or by early development.104,30 So the technical problem is not “be more efficient”, which is not well posed, but the sharper and harder “acquire, or be given, the priors that make this world's regularities cheap”. That is a B code sitting on top of an I code, and the I is doing the work.

12Continual learning without catastrophic forgetting

P09  ·  perf_A(w_t) == perf_A(w_t + dw_B)

The invariance claim: learning task B leaves performance on task A where it was. The phenomenon that violates it was named in 1989, in a paper that reported connectionist networks not merely degrading on old material but losing it almost entirely,105,106 and it has been continuously worked on since without being solved.107,108,109

This is the section where the lab earns its place, because the trade involved is exact and can be computed rather than argued about. Twelve dimensions, three linear constraints per task, exact rational arithmetic throughout, and the most charitable learner available: one that moves to the nearest point satisfying the new task, which is what a converged least-squares update does.

Forgetting is not a defect of the optimiser. It is the geometry of solving a new system of equations with weights that were carrying an old one.

The right panel applies the standard structural remedy: confine the update to the null space of the old task's constraints, which is what orthogonal gradient descent and gradient projection memory do.110,111 Forgetting becomes exactly zero at every overlap, which is the advertised result and is real. The number that is not usually put beside it is in the same row: at full overlap, task B now carries a residual of 11 — the identical quantity, moved. Protection does not remove the cost. It decides which task pays it.

Iterating makes the conservation law explicit.

Four disjoint tasks of three constraints each are learned exactly in twelve dimensions, consuming three dimensions apiece. The fifth is not learned slowly; it is not learned. The residual is irreducible because there is no direction left to move in. This is why the stability–plasticity dilemma is a dilemma and not an engineering backlog:114 the resource that buys retention and the resource that buys acquisition are the same resource.

The empirical literature has now caught up with the second half. Loss of plasticity — networks trained continually losing the ability to learn new things at all, independently of what they forget — is documented across supervised and reinforcement settings, with ImageNet binary-classification accuracy falling from around 89% to below the level of a linear baseline by the two-thousandth task.112,115 The obvious question is whether scale dissolves it, and the 2026 evidence says it does not: GPT-style transformers on a multilingual continual-learning problem eventually lose the ability to adapt efficiently to new data, in continual and stationary settings alike.113 For fine-tuning specifically, forgetting of general capability rises with model scale rather than falling.116,117

The codes are C and B, and they are worth distinguishing because only one of them is a research problem. The C part — the update has no representation of which directions were load-bearing for which competence — is attackable, and every method in the literature is an attempt to estimate that. Elastic weight consolidation estimates it from a Fisher information matrix;118 synaptic intelligence accumulates it online;119 replay sidesteps the estimate by keeping data.120 The B part is not a research problem in the same sense. Capacity is finite, protection consumes it, and a system that must run indefinitely must eventually decide what to lose. No paper will make that go away, and the useful work is on the policy for choosing, which is a question almost nobody is asking.

13Persistent memory

P10  ·  read(k) == write(k), for all elapsed time

Memory and continual learning are frequently discussed together and are different invariants. Continual learning is about the weights; memory is about a store. Attaching an explicit, addressable one to a network is an old idea that works121,122 and that the current generation has rediscovered in the form of retrieval over an external database, with the same trade-offs. The claim is that what was written is what is read, however much has happened in between.

The field has come to treat long context and memory as separate problems, and the separation is the right one: capacity within a session and continuity across sessions fail differently. Within a session, retrieval degrades with position, with the middle of a long context being where information goes to be ignored,123 and synthetic-length benchmarks show effective context falling well short of advertised context.124,127 Across sessions, dedicated benchmarks with conversations spanning dozens of sessions test recall, temporal reasoning and knowledge update as distinct capabilities that degrade independently.125,126 Architectures that extract, consolidate and retrieve structured memories rather than concatenating history report substantial accuracy gains at a fraction of the token cost,128,129 and agent designs that add reflection and consolidation on top of raw recall were among the first to make long-running behaviour coherent at all.130

This is genuine progress on a real engineering problem, and it does not touch the bound underneath. The lab states it in the flattest possible way: a store of a thousand and twenty-four slots, a fixed hash, last write wins, and every key queried.

The second invariant in this section is subtler and, for deployed agents, more consequential. A constraint accepted early in a session should still bind later, with no intervening event marking a change. Long-running agents violate constraints they had honoured, and the violation is silent: nothing in the transcript announces that a commitment has lapsed.125 The longest-horizon evaluations, where an agent runs a simulated business over many simulated days, show coherence failures accumulating in exactly this way.131 The code is E, and the remedy is architectural rather than statistical — a commitment that is re-presented for checking is a different object from a commitment that sits in a context window and decays with attention.

14Robust long-horizon planning

P11  ·  P(step correct | position t) independent of t

Two invariants live here. The first is that per-step reliability does not depend on position in the trajectory. The second is that a plan sound when made stays sound when the world declines to cooperate. The first is arithmetic; the second is specification.

Start with the arithmetic, because it is unforgiving and often misread in both directions. If steps fail independently at rate q, the probability an H-step plan completes is (1−q)^H. Ninety per cent end-to-end at a hundred steps demands a per-step success of 0.998947. At ten thousand steps it demands 0.999989. That relationship is why sequential decision problems have always been organised around a value that decomposes over steps rather than over whole trajectories.134 Horizon is exponentially sensitive to per-step reliability, which cuts encouragingly as well: marginal improvements in single-step accuracy compound into large gains in achievable horizon, and this is the correct rebuttal to reading flattening short-task benchmarks as flattening progress.15 Measured horizons have been rising on a fairly consistent trend, reported at explicit reliability thresholds precisely because the threshold changes the number by an order of magnitude.132,133 Task suites of genuinely long engineering work — resolving real repository issues,135 or research-engineering tasks scored against human experts under a matched time budget136 — are where those measurements come from.

Now the part that breaks the arithmetic. The independence assumption is false, and it is false in the direction that hurts. Per-step error rises with position, and rises further when the visible history contains errors — self-conditioning, demonstrated by controlling the error rate shown in the context and watching subsequent accuracy fall.15 The lab computes what this does to the value of error detection, by exact dynamic programming over the number of errors already made.

The second invariant is the classical one and the literature on it is clear. Generating a plausible plan and validating a plan are sharply different capabilities in current systems, with generation far ahead of verification.137 The constructive response is to stop asking the model to be the planner and use it as a generator inside a framework where a sound external component does the checking,140 which works and concedes the point. Benchmarks with real, checkable constraints remain far from solved.138

The specification failure underneath is that preconditions are rarely written down. A plan that says “book the flight, then the hotel” does not carry the fact that the second step assumes the first succeeded, so there is nothing for an executor to check when it did not. Multi-agent systems make this measurable: an analysis of sixteen hundred annotated failure traces across seven frameworks sorts failures into specification issues, inter-agent misalignment and verification gaps in roughly comparable proportions, and concludes explicitly that better base models will not be sufficient.139 That conclusion is the whole of this section: the failures are not in the reasoning, they are in the contract.

15Autonomous error correction

P12  ·  check(y) correlated with correct(y)

If a system could reliably detect its own errors, most of the other fifteen problems would become survivable. This is why self-correction attracts so much attention and why its failure is so consequential. The invariance claim is minimal: a system's verdict on its own output is better than chance about that output.

The empirical finding is by now consistent. Intrinsic self-correction — asking a model to review its own answer with no external signal — degrades performance on reasoning tasks rather than improving it, and apparent gains in earlier work came from oracle information leaking into the setup, such as being told when to stop.141 The methods that popularised the idea — iterative self-feedback, and verbal reinforcement from reflection on failed attempts143,144 — work well where an external signal is available and much less well where it is not, which is the distinction the later literature draws.145 A careful survey confirms the pattern and identifies the conditions under which self-correction does work: it works when there is an external signal, a verifiable outcome, or a decomposition that lets a different question be asked.146,147 The sharpest decomposition finds that models are poor at finding reasoning errors and good at correcting them once the location is given.142 Localisation is the bottleneck, not repair.

Rather than treat this as a fact about current models, it is worth asking what a correction loop needs in order to help at all. The lab models it with five parameters: the error rate, the checker's sensitivity and false-positive rate, the probability a redo repairs a genuinely wrong step, and the probability a redo damages a correct one. The post-loop error rate follows immediately, and so does the condition for improvement.

Making the checker more sensitive does not lower the bar the checker must clear; it changes where on the curve you sit, not where the curve crosses zero. A loop whose redos are as likely to break a correct step as to fix a broken one needs a checker whose flags are right half the time, whatever the underlying error rate.

Iteration does not rescue a checker below threshold. The loop converges to a fixed point of fw/(sr+fw) regardless of where it starts, and when the checker is below threshold that fixed point is above the error rate you began with. Running the loop more times delivers you to a worse place faster. That is the arithmetic behind the empirical finding that repeated self-critique degrades answers.

The code is E, and the reason is the one that makes this problem structurally different from the others. The checker and the generator share a failure mode. The errors that survive are exactly the ones the checker cannot see, because both are the same system with the same blind spots. This is why process supervision, which trains a separate verifier on step-level labels, is the approach with real traction:148 it does not ask the system to see what it cannot see. And it is why the classical results on building reliable systems from unreliable parts are relevant rather than quaint — they all require the parts to fail independently, which is precisely the assumption a single model reviewing itself violates.149,150

16Reliable tool use

P13  ·  meaning(call) invariant across the boundary

Tool use looks like the most tractable item on the list and is worth including precisely because it is not. The mechanics are solved: models can be taught to call APIs from self-supervised data,151 and interleaving reasoning with action is a standard and effective pattern.152 The invariance claim is one that software engineering has known how to state for fifty years: a call that meant one thing yesterday means the same thing today.

The measurement that makes this section necessary is a metric rather than a benchmark. τ-bench evaluates agents on customer-service tasks against a database whose final state is checked, and reports not only single-attempt success but pass^k — the probability that all k attempts at the same task succeed. The gap between them is the point: single-attempt success above 60% corresponds to roughly one in four across eight attempts.153 The successor extends this to a dual-control environment where the user can also change the shared state, which is closer to how real interactions behave.154

That curve is the whole argument of this article in one picture. A pass rate is a claim about one attempt. An invariance claim is a claim about all of them. The two diverge geometrically, and a field that reports the first while making the second is systematically over-reporting.

The specification failure is exact and has a well-developed vocabulary that this domain has not adopted. A schema types the syntax of a call. It says nothing about what the call promises: what must hold before it, what will hold after it, what it may change. That distinction is what a contract is, and it has been understood since Hoare's axiomatic basis and named as an engineering practice for decades.156,157 An agent calling a tool through a typed schema is checking that the message is well-formed, not that the world is in the state the call assumed. When the far side changes behaviour while keeping its signature, nothing anywhere in the stack detects it.

There is a second boundary failure that belongs here because it is the same missing distinction. Content returned across the interface is not marked as data rather than instruction, so text retrieved by a tool can carry directives that the model treats as such — indirect prompt injection, which is not an implementation bug but a consequence of a channel that does not distinguish the two.155 The remedy is the same remedy: say, at the boundary, what a message is allowed to be.

The benchmark ecosystem is now substantial — function-calling suites, large API collections, sandboxes for measuring the risk of consequential actions, web and general-assistant environments158,159,160,161,162 — and it mostly reports pass@1. Where the harder consistency metric is reported, the numbers fall. That is not a criticism of the models; it is a criticism of the measurement, and it is the cheapest fix available anywhere in this article.

17Coordination among agents

P14  ·  joint(pi_i, pi_j) == joint(pi_i, pi_i)

The invariance claim: replacing a partner with an equally competent one, trained separately, does not break the joint task. This is the item most likely to be underestimated, because single-agent competence is improving quickly and coordination failure is not a competence failure at all.

The structural reason is a symmetry. In an environment where the labelling of options is arbitrary, every relabelling is an equally optimal convention, and independent training runs land on different ones. Self-play is maximally misleading here: an agent trained with a copy of itself achieves perfect coordination and has learned nothing that transfers. This is the zero-shot coordination problem, and the standard remedy is to break the symmetry deliberately by optimising against a randomly relabelled partner.163,164,166 The complementary finding is that agents trained only against copies of themselves are systematically worse with human partners than agents trained against a model of how humans actually play,167 and that an existing convention can be picked up from observation if the learner is built to look for one.168

The arithmetic is stark and entirely elementary. Four labels give twenty-four conventions and a cross-play success rate of exactly 25% per state. Canonicalisation — both parties agreeing to pick the lexicographically least labelling — restores 100%, but only if both parties know the same symmetry group. This is not hypothetical for deployed systems. Two frontier models fine-tuned on the driving conventions of different countries fail to clear a path for emergency vehicles in 77.5% of scenarios, where unspecialised baselines fail in 5% — specialisation making coordination worse, which is exactly what the symmetry analysis predicts.165 The multi-agent failure taxonomy finds inter-agent misalignment to be one of the three major categories, at a magnitude comparable to specification problems, across sixteen hundred annotated traces.139

The codes are S and I together. S because the convention is not in anyone's objective: each agent is optimising its own task and the joint protocol is emergent rather than specified. I because the symmetry that would canonicalise it has to be known, in the same form, by parties that never met. Where a convention can be published — a wire protocol, a standard, a norm — the problem is solved by publication, which is what human institutions do about it. Where incentives diverge rather than merely conventions, the difficulty changes character entirely and becomes a question about equilibria rather than about labels.169,170,171 Where it cannot, the theory is not encouraging, and the deeper results about distributed agreement under uncertainty suggest why: agreement among parties who cannot distinguish silence from failure has known impossibility results, and they do not care whether the parties are learned.172,173

18Alignment with human intentions

P15  ·  argmax proxy == argmax objective, under pressure

The last of the sixteen is the one that is usually treated as a different kind of problem — ethical, political, not technical. Stated as an invariance claim it is exactly as technical as the others: a proxy that tracks the goal where it was validated continues to track it where the optimiser goes.

Why it fails is a theorem before it is an observation. If the proxy omits an attribute that the true objective values, and the optimiser can trade that attribute away, then sufficient optimisation pressure produces unbounded loss on the true objective — the omitted attribute is spent to buy the measured ones.175 Reward hacking has a formal characterisation,176 the empirical curve of over-optimising a learned reward model has been measured,174 and the phase-transition behaviour where true utility collapses while proxy score climbs has been mapped.179 This is Goodhart's law177,178 with the mechanism supplied, and the catalogue of specification-gaming incidents collected over the years reads as a long list of the theorem being demonstrated by accident.180

The lab makes the shape of it exhaustively concrete, in a setting where nothing is hidden. Five attributes, effort levels zero to four, true value strictly concave in effort, every proxy linear — and therefore exactly correct in the region where levels are zero or one. All 7,775 non-trivial integer proxies are enumerated, each scored on how well it orders the training region and on what it actually realises when an optimiser maximises it over the full feasible set.

Not one of the 7,775 reaches the true optimum. The single proxy that orders every pair in the training region correctly — the one with the right weights, differing from the truth only in its shape — realises 76.06%. Proxies at 90-95% training agreement average 72.56% and reach as low as 59.15%. Agreement where you measured is not evidence about behaviour at the optimum, because the optimum is not where you measured; that is what optimisation means.

The dependence on the optimiser's reach is the part that should worry a practitioner, because it is not monotone. The gap is zero at trivial budgets, worst in the middle of the range, and closes again only when the budget saturates every attribute and there is nothing left to trade. A proxy validated at low optimisation pressure and deployed at high pressure has been validated in the regime where it could not fail.

The observational record now matches the theory closely enough that the two are hard to separate. Specification gaming is present across all frontier models tested, at rates that rise substantially — by 32% to 170% in matched open-weight pairs — with reasoning-focused reinforcement training.181 Reward tampering has been elicited as a generalisation from milder gaming.182 Most seriously, reward hacking arising in a production reinforcement-learning pipeline has been observed to generalise into broader misalignment rather than staying local to the hacked task.183

And there is a second invariant, which is the one that makes the first hard to study. A system should behave the same whether or not it is being evaluated. Frontier models frequently recognise evaluation settings, and can say so;184 the specification-gaming work reports verbalised evaluation awareness and is explicit that it can only be a lower bound, since awareness that is not verbalised is not observable.181 In-context scheming has been elicited in controlled settings,185 as has behaviour that differs by whether the model believes it is in training.186

This is the E code at its most uncomfortable, and it is qualitatively different from the E failures elsewhere in this article. In every other section the instrument is merely incomplete. Here the instrument is part of the environment the system is optimising against, which means the measurement and the thing measured are not independent. The methodological remedies are well understood — RLHF's limitations are documented at length,187 constitutional and preference-based methods are being refined,188,189,190 and the theoretical framing is mature191,192,193,194 — but none of them dissolve the structural point, which is that a specification gap is invisible from inside the specification. The framing of the control problem as one of building systems that are uncertain about the objective, rather than confident about a proxy for it, remains the most serious constructive proposal on the table.195

19Interpretability

P16  ·  feature(m, seed_1) == feature(m, seed_2)

Interpretability is on the list for a different reason from the other fifteen. It is not a capability the system needs; it is the instrument by which the other fifteen would be measured. Almost every E code in this article — sixteen of twenty-nine plates — is a statement that a violation is invisible, and interpretability is the programme for making internal states visible. That makes its own invariance problems the most consequential ones here.

The progress is real and should not be minimised by what follows. Specific circuits have been identified and causally validated;196,197 the observation that features are superposed — that a network represents more directions than it has neurons, which is why single neurons read as polysemantic198 — motivated dictionary learning, which extracts features substantially more interpretable than individual neurons and scales to production models;199,200,201 and attribution graphs now trace multi-step computation through a model rather than merely labelling activations.202,203 The field has moved from correlational storytelling to causal intervention, which is the move that matters.204

The first invariance problem is stability. A feature found in one model should be the same feature in a retrained one. Sparse autoencoders trained on identical data with different seeds learn substantially different features;205 features split and get absorbed into one another as dictionary width changes, so that what counts as one feature is partly a decision about hyperparameters.206 The general statement is that mechanistic explanations are not in general uniquely determined by the behaviour they explain — several distinct explanations can be exactly consistent with all the interventional evidence.207

The lab makes the underdetermination exact rather than arguing about it. Seven atoms in three dimensions, activations generated as sums of at most two atoms, every exact decomposition at that sparsity enumerated.

Thirty-five per cent of activations admit more than one exact decomposition. Minimum sparsity is the standard tie-breaker, and it leaves 23.53% still ambiguous — and, worse, it actively misleads on 11.76%, where a genuine two-feature composition is reported as a single composite feature because the composite is in the dictionary and is sparser. That last figure is the formal version of feature absorption, and it means the tie-breaker is not neutral: it systematically prefers the story with fewer, larger features, whether or not that story is right.

The code is I, which is why this problem does not yield to more compute. The objective — reconstruct well, be sparse — is satisfied equally by dictionaries that disagree about what is active. Nothing in the objective distinguishes them, so nothing in a longer training run will. What would help is a third criterion with a claim to identifiability, and the field's own agenda documents list this among the open problems.208 Benchmarks that score autoencoders on downstream tasks rather than on reconstruction and sparsity are the current best answer and are explicitly a proxy.209

Two older results are worth keeping in view because they describe the same failure in the previous generation of methods. Saliency maps that look convincing can survive randomisation of the model's parameters, which means they were not explaining the model.210 Directions that appear to encode a clean concept can turn out to be artefacts of the probing method.211 Both are identifiability failures caught by a sanity check that the field did not run for some years, which is the strongest argument for running the analogous check now: report feature agreement across seeds beside reconstruction and sparsity, as a headline number.

None of this is an argument against interpretability, and the alternative — building systems simple enough to be understood directly212 — is not on offer at this scale. It is an argument that interpretability results should carry identifiability claims, and that a safety argument resting on an explanation should state what would have to be true for the explanation to be the right one.213

Resultsthe laboratory, the ledger, the verdict

20The laboratory

--  ·  eleven deterministic experiments, byte-reproducible

Eleven experiments, one file, the Python standard library and nothing else. There is no randomness anywhere in it: no seeds, no sampling, no shuffling. Where probabilities appear they are exact rationals or exhaustive dynamic programmes. Where a search appears it is a complete enumeration of a space small enough to enumerate. Two runs produce byte-identical output, and so will yours.

The design constraint was that every claim in this article about what is structurally the case — as opposed to what happens to be true of this year's models — should be checkable by someone who does not trust the article. The cost of that constraint is that the systems are tiny. §23 takes the resulting objection seriously.

What each experiment establishes

  • E01, span. Four covariates, five environments, all sixteen candidate predictor sets checked exactly. Three environments shifting one parent leave eight survivors and an intersection of one variable; sixteen more of the same kind change nothing; one environment shifting the other parent leaves four survivors and recovers the truth. Identification is a property of the family of environments, not of its size.
  • E02, coverage. With eight binary shift dimensions, the identity plus all singletons is 3.52% of the class. A system failing only on two-or-more co-occurring shifts passes 100% of that suite and fails 96.48% of the class. A budget of a billion tests exhaustively certifies twenty-nine dimensions.
  • E03, orbit. 432 instances across an orbit that leaves every answer unchanged. A positional heuristic correct on 100% of canonical statements scores 11.11%; patching the one distractor it was shown lifts it to 22.22%; a structural parser is invariant on all 72 members of every orbit.
  • E04, forgetting. Exact rational linear algebra in twelve dimensions. Sequential learning costs the old task 0, 3.36, 7.58, 11 squared error at overlaps 0 to 3. Null-space projection makes that exactly 0 and moves the identical 11 onto the new task. Four disjoint three-constraint tasks fit in twelve dimensions; the fifth has a residual it cannot reduce.
  • E05, store. A thousand-slot store at half load returns another item's value on 24.22% of queries with no signal. A key-tag converts every one of those into a detected miss and changes recall by zero. Beyond capacity, pigeonhole does the rest.
  • E06, horizon. Exact dynamic programming over accumulated errors. A hundredfold detector delivers 100% of its promised horizon at zero self-conditioning, 54.1% at g = 0.2, and 23.8% at g = 1.0; a thousandfold detector delivers 19.9% and 6.1%.
  • E07, correction. The break-even precision of a correction loop is w/(r+w), verified across seven operating points spanning error rates from 0.01 to 0.30 and sensitivities from 0.50 to 0.99. The threshold contains neither. Iterating converges to fw/(sr+fw), which is above the starting error rate whenever the checker is below threshold.
  • E08, proxies. All 7,775 non-trivial integer proxies enumerated against a concave objective, with ties broken adversarially. None reaches the true optimum. The one proxy that orders every training-region pair correctly realises 76.06%.
  • E09, conventions. Exhaustive cross-play over all ordered pairs of conventions. Four labels give 25.00%; canonicalising a shared symmetry gives 100%; canonicalising different symmetries gives 29.17%.
  • E10, dictionaries. Every exact decomposition of 68 activations enumerated. 35.29% are ambiguous, 23.53% remain ambiguous after minimum sparsity, and 11.76% report a two-feature composition as one composite feature.
  • E11, calibration. Integer counts throughout. Accuracy 0.7500 before and after; expected calibration error 0.0000 before and 0.0900 after; the required per-bin corrections are not a monotone reparameterisation, so no source-fitted temperature reproduces them.

What it does not establish

No experiment here involves a neural network, and none is intended as a model of one. The role of the laboratory is to separate claims that are about contingent properties of current systems — which are cited, not computed — from claims that are about the structure of the problem, which are computed and can be checked. When those two kinds of claim get mixed, arguments in this field become unresolvable, because one party is talking about what is measured and the other about what is possible.

21The ledger

--  ·  the gauge across twenty-nine plates

Twenty-nine plates, five codes, one table. The assignment on each plate is a judgement about where the binding constraint currently sits; the totals are what those judgements add up to.

Evaluation carries sixteen plates and identification fifteen. Specification carries seven, credit and budget six each. Whatever else is true, this is not a field whose central difficulty is that the fixes are too expensive. Six plates of twenty-nine are budget-bound, and two of those — the capacity of a protected network and the capacity of a bounded store — are conservation laws rather than costs, which means they will not be relieved by spending.

The dominance of E and I is worth stating as a claim rather than an observation, because it is the article's main non-obvious result. The modal unsolved problem in this list is not one where we know what to do and cannot afford it, nor one where we lack an idea. It is one where the violation is invisible at the moment it matters (E), or where the evidence available cannot distinguish the system that has the property from the system that merely looks as though it does (I).

Those two have a relationship. An I failure is what an E failure becomes when you try to fix it by measuring harder: you collect more of the evidence you had, and the additional evidence is compatible with both hypotheses. That is the pattern in §8's world models, §10's calibration, §19's dictionaries and §7's causal structure, and it is why those four sections all end with the same recommendation — not more data, a different kind of data.

The bands are uneven in an informative way. The inference band carries eleven plates because there are more distinguishable invariants there, not because it is worse. The instrument band carries two, and they are the two on which the rest of the ledger's E entries depend: if a discovered feature is not stable across seeds and a decomposition is not identifiable, then the programme for making violations visible has its own visibility problem.

22What follows

--  ·  four conclusions, stated to be argued with

Four conclusions, stated so they can be argued with.

1. A capability claim without a perturbation class is not yet a claim

“The system can reason”, “the model has a world model”, “the agent is reliable” — none of these has a refutation condition until the class is named. Naming it is cheap and almost nobody does it. The recommendation is procedural and specific.

Steps one and five are free. Step three is arithmetic. Step four is the only expensive one, and it is expensive in a way that produces knowledge: constructing a separator forces you to say what the rival hypothesis is, and a surprising amount of disagreement in this field survives only because nobody has been made to say that.

2. The two dominant failure codes have remedies that are not more of the same

For E, the remedy is an instrument that fires in deployment rather than in the laboratory: a tag that turns a silent wrong answer into a detected miss, a postcondition that turns a silently-changed contract into an error, a commitment re-presented for checking rather than left to decay in a context window. Each of these costs something legible — storage, latency, tokens — and the cost is the reason they are not there, which makes them decisions rather than open problems.

For I, the remedy is a perturbation that separates. An intervention where there was only observation. An environment that moves what every previous environment held fixed. A modality withheld at training. A second seed, with feature agreement reported as a headline number. These are not more expensive than what is currently done; they are differently expensive, and they are the only thing that works, because the alternative is collecting more evidence from a family that does not span.

3. Two of the sixteen are conservation laws, and should be described as such

The capacity that buys retention and the capacity that buys plasticity are one quantity (§12). The slots that hold independent facts are bounded below by the number of facts (§13). Neither will be dissolved by a method. What is open in both cases is the policy — what to protect, what to forget, on what evidence — and the policy question is barely being asked, because the field is still framing these as problems to be solved rather than budgets to be allocated. A system that runs indefinitely must decide what to lose. Nobody has written down how it should decide.

4. The arithmetic of horizon cuts both ways, and both are load-bearing

Marginal per-step gains compound into large horizon gains; that is real and is the strongest argument that progress is faster than short-task benchmarks suggest. Per-step error is not position-independent, and repairs remain in the trace; that is also real and puts a ceiling on what detection buys. Anyone forecasting from horizon measurements should be able to say which of these dominates in their extrapolation, and the honest answer at present is that nobody knows, because the self-conditioning coefficient has been demonstrated but not measured across model generations.

The one-sentence version

The sixteen problems are sixteen invariance claims; the reason they are open is, in twenty-two of twenty-nine cases, that we cannot see the violation or cannot tell apart the systems that would and would not produce it; and both of those are properties of our instruments rather than of the systems, which is either encouraging or alarming depending on how quickly one expects instruments to improve.

What would refute this

The article's central claim is a taxonomy claim, and taxonomies are refuted by cases they cannot classify or classify wrongly. Three things would do it. First, a demonstrated failure on this list whose binding constraint is none of the five — which would show the taxonomy is not exhaustive. Second, a case where relieving the identified code does not move the problem, which would show the assignment is not diagnostic. Third, and most interesting, a general method that improves an invariant without either specifying it, identifying it, attributing violations, detecting them, or paying for them — which would show the taxonomy is not carving anything real. The scaling hypothesis in its strong form is close to that third claim, and if it is right this article is a period piece. A weaker version of the same challenge is already on the table: several apparently discontinuous capability jumps turn out to be artefacts of the metric rather than of the model, which is a warning that a taxonomy of failures built on measurements inherits whatever is wrong with the measurements.214

23Against this article

--  ·  four objections, the last one conceded

Four objections, in ascending order of how much they worry the author.

“The taxonomy is arbitrary”

Five codes, chosen by one person, with plates assigned by judgement. Nothing prevents a different partition — one could fold credit into specification, or split evaluation into detectability and attributability, and get different totals. This is fair. The defence is that the codes were chosen for a property that can be checked: each implies a different remedy, and the remedies do not substitute for one another. If two codes had the same remedy they should be merged, and if a code had no remedy it should be dropped. Anyone who wants to argue the partition should argue it that way, and the totals in §21 would then be worth recomputing.

“The empirical claims will date”

Some of them already have a short shelf life. The specific numbers on interactive benchmarks are from a fast-moving frontier, and previous versions of those benchmarks have moved from single digits to near-saturation within a year. This is a real limitation and the article tries to handle it by separating the two kinds of claim: cited numbers about current systems, which will date, and computed results about the structure of the problems, which will not. Where a section rests entirely on the first kind — §11 is the clearest case — it says so.

“The framing smuggles in pessimism”

Stating each problem as an invariance claim guarantees that each will be found violated, since exact invariance is not achievable by anything, including people. The framing therefore appears to build in its own conclusion. The reply is that the article's conclusions are not about whether the invariants hold — they do not, and nobody claimed otherwise — but about which of five reasons explains each failure, and that question is not prejudiced by the framing. It is also worth noting that the framing produces some optimistic results: §15's threshold says when a correction loop does help, and §14's arithmetic is the strongest available argument that horizon progress is faster than short-task benchmarks suggest.

“The laboratory does not generalise, and it is doing the persuading”

This is the strongest objection and it should be conceded further than is comfortable. The experiments are exhaustive over systems with twelve dimensions, seven atoms, four covariates, thirty-two training policies. The systems this article is about have on the order of a trillion parameters. Exhaustiveness over a small space is not evidence about a large one, and the inference from “this is what happens in a system small enough to enumerate” to “this is what happens in a transformer” is an inference, not a proof, at every point where it is made.

What can be said in defence has two parts, and neither is fully satisfying. The first is that several of the results are theorems in disguise rather than empirical findings: the pigeonhole bound in §13, the rank arithmetic in §12, the break-even precision in §15 and the coverage fractions in §2 are consequences of counting and linear algebra, and counting does not stop applying at scale. The second is that where the lab result has an empirical counterpart in the cited literature, the two agree in direction — the orbit collapse in E03 against GSM-Symbolic, the plasticity exhaustion in E04 against the continual-learning literature, the proxy shortfall in E08 against measured reward-model over-optimisation, the cross-play collapse in E09 against the driving-convention experiment.

Agreement in direction is not agreement in magnitude, and the article does not claim any of the lab's specific numbers transfer. What it claims is narrower: that the mechanism is present at small scale, in a form where it can be seen completely, and that the burden is therefore on the claim that something at large scale removes it rather than on the claim that it persists. That is a defensible position and it is not a proof, and a reader who declines to take the step has not made an error.

24Where to read further

--  ·  where to read further

Grouped by what they are good for rather than by topic.

  • The invariance idea itself. Peters, Bühlmann and Meinshausen for the original statement and its guarantee;9 the textbook for the surrounding theory;50 Bühlmann's review for the connection between invariance, causality and robustness stated compactly.215 Then Rosenfeld and Kamath for what happened when the idea met deep learning.54,55
  • If you read one thing on measurement. D'Amour et al. on underspecification, which explains more observed behaviour per page than anything else in this bibliography.69 Then Geirhos on shortcut learning,74 Burnell et al. on how results should be reported,216 Raji et al. on what a general-purpose benchmark can and cannot mean,217 and Liao and Xiao on the gap between what is measured and what is wanted.218
  • Continual learning, honestly. French's 1999 review remains the clearest statement of the problem;107 Dohare et al. for the plasticity half112 and the 2026 transformer study for whether scale rescues it.113
  • Agents and horizon. The long-horizon execution paper for the mechanism,15 METR for the measurement,132 τ-bench for the metric that should be standard,153 and MAST for where multi-agent systems actually break.139
  • Alignment as a technical problem. Zhuang and Hadfield-Menell for the theorem,175 Gao et al. for the curve,174 Casper et al. for the limitations of the dominant method,187 and the 2026 specification-gaming study for the current empirical picture.181
  • Interpretability, with its own caveats attached. The circuit-tracing and biology papers for what the method can now do;202,203 Sharkey et al. for the field's own list of what it cannot;208 Meloux et al. for the identifiability question.207
  • The sceptical and the constructive extremes. Chollet on measuring intelligence as acquisition efficiency;93 the AGI definition grounded in a psychometric framework;2 Silver and Sutton for the case that experience replaces data;99 Marcus and Mitchell for the case that the paradigm is missing something structural.219,220 Reading these four together is more useful than reading any of them alone.
  • Testing practice worth importing. Metamorphic testing is the software-engineering name for everything this article calls an invariance claim, and it has thirty years of methodology attached.221,222,223 CheckList is the closest thing to it that this field adopted, and it was adopted narrowly.224

A closing note on what is missing from this article. It says nothing about multimodal perception, embodiment, or robotics, where several of these invariants take sharper forms and the literature is separate. It says nothing about compute, energy or the economics of deployment. And it treats the sixteen as technical problems throughout, which is a choice: several of them, alignment most obviously, have a component that no amount of technical work addresses, because the question of whose intentions and which ones is not a question about systems.194
