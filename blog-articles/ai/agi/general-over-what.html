§1

The word that does the work

Almost every disagreement about artificial general intelligence is a disagreement about the range of a quantifier.

Three letters, and the argument is almost entirely about the middle one. Nobody disputes that the systems in question are artificial. Few disputes about intelligence survive contact with a concrete task; ask whether a system can prove a theorem, drive a route or diagnose a fault and the question becomes tractable, if not always easy. The word that carries the disagreement is general, and it carries it silently, because it looks like an adjective when it is in fact a quantifier with an unstated domain.

Consider the difference between two claims. The first is that a system solves problems of type X. The second is that a system solves problems in general. The first is a statement about a set of instances; the second is a statement about a set of sets of instances, and it is meaningless until somebody says which sets. A chess engine is general over chess positions and not over anything else. A calculator is general over arithmetic expressions in a way no human is, and general over nothing else at all. When we say that a person is generally intelligent we do not mean that they can do everything; we mean something about the width of the class of things they can be brought to do, the speed with which they can be brought to do them, and the fact that the class was not fixed in advance by whoever built them.

This article takes the question general over what? as its organising instrument, and applies it to a specific inventory: the thirty-five capacities that recur in serious attempts to say what artificial general intelligence would consist of, grouped under generalization, learning, reasoning, planning, problem solving, adaptability and autonomy. The claim being defended is that these thirty-five are not a checklist of independent abilities that a system either has or lacks. They are thirty-five different answers to the question of what is allowed to vary, and they can be totally ordered — or nearly so — by how much variation the capacity has to absorb. That ordering is the article's device, and it does real work: it explains why measured progress on some of these capacities has been extraordinarily fast while progress on others has barely started, and it predicts which benchmark results will prove durable.

The argument runs in three movements. The first traces three distinct intellectual inheritances of the word general — from psychometrics, from computability theory, and from the engineering tradition of artificial intelligence itself — and shows that they license different and partly incompatible claims. The second establishes the formal result that constrains all of them: generality in any interesting sense is not a quantity a system can have more of in the abstract, because averaged over all possible tasks every method performs identically. Generality is always relative to a prior, and a prior is a bet on a vanishingly small corner of the space of possible problems. The third movement walks the thirty-five capacities one at a time, placing each on the ladder, and closes by asking whether they cohere into a single underlying factor — a machine analogue of Spearman's g — or merely appear to.

Throughout, numbers quoted in the text come from a small suite of deterministic experiments run at build time, whose complete output is reproduced verbatim near the end. They are not simulations of intelligence. They are demonstrations that the constraints under discussion are arithmetic rather than rhetorical: that no-free-lunch is a theorem you can compute in a few lines, that transfer changes sign at a measurable angle, that a proxy objective correlated at 0.753 with the real one still costs you something specific, and that a positive correlation between benchmark scores can be manufactured out of abilities that are, by construction, unrelated.

§2

Three inheritances of a single word

Psychometrics, computability and engineering each supply a meaning for general. They do not agree.

The phrase general intelligence did not originate in computer science. It arrives with a hundred-and-twenty-year history attached, and much of the confusion in current debate comes from importing the phrase without importing the conditions under which it was defined.

The psychometric inheritance. Spearman's 1904 observation was statistical, not psychological: scores on unrelated mental tests correlate positively, and a single common factor accounts for a substantial share of the variance [1]. This positive manifold is one of the most replicated findings in the behavioural sciences [2–4], and the modern three-stratum synthesis places g at the apex of a hierarchy of broad and narrow abilities [2,5], with correlates measurable well below the behavioural level [6]. Two features of this inheritance matter here and are routinely dropped in transit. First, g is a claim about the covariance structure of a population: it says that individuals who do well on one thing tend to do well on another. It is not a claim about a faculty inside any individual, and the same correlational data are consistent with a model in which no such faculty exists and the manifold emerges from mutual reinforcement among genuinely separate capacities during development [7]. Second, the tests are calibrated on a population with a shared architecture, a shared developmental history and roughly comparable exposure. Every one of those conditions fails for machines. A battery of tests administered to a set of systems that differ in training data by orders of magnitude does not measure what the same battery measures across a set of humans. The most careful current attempt to build an AGI definition on this foundation is explicit about borrowing the Cattell–Horn–Carroll structure and weighting its domains equally [8,9]; its most interesting result is not the headline score but the observation that scores are radically uneven across domains, which is precisely what a single dominant factor would not predict.

The computability inheritance. A quite different meaning of general descends from Turing: a universal machine is one that can simulate any other, so its generality is a statement about a class of computations, proved rather than measured [10,11]. The imitation game shifted the question from mechanism to behaviour but kept the ambition of a single decisive criterion [12] — an ambition inherited directly by the 1955 Dartmouth proposal, which asserted that every aspect of learning and intelligence could in principle be described precisely enough to be simulated [13]. That a purely behavioural criterion underdetermines the internal facts is the standing objection, pressed from three directions: that syntax does not suffice for semantics [14], that a large enough lookup table would pass any finite behavioural test [15], and that the situated know-how underlying competence resists the propositional encoding the programme requires [16]. The most rigorous modern development in this line defines intelligence as expected performance across all computable environments, weighted by a universal prior on their descriptions [17–20]. This is a genuine definition with genuine consequences: it makes generality precise, it makes it measurable in principle, and it makes explicit that the measure depends on a choice of reference machine. The difficulty is that the dependence is not a technicality. The prior is uncomputable; the ordering it induces on agents can be altered by choosing a different but equally legitimate universal machine [21]; and the resulting quantity is undefined for any system anyone has built. The tradition's honest descendants therefore turn the definition into a measurement programme with an explicit reference machine and an explicit budget [22,23].

The engineering inheritance. The third meaning is the oldest inside the field and the least glamorous. McCarthy's 1987 Turing Award lecture used generality to name a practical deficiency: programs of that era had to be told, in advance and in full, the vocabulary of the situations they would face [24]. Generality in this sense is the property of not needing to be rebuilt when the world supplies something the builder did not anticipate. Newell's programme sought a single architecture whose mechanisms would cover the whole range of cognitive tasks rather than a family of specialists [25–29]. The term artificial general intelligence itself was consolidated in this tradition, as a name for a research programme deliberately distinguished from task-specific AI [30,31], and the standard textbook treatment still organises the field around agents whose competence is bounded by their environment rather than by a task list [32]. Wang's formulation is the sharpest: intelligence is adaptation under insufficient knowledge and resources — the system is finite, works in real time, is open to novel input, and must learn from its own experience [33]. On that definition generality is not width of coverage at all; it is a relationship between a system and the conditions under which it must operate, and a system with unlimited resources solving a fixed problem class would not qualify however wide the class.

These three do not merely emphasise different things; they disagree about what would count as evidence. On the psychometric view, evidence is a correlation matrix over a battery. On the computability view, evidence is a proof about a class, and empirical scores are beside the point. On the engineering view, evidence is behaviour under conditions the builder did not foresee — which is exactly what a fixed battery cannot supply, because a battery is foreseen by construction. A great many arguments about whether some system is or is not approaching general intelligence are two people applying two of these three standards and finding, correctly, that the other's evidence does not bear on their question.

What all three do share is a structure: each specifies a set over which competence must hold, and each says something about what may vary within that set. That shared structure is what makes an ordering possible.

§3

The scope ladder

Seven rungs, ordered by how much variation a capacity has to absorb between training and test.

Fix a system and a capacity. Ask two questions about it. What is allowed to differ between the conditions under which the system acquired the capacity and the conditions under which it is asked to exercise it? And what is being held constant, without which the capacity would not be exercisable at all? The pair of answers locates the capacity on a ladder with seven rungs, reproduced in the figure below and used to organise everything that follows.

The rungs are not arbitrary levels of difficulty. Each is defined by a specific object that becomes free to vary. At rung 2 the sample varies and the distribution does not. At rung 3 the distribution varies and the task does not. At rung 4 the task varies and the family it is drawn from does not. At rung 5 the family varies and the interface does not. At rung 6 the interface itself varies — the world supplies instruments and dynamics that were not in the specification. At rung 7 even the objective is free. Rung 1 is included because it is the floor: a capacity exercised on an item already seen is retrieval, and a contaminated benchmark silently demotes every item it contains to this rung.

Three properties of this ladder are worth stating before it is used. First, it is a ladder of quantifier scope, not of performance. A system can be extremely good at a rung-2 capacity and useless at a rung-4 one, or the reverse; nothing about the ordering implies that higher rungs are harder for any particular architecture. What the ordering does imply is that a claim at rung k does not entail a claim at rung k + 1, and that most overclaiming in this field consists of a measurement at one rung reported as evidence for the next.

Second, the ladder is a statement about the test, not about the system. The same behaviour can sit at different rungs depending on how it was elicited. Solving a physics problem is a rung-5 capacity if the problem is unlike anything in training and a rung-1 capacity if the problem is in the training corpus verbatim. This is why contamination is not a hygiene issue but a measurement-theoretic one: it does not add noise to the estimate, it changes what is being estimated [34].

Third, the higher rungs are progressively less measurable, and the drop-off is steep. Rungs 2 and 3 have mature methodology, standard datasets and agreed statistics. Rung 4 has meta-learning protocols with held-out task splits. Rung 5 has a handful of novelty-controlled batteries. Rung 6 has a small number of interactive benchmarks, most of them under two years old. Rung 7 has essentially nothing, because the standard form of a benchmark item is a task, and supplying the task is precisely what a rung-7 test must not do. The distribution of measurement effort is close to the inverse of the distribution of remaining difficulty, which is a structural reason to expect benchmark progress to keep outrunning progress on the thing the benchmarks are proxies for.

§4

Why generality is always a bet

Averaged over all tasks, every learner is identical. Generality is not a quantity; it is a wager on which corner of task space is real.

The most important formal fact about generality is a negative one, and it is easy to state and easy to compute. Fix a domain of eight points and consider every possible Boolean function on it: two hundred and fifty-six tasks, no more and no fewer. Give a learner the labels on four points and score it only on the four it has not seen — off-training-set error, which is the only kind the theorem concerns. The result is not approximate:

Every learner scores exactly 0.5000 when averaged over all two hundred and fifty-six tasks. Not approximately; exactly, and for the same reason in each case. The learner that always predicts zero, the nearest-neighbour learner, the learner that picks the shortest hypothesis consistent with the data, and the learner that deliberately picks the longest consistent hypothesis are indistinguishable in aggregate. This is the no-free-lunch theorem for supervised learning [35,36], with its optimisation counterpart [37], and computing it rather than citing it makes the shape of the result visible: for every task on which a bias helps, there is a task on which it hurts by exactly as much.

The interesting numbers are in the other two columns. Restricted to the thirty-two functions of low description length — those computable by a decision tree with at most three leaves, 12.50% of the space — the Occam learner scores 0.6875, an advantage of 18.8 points over chance. On the complement it scores 0.4732, a deficit of 2.7 points. The asymmetry is not a violation of the theorem; it is the theorem's structure. The advantage on a small subclass is large and the compensating loss on the large complement is small, and the products are equal. A prior is a bet that reality lives in the small subclass.

How small is that corner? Small enough to be worth counting exactly. Take every binary string of length sixteen, all 65,536, and ask how many can be described in fewer bits than they contain under a fixed code. Two honest prefix codes were tried: a run-length code and a periodicity code. The counting bound applies to any code whatsoever — there are fewer than 216−k descriptions shorter than 16 − k bits, so at most that many strings can be compressed by k bits, whatever the encoding [38–42].

At a saving of four bits the universal bound admits at most 4,095 strings, or 6.25% of the space. The run-length code actually reaches six of them and the periodicity code 232. Their union is 0.360% of the space, and 99.640% of all length-sixteen strings are simple under neither code. More striking is the overlap: the two sets of simple strings share a Jaccard similarity of 0.0085. Two perfectly reasonable notions of simplicity carve out almost disjoint corners of the same tiny region.

This is the sense in which generality is a wager rather than a property. A system is general with respect to a prior; the prior is what allows it to prefer one of the many hypotheses consistent with its data; and the prior is necessarily concentrated on a set of measure close to zero. Two systems can both be general and be general over almost disjoint territory. The question general over what? is therefore not pedantry but the whole content of the claim, and the only substantive way to compare two systems' generality is to compare the corners they bet on and ask which one the world happens to occupy.

This reframing has an immediate consequence for how the rest of the article proceeds. The right question to ask about each of the thirty-five capacities is not “can the system do this?” but “over what does the system's competence at this range, and what prior is buying that range?” Where the prior is a well-chosen bet on the structure of the actual world, the capacity is genuine and its scope is exactly the scope of the bet. Where the prior is a bet on the structure of the benchmark, the capacity evaporates on contact with anything else. Chollet's formulation of intelligence as skill-acquisition efficiency at fixed prior is the sharpest available attempt to hold the prior constant so that the remainder can be measured [43], and the developmental-psychology notion of core knowledge is the standard candidate for what the fixed prior ought to contain [44,45].

§5

Generalization: the five scopes

Five capacities that differ only in what is allowed to move between training and test.

The first cluster is the ladder in miniature. Its five members are usually presented as a list of techniques; they are better understood as five settings of a single dial, and reading them that way explains why results transfer so poorly between them.

In-distribution generalization holds the distribution fixed and lets the sample vary. That this works at all in high-capacity models is still not fully explained: the same architectures that generalise from real data will also fit random labels perfectly, which rules out any account resting on capacity alone [46], and error can fall a second time past the point of interpolation [47]. Out-of-distribution generalization frees the distribution and holds the task. Here the theory is clear about the shape of the answer — the achievable bound depends on a divergence between the two distributions, so the guarantee weakens exactly as the shift grows [48] — and the empirical record is sobering: rebuilding a test set by the original protocol costs accuracy [49], and under a controlled comparison no proposed domain-generalisation method reliably beats ordinary risk minimisation [50].

Transfer moves one rung further, to a new task. The single most useful fact about it is that it is signed. The lab trained a small regression model on a source task and then on targets rotated away from it by an increasing angle:

At perfect alignment the head start removes 97.9% of the from-scratch loss. The advantage crosses zero at 63.0 degrees of misalignment, and at full opposition the pre-trained initialisation makes the final loss 3.93 times worse than starting from nothing. Negative transfer is not a pathology of a bad method; it is what transfer looks like when the bet is wrong [51–53].

Few-shot and zero-shot learning are where the prior becomes impossible to ignore, because they are the settings in which the data manifestly do not determine the answer. In the classic number game, the learner sees a handful of positive examples and must generalise [54]. What the data underdetermine has to be supplied, and the formal learning-theory tradition made the point structurally long before the Bayesian one made it quantitatively: whole classes of languages cannot be identified from positive examples alone however many are supplied [55], and changing what the learner may ask for changes what is learnable at all [56]. The lab's implementation, with thirty-four mathematical concepts and 5,050 intervals in the hypothesis space, puts posterior mass 0.9903 on powers of two after three examples. The ablation is the point: delete the mathematical concepts, leaving only intervals, and the same four examples — 16, 8, 2, 64 — give a generalisation probability of 1.0000 for both 32 and 60. The learner is not worse at inference. It is inferring correctly within a space that cannot express the right answer, and the visible symptom is over-general extrapolation rather than error. Few-shot competence is a property of the hypothesis space first and the inference procedure second [57–59].

§6

Learning: who controls the distribution

What varies is not only the source of the signal but who controls the distribution it arrives in.

The six learning capacities in the taxonomy are usually distinguished by where the training signal comes from: examples, instructions, experience, or the data itself. A more useful axis is who controls the distribution. In learning from examples and self-supervised learning, the distribution is fixed by whoever assembled the corpus, and the system's competence is bounded by it. In learning from instruction, a description substitutes for the data, which moves the quantifier from a distribution to the set of tasks a language can specify — a much larger set, and one whose boundary is fuzzy in an important way, since the ability to follow an instruction is not the same as the ability to identify what the instruction was for [68,69]. In learning from experience, the system's own policy determines the data it will see next, which is what makes it a rung-6 capacity and also what makes it unstable [70,71].

Continual learning and meta-learning are the two that are most often misdescribed. Meta-learning is frequently glossed as “learning to learn”, which suggests an escape from the constraints of the previous section. It is not one. Meta-learning explicitly optimises expected post-adaptation loss over a task distribution p(T), and its generality stops precisely at the support of that distribution [72–74]. This is not a criticism; it is the mechanism. What meta-learning buys is an inductive bias tuned to a family, which is the best available construction for rung 4 and no help at all at rung 5 unless the family was drawn wide enough to contain the target, in which case the target was never outside it.

Continual learning is the capacity where the arithmetic is most brutal, and the lab measures it directly. A linear model trained to convergence on task A reaches loss 0.0010 there. Trained afterwards on a task 60 degrees away, it reaches 0.0012 on the new task and 0.9971 on the old one — the error on A multiplied by 969. Retention and plasticity trade against each other along a curve, and the methods move along the curve rather than lifting it. Worse, the trade-off is not static: a network trained long enough on a stream of tasks loses the ability to learn new ones at all, a distinct failure from forgetting and one that plain gradient descent exhibits reliably [76]. The field has not settled on what continual competence should even be defined as [77–80].

§7

Reasoning: seven inferences, one of them safe

Only one of the seven is truth-preserving. The other six trade validity for reach, and the exchange rate is the prior.

The reasoning cluster is the most heterogeneous in the taxonomy, and the heterogeneity is principled. Deduction is the only member that is truth-preserving: if the premises hold, the conclusion holds, in every structure whatsoever. That is the widest quantifier in the whole inventory, and it is bought by having no content — a deductive system knows nothing about the world and therefore cannot be wrong about it [100]. The remaining six all extend beyond their premises, and each pays for the extension in a different currency.

Induction pays with a prior, as established above. Abduction pays with a hypothesis space: inference to the best explanation is only as good as the worst explanation you thought to consider, and Peirce's original point was that abduction is the sole form of inference that introduces a new idea rather than rearranging old ones [101]. The computational literature has largely studied the easier problem in which the candidate explanations are supplied, and even that is intractable in general [102]. Generating the candidates is the part that would matter for general intelligence, and there is essentially no benchmark for it, because writing one requires the test designer to withhold the hypothesis space while still being able to score the answer.

Analogy pays with a choice of what counts as the same. Structure-mapping theory formalises this as alignment of relational structure while ignoring surface features [103,104], and abstraction of this kind has been argued to be the central unsolved problem of the field [105]. The empirical record here is a clean illustration of rung confusion. Large language models perform well on standard matrix and letter-string analogies, which was reported as emergent analogical reasoning [106]. Reconstructing the same items with counterfactual variants — same relational structure, different surface conventions — degrades performance substantially while leaving human performance largely intact [107]. The first result is a rung-4 measurement; the claim it was taken to support is a rung-5 claim; the second study is the rung-5 measurement.

Causal reasoning pays with a graph. The formal contribution of the causal-inference literature is to show that interventional and counterfactual quantities are not functions of the observational distribution: no amount of data from p(y | x) determines p(y | do(x)) without assumptions about mechanism [108,109]. This is a scope result of exactly the kind this article is organised around: it says which rung a purely predictive system can reach and where it must stop. The open engineering problem is learning representations in which the assumptions are even statable [110].

Probabilistic reasoning pays with a sample space, and its distinctive failure mode is not incoherence but miscalibration — confidence that tracks fluency rather than evidence [111–114]. Mathematical reasoning pays with axioms and, crucially, buys back a verifier. That last point deserves emphasis, because mathematics is the one domain in this cluster where the correctness of a candidate answer can be checked mechanically and cheaply relative to the cost of producing it. Reinforcement learning inside a proof assistant turned that asymmetry into olympiad-level performance, generating variants of the target problem at test time and training on them [115], following the same pattern in geometry [116]. The caveat is instructive rather than incidental: the competition problems were formalised into the proof assistant's language by human experts before the system saw them, so the reported result is a rung-5 measurement inside a formal interface and not a measurement of the capacity to construct that interface. The lesson generalises poorly for a precise reason: most tasks do not come with verifiers, and the capacities that would supply one — self-monitoring and error correction — are themselves near the top of the ladder.

§8

Planning: the tyranny of the exponent

Long-horizon competence is bounded by per-step reliability before it is bounded by reasoning.

Planning is where a system's competence has to survive being multiplied by itself. The classical results are about search: given a model of the world's dynamics and a goal, find a sequence of actions reaching it [25,126,127]. The complexity results are unforgiving even in the propositional case [128], which is why every practical planner is a heuristic, and a heuristic is a prior in a different costume.

The result that has organised the last two years of empirical work, though, is simpler than any of that, and it is arithmetic. If a task requires H steps and each step succeeds independently with probability p, the task succeeds with probability pH. Inverting gives the horizon at which the system is right half the time:

At 95% per-step reliability the fifty-percent horizon is 13.5 steps. At 99% it is 69.0. To complete a thousand-step task at even odds, per-step reliability must reach 0.99931. This is the curve on which measured time horizons for frontier systems are reported, and reading capability growth as horizon growth rather than as accuracy growth is what makes progress on it legible [129]. It also explains a persistent puzzle: why systems that answer hard questions correctly nonetheless fail at long tasks that a competent person finds routine. The two are different statistics of the same underlying competence, related by an exponent.

Error correction changes the exponent's base, which is why it matters out of all proportion to its apparent modesty. If an external checker catches a fraction d of errors and one retry is permitted, effective per-step reliability rises from p to p + (1 − p)dp. At p = 0.95 and d = 0.80 that is 0.9880, lifting the horizon from 13.5 to 57.4 steps, a factor of 4.2. But the checker's own reliability enters multiplicatively: a checker that is itself only 90% reliable delivers 43.5 steps instead of 57.4. Verification is not a free operation applied from outside the system; when the system verifies itself, the verifier's competence is drawn from the same pool as the generator's, which is the formal reason that intrinsic self-correction does not reliably help [130,131].

Hierarchical planning is the classical answer to the exponent, and the lab measures both what it buys and what it costs. In a world of rooms joined by locked doors, where the key to each door is hidden in an earlier room and the correspondence between keys and doors is scrambled, a flat search over position-and-inventory is optimal but expands states exponentially in the number of keys. Decomposing into subgoals is cheaper:

At seven keys the flat search expands 2.68 times as many states as the decomposed one, and the ratio grows monotonically with the number of subgoals. The price is stated in the last two columns. A greedy decomposition — always fetch the nearest reachable key — returns an optimal plan on 73.3% of instances at seven keys, down from 100% at one. Taking the subgoals in the order the goal names them, which is the decomposition a reader of the goal description would naturally write down, succeeds on 3.3%. Decomposition is not a general technique; it is a bet that the subgoals are serially independent, and the bet's failure rate is a measurable property of the domain [132–135].

§9

Problem solving: the part that happens before the search

Representation is the highest-leverage capacity in the taxonomy and the least measured.

Three of the four capacities in this cluster — decomposition, strategy selection, error correction — operate on a problem that has already been formulated. The fourth operates on the formulation itself, and it is the one that matters most and is measured least.

Amarel's demonstration remains the canonical statement. Taking the missionaries-and-cannibals problem and successively re-encoding it, he showed that a change of representation can collapse a search problem to something a person solves by inspection, without any change to the search algorithm [145]. The problem space, in the classical theory, is not a container for the problem; it is a commitment about what the problem is [146]. Choosing it well is the highest-leverage act in problem solving, and it is the act that benchmarks almost never require, because a benchmark item arrives with its representation attached. When a dataset presents a task as a grid of coloured cells, or a natural-language question, or a state vector, the hardest decision has already been made by the test designer. This is a systematic blind spot: the capacity most associated with human problem-solving insight is the one our evaluation instruments are structurally incapable of testing. Recent work probing whether a trained model has recovered anything like the right state space — rather than a surface-fitting substitute that predicts well and generalises badly — suggests the concern is not hypothetical [147].

Strategy selection is better behaved, because it can be posed formally: given a portfolio of methods and features of an instance, choose the method with least expected cost [148,149]. It is also the place where no-free-lunch reappears one level up. Selecting among methods requires a prior over instances, so the meta-level inherits exactly the problem it was introduced to solve, and the regress terminates only where some prior is accepted without justification [37].

Error correction has been discussed above in its arithmetic role. Its epistemic role is worth separating. To correct an error you must first be able to tell that it is one, and the interesting cases are precisely those where no external signal is available. The empirical finding is consistent across studies: without an oracle, revision does not reliably improve reasoning and frequently degrades it, because the same model that produced the error scores it [130,131]. Where an independent checker exists — a compiler, a proof assistant, a unit test — the picture changes completely, which is why the domains showing the steepest recent progress are the domains with cheap verifiers.

§10

Adaptability: the open world

Three of these four capacities require the environment to supply something the specification did not.

Adaptability is where the taxonomy leaves the space of well-posed problems. Novel situations, new tools and open-ended environments share a property that distinguishes them from everything below: the system must respond to something that was not in the specification, and no amount of coverage within the specification prepares it. This is the condition McCarthy named in 1987 and it has not changed [24,33]. It is also where the field's oldest empirical surprise lives: the capacities that are hardest to automate are not the ones humans find hard but the ones humans find effortless, because effortless competence is exactly the competence that was tuned by evolution against an open world rather than a specification [151,152].

The clearest empirical handle on this is the ARC-AGI programme, precisely because it was designed as a scope instrument rather than a difficulty instrument. Every task is unique, requires only core knowledge, and is verified solvable by ordinary people; in the second version, 100% of tasks were solved by at least two independent non-expert testers, with 407 participants across 515 sessions and a median solving time of 2.3 minutes per test pair [153,154]. The benchmark deliberately targets compositional generalization: multiple interacting rules, multi-step chains where each step depends on the last, rules whose application is gated by context, and symbols whose meaning is defined within the task itself.

The trajectory is the interesting part. At release in May 2025 the best frontier systems scored 3.0% on ARC-AGI-2, and the benchmark's authors noted that anything below 5% is indistinguishable from noise [153]. By July 2026 the verified leaderboard showed 92.5%, with several systems above 60% and the first version saturated at 98.5% [155]. Fourteen months moved a novelty-controlled benchmark from noise to near-solved.

What happened next is the reason this article is organised around scope rather than difficulty. The third version of the benchmark changed one thing: instead of presenting a static input–output puzzle, it puts the system inside an interactive environment in which it must explore, infer what the goal even is, build a model of the dynamics, and plan — with no natural-language instructions and no external knowledge [156]. Humans solve 100% of these environments. At the benchmark's release in March 2026 frontier systems scored below 1%. On the verified results table five months later, the best score is 30.16%, with most entries in the low single digits and several established systems not evaluated on it at all [155].

This is a rung change, not a difficulty increase. The static and interactive versions share their visual vocabulary, their core-knowledge priors, and their designers. What differs is that in the static version the task is given and in the interactive version it must be acquired. A field that had moved from 3% to 92.5% on the given-task version had moved to roughly 30% at best on the acquired-task version, in an environment humans find easy. If the thirty-five capacities were a checklist of difficulty levels this would be surprising. On a ladder of scope it is exactly what one expects, because rungs 6 and 7 are not further along the same road.

The constructive response to rung 6 is to stop treating the task distribution as fixed and start generating it. Unsupervised environment design produces training environments at the edge of the system's competence rather than sampling them from a static set [157]; open-ended agents built on language models set and revise their own curricula inside a persistent world [158]; and the position that open-endedness — the continual production of artefacts that are both novel and learnable — is a necessary ingredient rather than an optional one has been argued explicitly [159]. All three are attempts to move the quantifier by moving the generator, which is the only lever available once the task family stops being the thing that is fixed.

§11

Autonomy: the objective is not part of the problem

At the top of the ladder the system must supply what every benchmark supplies for it.

Autonomy is commonly read as a deployment property — the system runs without a human in the loop — and that reading undersells it. The four capacities in this cluster are the ones for which no external party supplies the missing information. In goal-directed behaviour, nobody supplies the goal's meaning across contexts. In self-monitoring, nobody supplies the label that says the output was wrong. In independent execution, nobody supplies the intervention when it goes off track. In resource management, nobody supplies the budget allocation.

Goal formulation and resource management are the only two capacities in the entire thirty-five that sit at rung 7, and they are the two that the standard evaluation format cannot reach. A benchmark item is, by construction, a task; an evaluation harness is, by construction, a budget. Supplying both is what makes the measurement reproducible, and withholding both is what a rung-7 test would require.

The cost of getting the objective wrong is not proportional to how wrong it is, and the lab quantifies the relationship. A small gridworld with three rewarding outcomes of different value, a discount of 0.95, and a proxy objective formed by perturbing the true rewards:

A proxy correlated at 0.753 with the true objective across states leaves 6.6% of the achievable return on the table on average, and reproduces the optimal policy exactly on only 55% of draws. At correlation 0.562 the average loss is 40.2%. The degradation is not smooth in the way the correlation suggests, because the policy is invariant to perturbations that do not move the argmax and then changes discontinuously when they do. Correlation between objectives is therefore a poor guide to the cost of substituting one for the other — the relevant statistic is whether the ordering of outcomes survives, not whether the values are close [84,136–138].

The optimisation-pressure story is subtler than folklore suggests, and the lab is worth reporting against the folklore. Sweeping the softmax temperature against one fixed misspecified proxy, the best temperature achieved 98.2% of optimum and fully greedy behaviour achieved 98.0% — a gap of 0.2 points. In this instance the damage was done at specification time, not at optimisation time. That is a finding about this domain rather than a general law, but it points somewhere useful: the tractable intervention is usually the objective, not the strength with which it is pursued.

Self-monitoring is the capacity that would most change the picture if it were solved, because it is the input to almost everything above it. A system that could reliably say I do not know this one would convert a large class of silent failures into abstentions, which are recoverable. Models do carry usable information about their own error rates [122], calibration is measurable and partly repairable [114,121], and the structural obstacle has been named precisely: evaluations that award nothing for abstention make confident guessing the score-maximising policy, so the training signal actively selects against the behaviour we want [123]. Independent execution inherits all of this, and the empirical literature on agents suggests the binding constraints are specification and coordination rather than raw capability [172–174].

§12

Is there a g for machines?

A positive manifold can be manufactured out of abilities that are, by construction, unrelated.

If the thirty-five capacities were manifestations of one underlying quantity, the case for treating general intelligence as a scalar would be strong, and a single number would be a reasonable summary of a system. The psychometric precedent is the reason people expect this: in humans, the positive manifold is real and robust [1,2]. The question is whether an analogous manifold in machine benchmark scores would mean the same thing.

It would not, and the reason is a confound that human psychometrics largely escapes and machine evaluation cannot. Human test batteries are administered to individuals who differ in ability but share an architecture and, within an order of magnitude, a lifetime of exposure. Machine batteries are administered to systems differing in training compute by factors of thousands. Anything that improves all scores at once — scale, data volume, post-training effort — will induce correlations among tasks regardless of whether the underlying competences are related.

The lab isolates the effect. Four hundred simulated systems are scored on nine tasks. The generative model gives each system three latent abilities, drawn independently, with three tasks loading on each; a single shared resource then contributes to every task with weight β. Nothing links the abilities to one another.

With nine unrelated abilities the first principal component takes 13.3% of the variance, close to the 11.1% floor for nine orthogonal dimensions. With three abilities in blocks and no shared resource it takes 34.0% and the mean inter-task correlation is 0.185 — already a positive manifold, produced by nothing more than tasks clustering within ability. Adding a shared resource at β = 1.0 raises the first component to 61.4% and the mean correlation to 0.565. That is a textbook g, extracted from a population in which the abilities are independent by construction.

The methodological conclusion is not that a machine g is impossible, but that finding one is close to uninformative unless the shared-resource confound is controlled. Reporting per-instance results rather than aggregate scores is the standard recommendation, for precisely this reason [182], and the same logic applies to the debate over emergent abilities, where sharp capability jumps have been shown to depend partly on the choice of metric rather than on a discontinuity in the underlying model [183,184]. The dissociation literature makes the same argument from the other end: formal linguistic competence and the reasoning competence it is routinely taken to indicate come apart under scrutiny, which is precisely a failure of the single-factor reading [185]. The most careful CHC-grounded AGI definition finds jagged profiles across domains rather than a smooth ascent [8], which is what a confound-driven manifold would look like once the confound is partly removed. And the field's own expectations are not uniform: in a survey of 475 respondents run under the auspices of the 2025 AAAI presidential panel, roughly three quarters judged that scaling current approaches is unlikely to produce general intelligence [186].

§13

What the measurements can and cannot say

A benchmark measures a rung, and contamination silently lowers it.

Everything above depends on evaluations meaning what they appear to mean, and this section states the four ways they fail. Each failure is a scope failure: the measurement is taken at a lower rung than the claim it is used to support.

Contamination lowers the rung. If an evaluation item appears in training data, the capacity being measured is retrieval, which is rung 1. This is not noise added to an otherwise valid estimate; it is a different estimand [34]. The ARC programme's design choices are best read as engineering against exactly this: unique tasks, first-party human calibration, held-out private sets, and an explicit accounting of information leakage through repeated leaderboard feedback [153,187].

A single threshold hides the axes. The most-cited attempt to operationalise progress replaces the binary question with a matrix of performance level against generality of coverage, and separates capability from autonomy explicitly [188] — two moves this article's ladder makes for the same reason.

Aggregate scores hide the profile. A single number over a heterogeneous battery cannot distinguish a system that is uniformly mediocre from one that is superb on two thirds of the items and helpless on the rest — and for questions about generality the difference is the entire content [182,189,190].

Performance is not competence. Inferring an internal capacity from a behavioural score requires ruling out simpler explanations of the score, and the machine-learning literature has been repeatedly forced to do so after the fact [168,191,192]. The methodological remedy is well established in the analogy literature: construct items that preserve the target structure while breaking the surface conventions, and see whether performance survives [107]. Where such probes have been run, they often find capabilities shaped by the training objective in ways that a competence account would not predict [118,119,147].

The measurable rungs are the low ones. This is the deepest problem and it is structural rather than remediable by better practice. Rungs 2 through 4 admit standard protocols; rung 5 admits a handful of novelty-controlled batteries; rung 6 has a small and recent set of interactive environments; rung 7 has almost nothing, because the format of an evaluation supplies the objective. The consequence is a systematic bias in the field's self-image: the capacities we can measure improve visibly and quickly, and the capacities we cannot measure are represented in the discourse mainly by their absence.

None of this implies that benchmark progress is illusory. Fourteen months from noise to 92.5% on a benchmark explicitly designed to resist memorisation is a real result about real capability at rungs 4 and 5, and the accompanying shift from brute-force program search to test-time adaptation is a real methodological change [153,187]. The claim is narrower and, I think, harder to argue with: that result licenses conclusions about the rung at which it was measured, and the interactive results from the same organisation, on the same visual vocabulary, with the same priors, show what happens one rung up [155,156].

Four observations follow directly from the distribution.

Nothing in the requested taxonomy sits at rung 1. Nobody proposes memorisation as a component of general intelligence. Yet rung 1 is where a contaminated evaluation places whatever it measures, which means the floor of the ladder is occupied entirely by measurement artefacts rather than by anything anyone wants.

The mass is in the middle. Rungs 3 and 4 together hold the largest share of the taxonomy, and those are exactly the rungs with the most mature evaluation methodology and the fastest measured progress. This is not a coincidence in either direction: capacities that can be posed as a task distribution attract both benchmarks and methods, and the two reinforce each other.

Rung 7 has two members. Goal formulation and resource management. Both are supplied by the harness in every standard evaluation. Both are constitutive of what anyone means by autonomy. Neither has a benchmark worth the name.

The clusters do not respect the ladder. Reasoning spans rungs 2 to 6; learning spans 2 to 6; adaptability spans 3 to 6. The conventional groupings track the vocabulary of the field rather than the structure of the problem, which is why a system can be described accurately as strong at reasoning and weak at reasoning in the same breath, depending on which member of the cluster the speaker has in mind.

§15

General over what?

An answer, and the shape of the evidence that would change it.

The question this article set out to make precise admits an answer, though not a short one.

General, in artificial general intelligence, is a claim about the range of a quantifier over tasks, and the claim is only meaningful once three things are specified: the set of tasks over which competence is asserted, the prior that makes competence over that set possible at all, and the resources within which it must be achieved. Drop the first and the claim is empty. Drop the second and the claim contradicts a theorem, since averaged over all tasks every method is identical — exactly 0.5000 in the enumeration above. Drop the third and the claim describes something no physical system could be, since generality without a resource bound is search, and search over a large enough space solves everything and explains nothing [33,176,177].

On that reading the three inheritances stop competing and start dividing labour. The computability tradition supplies the form of the definition: expected performance over a weighted class of environments, with the weighting made explicit [17,19]. The psychometric tradition supplies a warning about inference from batteries, and the specific caution that a positive manifold is evidence of a shared cause, which may be a shared resource rather than a shared ability. The engineering tradition supplies the operational criterion: generality is what you observe when the world hands the system something the builder did not anticipate, which is to say, at rungs 6 and 7 [24].

It also explains the field's strange progress profile without recourse to either enthusiasm or deflation. The capacities at rungs 2 through 5 have improved enormously and the improvement is real. The capacities at rungs 6 and 7 have improved much less, are much less measured, and are the ones that the word general was doing service for in the first place. A system can be superb at every capacity on rungs 2 through 5 and still require a person to decide what it should be doing, notice when it has gone wrong, and decide how much it may spend finding out. The programmes that treat the gap as architectural rather than quantitative — hybrid systems with explicit structure [193], or agent designs built around a learned world model, a configurator and an explicit cost [194] — are best read as bets about which rung the missing machinery lives on. That gap is not a matter of scale, and it is not obviously a matter of architecture either; it is a matter of what the training and evaluation loop supplies for free.

What would change this analysis? Three things, in ascending order of significance. First, an evaluation at rung 7 that is reproducible — some way of scoring a system on objectives it set for itself that does not collapse into scoring it on objectives the evaluator set. Second, evidence that competence at high rungs is predicted by competence at low ones after controlling for the shared-resource confound; the lab above shows how easily that confound manufactures the appearance of such a relationship, so the control is the whole experiment. Third, and most decisively, a system that acquires competence in a genuinely novel domain at a rate comparable to a person's, with its prior held fixed and measured — which is the quantity Chollet's formulation was constructed to isolate and which remains, six years later, the sharpest available operationalisation of the word [43].

Until then the honest form of the claim is conditional, and the condition is the interesting part. Not is it general? but general over what, at what prior, within what budget? — three questions with answers, in place of one question without.
