Architectures are usually compared by what they contain. This article compares them by when their contents may change. On that reading a symbolic knowledge base and a foundation model are not rival theories of intelligence but rival answers to a scheduling question, and most of the field's long-running arguments turn out to be disagreements about which of five stores should carry a particular kind of content. Nine exhaustive experiments price the answers.

Part IThe questionwhat an architecture decides

1.1 An architecture is a decision about time

Ask what distinguishes the architectures in this article and the usual answers are about components: this one has a symbol store, that one has attention, the third has a planner. The components are real, but they are not what is being decided. A component is a place to put something. The decision is what goes where, and how often it may change.

Consider two systems that both know the boiling point of water. In the first it is a row in a table; a person can correct it in a second, and the correction touches nothing else. In the second it is a pattern spread across a hundred million parameters written during a training run that finished last year; correcting it requires either another training run or a mechanism that writes over some of those parameters and hopes. The two systems may be indistinguishable when asked. They are not remotely the same architecture, and the difference has nothing to do with accuracy. It is a difference in the timescale on which that content can change, and in who is allowed to change it.

Turing's founding paper framed the issue as one of behaviour and left the internal organisation entirely open1, which is part of why the architectural question has stayed unsettled for so long: nothing in the behavioural criterion constrains where a system keeps anything.

This is the organising question of the article: what changes, and how fast? Every architecture is a partition of a system into stores that differ in how quickly their contents can be rewritten, together with a rule about what may write to which. I will argue that the long-running disputes in this field — symbolic against neural, retrieval against fine-tuning, context against weights, model-free against model-based — are not disputes about capability at all. They are disagreements about which store should carry which kind of content, and they are frequently conducted as though they were empirical when the disagreement is about a design commitment whose costs are known in advance.

The framing has a pedigree. Newell's insistence that a cognitive architecture is the fixed part of a system, with knowledge as the variable part2, is exactly a claim about timescales: architecture changes on the scale of theory revision, knowledge on the scale of experience. Simon's account of near-decomposability3 and Parnas's criterion for modules4 are both, read carefully, arguments that a good decomposition puts things that change together in the same place. What has changed since is not the principle but the number of stores available and the speed of the fastest one.

1.2 Five stores

Throughout the article I use five stores. They are not a taxonomy of mechanisms; they are a taxonomy of write privileges and rewrite rates, and the same physical structure can appear in different ones depending on how it is used.

S — structure. Discrete, addressable content that a person edits: rules, schemas, ontologies, graph edges, type definitions, the source code. Rewrite rate: whenever someone changes a file. Write privilege: humans, overwhelmingly.

W — weights. Parameters written by an optimiser over a training corpus. Rewrite rate: once per training run. Write privilege: the optimiser, and nothing else — which is why the interesting recent work on test-time adaptation is interesting.

C — context. Activations, and in a transformer the key-value cache. Rewrite rate: every forward pass. Write privilege: the forward pass. Erased when the pass ends. This store is young: before attention there was no large, exact, weight-free store in a neural system at all.

M — external memory. Files, databases, vector stores, scratchpads, the environment itself. Rewrite rate: whenever the system takes an action that writes. Write privilege: the system, and usually also everyone else. Persists indefinitely.

L — the control loop. The program that decides what runs, in what order, how many times, and when to stop. Rewrite rate: whenever a developer edits it. This store is easy to overlook because it does not look like memory, but it holds content: a retry limit is a claim about reliability, a stop condition is a claim about sufficiency, and both are knowledge that someone put there.

Two remarks before the taxonomy does any work. First, the five are not exhaustive and are not meant to be; they are the smallest set that makes the disputes legible. Second, a store's rewrite rate and its edit locality are different properties, and confusing them is the source of a great deal of loose talk. Section 3.1 measures a case where an edit is local in behaviour and global in the store; section 2.3 measures the exact mirror image.

1.3 How to read this article

Three devices run through what follows.

Thirty-three commitment plates. Each names one architectural commitment and answers four questions in a fixed order: what is committed, what it buys, what it forecloses, and the tell — how you would recognise the commitment in a system you did not build, from the outside. Each carries a five-cell gauge marking which stores the commitment puts content into. The plates are collected into a ledger in section 10.2, and the ledger is generated from the plates rather than written separately, so it cannot disagree with them.

A computational lab. Nine experiments, in a single standard-library Python file, are executed at build time and their output is embedded verbatim in the appendix. Every number quoted in the prose and drawn in the figures comes from that output. The experiments are exhaustive enumerations or exact rational computations — there is no sampling, no seed, and no stochastic estimate anywhere in them — so they reproduce byte-identically and can be checked line by line. They are, of course, small. Section 10.4 treats that as the strongest objection to the article rather than a footnote to it.

A commitment to the tell. Architectural claims are notoriously unfalsifiable in practice, because any behaviour can be explained by any architecture given enough auxiliary assumptions. The plates' fourth field is an attempt to resist this: if a commitment has no observable consequence, it should not be on a plate. Where I could not write a tell, I have said so.

One caution about scope. This is an article about architecture, not about whether any of these systems understands anything, is conscious, or deserves moral consideration. Those are real questions and they are not these questions. Where the architectural literature borrows a term from that debate — as global-workspace-inspired designs borrow from a theory of consciousness5 — I use the term in its engineering sense and flag the borrowing.

Part IISymbolic architecturesknowledge as structure

2.1 The physical symbol system hypothesis

The founding commitment of symbolic AI is stated with unusual precision. Newell and Simon's hypothesis is that a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action6, 7. What matters architecturally is not the metaphysics but the engineering consequence: knowledge is held in expressions that can be composed, inspected and edited independently of any process that uses them.

COMMITMENT 01 Symbolic — knowledge as structure

Knowledge is an inspectable structure

The commitment
What the system knows is held in discrete, addressable expressions that a person can read, write and delete without running the system.
What it buys
Edits are local and exact. A single fact can be added, scoped, dated, attributed, retracted or audited, and the cost of doing so does not depend on how much else the system knows.
What it forecloses
Everything the system knows must have been written down. There is no route from raw signal to knowledge that does not pass through a person choosing a predicate.
The tell
Ask for the source of a particular belief. If the answer is a row rather than a paragraph of speculation, the knowledge is in S.

That independence is the whole of the payoff. McCarthy's advice-taker proposal8 is essentially an argument that a system should be improvable by being told things, and being told things requires a store whose entries are separable. Every property that symbolic systems are praised for — auditability, provenance, scoping, retraction, the ability of a hundred people to contribute to one knowledge base over thirty years — follows from edit locality in S rather than from logic as such.

COMMITMENT 02 Symbolic — knowledge as structure

Inference is derivation, not association

The commitment
Conclusions are produced by applying truth-preserving rules to expressions, and the sequence of applications is itself an object.
What it buys
Soundness relative to the axioms, a proof that can be checked independently of the process that found it, and the ability to say why a conclusion holds rather than only that it does.
What it forecloses
Nothing follows from a premise you did not encode, and everything follows from a premise you encoded wrongly. Consistency becomes a global property that a local edit can destroy.
The tell
Wrong answers are wrong in a legible way: they are valid consequences of a false premise, not plausible-looking noise.

The costs are equally structural, and were identified early by the people doing the work rather than by critics. The frame problem9 is not a puzzle about robots and blocks; it is the observation that representing what does not change under an action is unbounded unless something outside the logic decides what is relevant. Default and non-monotonic reasoning10 were the discipline's serious attempt to handle this within the formalism, and McDermott's retrospective on the programme is a candid account of what that attempt did and did not achieve11. Two decades of work on commonsense knowledge, most visibly CYC12, established that the required content is enormous and that assembling it by hand is possible but slow; a survey a generation later found the position substantially unchanged13.

It is worth being precise about what this does and does not show. It does not show that symbolic representation is the wrong commitment. It shows that S has no acquisition mechanism: the store is excellent at holding content and has no way of getting any. Every hybrid architecture in Part IV is an attempt to attach an acquisition mechanism to it.

2.2 Rules and the production cycle

Production systems make the control loop explicit. Working memory holds a small set of elements; rule memory holds condition-action pairs; a cycle matches every rule against working memory, selects one match, and executes it. The Rete algorithm14 — and the refinements needed once rule sets reach six figures15 — make the match step incremental, so that the cost of a cycle depends on how much working memory changed rather than on how many rules exist. That is what allows the rule base to grow without the step time growing.

COMMITMENT 03 Symbolic — knowledge as structure

A cycle of match, select and act

The commitment
Control is a loop that matches every rule against a small fast store, chooses one match by a fixed preference, and executes it.
What it buys
An unbounded rule set with a bounded step. The architecture is constant while the content grows, which is what lets a rule base be maintained by many hands over decades.
What it forecloses
Serial deliberation at the rate of the cycle. Parallel evidence must be serialised through one selection, and the preference that does the selecting is itself hand-written.
The tell
Behaviour changes in discrete jumps when rules are added, and the system can be single-stepped.

The architectural point in the figure is easy to state and often missed: the store that holds knowledge is not the store that changes during a run. Rule memory is S; working memory is C. A production system that could not write from C back into S would learn nothing, and the mechanism that does this — chunking in SOAR, production compilation in ACT-R — is treated in Part V because it is the point at which cognitive architectures make a claim beyond engineering.

Two properties of the cycle are worth separating, because contemporary systems inherit one and not the other. The first is that the loop is inspectable: at any moment there is a named set of matching rules and a named reason why one was chosen, so the system can be single-stepped and a wrong step attributed. The second is that the loop is terminating in a legible way: it halts when nothing matches, and that condition is a statement about the rule base rather than a budget someone set. Part VII's agent loops have neither property. They stop when a step counter runs out, and no equivalent of the conflict set exists to say what else the system might have done — which is why their failures are diagnosed by reading transcripts rather than by inspecting state.

The expert-system era is usually narrated as a commercial failure, which obscures the architectural lesson. MYCIN and its successors16 worked, within their scope, and their scope was set by exactly the property plate 2 names: a rule base is a theory, and a theory's consistency is a global property that any local edit can break. My lab makes this measurable.

2.3 The blast radius of a local edit

Experiment E9 builds a small knowledge base — twenty-one asserted facts about a taxonomy of parts and requirements — closes it under four rules (transitivity of subclass and part-of, inheritance of requirements down the subclass relation and up the part relation), and then asks what a single edit costs.

The closure is 72 facts, a little over three times the asserted set. Removing one asserted fact costs between two and twenty-four conclusions; the median is six; the largest single loss is a third of everything the system believes. Not one of the twenty-one facts can be removed without losing at least one further conclusion. In the other direction, a single wrong assertion — declaring the most general class to be a subclass of a specific one — adds fifty-two false conclusions, which is 72% of the size of the true closure.

This is the exact mirror of the property that section 3.1 will measure for distributed stores, and stating both together is the point of this article's framing. In a symbolic store, an edit is local in the store and global in the theory: you change one row, and an unbounded and unsignposted set of conclusions changes with it. In a distributed store, an edit is global in the store and (sometimes) local in the behaviour: you change half the parameters, and often nothing else visibly moves. Neither is the safe one. They fail in opposite directions, and an engineer who has internalised one set of instincts will be exactly wrong about the other.

The practical consequence for knowledge-base engineering is that the maintenance cost of S is not proportional to its size but to its closure's sensitivity, which nobody measures and which the tooling does not report. The most load-bearing facts in the experiment are all links high in the subclass chain — the assertions that nobody thinks of as interesting.

2.4 Knowledge graphs and automated proof

Knowledge graphs are the symbolic commitment stripped to its cheapest form17. Drop most of the logic, keep identity and typed relations, and a great deal survives: Wikidata is maintained by a large distributed community precisely because an edit to one statement is an edit to one statement18, and its predecessors established the pattern19. The closure behaviour measured in the previous section is the standard one for a rule language over such a store20. The subsequent embedding literature21, 22 is best read as an attempt to buy the generalisation properties of W while keeping the graph's identity discipline; the survey literature is candid that the two do not merge cleanly22.

COMMITMENT 04 Symbolic — knowledge as structure

Identity by node, meaning by edge

The commitment
Entities are named once and the content of a claim is carried by typed relations between names rather than by text.
What it buys
Joins. A question nobody anticipated can be answered by traversing edges that were asserted for other reasons, and the same entity can be described by many parties without collision.
What it forecloses
Everything hangs on identity resolution. Two names for one thing, or one name for two, corrupts every traversal that passes through it, and the corruption is silent.
The tell
The system is confident and precise about relations it was told and has nothing at all to say about relations it was not.

Automated theorem proving is the branch of the symbolic tradition that never stopped delivering, and it is instructive about why. Since resolution23 the field has kept the search and the correctness criterion strictly separate: a prover may use any heuristic whatsoever, because a proof is checked by machinery small enough to audit24, 25. SMT solvers made this industrially routine26. The two landmark results — the Robbins conjecture27 and the formal proof of Kepler's28 — are results a person could not find and can nonetheless check.

COMMITMENT 05 Symbolic — knowledge as structure

Search over a formal calculus

The commitment
Finding a proof is a search problem over a space defined by a complete inference rule, and the search is separated from the criterion of correctness.
What it buys
Results a person could not have found and can nonetheless check. The checker is small enough to trust and does not have to trust the searcher.
What it forecloses
The search space is astronomical and the heuristics that make it tractable are exactly what the formalism does not supply. Completeness guarantees say nothing about time.
The tell
Performance is wildly uneven across superficially similar problems, and the variance is a property of the search, not of the difficulty.

That separation is the single most transferable idea in this Part, and it reappears in Part IV as the architecture behind every recent neuro-symbolic success. Where a cheap decisive checker exists, an unreliable generator can be made reliable. Where one does not, it cannot. Dynamic backtracking is a good illustration of how much of the practical gain lies in the search discipline rather than the logic29. Logic programming's contribution30 was to notice that the same formalism can be read as specification and as program; the modern reading is that a checker is a specification you can run, and running specifications is the only known route from a fallible proposer to a trustworthy system. Everything the symbolic tradition still contributes to contemporary architecture flows through this observation31.

Part IIINeural architecturesknowledge as parameters

3.1 Distributed representation and the cost of an edit

The connectionist commitment is the exact negation of plate 1. Content is not held in separable expressions; it is superposed across parameters that hold everything else32, 33. The generalisation properties this buys are genuine and were the original argument for it: similar inputs share machinery without anyone specifying the similarity metric.

COMMITMENT 06 Neural — knowledge as parameters

Content distributed over shared parameters

The commitment
No parameter belongs to any one item; every item is stored as a pattern across parameters that also store everything else.
What it buys
Similar inputs share machinery automatically, so generalisation is a property of the representation rather than a rule someone wrote. Capacity degrades gracefully instead of overflowing.
What it forecloses
Locality of edit. Changing one item writes a large fraction of the store, and whether that disturbs other items depends on how full the store is — a global property nobody is tracking.
The tell
You cannot delete one fact. Attempts to do so either miss it or damage neighbours, and which of the two happened is not visible from outside.

Experiment E1 makes the trade concrete in the simplest possible setting — a linear associative memory of the sort studied since the 1970s34, 35, storing key-value pairs in one shared matrix of 4,096 integers, with retrieval by thresholded matrix product.

Two numbers matter. The first is capacity: nineteen pairs are recalled exactly; the twentieth costs a bit error somewhere. That figure is not the interesting one — it depends on width, coding and readout, and the literature has characterised it thoroughly. The second is: overwriting one pair's value writes 1,920 of the 4,096 parameters — 46.9% of the store — at every load tested. The edit is never local. Whether it is visible depends entirely on how full the memory is: below capacity, zero other pairs change; above it, up to ten of thirty-five.

The consequence for practice is that the safety of an edit in W is a function of a global property that nothing in the system reports. This is the mechanism underneath catastrophic forgetting36, underneath the elaborate machinery required to protect old parameters when learning new tasks37, and underneath the finding that networks trained continually lose the ability to learn at all — ImageNet binary accuracy falling from 89% to roughly 77% by the two-thousandth task38. It is also why the brain is theorised to run two stores at different rates rather than one39, a hypothesis whose architectural content is precisely the claim this article organises everything around.

3.2 One writer, one objective

The second neural commitment is about who may write. Every piece of content in W arrives by the same route: small steps against the gradient of a scalar loss40, 41.

COMMITMENT 07 Neural — knowledge as parameters

Learning is descent on a differentiable objective

The commitment
All content in W is written by the same mechanism: small steps against the gradient of a scalar loss over a corpus.
What it buys
One optimiser for every kind of content, and improvement that follows from more data and more compute without a new idea. This is the property that has scaled.
What it forecloses
Anything that is not differentiable, and anything that must be learned from one example. The objective is the only thing the architecture can be told, so every requirement must be expressed as a loss.
The tell
Capabilities appear in the order the corpus rewards them, not the order a designer would choose.

The advantages are so large and so well established that they are easy to state and hard to overstate. One mechanism handles vision, language, control and protein structure. Improvement follows from more data and more compute without requiring a new idea, which is the observation Sutton sharpened into a polemic42 and which the scaling-law literature43, 44 turned into a planning instrument.

The cost is that the objective is the only channel through which a requirement can reach the system. Anything a designer wants must be expressible as a loss over a corpus. This is not a complaint about alignment; it is an architectural observation with everyday consequences. A recent analysis argues that language models hallucinate in part because evaluations reward confident guessing over acknowledged uncertainty45 — that is, the behaviour is not a defect of W but a faithful rendering of what the objective asked for. The same logic explains why model behaviour bears the fingerprints of the training task in ways that look like reasoning failures and are not46.

A subtler consequence concerns what cannot be told to the system at all. A requirement of the form "never assert a fact you have not checked" is not expressible as a per-example loss, because it quantifies over the system's own epistemic state rather than over outputs. It can be approximated — by penalising unsupported claims in training data, by a reward model, by a verifier at inference — but each approximation lives in a different store, and none of them is in W. This is the general shape of the alignment problem stated architecturally, and it explains why so much practical safety work takes the form of adding components in L rather than improving the objective in W.

There is a second, quieter cost. Because W is written once and read for ever, a foundation model's knowledge has a date, and nothing inside the architecture knows what that date is or which of its beliefs it invalidates. Plate 23 treats the standard response.

3.3 Attention introduced a second store

The usual account of the transformer is about parallelism and long-range dependency47, building on the alignment mechanism introduced for translation48. Both are true and neither is the architectural news. The news is that attention created, inside a neural system, a large, exact, addressable store that no gradient writes.

COMMITMENT 08 Neural — knowledge as parameters

A second store the gradient never writes

The commitment
Attention keeps every token of the input available for exact retrieval for the duration of the forward pass, in a store that training does not fix.
What it buys
Exact recall of arbitrary content without changing a parameter, and therefore a way to supply the model with facts, instructions and history at inference time.
What it forecloses
Bounded memory. The store grows with the input, and everything in it is discarded when the pass ends, so nothing learned in context survives.
The tell
The system is excellent at what is in front of it and unreliable about what it was told last week.

Once this store exists, a great deal that looks like separate innovation is one idea applied repeatedly. In-context learning is writing to C instead of W. Chain-of-thought49 is using C as a scratchpad, which matters because a fixed-depth network's serial computation is otherwise bounded by its depth — the formal result being that log-precision transformers sit inside uniform TC050, so generated tokens are the only way to buy depth at inference time. Retrieval augmentation51, 52 is filling C from M. Prompting is an interface to C. The entire practice that grew up around these models is an interface to a store the original paper did not advertise.

The store's limits are equally architectural. It is discarded at the end of the pass, so nothing learned in context survives; it grows linearly with the input, so it is expensive; and its contents are not used uniformly, with the middle of a long context systematically under-consulted53 — a finding that benchmark work on real context length has repeatedly confirmed54. The claimed context window and the usable one are different numbers, and the gap is a property of C, not of the task.

3.4 What a fixed state can and cannot hold

Recurrence was the original answer to sequence: a hidden state carried forward and updated55, with gating added to keep gradients alive over long spans56. State-space models and modern linear-attention variants make the same commitment: compress the past into a state of constant size57, 58, 59, 60, which restores the constant memory and linear time that attention gave up. The relationship between the two families turns out to be closer than it looked61, and the field's return to recurrence has been surveyed as such62.

COMMITMENT 09 Neural — knowledge as parameters

A fixed-width recurrent state

The commitment
The past is compressed into a state of constant size, updated once per token.
What it buys
Constant memory and linear time, and — less often noticed — the ability to carry a genuinely sequential computation whose depth grows with the input.
What it forecloses
Any task whose set of distinguishable histories outgrows the state. Exact recall of arbitrary content is the canonical case and cannot be fixed by training.
The tell
Performance falls off a cliff, not a slope, at a length that depends on the content rather than on the task.

The question of what this costs has a clean answer that predates all of it. The Myhill-Nerode theorem says that a task is solvable by a fixed finite state exactly when the number of distinguishable input prefixes is bounded63, 64, 65. Experiment E3 computes those indices exhaustively.

The results run in both directions, which is why this is the most useful experiment in the article. Parity settles at an index of 2, a threshold count at 5, and the word problem for the symmetric group S3 at exactly 6 — its group order — and all three stay there as inputs lengthen. A fixed state suffices for these at every length. Copying settles nowhere: the index doubles with every additional symbol, so a d-bit state fails beyond d bits of content. Associative recall is the case that matters for language: its index is exponential in the number of live bindings, so the state required is linear in how many key-value pairs must be simultaneously available — 1.00, 3.00, 4.70, 6.32 and 7.92 bits for one to five bindings.

This is exactly the empirical picture. Transformers beat state-space models at copying, with a lower bound on the required state doing the work66; recall is where the gap shows in practice67, 68, 69. And it runs the other way too: fixed-depth transformers cannot do state tracking that a recurrence handles trivially70, 71, which is the same theorem read from the other end. The theoretical literature on what transformers can express has converged on this shape72, 73, 74.

3.5 Hybrids, and the honest reason for them

Given a result that runs in both directions, the engineering response is predictable and correct: interleave the two kinds of layer75, 69.

COMMITMENT 10 Neural — knowledge as parameters

Hybridise the two memories rather than choose

The commitment
Interleave layers that compress the past with layers that retain it exactly.
What it buys
Most of the recall of attention at most of the cost of recurrence, because the tasks that need exact retrieval need it at a few places rather than everywhere.
What it forecloses
A clean account of what the system can do. The guarantees of neither component survive the mixture, so the operating envelope becomes an empirical question.
The tell
The failure modes are those of the compressive component, arriving later than they would have alone.

It is worth being clear about what this does and does not resolve. It does not find a store with both sets of properties; no such store is on offer. It exploits a contingent fact about workloads — that exact retrieval is needed at a few points rather than continuously — and buys most of the recall for a fraction of the memory. That is a good trade and an empirical one, which means it can go stale if workloads change.

The more interesting recent move is to make the fast store writable in a weight-like way at inference time: a memory module whose parameters are updated by gradient steps during the forward pass, so that surprising content is retained beyond the context76, 77, generalised into a framework that treats a model as nested optimisation problems running at different update frequencies78. Read through this article's frame, that last proposal is unusually explicit: it is a claim that the right architecture is a hierarchy of stores distinguished by rewrite rate, which is the thesis of this article stated as a design. Whether it works at scale is open; the reframing is valuable regardless, and it is the direct descendant of a thirty-year-old proposal for fast weights79.

What none of these approaches changes is the arithmetic in E3. A store of fixed width holds a bounded number of bindings; a store that grows holds an unbounded number at unbounded cost; and a store that is written during inference inherits, exactly, the edit-locality problem measured in E1.

Part IVNeuro-symbolic systemstwo stores and a seam

4.1 The binding problem, measured

The classical objection to distributed representation is that it has no account of structure. Fodor and Pylyshyn's argument is that thought is systematic — anyone who can represent John loves Mary can represent Mary loves John — and that a representation which merely superposes features cannot explain this, because it does not distinguish the roles the constituents play80. Marcus's later restatement sharpened it into a claim about variables and operations over them81.

The connectionist reply is that a fixed-width vector can encode role-filler structure, by binding roles to fillers with an invertible operation and superposing the results. Smolensky's tensor product construction is the principled version82; holographic reduced representations and binary spatter codes are the practical ones that keep dimensionality fixed83, 84, 85, 86, and the family is now surveyed as vector-symbolic architecture87. A large-scale functioning model built on this substrate ran a battery of cognitive tasks88.

The reply is correct, and experiment E2 quantifies what it costs. Binding with XOR, bundling by majority, and cleaning up against a sixty-four-item codebook, I scan upward in the number of superposed pairs until the first one is lost.

Capacity is 11, 17, 29, 63 and 101 pairs at widths of 256 through 4,096 bits — a mean of 1.76× capacity for each doubling of width, so roughly linear. The vector does not run out of addresses; it runs out of signal-to-noise, and the noise is the other pairs. Degradation is graceful, which sounds like a virtue and is a hazard: at 1,024 bits the system recovers 29 of 29 pairs and then 30 of 31. The first failure is a single silent error inside an otherwise perfect result.

So the Fodor-Pylyshyn challenge is met in the sense that matters formally and not in the sense that matters architecturally. Structure fits in a vector; an unbounded amount of structure does not; and the boundary is not signposted from inside. An explicit list of the same pairs costs twelve bits each, never degrades, and cannot be compared, transformed or superposed in one operation. That is the trade, and it is the same trade as section 3.1 in different clothing.

4.2 Four ways to join two stores

Neuro-symbolic architectures are usually catalogued by mechanism89, 90. Catalogued by which direction information may cross the seam, there are four, and their costs follow from the direction rather than from the mechanism.

COMMITMENT 11 Neuro-symbolic — two stores and a seam

Perception proposes, symbols dispose

The commitment
A learned front end maps raw signal to discrete structure, and a classical engine reasons over that structure.
What it buys
Grounding for the symbols and rigour for the inference, with each component doing what it is good at and neither asked to do the other's job.
What it forecloses
Error recovery across the seam. The interface is a commitment, and a mis-parsed scene is not a low-confidence symbol but a confident wrong one.
The tell
The system reasons impeccably about a situation it has misread, and the two failures cannot be told apart from the output.

Pipeline. Perception produces symbols; a classical engine reasons over them. Neuro-symbolic visual question answering is the clean example91, 92. The interface is a commitment, so a misread scene becomes a confident wrong symbol rather than a low-confidence one, and the downstream reasoning is impeccable about a situation that is not the case.

COMMITMENT 12 Neuro-symbolic — two stores and a seam

Relax the symbols until gradients pass

The commitment
Logical operations are replaced by differentiable approximations so that rule structure and perception can be trained end to end.
What it buys
Rules that are learned rather than written, and a single objective spanning both stores, which removes the interface failure of the pipeline.
What it forecloses
Scale, and the guarantees. Relaxed conjunction is not conjunction; the cost of the relaxation grows with the number of clauses, and soundness is recovered only in the limit.
The tell
Impressive on small closed domains, absent from anything with a million facts.

Relaxation. Make the logic differentiable so that gradients reach the rules: probabilistic logic programming with neural predicates93, differentiable inductive logic programming94, and the broader case for relational inductive biases95. This fixes the pipeline's interface problem by removing the interface, and pays for it in scale: relaxed conjunction is not conjunction, and the cost of the relaxation grows with the number of clauses.

COMMITMENT 13 Neuro-symbolic — two stores and a seam

The program is the learned object

The commitment
What the system induces is not a policy or a classifier but an explicit program, retained and reused.
What it buys
Compositional reuse with an editable artefact at the end. A learned subroutine can be inspected, named, called by something else, and kept when the model is replaced.
What it forecloses
Coverage. Program spaces are discrete and vast, and search in them does not benefit from gradients, so the method works exactly where a short program exists.
The tell
The system solves a hard instance and a trivially different one, and fails on a third that a person would call the same problem.

Synthesis. Make the program itself the learned object. DreamCoder's wake-sleep library learning96 and Bayesian program induction for concept learning97 both produce an artefact that survives the system that made it. The limit is that program spaces are discrete and vast and gradients do not help.

COMMITMENT 14 Neuro-symbolic — two stores and a seam

Neural proposes, formal system verifies

The commitment
A learned component generates candidates and an independent checker accepts or rejects, with the checker treated as ground truth.
What it buys
Reliability far above the generator's own, in any domain that has a cheap decisive checker; and a training signal that does not need human labels.
What it forecloses
Every domain that lacks one. The architecture's reach is exactly the reach of its verifier, which is why the successes cluster in mathematics, code and games.
The tell
Ask what would happen if the checker were wrong. If the answer is 'nothing would notice', the checker is load-bearing.

Verification. A learned generator proposes; a formal system decides. This is section 2.4's separation of search from checking, rebuilt with a neural searcher, and it is the arrangement behind essentially every recent result of note.

4.3 What the verifier architecture has actually delivered

AlphaGeometry solved olympiad geometry by pairing a language model that proposes auxiliary constructions with a symbolic deduction engine98. AlphaProof extended the pattern to formal mathematics with reinforcement learning against a proof assistant99; its reported result — one point below a gold medal at the 2024 olympiad — came with the caveat that the non-geometry problems were manually formalised into Lean by experts, which is the interface cost of plate 11 arriving in a system built to avoid it. The retrieval-augmented proving literature attacks exactly that bottleneck100, 101. AlphaEvolve applies the same shape to algorithm discovery, with an evaluator standing in for the proof checker, and reports a 4×4 complex matrix multiplication in 48 scalar multiplications — the first improvement in that setting in fifty-six years102.

The common structure is worth stating flatly, because it is often narrated as a story about better models. In each case an unreliable proposer is made reliable by a checker that is cheap, decisive and independent. Remove any of the three adjectives and the architecture stops working. This is why the successes cluster in mathematics, formal verification, code and games, and why they have not transferred to domains where correctness is a matter of judgement. The reach of the architecture is the reach of its verifier, exactly.

There is a second, less-remarked property these systems share: the checker is where the specification lives. In an end-to-end system trained on input-output pairs, what counts as success is distributed across a dataset and cannot be read off. In a verifier architecture it is a piece of code, written by a person, versioned and reviewable. That is content in S and L, and it is doing the work that the field usually attributes to the model. When a result of this kind is reported as evidence that models are becoming capable of mathematics, a reasonable response is to ask which component would have to be removed for the result to disappear.

It is also why the AAAI 2025 presidential panel's community survey is more interesting than it first appears: 76% of 475 respondents judged that scaling current approaches is unlikely to yield general intelligence, and over 60% thought a human-like reasoning system would need to be at least half symbolic103. One can disagree with the prediction and still notice that the working systems in this section are precisely half-symbolic, and that the symbolic half is doing the part that carries the guarantee.

Part VCognitive architecturesa fixed frame, variable content

5.1 SOAR: one long-term store, one learning mechanism

SOAR began from an unusually strong commitment: all long-term knowledge in productions, all problem solving as search in problem spaces, and a single learning mechanism104. When the architecture cannot select an operator, it reaches an impasse, creates a subgoal, solves it by search, and then compiles the result into a new production so that the same impasse does not recur — chunking105. The mature architecture added episodic and semantic memories and reinforcement learning106, which is a substantial retreat from the original austerity and an honest one.

COMMITMENT 15 Cognitive — a fixed architecture

A fixed architecture with variable content

The commitment
The set of modules, their connections and the cycle time are fixed by the theory; only the contents of the memories vary between tasks.
What it buys
Falsifiability. Because the architecture is constant, a model of one task and a model of another are comparable, and fitting many tasks with one architecture is evidence about the architecture.
What it forecloses
Growth. A capability the architecture did not anticipate cannot be added by data, only by amending the architecture — which changes every previous result.
The tell
The literature reports latencies and error patterns rather than benchmark scores, because the claim is about mechanism.
COMMITMENT 16 Cognitive — a fixed architecture

Experience is compiled into structure

The commitment
A result reached by deliberation is written back as a new rule, so the next occasion is answered by retrieval rather than by search.
What it buys
A principled account of practice, and a route by which content moves from the fast store to the slow one without a training run.
What it forecloses
Unlearning. Compiled structure is correct only under the conditions that produced it, and the architecture does not record what those were.
The tell
Performance improves and flexibility falls together, on the same schedule.

Chunking deserves attention because it is the cleanest answer anyone has given to the acquisition problem of section 2.1: a route by which content moves from the fast store to the slow one without a training run and without a person. What it does not have is an account of unlearning. A chunk is correct under the conditions that produced it, the architecture does not record what those were, and so the same mechanism that produces expertise produces brittleness on the same schedule. Any architecture that caches deliberation inherits this, including every agent framework in Part VII that writes lessons to a memory file.

5.2 ACT-R: discrete records, continuous access

ACT-R makes a different and, for this article's purposes, more interesting commitment. Declarative knowledge is held in discrete chunks — editable, inspectable, symbolic — but whether a chunk is retrieved is governed by a continuous activation reflecting frequency, recency and context107, 108. Modules for vision, motor action, declarative retrieval and goals run in parallel and communicate through capacity-one buffers109.

COMMITMENT 17 Cognitive — a fixed architecture

Discrete records with continuous availability

The commitment
Declarative content is stored as discrete chunks, but whether a chunk is retrieved is decided by a continuous quantity reflecting how often and how recently it was useful.
What it buys
The editability of a symbolic store with the graded, context-sensitive access of a distributed one — the two properties this article treats as opposed, in one mechanism.
What it forecloses
Determinism. Retrieval failures are a feature of the model, so the system's knowledge and the system's access to it come apart, and only the second is measured.
The tell
The same question gets different answers as a function of history rather than of input.

This is the only architecture in the article that has both properties this article treats as opposed. Its store has the edit locality of S and the graded, context-sensitive availability of W, because it separates what is stored from what is reachable. That separation is a genuine architectural contribution, and it is worth asking why it has not been adopted more widely.

The answer, I think, is that it is expensive in exactly the currency modern systems are short of. The activation equation has parameters that must be fitted, the chunks must be authored, and the payoff is measured in latency distributions and error patterns rather than in task success. ACT-R's literature reports what a theory reports, and the field's incentives reward what a benchmark reports. The two are not the same and the gap is not evidence about the architecture.

5.3 The Common Model and the shape everyone agrees on

After thirty years of divergence, the principals of the main cognitive architectures wrote down what they agreed on: a small set of modules — perception, motor, working memory, declarative memory, procedural memory — with procedural knowledge as rules, a cycle that selects and applies one at a time, and all inter-module communication routed through working memory110. Subsequent work reported connectome evidence consistent with the decomposition111. Other architectures in the tradition — CLARION's explicit-implicit division112, LIDA's cognitive cycle113, Sigma's attempt at a graphical unification114, and the AGI-motivated systems115, 116 — differ inside the boxes more than between them. The forty-year survey catalogues eighty-odd systems and finds convergence on this shape117, and the question of what role such architectures should play in general AI has been put directly118; the textbook treatment of artificial cognitive systems sets out the same decomposition119.

Read as stores, the Common Model says something simple: one fast shared store in the middle, several slow stores around it, and a cycle that moves one item at a time through the centre. The disputes concern the cycle time and what counts as one item. And that description is, word for word, the global workspace picture.

5.4 The workspace bottleneck, and what it is worth

Global workspace theory proposes that many specialised unconscious processes compete, and a small winning selection is broadcast to all of them5; the neuronal version locates the mechanism in long-range cortical connectivity120, 121. The architectural transplant has been made repeatedly: a shared workspace coordinating neural modules with limited bandwidth122, an argument that cross-attention to a small latent array is functionally a workspace123, 124, and a broader case for the theory as a design guide125. The connection to higher-level cognitive inductive biases has been developed explicitly126, and the framework of two systems is the usual informal gloss127.

COMMITMENT 18 Cognitive — a fixed architecture

A capacity-limited global broadcast

The commitment
Many specialised processes run in parallel but only a small selection is broadcast to all of them each cycle.
What it buys
Coordination without all-to-all wiring, and an explanation of why serial deliberation coexists with massive parallelism. The bottleneck is the mechanism, not a defect.
What it forecloses
Throughput, and worse, everything depends on the selector. A bottleneck with a poor selector costs cycles in proportion to how wrong it is.
The tell
The system is unboundedly parallel in perception and stubbornly serial in decision, with a measurable capacity.

Whether the bottleneck is a good idea is an engineering question with an answer. Experiment E6 gives eight modules a conclusion requiring five facts held by five different modules, plus six irrelevant facts each, and varies how many items the workspace broadcasts per cycle.

With perfect salience, capacity one reaches the conclusion in 5 cycles with 35 item-receipts; unrestricted broadcast reaches it in 1 cycle with 371. The bottleneck costs 4 cycles and saves 336 receipts — a 10.6× reduction in delivered information for a five-fold increase in latency. That is a real and favourable trade, and it is a reasonable computational gloss on why a serial bottleneck might be worth having.

The second half of the experiment is the part worth carrying away. With a selector that ranks a relevant item first only one time in three, the same capacity-one workspace needs 15 cycles instead of 5. The cost of a bottleneck is not paid in bandwidth; it is paid in cycles, in proportion to how wrong the selector is. Architectures that adopt a workspace because it sounds principled, without a corresponding investment in what gets to pass through it, have bought the cost and not the benefit.

A caution on borrowing. Global workspace theory is a theory of consciousness, and the recent adversarial collaboration testing it against integrated information theory left neither with its core predictions intact128. The indicator-based approach to assessing AI systems is explicit that the architectural properties it enumerates are derived from theories that are themselves contested129. None of this bears on whether a capacity-limited broadcast is a good coordination mechanism, which is the only claim plate 18 makes.

5.5 Memory-centred architectures

The other cognitive-architecture idea that transplanted into deep learning is the external memory: a differentiable read-write store outside the parameters, with a learned addressing policy. Neural Turing machines and the differentiable neural computer are the canonical designs130, 131; memory networks132 and memory-augmented meta-learners133 are the lighter variants.

COMMITMENT 19 Cognitive — a fixed architecture

An external memory the network learns to address

The commitment
A differentiable read-write store sits outside the parameters, and the addressing policy is learned rather than designed.
What it buys
Separation of computation from storage, so capacity can be increased without retraining and content can outlive the forward pass.
What it forecloses
Simplicity of training. The addressing scheme is the hard part, it is learned from the same sparse signal as everything else, and it is the first thing to fail at scale.
The tell
Capacity is advertised in the architecture and not realised in the results.

The honest summary is that the advertised capacity was rarely realised. The addressing scheme is the hard part; it is learned from the same sparse signal as everything else; and it was the first thing to fail as tasks grew. What survived is the idea rather than the mechanism — and it survived by giving up differentiability. Retrieval augmentation51 is an external memory with a hand-written addressing policy, and it works. The agent scratchpad of Part VII is an external memory addressed by the model writing a filename, and it works. In both cases the architecture succeeded once it stopped trying to learn the thing that was easy to specify.

Laid out chronologically, the sequence in the figure reads as progress. Laid out as a table of stores it reads as a pendulum: content is pushed out of the weights when editability matters, and pulled back in when speed matters. The test-time memory proposals of section 3.5 are the two positions collapsed — a weight-shaped store running on a context-shaped clock — which is either the synthesis or the point at which the distinction stops being useful. It is too early to say which.

Part VIFoundation-model AGIone store, scaled

6.1 The scaling commitment

The foundation-model commitment is the most consequential architectural decision of the last decade and the simplest to state: one self-supervised objective, one undifferentiated corpus, adaptation afterwards134, 135.

COMMITMENT 20 Foundation-model — one store, scaled

One objective, one corpus, every task

The commitment
A single self-supervised objective over an undifferentiated corpus produces a model that is then adapted, rather than a model per task.
What it buys
Transfer, and an engineering economy: improvements accrue once and are inherited everywhere. It is the most consequential architectural decision of the period.
What it forecloses
Control over what is learned. The corpus is the specification, the objective is the only lever, and the resulting competences are discovered afterwards rather than designed.
The tell
Capability and liability arrive in the same release, from the same cause, and cannot be separated after the fact.

What the commitment buys is an engineering economy. Improvements accrue once and are inherited everywhere, and the relationship between compute, data and loss is regular enough to plan against43, 44. What it forecloses is control over what is learned: the corpus is the specification, the objective is the only lever, and the resulting competences are discovered after the fact rather than designed. The debate over whether capabilities emerge discontinuously136 or whether the discontinuity is an artefact of the metric137 is, on this reading, a debate about whether the discovery process is as unpredictable as it looks; the architectural point stands either way, since a designer who must discover what their system can do has given up something real.

Two constraints deserve mention because they are architectural rather than economic. The first is data: estimates of the effective stock of public human text put exhaustion somewhere between 2026 and 2032, with a median around 2028138. The second is that inference-time compute has turned out to be a partial substitute for training-time compute139, and the reasoning models trained with reinforcement learning against verifiable rewards140 are, in this article's terms, systems that move work out of W and into C at run time. That is a store migration, not a scaling result.

6.2 Routing: the loop chooses which weights to read

Mixture-of-experts layers decouple parameter count from per-token compute: a learned router selects a small subset of expert sub-networks for each token141, an idea that goes back to adaptive mixtures of local experts142 and became standard practice through a sequence of scaling papers143, 144, 145, 146.

COMMITMENT 21 Foundation-model — one store, scaled

The loop decides which weights to read

The commitment
Only a small, input-dependent subset of the parameters participates in any forward pass, chosen by a learned router.
What it buys
Parameter count decoupled from per-token compute, which is the only known way to keep growing W without growing the cost of using it.
What it forecloses
Uniform utilisation. Routing is a balls-in-bins problem: the memory reserved for tokens that never arrive is the price of the compute saved, and it rises as experts multiply.
The tell
The model is enormous and cheap, and its throughput depends on the batch's content rather than only its size.

In this article's terms a router is a control-loop decision about which part of the weight store to read, which is why the plate loads both W and L. And because it is a loop decision made independently per token, it inherits a balls-in-bins problem. Experiment E7 computes the consequence exactly, in rationals rather than by simulation: with a per-expert capacity equal to the mean load, the expected fraction of tokens dropped is 3.296% for eight experts over 1,024 tokens, and 13.904% for 128 experts over the same tokens.

The capacity factor needed to hold expected drops below 1% rises from 1.063 at eight experts to 1.500 at 128 — that is, from 6.3% to 50% reserved memory that will usually go unused. The mechanism is not subtle: more experts at a fixed token count means a smaller mean load per expert and therefore a larger relative fluctuation. Sparsity is bought with slack, and the price rises with the sparsity.

The practical responses all amount to moving the decision. Auxiliary load-balancing losses push the router toward uniformity during training144, 145; finer-grained experts with shared always-on components reduce the variance of what any one expert must absorb146; a globally balanced assignment drops nothing at all, at the cost of being a constraint over the whole batch rather than a per-token choice. Each is a different answer to the question of where in the loop the routing decision should sit, which is an architectural question and not a tuning one.

6.3 One space, several sensors

Multimodal foundation models project inputs of different kinds into a shared representational space and process them with shared machinery147, 148. The generalist formulation goes further and treats control, vision and language as one sequence-modelling problem149.

COMMITMENT 22 Foundation-model — one store, scaled

One representation, several sensors

The commitment
Inputs of different modalities are projected into one representational space and processed by shared machinery.
What it buys
Cross-modal transfer that nobody has to specify: a concept learned from text constrains what is expected from an image, because they are the same vectors.
What it forecloses
Modality-specific structure. Whatever the shared space cannot express is not merely hard for one modality but unavailable to all of them.
The tell
The system describes a scene fluently and mis-states a spatial relation that the description logically requires.

The architectural payoff is transfer nobody has to specify: a concept constrained by text constrains what is expected from an image, because they are the same vectors. The cost is that whatever the shared space cannot express becomes unavailable to every modality rather than difficult for one. The characteristic failure — fluent scene description containing a spatial relation that the description itself logically forbids — is a symptom of a representation optimised for a contrastive or generative objective in which that relation carried little weight.

It is worth separating two things that "multimodal" is used to describe, because they are different store commitments. The first is a shared encoder space: images and text are mapped into one geometry so that similarity is comparable across them, which is what contrastive pretraining buys. The second is a shared processing stream: tokens from every modality flow through the same layers, so that a computation learned on one can be applied to another. The first is a claim about representation and is relatively cheap; the second is a claim that the modalities admit a common algorithm, which is much stronger and is what the generalist formulation asserts. Most systems described as multimodal make the first commitment and gesture at the second, and the distinction predicts where they fail: cross-modal retrieval works, cross-modal reasoning is uneven.

There is a deeper question here that the architecture cannot settle. A probe of what foundation models actually recover found that a model trained on orbital trajectories predicts them accurately while implying a force law that is nonsensical150. Whether one calls that a world model is a matter of definition. What it demonstrates architecturally is that a shared space is a commitment to some factorisation of the domain, that the objective chooses the factorisation, and that predictive accuracy does not identify it. Part VIII returns to this with a measurement.

6.4 Tools, and the question of which store should hold what

Tool use is usually presented as a capability extension. It is more precisely a content migration: facts, computation and state that W cannot hold reliably are moved to systems that can, and reached by an action the model chooses151.

COMMITMENT 23 Foundation-model — one store, scaled

Content moves out of the weights

The commitment
Facts, computation and state that the weights cannot hold reliably are placed in external systems and reached by an action the model chooses.
What it buys
Exactness, currency, privacy and auditability for the content that needs them, none of which W can provide, plus the ability to update knowledge without touching the model.
What it forecloses
Self-containment. Behaviour now depends on systems with their own failure modes and latencies, and the decision of when to call one is itself unreliable.
The tell
The system's knowledge cut-off stops mattering and its dependency list starts to.

The migration criterion is not capability but store properties. Arithmetic goes out because a calculator is exact and W is not. Current facts go out because W is dated the day training stopped and cannot be told so. Private data goes out because it was never in the corpus and should not be. Long documents go out because retrieval is cheaper than a longer context and because C is consulted unevenly53. Language itself stays in, because no external interface holds it.

Stated this way, the perennial question of retrieval versus fine-tuning answers itself, and it is not an accuracy comparison. Content that must be exact, current, scoped, private, attributable, deletable or auditable belongs outside the weights, because W offers none of those properties and no amount of training makes it offer them. Content whose value lies in being blended with everything else — style, syntax, the shape of a domain — belongs inside, because that blending is the one thing M cannot do.

What the migration costs is self-containment, and the bill arrives in Part VII. A system whose competence depends on external calls has acquired the failure modes, latencies and permissions of everything it calls, and the decision of when to call is itself made by the unreliable component.

Part VIIAgent architecturesthe loop as the architecture

7.1 When the loop becomes the architecture

An agent architecture makes the model a step inside a control loop rather than the system itself. The loop observes, decides, acts and records; it decides what is observed, when to stop, and what carries forward. ReAct's contribution was to interleave reasoning traces with actions so that each informs the other152; everything since has been an elaboration of the same four boxes.

COMMITMENT 24 Agent — the loop as architecture

The loop is the top-level program

The commitment
The model is a step inside a control loop rather than the system, and the loop decides what is observed, when to stop, and what is carried forward.
What it buys
Composition of a fallible component into a longer task, and a place to put everything the model cannot do: retries, limits, permissions, logging.
What it forecloses
An end-to-end account of behaviour. The loop is hand-written, so the system's competence is now partly a property of code that was never trained or evaluated.
The tell
Two deployments of the same model behave differently, and the difference is in a file nobody calls a model.

Three observations about this that the enthusiasm tends to obscure.

First, the loop is not new. It is sense-plan-act with a language model in the decide box, and the reasons Brooks gave for distrusting the arrangement in robotics153 have not been retired by putting a better component in one of the boxes. Part IX returns to this.

Second, and more consequentially: the loop is hand-written. It is content in L, authored by a developer, and it is where retries, limits, permissions, stop conditions and error handling live. A system's competence is therefore partly a property of code that was never trained and is rarely evaluated. Two deployments of the same model behave differently, and the difference sits in a file that nobody calls a model — which is a familiar species of technical debt in machine-learning systems, arriving in a new place154.

Third, evaluation of these systems is unusually treacherous. The critique that agent benchmarks conflate capability with cost, and that reported improvements frequently do not survive a controlled accounting, is well made155. Task-grounded benchmarks built from real software issues are a partial corrective156. The most useful quantitative framing is METR's, which measures the length of task a system completes at a given success rate157 — useful because horizon is exactly the quantity sections 7.4 and 8.2 show to be architecturally determined.

7.2 Plans as objects

Making the next several decisions explicit before executing any of them turns a sequence of choices into an artefact that can be examined, constrained and revised. Tree-of-thoughts search over intermediate states158 is the light version; classical planning is the heavy one.

COMMITMENT 25 Agent — the loop as architecture

The plan is a first-class object

The commitment
The next several decisions are represented explicitly, before any of them is executed, as an artefact that can be examined and revised.
What it buys
Lookahead, reuse, and a place to attach constraints. A plan can be checked against a policy before it acts, which no single decision can be.
What it forecloses
Robustness to a wrong model. Every step of a plan assumes the previous step's outcome, so plan quality decays with the horizon at a rate set by model error.
The tell
The system commits early and confidently, then executes past the point where reality diverged.

The benefit that matters for safety is not lookahead but inspectability: a plan can be checked against a policy before anything happens, and a single decision cannot. The cost is that every step of a plan assumes the previous step's outcome, so plan quality decays with the horizon at a rate set by model error — which is measured in section 8.2, and turns out to be worse than the naive compounding argument suggests.

There is an asymmetry here that is easy to miss. A plan is valuable in proportion to how expensive the actions are and how hard they are to reverse — which is exactly the regime in which the model's own errors are most costly, and therefore the regime in which committing several steps ahead is most dangerous. The two effects pull in opposite directions and the resolution is not theoretical: it is to make plans that are cheap to abandon. Every practical device in this area — short horizons, checkpointing, dry runs, requiring confirmation before irreversible actions — is a way of paying for lookahead without paying for commitment.

Voyager's demonstration is the clearest illustration of the good case: an agent in an open-ended environment that builds a library of reusable skills159. What makes it work is not the planning but the library — a store in M that outlives any one plan, which is the same observation as plate 13.

7.3 Memory that outlives the context

Once a system runs longer than a context window, the question of what persists becomes architectural. The available answers are: keep it in C and lose it; write it to M and retrieve it; or write it into W, which no deployed system does at useful granularity.

COMMITMENT 26 Agent — the loop as architecture

State persists outside the context

The commitment
What the system must remember across episodes is written to a store it can read back, rather than left in the context window.
What it buys
Continuity beyond one session and beyond one context length, with the record subject to inspection, correction and deletion by someone other than the model.
What it forecloses
Recall as a solved problem. Memory becomes retrieval: what is not surfaced was not remembered, and what is surfaced wrongly is worse than nothing.
The tell
Failures are of the form 'it knew and did not use it', which is a different bug from 'it did not know'.

The designs are by now familiar: a memory hierarchy with paging between a fixed context and external storage160; generative agents maintaining a timestamped stream with reflection and retrieval161. The benchmarks are more informative than the designs. Long-term conversational memory evaluations162, 163 consistently find that the hard part is not storage but surfacing: what is not retrieved was not remembered, and what is retrieved wrongly is worse than having nothing, because the system now argues from it.

This is the point at which the article's framing pays off most directly. "Giving the model memory" is not one thing. Writing to M is easy and nearly free. Deciding what to write is a judgement the system makes badly. Deciding what to read back is a retrieval problem with no ground truth. And the failure mode changes character entirely: an agent with memory fails by having known and not used, which is a different bug from not knowing, and is diagnosed differently.

7.4 Reflection, and the ceiling it cannot cross

Reflection feeds the system's own outputs, actions and errors back as observations, so a later step can revise an earlier one164. It works, and the reason it works is arithmetic.

COMMITMENT 27 Agent — the loop as architecture

The trace is available as input

The commitment
The system's own previous outputs, actions and errors are fed back as observations, so that a later step can revise an earlier one.
What it buys
Error correction without a parameter update, and a large practical gain over long tasks — the first retry is worth more than any subsequent one.
What it forecloses
Escape from the verifier. Reflection can only correct what something notices, so the achievable reliability is bounded by detection, not by effort.
The tell
Adding retries improves results sharply, then stops improving them while continuing to cost.

Experiment E8 computes the arithmetic exactly. A task of n dependent steps each succeeding with probability p completes with probability pn. For an even chance of finishing, per-step reliability must be 0.96594 at twenty steps, 0.99309 at a hundred and 0.99861 at five hundred. Nothing about a longer horizon is a matter of the model being smarter; it is a matter of a failure budget of 0.691% per step at n = 100.

Now add checkpoints. A verifier with recall ρ catches a failed block with probability ρ, and a caught failure buys a fresh attempt. With unlimited retries the per-block success saturates, and so does the task. At p = 0.99 and n = 100, the ceiling is 1.000 at ρ = 1.0, 0.904 at ρ = 0.9, 0.739 at ρ = 0.7 and 0.604 at ρ = 0.5. No retry budget crosses those numbers. The achievable reliability of a reflective agent is bounded by what its verifier can detect, not by how hard it tries.

The finite-retry table adds a practical note: at p = 0.99, ρ = 0.9 and blocks of ten steps, the first retry lifts task success from 0.366 to 0.836, the second to 0.894, and the fifth to 0.900. The first retry is worth more than all subsequent retries combined, and the fourth costs what it always cost while buying nearly nothing. Any agent framework that exposes a retry count should default it to one or two, and the reason is in the table rather than in taste.

7.5 Many loops, and where they fail

Multi-agent designs replicate the loop and type the messages between copies: distinct roles, a defined protocol, no shared state. The argument for it is the argument for processes over threads, and it long predates language models165, 166.

COMMITMENT 28 Agent — the loop as architecture

Replicate the loop and type the messages

The commitment
Several instances run with distinct roles and communicate through a defined protocol rather than a shared state.
What it buys
Specialisation, parallelism, and an interface at which behaviour can be constrained and observed — the same argument that favours processes over threads.
What it forecloses
A single locus of responsibility. The dominant failures are specification and inter-agent misalignment rather than reasoning, and neither is fixed by a better base model.
The tell
The transcript shows two components each behaving reasonably given what it was told, and the system failing anyway.

The most useful empirical result here is a taxonomy built from 1,600-plus annotated traces across seven frameworks, which sorts fourteen failure modes into three categories: roughly 41.8% specification issues, 36.9% inter-agent misalignment and 21.3% verification failures, with reported system failure rates between 41% and 87%167. The paper's own conclusion is the architecturally important one: better base models will be insufficient, because the failures are not reasoning failures. They are failures of what was specified and of what was communicated — content in L, authored by a person.

Read against plate 24, this is not surprising. When you move the top-level program into hand-written code and then replicate it, you have multiplied the surface of the least-tested store in the system. The reason to do it anyway is that a typed interface is a place where behaviour can be constrained and observed, and a monolithic loop offers no such place.

Part VIIIWorld-model architecturesthe loop simulates first

8.1 Learned simulators

A world model is a learned representation of the environment's dynamics that can be rolled forward internally, so that candidate actions are evaluated in imagination before being taken in the world. The idea is old — Craik's account of the nervous system as modelling reality168, Tolman's cognitive maps169 — and its computational form dates to Dyna's integration of learning, planning and reacting170, 171.

COMMITMENT 29 World-model — the loop simulates

Act in imagination before acting in the world

The commitment
A learned model of the environment's dynamics is used to evaluate candidate actions by rolling them forward internally.
What it buys
Sample efficiency and transfer: the same model serves any goal, and experience is reused rather than consumed. It is the clearest architectural sense of 'understanding'.
What it forecloses
Trust proportional to average accuracy. A model can be wrong almost everywhere and adequate on the trajectory the policy takes, or accurate almost everywhere and catastrophically wrong where it matters.
The tell
Performance depends on how errors are distributed relative to the policy, which is not what the model's validation loss measures.

The modern lineage runs from a compressed recurrent model trained on collected rollouts172, through latent-space planning from pixels173, to an agent that masters a wide range of domains from a single configuration174. In parallel MuZero showed that the model need not predict observations at all: it need only predict the quantities the planner consumes175, which extended the search-based line from AlphaGo176, 177 to domains without a given simulator.

COMMITMENT 30 World-model — the loop simulates

Predict in latent space, not in pixels

The commitment
The simulator predicts the evolution of a learned representation rather than of the observation itself.
What it buys
Tractability, and a principled account of abstraction: what the representation discards is what the architecture asserts is irrelevant to control.
What it forecloses
Verification against reality. Latent predictions cannot be checked by looking; the representation's adequacy is only ever tested through the policy that consumes it.
The tell
The rollouts look nothing like the world and the policy works, or the rollouts look convincing and the policy does not.

Plate 30 states the commitment that MuZero makes explicit and that every latent world model makes implicitly: what the representation discards is what the architecture asserts is irrelevant to control. That is a strong claim and it is not checkable by looking at the rollouts. It is only ever tested through the policy that consumes them, which means a world model can be visually convincing and useless, or visually nonsensical and sufficient. The video-generation line178, 179 is impressive on the first axis and largely untested on the second, and the two should not be conflated. LeCun's proposal for autonomous machine intelligence is essentially an argument that they must not be — that prediction should happen in a learned representation space and not in observation space180, a position with an antecedent in predictive-processing accounts of the brain181.

8.2 Planning through a model that is wrong

Every model-based system faces the same question: how much can you trust a learned simulator, and for how many steps? The textbook answer is the simulation lemma — value error grows with model error and with the effective horizon182 — and the practical answers are to limit rollout length183, to model uncertainty184, 185, and to keep the policy near the data that trained the model186.

Experiment E5 asks the question directly. A 9×9 maze with 52 free cells; optimal path 24 steps; a model corrupted by redirecting a deterministic subset of transitions, with any corruption that would make the goal unreachable rejected — so every model tested still describes a solvable world. Three execution regimes: commit to the plan; replan every step from the true state; replan and write each contradicted prediction back into the model.

The first result is stark. Open-loop execution fails at every corruption level tested, and so does replanning without repair. Even at 1.9% of transitions wrong, acting greedily from the true current state under a wrong model deadlocks. Feedback is not the fix; a wrong model plus perfect state observation still fails, because the error is in the model's evaluation of alternatives and not in its estimate of where the agent is.

Repair works, and degrades gracefully: 30, 32, 36, 55 and 76 steps against an optimum of 24, as corruption rises from 1.9% to 30.8%.

It is worth dwelling on why replanning alone fails, because the intuition that feedback fixes model error is very strong and is wrong in a specific way. Replanning corrects the agent's estimate of where it is. It does not correct the model's estimate of what the alternatives are worth. If the model believes a wall has a door in it, then at every state the policy will head for that door, discover it is a wall, be returned to a state from which the door still looks like the best option, and repeat. Observation of the current state is not observation of the model's error, and the two are conflated whenever someone says that a closed loop is robust to modelling error.

The second result is the one I did not expect and consider the most useful in the article. At 30.8% corruption the agent encounters 29 of the 64 wrong transitions; at 8.2% it meets 6 of 17. Most of the model's errors are never exercised at all. A model can be badly wrong in aggregate and entirely adequate along the trajectory its policy actually takes — and, symmetrically, a model with excellent average accuracy can be catastrophic if its errors sit where the policy is drawn. This is why validation loss is close to uninformative about planning quality, and it has a nastier corollary: an error that makes a shortcut look real is self-concealing. The policy is attracted to it, believes it has already succeeded, and therefore never executes the step that would refute it.

8.3 What a world model is not

The phrase "world model" has drifted to cover any internal representation that correlates with something about the world, which drains it of architectural content. Plate 29 is deliberately narrow: a world model is a store the system rolls forward to evaluate actions it has not taken. If nothing in the system does that, the term is doing no work.

The distinction matters because the two readings license different inferences. A model that predicts well may have recovered a representation that supports counterfactual reasoning, or may have recovered a compression that happens to predict — and the probe of inductive biases mentioned in section 6.3 is a direct demonstration that these come apart: accurate orbital prediction alongside an implied force law that is not Newton's150. Predictive accuracy does not identify the representation.

For the AGI question this cuts both ways, and I want to be even-handed about it. Against the strong claim: a system that predicts without a manipulable model cannot plan through novel interventions, and the evidence that current models frequently lack such structure is real. Against the strong denial: the architectural requirement is that something support forward evaluation of untaken actions, and nothing in the requirement says it must be legible, or resemble physics, or be separable from the policy. MuZero's model is none of those things and plans effectively175. The honest position is that this is an open empirical question with a badly specified predicate, and that section 10.3 owes an account of what would settle it.

Part IXEmbodied architecturesstructure outside the model

9.1 The body as a design variable

The embodied tradition's founding claim is not that robots are important but that representation is often the wrong tool: Brooks's subsumption architecture built competent behaviour from layered reactive processes with no central model187, 153, and the broader argument is that morphology and material properties perform computation that would otherwise fall to a controller188.

COMMITMENT 31 Embodied — structure outside the model

The body is part of the computation

The commitment
Mechanism, materials and the control loop's rate are treated as design variables that remove problems rather than as a fixed platform that poses them.
What it buys
Problems that never reach the learned components. Compliance, damping and geometry can make a contact task stable without any policy representing the contact at all.
What it forecloses
Portability. What the body solves, the model never learns, so the competence does not transfer to a different body and cannot be inspected in the weights.
The tell
The same policy on stiffer hardware fails in a way that looks like a perception problem and is not.

Stated in this article's terms the claim is precise and testable: structure and loop rate can remove problems that would otherwise require content in W. A compliant gripper makes a contact task stable without any policy representing the contact. A leg with the right passive dynamics walks down a slope with no controller at all. This is the strongest form of the embodiment argument, and it is straightforwardly measurable — unlike the weaker form, which asserts that intelligence requires a body and is not obviously an architectural claim at all.

The cost is portability. What the body solves, the model never learns, so the competence does not transfer to a different body and is not visible in the weights. Moravec's observation that sensorimotor competence is computationally expensive and cognitive competence cheap189 has held up well; the architectural gloss is that a great deal of what looks like sensorimotor intelligence is held in S, not in W, and therefore does not benefit from any of the scaling in Part VI. The extended-mind argument190 makes the same structural point about external stores in general.

9.2 Vision-language-action models

The dominant contemporary approach inverts the embodied tradition's scepticism about representation: take a large pretrained vision-language model and fine-tune it to emit actions, so that semantic competence acquired from corpora no robot produced becomes available to control. The line runs from large-scale grasping data191 through the first robotics transformers192, 193, embodied multimodal models194, language-conditioned affordance grounding195, the pooled cross-embodiment datasets196, open implementations197 and flow-based generalist policies198, 199, 200.

COMMITMENT 33 Embodied — structure outside the model

One policy across many bodies

The commitment
A single model is trained on data from heterogeneous embodiments, with the differences between them handled by the architecture rather than by separate models.
What it buys
Data pooling, which is the binding constraint in robotics, and semantic competence inherited from corpora no robot produced.
What it forecloses
Guarantees about any particular body. The architecture is now committed to a shared action representation, and whatever a given robot can do that this representation cannot express is unreachable.
The tell
Broad, shallow competence across tasks, with the dexterity ceiling set by the shared representation rather than by the hardware.

The architectural bet is that robotics is data-limited rather than representation-limited, and that pooling across bodies is the way to get data. The bet has paid off for breadth. What it commits to is a shared action representation, and whatever a particular robot can do that this representation cannot express is unreachable — which is why the characteristic profile of these systems is broad shallow competence with a dexterity ceiling set by the representation rather than by the hardware.

This is a genuine reversal of the embodied tradition's position and it should be marked as such. Brooks's argument was that a central world model is the wrong intermediate structure for a physical agent; the vision-language-action programme's bet is that a very large, very general representation acquired from non-robot data is precisely the right one, and that the reason the earlier attempts failed is that their representations were hand-built and small. Both positions can be right about different things — the reactive layer for stability at kilohertz, the learned representation for what to do next at hertz — and the resulting systems are in fact layered that way. What is unresolved is whether the semantic layer's competence extends to the physical judgements the reactive layer does not cover, and the honest answer at present is that the demonstrations do not distinguish the cases.

Notice also which store the semantic competence sits in. It is in W, written during a pretraining run on text and images, and it is therefore subject to everything in Part III: it cannot be edited, it has a date, and it generalises in whatever way the objective rewarded. A robot that knows what a colander is because of a web corpus is a robot whose knowledge of colanders you cannot correct.

9.3 Randomising what you cannot measure

Sim-to-real transfer poses the world-model problem of Part VIII in its sharpest form: the simulator is definitely wrong, and the question is what to do about it. Domain randomisation's answer is to stop trying to be right — train across a distribution of worlds wide enough to contain the real one201 — and it has carried dexterous manipulation202 and legged locomotion203, 204 into the physical world. Rapid motor adaptation adds an estimator that infers the current environment's parameters online205, which in this article's terms is a small fast store standing in for the part of the model the training run could not fix.

COMMITMENT 32 Embodied — structure outside the model

Randomise what you cannot measure

The commitment
Rather than model the world accurately, train across a distribution of worlds wide enough to contain it.
What it buys
Transfer from simulation without system identification, and robustness to parameters nobody measured — which is most of them.
What it forecloses
Precision. Widening the distribution buys robustness by paying performance, and there is no way to know from inside the simulator whether the distribution covers reality.
The tell
The policy is conservative in a characteristic way, and its margins do not improve with more simulated experience.

The trade is exact and worth stating plainly: widening the distribution buys robustness by paying performance, and there is no way from inside the simulator to know whether the distribution covers reality. That last clause is what distinguishes this from ordinary regularisation. E5's finding applies directly and uncomfortably: what matters is not the average fidelity of the simulator but whether its errors sit where the policy will go, and a policy optimised in simulation is drawn precisely to the regions the simulator gets optimistically wrong.

Part XComparisonthe ledger and its refutation

10.1 The split, not the learner

Every family in this article claims to generalise, and the claims are usually adjudicated by benchmark. Experiment E4 is a warning about that adjudication.

A command space of 96 items factorises as verb × modifier × direction, and the meaning is the composition of the three slot meanings. Three learners see the same training data: a nearest-neighbour memoriser; a learner that treats (verb, modifier) as one joint unit and direction as another — a wrong factorisation, but not a lazy one; and a correctly factored learner.

Under an i.i.d. holdout, the joint learner and the factored learner both score 100%. They are indistinguishable. Under a primitive holdout — where one verb appears in only one context — the joint learner scores 20.0% and the factored learner still scores 100%. Same learners, same metric, same test-set size; an 80-point separation appears and disappears with the split alone. Averaged over all six primitive-holdout splits the figures are identical, so this is not a quirk of one choice.

This is the SCAN result206 in miniature, and the compositional-generalisation literature exists because of it207, 208. The architectural moral is sharper than the usual one. It is not that neural networks fail to compose; a meta-learning approach reaches human-like systematic generalisation209. It is that an evaluation that does not vary the factorisation cannot distinguish architectures that differ in it — and most evaluations do not.

E4 also prices the assumption. The factored learner reaches the whole 96-command space from 6 examples; the memoriser needs all 96. A 16× sample saving, bought entirely by an assumption about how the domain factorises that could have been false — which is the general form of every inductive bias in this article, and the reason no-free-lunch results are worth remembering210.

Three things in the totals are worth naming.

The loop is the most-loaded store after the weights. That is not what the field's discourse looks like. The overwhelming majority of public attention goes to W — parameter counts, training runs, architectures in the narrow sense — while a large fraction of the commitments that actually determine system behaviour are content in L, hand-written, untested and unversioned. The multi-agent failure taxonomy in section 7.5 is a direct measurement of this imbalance: the failures are in the specification and the protocol, not in the reasoning.

Most commitments load two or three stores, not one. The plates that involve a single store are mostly the oldest ones. Almost every decision of consequence in a modern system is about the seam between two stores — what crosses it, in which direction, and who is allowed to be wrong. This is why the symbolic-versus-neural framing has aged so badly: it is a dispute about which store should exist, and the answer for twenty years has been both, and the interesting question is the interface.

The ledger also makes a prediction that the article's own narrative does not. If most commitments concern seams, then the productive research direction is not a better store but a better interface discipline — ways of making explicit what one store may assume about another, of detecting when the assumption has failed, and of recovering. Almost nothing in the architectural literature is written this way. There is an enormous amount on how to build each store and remarkably little on how to specify what crosses between them, which is the opposite of the situation in the software architecture literature, where interface specification is the mature part of the field.

C is the least-loaded store and the most recently invented. Almost everything built on it — in-context learning, prompting, chain-of-thought, retrieval, agent scratchpads, reasoning traces — postdates 2017 and none of it was predicted by the paper that introduced it. That should induce some humility about the completeness of any store taxonomy, including this one.

10.3 What would tell this framing apart from its rivals

A framing that cannot fail is not worth having, so here is what would count against this one.

The claim. Architectural disputes are disputes about which store carries which content, and the costs of each assignment are largely knowable in advance from the store's properties rather than discoverable only by experiment.

What would refute it. A store with the edit locality of S and the acquisition mechanism of W — content that can be added, scoped and deleted one item at a time and acquired from raw signal without supervision. Nothing in this article rules that out; the closest existing approaches are ACT-R's separation of storage from availability (section 5.2) and test-time memory (section 3.5), and if the latter matures into precise, revocable, inspectable editing of learned content, the framing's central trade collapses and the article is substantially wrong.

A weaker refutation: demonstrate a case where the assignment of content to stores makes no difference to any observable — where a system holding its facts in W and one holding them in M behave identically under editing, ageing, attribution and deletion. I claim this is impossible for the four properties named in section 6.4, and that claim is a straightforward empirical target.

What would not refute it. A system that is much more capable than today's while making the same store commitments. Capability is not what the framing predicts; it predicts the shape of the failures and the cost of the fixes. Nor is a definitional dispute about AGI relevant here — the various formal proposals211, 212, 213, 214, 215 disagree about the target, not about how the stores behave.

It is worth adding what this framing does not settle, since the question motivating the field is which architecture reaches general intelligence. The framing is neutral on that. It says that the choice is a choice about timescales; it does not say which set of timescales is sufficient, and I do not know of an argument that settles it. Newell's warning about accumulating phenomena without a unifying theory216 applies to architecture research as much as to the psychology he aimed it at, and the standard invocations of the levels-of-analysis distinction217 and of what a control system needs to be218 do not settle it either. The research challenges Langley and colleagues set out remain open219, and the observation that progress is harder than expected for reasons that recur has been made carefully220. A standard reference gives the canonical survey of the alternatives221. The honest summary is that this article organises the disputes; it does not adjudicate the one that motivates them.

10.4 Against this article

Four objections, in ascending order of how much they worry me.

The five stores are arbitrary. Partly true. Why not separate the KV cache from other activations, or optimiser state from parameters? Because the five are the coarsest partition that makes the disputes in Parts II to IX legible, and a finer one would multiply plates without changing verdicts. But "the coarsest partition that works for my purposes" is a weaker justification than it sounds, and someone with different purposes would draw different lines.

Timescale is not the only architectural dimension. True. Parallelism, precision, differentiability, locality of computation and cost structure are all real and none of them reduces to rewrite rate. My defence is that timescale predicts more of the observed disputes than the alternatives, not that it predicts all of them.

Reframing is not explaining. The strongest form: nothing in this article tells you which architecture will work, and a taxonomy that organises the debate without settling it may be adding vocabulary rather than knowledge. I accept the shape of this and resist the conclusion, because several of the lab's results are consequences of the framing that I did not anticipate and would not have looked for otherwise — E5's self-concealing errors and E8's verifier ceiling in particular. A frame that generates surprises is doing more than renaming.

The experiments are small, and the generalisation is an inference. This is the objection I think is correct and cannot fully answer. Nineteen pairs in a 4,096-integer matrix is not a language model. A 9×9 maze is not a robot. Fifty-two free cells, thirty-three plates, ninety-six commands: these are exhaustive over their own domains and tiny relative to the systems the article generalises to. What the experiments establish is that certain trade-offs are structural — they follow from the arithmetic of superposition, enumeration and independent routing, and not from any contingent property of a particular model. What they do not establish is the magnitude of those trade-offs at scale, or that no mechanism intervenes between the toy and the real system to change the sign. I have tried to state each result as the structural claim it supports rather than the quantitative claim it does not, and readers should treat every number in this article as a demonstration that a phenomenon exists rather than a measurement of how large it is.
