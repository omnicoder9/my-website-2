1 The shape of the dispute

1.1Two machines

Set two machines side by side. The first is a large neural network trained on text and images; it discusses Kant competently, writes working code, and revises its answer when you point out a mistake. The second is a functional duplicate built to a different specification, in a different substrate, by a laboratory that has published every detail of its construction. Every physical fact about both is on the table. Every input-output pair either will ever produce is on the table too.

Now ask: does either understand anything? Is either conscious? Could either be wronged? Does either know that Kant died in 1804?

The striking feature of these questions is not that they are hard. It is that two well-informed people can agree about every fact just stipulated and still answer them differently, and that neither can name an experiment that would move the other. This is not a temporary condition awaiting better instruments. It is the structure of the disagreement.

That structure is the subject of this article. The claim it defends is that the fourteen questions in its table of contents are not fourteen independent puzzles about machines. They are one question asked fourteen times: what kind of fact would make the ascription true? Disputants who answer that question differently will disagree about machines no matter what machines turn out to do, and disputants who answer it the same way usually find that their remaining disagreement is tractable.

This is not a deflationary thesis. It does not say the questions are verbal1 or that they should be dissolved rather than answered. Several of them are straightforwardly empirical and are being settled right now by ordinary research. What it says is that the empirical ones and the constitutive ones look identical from the outside, that they are constantly mistaken for one another, and that almost all of the futility in the public debate about artificial general intelligence comes from answering one with the other.

1.2Fourteen questions of one form

Each of the fourteen questions has the form does system S have property P?, or its close relative what is it for a system to have P? The properties differ — intelligence, understanding, concepts, consciousness, moral status, emotion, desire, intention, selfhood, knowledge — but the logical situation is the same in every case. Someone proposes that P is present. Someone else denies it. Both accept the same description of the system.

When that happens, one of three things is going on. Either the disputants disagree about a fact neither has checked, in which case the dispute is empirical and can be closed. Or they disagree about which facts are relevant to P, in which case the dispute is about the individuation of P and no amount of further fact-gathering will close it. Or they agree on both and are using P to do different work — one to describe, one to license a policy — in which case the dispute is practical and should be conducted as such.

The middle case is the interesting one, and it is where most of these fourteen live. Calling a dispute constitutive is not calling it unreal. Whether a virus is alive was a constitutive dispute; so was whether Pluto is a planet; so was whether a foetus is a person. Two of those were settled by stipulation that turned out to be useful, one was not settled at all, and the difference between them is not a difference in how much biology or astronomy anyone knew. It is a difference in what turned on the answer.

The historical parallel worth keeping in view is not Copernicus but the period after a paradigm has become unstable and before a new one has taken hold2, when practitioners agree about the observations and disagree about what the observations are observations of. The debate about artificial general intelligence is in that condition, and it has been for seventy years3.

There is a closer precedent still. Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation is the claim that two manuals for translating a language can be compatible with the totality of speech dispositions and yet assign incompatible meanings to individual sentences4. Whatever one makes of the thesis, its form is the form of every dispute in this article: the evidence is exhausted, the rival hypotheses survive, and the question of which is correct turns out to require a fact of a kind the evidence was never going to contain. Quine's own conclusion was that there is no such fact. That is one option among several, and this article does not take it — but it is worth noticing that a philosopher who took the problem seriously enough to follow it through reached a position most people find intolerable, and that no one has since dislodged it by gathering more data5.

The numbering scheme below is borrowed, with some cheek, from a book that ends by telling the reader to throw away the ladder they climbed6. The borrowing is not entirely ornamental. That book's central device — saying what can be said clearly and then marking precisely where saying stops — is the device this article is trying to apply to a subject its author would have found unrecognisable.

1.3The five determination bases

If disputes turn on what makes an ascription true, it is worth being explicit about the candidates. There are five, and everything in the rest of this article is an application of them.

Constitution (C). The material the system is made of and the causal powers of that material. Positions that load C say that being made of the right stuff, or the wrong stuff, settles the question independently of what the system does. Searle's biological naturalism is the clearest case7; so, less obviously, is integrated information theory, which makes phenomenal experience depend on a physical cause-effect structure that a functionally equivalent feedforward implementation lacks8.

Organisation (O). The abstract form: the state structure, the causal roles, the computation being run. Positions that load O hold that the property supervenes on organisation, so any material carrying the same organisation carries the property9,10. Functionalism in all its varieties lives here, and so does most of computer science's implicit philosophy of mind.

History (H). How the system came to stand in the relations it stands in — what selected it, what trained it, what it has been in causal contact with, what it has been used for. Positions that load H hold that two systems can be organisationally and materially identical and still differ, because one has a history the other lacks. Teleosemantics is the canonical example11, and so is any externalism about content.

Phenomenality (P). Whether there is something it is like to be the system12. Positions that load P hold that the answer turns on a fact about the system's point of view, which is precisely the fact that no third-person description contains.

Norms and practice (N). What an interpreting community is entitled, obliged, or well advised to say. Positions that load N hold that the ascription records a standing rather than reporting an inner state — Dennett's intentional stance13, Brandom's inferentialism14, relational accounts of moral status15.

These are not five theories. They are five kinds of fact, and a position typically loads two or three. The value of naming them is that it makes visible what would otherwise be invisible: two people can produce the same verdict about a machine while quantifying over different bases, and two people quantifying over the same base can usually find the disagreement that separates them.

1.4What would count as separating two answers

The method throughout is Platt's16: for each question, ask which observation the rival answers do not both predict. Where such an observation exists, the question is empirical and the article says what to look for. Where none exists, the article says so plainly and then asks the next question — what would have to be decided, rather than discovered, for the dispute to close?

Ten computational experiments accompany the argument. They cannot settle a philosophical question and are not offered as though they could. What they do is make the structure of a dispute exact: how much a given kind of evidence constrains, how many candidate answers survive a given constraint, and where two criteria that are usually run together come apart. Each is an exhaustive count over a finitely specified space, so the numbers are not estimates. The full listing runs at the end of the article and reproduces exactly.

One objection can be answered immediately, because it is the most common response to any analysis of this shape: just define your terms and the problem goes away. It does not, and the reason is worth stating. A stipulation settles what a word will mean in the stipulator's mouth. It does not settle what turns on the question the word was being used to ask. If someone stipulates that 'understanding' means passing a particular battery, they have not thereby made it the case that systems passing the battery should be trusted with the tasks we trust understanders with; that inference needs a further argument, and the further argument is the original question in new clothes. Stipulation is useful for the empirical band, where it converts a vague question into a sharp one that can then be answered. It is useless for the constitutive band, where the vagueness is not in the question but in what we want the answer for.

A word on what this article is not. It is not a survey; the literature is enormous and the citations here are load-bearing rather than representative. It is not a forecast; nothing below turns on when or whether artificial general intelligence arrives. And it is not neutral: it defends a specific claim about where the disagreements live, and that claim is refutable in the way described in §7.2.

2 Intelligence

2.1What is intelligence?

The question looks like a request for a definition and is usually answered with one. The trouble is that the leading definitions are not competing measurements of a single quantity. They pick out different subjects.

Turing's proposal was frankly operational and frankly social: replace the unanswerable question with a test whose outcome is a verdict by a human judge17. Whatever else it is, indistinguishability under interrogation is a fact about observers, and it has the properties facts about observers have. It is sensitive to the judge's expectations, to the interrogation protocol, and to the state of public familiarity with the technology — which is why the test was passed by a program of a few hundred lines in 1966 for judges who were not expecting it18, and why controlled versions with informed judges and modern systems produce a quite different and more interesting result19.

Newell and Simon's physical symbol system hypothesis is a claim about mechanism: a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action20. This is a claim about form, and it is substantive precisely because it says that the form is what matters and the matter is not.

Legg and Hutter's formalisation defines intelligence as expected performance across all computable environments, weighted by algorithmic simplicity21,22. This is exact, and its exactness makes its dependence visible: change the prior over environments and you change the ranking. The no-free-lunch results make the point sharply — averaged over all environments with no prior, every optimiser performs identically23. A measure of general intelligence is therefore always a measure relative to a class of problems somebody chose.

Chollet's proposal is the sharpest departure: intelligence is skill-acquisition efficiency, so a system's score should be discounted by the prior knowledge and experience it was given24. On this measure a system that solves a task after a million examples is less intelligent than one that solves it after three, even if both end at the same accuracy. This is a genuinely different subject of measurement — a learning history rather than a competence — and the benchmark built on it behaves accordingly25. Efforts to ground the notion in the psychometric tradition take a third route again, decomposing general ability into a fixed set of cognitive domains and scoring coverage across them26,27,28.

None of these is wrong. They are answers to different questions, and a system can score high on one and low on another without either measurement being in error. The determination bases differ too: Turing's criterion loads N, Newell and Simon's loads O, Chollet's loads H. Which means that an argument about whether a system is really intelligent, conducted without fixing the measure, is an argument in which each party is entitled to their conclusion.

A deeper point lurks here, and it recurs in every part of what follows. The field's founding move was to replace an intractable question with a tractable substitute17. That move is often good practice, and it is what allowed artificial intelligence to become an engineering discipline at all. But a substitute is only as good as the inference from it back to the original, and the inference is almost never stated. Turing was clear that he was changing the question. Most of what has followed has not been clear about it, and the result is a discipline whose central term has an operational definition and a non-operational job, with nothing connecting the two.

There is a practical corollary. The recurring public pattern — a system achieves something that was taken as a marker of intelligence, and the marker is promptly declared not to have been a marker after all — is usually diagnosed as goalpost-moving, and sometimes it is. But it is also what you would expect if the community had never fixed which of these four subjects it was measuring29. When a chess program wins, the Newell-Simon criterion is satisfied and the Chollet criterion is not, and both parties are describing the same event correctly.

2.2Is intelligence substrate-independent?

The standard argument for substrate independence is multiple realisability: mental kinds are individuated functionally, functional kinds can be realised in many materials, therefore mental kinds are not identical to material kinds9,10. The argument is old enough that its weaknesses are well mapped. Bechtel and Mundale pointed out that the argument's force depends on describing the psychological state coarsely and the neural state finely, and that at matched grain the multiple realisation often disappears30; Polger and Shapiro pressed the point into a general scepticism about whether the paradigm cases are cases at all31.

But there is a prior problem, and it is worth making exact. Multiple realisability says that behaviour and function do not fix the material. It is sometimes read as also saying that behaviour fixes the function — that if we know what a system does, we know what computation it is running. The first experiment shows how badly that fails.

Enumerate every three-state, two-input, two-output machine: 5,832 of them. Fix behaviour completely by recording the output produced for every input string up to length five — 63 strings, more than enough to distinguish anything this small can distinguish. The 5,832 machines fall into 1,054 behavioural classes. Not one class has a single member. The most-realised behaviour is produced by 1,188 machines, and after quotienting by state relabelling — so that mere renaming does not inflate the count — 603 genuinely different organisations remain.

The constraint runs in one direction only. 1,028 of the 1,054 behaviours are unavailable to any machine with fewer than three states, so behaviour does bound organisation from below: it tells you what the system must have at least. It tells you nothing about what it has at most. Anyone who moves from a system's performance to a claim about its internal organisation is making an inference this experiment shows to be invalid, and it is invalid in the small case, where all the facts are available.

This matters for a specific contemporary argument. Claims that a language model must, or must not, be performing some particular internal operation because of what it produces are claims of exactly this form. The honest version of the claim requires looking inside — which is why interpretability work is philosophically load-bearing and not merely engineering32,33.

2.3The implementation relation, in four grades

Substrate independence needs a notion of implementation: what it is for this lump of matter to be running that computation. Putnam and Searle both argued that the notion, unconstrained, is trivial — that on a permissive enough mapping every open physical system implements every finite automaton, so saying that the brain computes says nothing34,35. The constructive reply is Chalmers': require the mapping to respect the system's counterfactual structure, so that the physical states must mirror the computational transitions not only for the inputs that arrived but for every input that could have36,37. Godfrey-Smith and Piccinini give related repairs from different directions38,39.

The second experiment prices the repair. A twelve-state physical device is built explicitly as a product: a three-state core whose state the output reads off, times a four-tick counter the output ignores. The device therefore does have a determinate computational profile. The question is which grade of the implementation relation can see it.

Under grade 1 — Putnam's, with physical states taken as time-indexed and the choice of which physical property counts as an output left open — all 5,832 candidate machines qualify. Under grade 2, where the output interpretation is fixed in advance but the state mapping is still free, the count depends entirely on how long you watched: 2,916 before you observe anything, 180 after four steps, 2 after nine. Grade 3, which requires a single mapping from physical state types rather than time slices, differs from grade 2 in the middle of the range — 18 against 36 at five steps — and converges to the same place.

Grade 4, Chalmers' combinatorial state automaton, requires the mapping to mirror the transitions for every one of the 4,096 possible input sequences. It returns 2, and it returns 2 without observing anything at all.

Three things follow. First, the triviality objection is correct about the unconstrained relation and irrelevant to the constrained one; the ladder is not a slippery slope but a series of steps with different prices. Second, the behavioural route to the same answer is hostage to how long you watched and gives no signal that it has arrived — at four observed steps it was off by a factor of ninety and looked perfectly stable. Third, and most importantly for the philosophy, the counterfactual constraint is not a behavioural constraint at all. It quantifies over runs that did not happen. Substrate independence is a substantive thesis only when implementation is read modally, and read modally it is a thesis about causal structure — which is to say it has already conceded that not every physical arrangement will do.

It is worth being clear about what a functionalist is committed to when she says that a mental kind is defined by its causal role. The precise version is the Ramsey-Lewis method: take the best theory of the mental, replace every mental term with a variable, existentially quantify, and let the resulting open sentence define the terms40,41. What that method delivers is a definition of a role, and a role is realised by whatever occupies it. The method is elegant and it makes the substrate question look easy — anything that occupies the role has the property, by construction.

The experiment above says why the ease is illusory. Occupying a role is a modal notion: it is a matter of what the occupant would do across the whole space of circumstances the theory quantifies over, not of what it did. A system's actual trajectory is compatible with occupying many roles, and grade 4 is what occupying one comes to. This is the same distinction Turing's own construction turns on — a machine is individuated by its table, which specifies a response for every configuration, not by any particular run42. Functionalism read modally is a serious thesis about causal structure. Functionalism read as a claim about input-output profiles is the thesis the triviality arguments demolish.

Whether that concession is fatal is a further question, and it is where Maudlin's counterfactual-stripping argument bites43, where the externalist worry about whether computational identity depends on content enters44,45, and where the disagreement between mechanistic and abstract accounts of computation is joined46. What the experiment fixes is the shape of the ground being fought over.

2.4Must intelligence be embodied?

Three theses travel under the name of embodiment, and they have almost nothing in common except a slogan.

The causal thesis says a body is how a mind reliably acquires the data and the corrective feedback it needs. Held and Hein's kittens are the classic demonstration that self-generated movement, not passive exposure, builds visuomotor competence47; Brooks' argument that intelligence requires situatedness was pitched at this level, against representational systems that had never touched anything48. Read this way, embodiment is an engineering bet about training regimes, and it is testable in the ordinary way. Systems trained on text and images and then transferred to robotic control are running the experiment49,50, as is the reverse case, and the results are neither uniform nor finished.

The constitutive thesis says bodily and environmental processes are proper parts of the cognitive process rather than inputs to it — the extended mind51, enactivism52, morphological computation53. Adams and Aizawa's reply is that this confuses coupling with constitution, and that a mark of the cognitive is needed before the boundary can be drawn54. Notice what the dispute is: both sides agree about every experiment. They disagree about where a system's boundary falls, which is a question about individuation, not about data.

The conceptual thesis says contents are individuated by sensorimotor engagement, so that without a body there is nothing for a thought to be about. Dreyfus argued something in this vicinity for forty years, and his later diagnosis — that the failure was not of engineering but of the Cartesian picture of a subject facing a world of context-free facts — is the sharpest version55,56. This thesis is not refutable by building anything, because it fixes in advance what would count as the thing being built.

Barsalou's grounded-cognition programme supplies the empirical middle57, and Shapiro's taxonomy is the standard map58. Clark's development of the causal thesis into a general picture of the mind as an opportunistic exploiter of bodily and environmental resources is the version that has aged best, in part because it makes claims that can be checked one at a time59. The practical recommendation is narrow and, I think, unavoidable: when someone says intelligence requires embodiment, ask which of the three they mean, because the first is a research question, the second is a boundary decision, and the third is a constitutive claim wearing empirical clothes.

2.5What a capability result can show

Since 2020 the debate has been driven by capability results, and it is worth being explicit about what a capability result is evidence for.

The Winograd schema challenge was designed as a test that could not be passed by surface statistics: a pair of sentences differing in one word, where resolving a pronoun requires knowing something about how the world works60. It was proposed as a successor to the Turing test precisely because it seemed to require the thing the Turing test only indirectly probed. It is now saturated. So is almost every benchmark proposed in the same spirit.

Two readings of that history are available, and the determination framework says both are partly right. On the first, the benchmarks were bad operational definitions of the underlying capacity and their saturation shows only that the capacity was underspecified. On the second, the capacity was real, the systems have it, and the goalposts moved. What settles which reading applies is whether the benchmark's designers said in advance what internal fact passing would establish — and mostly they did not, because the working definition of intelligence in the field has been operational for seventy years and operational definitions do not have internal facts attached61,62.

Moravec's observation cuts across all of it: the tasks that feel like intelligence to us are computationally cheap, and the ones that feel like nothing — perception, locomotion, the ordinary handling of objects — are expensive63. The systematic misprediction this generates is not a failure of forecasting technique. It is what happens when a capacity is individuated by how difficult it feels from the inside, which is a fact about introspective access and not about the capacity. Human psychometrics has the same structure at its base: the general factor is a statistical regularity across tests, not a mechanism anyone has isolated64,28.

None of which means capability results are uninformative. They bear directly on the empirical questions in the article's first band, and they bear on the empirical cores of the mixed ones. They do not bear on the constitutive band at all, and the recurring public argument in which one side cites a capability result and the other says 'but it does not really understand' is precisely an argument in which one party is answering an empirical question and the other a constitutive one. The community's own view of where the difficulty lies is worth recording: in a 2025 survey of AI researchers, a large majority judged that scaling current approaches is unlikely to yield general intelligence, and a majority held that a human-like reasoning system would need to be substantially symbolic65.

3 Content

3.1Can syntax produce semantics?

The Chinese Room is forty-five years old and has not moved anybody66. That fact is itself a datum, and the determination framework explains it.

The argument is valid and its premises are stated plainly: programs are formal; minds have contents; syntax is neither constitutive of nor sufficient for semantics; therefore running a program is neither constitutive of nor sufficient for a mind67. Four of the five standard replies attack the third premise. The systems reply says the man is not the system and one should ask whether the room as a whole understands. The robot reply says that once the symbols are hooked to a world by causal traffic, the syntax is no longer alone. The brain-simulator reply questions whether a simulation at neural grain counts as 'formal' in the sense the first premise needs. Copeland's version attacks the inference itself, arguing that the argument commutes a property of a part into a property of the whole68; Harnad's runs the other way, granting the conclusion for pure symbol systems and locating the repair in grounding69,70.

Here is what has never happened: an observation of the room that moved anyone. It cannot happen, because the room is stipulated to be behaviourally perfect. The third premise is a claim about what makes content facts true. It loads O negatively — it says organisation is not enough — without specifying what is enough, which is why the argument is easier to state than to extend.

The productive descendants of the argument are the ones that answer the question Searle left open. Two are worth taking seriously, and they are the subject of the next two clauses.

It is worth asking why an argument that cannot be tested has nonetheless been so productive. The answer, I think, is that its third premise is not arbitrary. It encodes a genuine asymmetry: we do not think a book about photosynthesis photosynthesises, or that a weather simulation is wet, and Searle's question is why a simulation of thinking should be different35. The functionalist has an answer — that thinking, unlike raining, is individuated by what it does rather than by what it is made of, so a sufficiently faithful simulation of thinking is thinking. But that answer is the thesis under dispute, not an argument for it. Both sides are standing on their conclusion, which is exactly what happens when a question is constitutive and both parties believe it is empirical.

Millière and Buckner's survey of how the classic debates map onto language models is the right starting point for anyone who wants to see how much of the 1980s argument is still live and how much has been superseded by facts about how these systems are actually built71. Their verdict, which this article shares, is that the continuity is greater than either the enthusiasts or the dismissers usually allow, and that the parts which have genuinely moved are the parts where somebody made a testable claim.

3.2Newman's problem, and why more theory never helps

The first descendant is a result from 1928 that most discussions of machine semantics do not cite and should. Newman, reviewing Russell's structuralism, observed that a purely structural description of the world is satisfied by any domain of the right cardinality with a suitably chosen relation — so structure alone conveys almost nothing72. Putnam's model-theoretic argument is the same point aimed at reference73, and the standard replies invoke something outside the structure: Lewis' natural properties74, causal constraints, or eligibility conditions of some kind75.

The third experiment makes the arithmetic explicit on a domain of seven objects.

There are just over four trillion binary relations available on that domain. The complete structural description of the actual one — every permutation- invariant fact there is — narrows the field to 2,520, which is the number of labelled copies of a structure whose automorphism group has order two. It does not narrow it further, and it cannot: three additional structural axioms, chosen independently and checked exhaustively, leave the admissible set at exactly 2,520. This is the formal content of Putnam's 'just more theory' response. Adding structure to a structural theory changes nothing, because the addition is itself permutation-invariant.

What does cut the space is a fact that names an object: that this thing stands in the relation to that one. Each such anchor cuts the admissible set, and it takes twenty-two of them to reach a unique interpretation. Twenty-two de re facts, on a domain of seven objects, to fix reference that unlimited internal structure could not fix at all.

Applied to the case at hand: an argument that a system's internal representational structure is rich enough to constitute understanding is an argument that runs directly into this result. Richness of structure is exactly what does not help. What helps is world-involving fact — which is why the serious contemporary position on machine content is not structuralist but teleosemantic, and why multimodality by itself is not the answer people take it to be76.

3.3The grounding floor

The second descendant is Harnad's: symbols defined only in terms of other symbols are like trying to learn Chinese from a Chinese-Chinese dictionary70. Vincent-Lamarre and colleagues turned this into a graph-theoretic question about real dictionaries and found a definable kernel from which the rest of the vocabulary can be reached, with a minimum grounding set inside it77.

The fourth experiment reproduces the structure on synthetic dictionaries where the exact answer is computable.

A word becomes available when every word in its definition is available. Start with nothing and the closure is empty: zero words out of any vocabulary size. The minimum set that must be supplied from outside is 6 words for a 20-word dictionary with three-word definitions, and 52 for a 200-word one — 26 per cent. The floor does not vanish as vocabulary grows. At two-word definitions it falls and then stabilises around an eighth of the vocabulary. At five-word definitions it rises past sixty per cent, because longer definitions add circularity faster than they add reach.

Two cautions, because this result is easy to over-read. It shows that a closed definitional system has a non-empty grounding requirement. It does not show that a language model's representations are a closed definitional system — that is precisely what is in dispute, and the strongest current argument is that they are not, because the training signal was produced by world-involving processes and some internal states are selected for tracking world features rather than text features76,78. Bender and Koller's argument runs the other way, holding that form alone cannot fix meaning and that a system trained on form alone therefore cannot acquire it79; Pavlick's review of the empirical situation is more equivocal than either camp80.

What the experiment settles is the shape of the requirement, and one negative claim: whatever grounds a symbol system, it is not more of the symbol system.

The corollary for multimodality is worth spelling out, because it cuts against a common assumption. Adding vision to a language model adds a second symbol system and a correlation between the two. It does not, by itself, add a de re fact of the kind the Newman result showed to be necessary: a pixel array is a representation, and a representation of a representation is still inside the dictionary. What would add such a fact is a causal channel with a selection history — which images in a training corpus may or may not supply, depending on how the objective was constructed. This is exactly the reason Coelho Mollo and Millière reach the conclusion that multimodality and embodiment are neither necessary nor sufficient for grounding76, a conclusion that surprises people on both sides of the debate and follows straightforwardly once the requirement is stated correctly.

Note where teleosemantics puts the burden. Millikan's proper functions, and Dretske's and Neander's informational variants, make content depend on a history of selection81,82,83,84. If that is right, the question about a trained model is not whether it is clever or fluent or multimodal. It is whether gradient descent against an objective that was itself shaped by a world counts as a selection process of the right kind. That is a real question with a real answer, and it is being approached from the interpretability side32,85.

3.4What constitutes understanding?

Ask what would show that a system understands and you get a ladder, not a line.

Prediction is the bottom rung, and any adequate curve fit clears it. Counterfactual competence is higher: getting right what would have happened had things been otherwise requires structure that a fit need not have86. Intervention is higher still — knowing what happens when you reach in and change something is the mark of a causal model rather than an observational one87. Transfer to a domain the training never covered requires the abstraction to have been the right one. Explanation — saying which features carry the result — requires the model to be inspectable, and the honest finding here is that a system's own explanation of its behaviour need not be the explanation of its behaviour88.

These five rungs are climbable by evidence, and the evidence is accumulating in both directions. Emergent world representations in a model trained on a synthetic task85, linear representations of space and time89, and interpretable feature decompositions32 all push one way. Vafa and colleagues push the other, with an inductive-bias probe showing that a model can predict orbital trajectories accurately while the force law recovered from its internal states is nonsensical90 — which is the counterfactual rung failing while the prediction rung is passed perfectly.

The sixth rung is different. Grasping — the sense that one sees why, the aspect under which a structure becomes intelligible — is what a good deal of ordinary talk about understanding is about, and no external test reaches it. Grimm's question of whether understanding is a species of knowledge is partly a question about how much of this residue there is91; de Regt's contextual account tries to cash it out in terms of what a practitioner can do with a theory92, and Wilkenfeld's in terms of representation manipulability93 — both of which are attempts to convert the sixth rung into the fourth.

Mitchell and Krakauer's survey of the debate reaches a conclusion consonant with this one: the disagreement about whether large language models understand is substantially a disagreement about what understanding is, and it will not be closed by better benchmarks94. Shanahan's advice — be careful which vocabulary you reach for, because the folk-psychological one imports commitments95 — is the practical corollary.

3.5Can machines possess concepts?

Concept possession has more candidate criteria than almost any other property in this article, and they divide neatly along the determination bases.

The classical account requires mastery of necessary and sufficient conditions, which is a poor fit for almost every concept anyone actually has. Prototype accounts trade that for a central tendency and pay with compositionality96. Inferential-role accounts — you have the concept if you are disposed to make the transitions it licenses — are testable from behaviour, though the previous section's warning applies: dispositions underdetermine what produces them14. Peacocke's possession conditions are explicitly normative: to have a concept is to find certain inferences primitively compelling, which is a matter of entitlement rather than disposition97. Informational and teleosemantic accounts require a causal or selectional history98,11. Machery's conclusion, that 'concept' names a heterogeneous collection that cognitive science would do better without, is a live option99.

The central empirical dispute has been about systematicity. Fodor and Pylyshyn's argument was that thought is systematic — anyone who can think aRb can think bRa — and that connectionist architectures either fail to explain this or explain it only by implementing a classical architecture100,101. The argument stood for decades, with Smolensky's tensor-product binding as the main technical reply102. Lake and Baroni's result is the one that changed the situation: a standard sequence model, trained with a meta-learning procedure over a distribution of compositional tasks, achieves human-like systematic generalisation103. The in-principle version of the argument does not survive that; the question of what training regime produces the capacity remains open, and is now an empirical question of exactly the right kind104.

A methodological caution attaches to all of this. Probing studies establish that some property is linearly decodable from a system's internal states. Decodability is weaker than representation: information can be present in a state without the system using it, and a probe trained to find a feature may find it in the probe's own capacity rather than the model's. The strongest results control for this by intervening — changing the internal state and checking that the behaviour changes as the interpretation predicts85,32. This is the same escalation from correlation to intervention that separates the lower rungs of the understanding ladder from the higher ones, and it is not a coincidence that the concepts question and the understanding question turn out to need the same methodology.

Which leaves the normative criterion untouched. If having a concept is being entitled to certain inferences rather than being disposed to make them, then two systems with identical dispositions can differ in concept possession, and no probe distinguishes them — because a probe measures dispositions. This is Kripke's Wittgenstein applied to machines105,106, and it is the point at which the concepts question stops being a question about machines and becomes a question about what rule-following is.

4 Experience

4.1Does intelligence require consciousness?

This question is almost always answered before it is disambiguated, and the disambiguation does most of the work.

Block's distinction is between access consciousness — a representation being poised for use in reasoning, report and the rational control of action — and phenomenal consciousness, there being something it is like to be in the state107,12. The distinction is contested at the margins and indispensable in the middle, because the two come apart in the evidence. Blindsight patients discriminate stimuli they deny seeing108; implicit learning proceeds without report; expert performance becomes less accessible as it becomes more reliable.

Now the question splits. Does general intelligence require access consciousness? Plausibly yes, and the reason is architectural rather than mysterious: a system that must combine information from many specialised sources to act coherently needs something like a bottleneck that makes representations globally available, which is the core of global workspace theory109,110. This is a functional requirement, it is measurable, and it is a design target rather than an obstacle.

Does general intelligence require phenomenal consciousness? Nothing in the evidence suggests so, and nothing in the evidence could, because the evidence is all evidence about access. That is not a temporary limitation of method. It is what the distinction says.

There is a third reading of 'require' that gets less attention and may be the one people actually have in mind. Perhaps general intelligence does not require phenomenal consciousness as a matter of metaphysical necessity, but any physical system that achieves it will as a matter of fact have it, because the architecture that delivers the one delivers the other. This is a nomological claim rather than a conceptual one, and it is the claim most of the indicator literature is really making111. Its distinctive feature is that it cannot be established by finding a case — you would need to establish that no case of the first without the second is physically possible, and nothing in the current evidence bears on that.

Which leaves a question worth putting plainly, because it is rarely put at all. If a system had every functional property associated with consciousness and you were certain it had no phenomenal states, would anything about how you should build it, deploy it or treat it change? For the intelligence question the answer is no, which is why §4.1 can be settled without settling §4.2. For the moral status question in §4.4 the answer is emphatically yes, which is why that one cannot be set aside as an idle metaphysical extra.

The practical consequence is that almost every empirical result announced as bearing on machine consciousness bears on the left-hand box in the figure, and almost every philosophical worry is about the right-hand one. Conflating them makes the evidence look decisive when it is not, and makes the worry look unanswerable when part of it is not.

4.2Could an AGI be conscious?

Take the question to be about phenomenal consciousness, since that is where the interest is. The state of play is that the leading theories disagree about machines in a way that maps exactly onto their determination bases.

Global workspace theory, recurrent processing theory, higher-order theories and attention schema theory all specify a form109,112,113,114. Every one of them describes something that can be built in silicon, and Butlin, Long and colleagues did the serious work of deriving computational indicator properties from each and assessing current systems against them111,115. Their conclusion in the original report was that no current system satisfies them and that no obvious technical barrier prevents building one that does. Note the methodological honesty of the framing: indicators derived from theories are only as good as the theories, and behavioural evidence is explicitly discounted because it can be gamed.

Integrated information theory specifies a substrate: consciousness is identical to a maximally irreducible cause-effect structure, and a functionally equivalent feedforward implementation of a conscious system has, by the theory's own arithmetic, no experience at all8,116,117. Searle's biological naturalism does the same thing more bluntly7, as does Godfrey-Smith's metabolic proposal, though with an argument rather than a stipulation118.

The theories that make machine consciousness easy are the ones that specify a form; the theories that make it impossible are the ones that specify a substrate. This is not a coincidence, and it means the question is downstream of a prior one that no experiment on a machine touches.

The prior question is Chalmers': why any physical process should be accompanied by experience at all119,120. Every theory in the figure answers an easier question — which processes correlate with report, which architectures support integration — and each is a substantive scientific proposal about that. The hard question is not a harder version of the same problem; it is the question of why the answer to the easier one is not the whole story. A researcher may reasonably hold that it will dissolve, and Seth's predictive-processing programme is an attempt to make it dissolve by explaining away the intuitions that generate it121,122. But the dissolution is a philosophical result, not an experimental one, and it has not happened yet.

Two developments deserve recording. First, the unfolding argument: if a recurrent network and its feedforward unfolding are input-output equivalent, then no experiment discriminating conscious from unconscious processing by behaviour can support a theory that distinguishes them123; Kleiner and Hoel generalise the difficulty into a formal result about falsification in consciousness science124. Second, the Cogitate consortium ran the first preregistered adversarial test of integrated information theory against global neuronal workspace, with proponents agreeing the predictions in advance. Neither theory emerged with its core predictions intact125.

Chalmers' assessment of language models specifically is a model of how to hold this question: the current evidence is weak in both directions, the relevant architectural features are mostly absent but not in principle unattainable, and the appropriate response is a credence rather than a verdict126. Against which should be set the finding that ordinary people already attribute phenomenal states to these systems at considerable rates127, which is a fact about the social situation the question is being asked in.

4.3What behaviour cannot see

The fifth experiment gives the unfolding argument a body count.

Enumerate every four-unit system whose units update by a Boolean function of two others, deduplicating rules that compute the same function: 279,841 distinct dynamics. Designate two units as the interface, and define behaviour as what the interface does over three steps from each of its four initial conditions. The 279,841 dynamics fall into 4,176 behavioural classes.

Take the largest non-constant class that contains both feedforward and recurrent members: 8,796 dynamics, indistinguishable from the interface at every step, on every input. Of those, 329 are strictly feedforward — no unit depends, directly or transitively, on itself — and 8,467 contain feedback. Computing a partition-based effective-information measure over each, 1,337 have an integration of exactly zero and the highest reaches 1.1887.

The measure used is Tononi's effective information rather than integrated information theory's current formulation, and the point does not depend on that choice. Any theory that keys experience to recurrence, to integration, or to architecture cuts straight through a class that no behavioural test can cut. This is not an argument that such theories are false. It is a precise statement of what accepting one costs: you have committed to a fact about experience that varies while everything observable from outside stays fixed.

The result is a computational restatement of a thought experiment Block ran in 1978. Imagine the population of China organised, one person per neuron, to realise your functional organisation for an hour128. Functionalism says the arrangement has your experiences; the intuition of most readers says it has none. Block's argument was that if the intuition is right, functionalism is false; the functionalist reply was that the intuition is untrustworthy. Neither side produced an observation, and neither could, for the reason the experiment above makes exact: the two hypotheses agree on every behavioural fact by construction. What the experiment adds is that you do not need a hypothetical nation to make the point. Eight thousand seven hundred and ninety-six four-unit systems will do, and they can be enumerated in under a minute.

Someone who finds that cost acceptable is a realist about phenomenal consciousness in a strong sense, and should say so. Someone who finds it unacceptable is on the road to illusionism129,130, and should say that. What nobody should do is treat the choice as an empirical matter awaiting more data, because the experiment shows the data cannot arrive.

4.4Could an AGI possess moral status?

This is the question with practical stakes, and it is also the one where the determination framework yields the sharpest result.

The candidate grounds are familiar. Sentience, in the utilitarian tradition descending from Bentham's question about suffering131,132. Rational autonomy, in the Kantian one133, though Korsgaard's development of it extends consideration to animals rather than withholding it134. Having interests or a good of one's own. Being a member of a moral community, on relational accounts15,135. Species membership, which almost nobody defends explicitly and which does a great deal of implicit work136,137,138.

The ninth experiment turns the argument from marginal cases into a search. Twelve cases are declared as rows of nine facts — sentient, rational, potentially rational, has preferences, goal-directed, our species, socially embedded, capable of reciprocity, biological. Six are anchors that reflective practice includes without controversy: an adult human, an infant, a profoundly cognitively disabled human, a human in a coma, a dog, an octopus. Three are anchors it excludes: a thermostat, a calculator, an oak tree. Three are artificial: a language model, an agentic system without sentience indicators, and a system with them. A criterion is any predicate over the nine facts. It may not consult a case's name.

Of ten named criteria, exactly one is anchor-adequate — it includes all six and excludes all three. That criterion is having interests, and it admits two of the three artificial cases. Sentience fails on the comatose human. Rationality fails on five of the six. Species membership fails on the dog and the octopus. Biological life fails because the oak tree is alive.

Then the exhaustive search: 16,491 predicates, comprising every conjunction and disjunction of up to three literals over the nine facts and every two-term disjunctive normal form over conjunctions of up to two. 602 of them get every anchor right. 164 of those also exclude all three artificial cases. And every single one of the 164 buys the exclusion by naming species membership or biological substrate.

That is the argument from marginal cases as an exhaustive fact rather than a rhetorical move. It also shows why the debate has been so resistant to the obvious repair of admitting degrees. Kagan's hierarchical proposal — that moral status comes in degrees, so that an animal counts but counts for less — changes the shape of the verdicts without changing the search139. A graded criterion is still a function of the declared facts, and a graded exclusion of the machine still has to be graded on something. There is no line in the space that keeps the machine out on any other ground. A critic can of course dispute the fact table — can deny that an agentic system has preferences in the relevant sense, or that a comatose person retains interests — and that is exactly the right place to have the argument. What the critic cannot do is grant the rows and find a criterion.

Two consequences. The first is the one Sebo and Long draw: if there is a non-negligible chance that some systems are moral patients, and the standard grounds do not exclude them, then the reasonable response is precaution rather than confidence140,141,142,143. The second is Schwitzgebel's, and it is more uncomfortable: we may build systems whose moral status is genuinely uncertain, in which case both treating them as persons and treating them as tools risk serious wrong, and there is no safe option144. Metzinger's proposed moratorium on synthetic phenomenology is a response to precisely this145; Bryson's argument that we should build systems that clearly do not have status is a different response to the same problem146. Shulman and Bostrom's analysis of what it would take to share a world with digital minds is the systematic treatment147, and the New York declaration on animal consciousness shows the precautionary structure already operating in the nearest analogous case148,149.

4.5Who decides, and when

The questions in this part have a property the others do not: they are being answered in practice whether or not they are answered in theory. Systems are being deployed, described, and reasoned about, and every description carries a verdict.

Two facts about the practical situation deserve recording. The first is that ordinary users already attribute experience to conversational systems at substantial rates, and the attribution tracks conversational fluency rather than anything an indicator framework would recognise127. Whatever the correct answer, the social process that will produce the operative answer is not tracking the evidence.

The second is that the precautionary structure being proposed for AI moral status is not novel. It is the structure already applied to animals whose sentience is uncertain: a declaration that the evidence is sufficient to warrant serious consideration, without a claim that the question is settled148,142. That structure has a specific virtue. It separates the constitutive question, which stays open, from the practical one, which does not wait.

Which suggests a discipline for anyone writing about these systems. State the verdict you are relying on, state the base it quantifies over, and state what would change it. A claim that a system definitely lacks experience and a claim that it definitely has it are equally unsupported by the evidence, and the second is currently the more common of the two in public discourse while the first is the more common in technical discourse. Neither is a finding. Both are decisions being reported as findings, and the framework's practical value is that it makes the difference easy to see.

5 Motive

5.1Can intelligence exist without emotion?

The literature's answer is usually 'no', and the argument usually cited for it is Damasio's: patients with ventromedial prefrontal damage retain intelligence on every standard measure and become catastrophically bad at deciding150,151. The somatic marker hypothesis explains this by giving affect a role in narrowing the option space before deliberation begins.

The eighth experiment isolates what that role is worth.

A decision tree of branching six and depth six has 46,656 leaves. Give the agent a budget of 2,000 evaluations. Exhaustive search does not return an answer — not a worse answer, no answer. Now give the agent a cheap tag on each subtree: the value of one designated leaf inside it, obtainable in a single evaluation. Keep the top two children at each node and the agent visits 442 leaves, 0.9 per cent of exhaustive, and lands within 0.2 per cent of the best available. Keep only the top one and it visits 37 and lands within 6.8 per cent.

The frontier is steep in a way worth noting: the marginal value of widening the search collapses quickly, and at the widest setting the tagged search costs more than plain exhaustive search, because it pays for probes it does not use. This is Simon's satisficing152 and Gigerenzer's fast-and-frugal heuristics153 in a form where the numbers are exact.

So a cheap valuation signal is not a decoration on deliberation. Without one there is no deliberation inside the budget. Simon made the general architectural argument long before the neuroscience154, and Minsky's later account of emotion as a way of switching between ways of thinking is the same idea in a different vocabulary155.

And here is where the question splits, exactly as it did for consciousness. What the argument establishes is that a general intelligence needs a valuation channel and a mechanism for reallocating resources under changed circumstances. What it does not establish, and cannot, is that anything is felt. A system with the control signal and no phenomenal valence is precisely what the functional evidence leaves standing. Appraisal theories of emotion make the functional structure explicit and are correspondingly implementable156,157; affective computing has been implementing them for thirty years158; Barrett's constructionist account makes the felt quality itself a product of categorisation, which cuts the connection from the other direction159.

The failure mode is specific and common: the experimental case for emotion being necessary is a case about control signals, and it is then read as a case about feeling. The reading does not follow, and noticing that it does not is most of the progress available here.

5.2Does agency require desires?

The Humean thesis is that belief alone cannot move anything: action requires a state with a direction of fit that beliefs do not have160,161. Anti-Humeans hold that responsiveness to reasons can motivate without an antecedent want162,163. The dispute is old and, in its general form, immovable. But part of it is exactly countable.

Take four options and every complete strict preference relation over them: 64 of them. Twenty-four are transitive, and by the representation theorems those and only those admit a real-valued utility function164,165. The other forty contain a cycle — every one contains a three-cycle — and every one of the forty can therefore be walked around a loop back to where it started, poorer by three trades, without limit166.

Now the agent with no preferences at all. It cannot be money-pumped: there is no cycle to exploit. It also cannot choose. Of the fifteen non-empty subsets of four options, a preference relation with no content determines a choice on four — the singletons — and leaves eleven undetermined. Sen's work on choice functions is the canonical treatment of what structure has to be present for choice to be well defined at all167.

This does not settle the Humean question, and it is important to say why. What it settles is that something with the formal role of a preference ordering must be present for action to be determinate. It does not settle whether that something is a desire, a commitment, a standard held to, or a description we impose on a system that has no such state13. Any pattern of norm-guided action can be redescribed as maximising some utility function, and any utility function can be redescribed as a standard the agent holds itself to. The redescriptions are behaviourally equivalent, which is precisely the mark of a dispute that no behaviour will resolve.

Two contemporary threads run through here. The reward hypothesis — that intelligence and its associated abilities can be understood as subserving the maximisation of reward168 — is a strong Humean thesis in engineering dress, and the instrumental-convergence arguments about advanced systems presuppose it169,170,171. Against which: work claiming to measure emergent value systems in language models172 bears on whether such systems have coherent preferences, which is an empirical question, but not on whether having them is what makes them agents, which is not. Barandiaran and Di Paolo's conditions on agency — individuality, normativity, asymmetry — are the main attempt to say what more is needed173, and Frankfurt's hierarchy of desires the classic account of what distinguishes a person's wants from a mere system's174.

5.3Can artificial systems have intentions?

Brentano's mark of the mental is intentionality: mental states are about things175. The question for machines is whether their states are about anything in their own right or only in the way a word on a page is about something — because someone reads it that way.

Searle's answer is that machine intentionality is always derived, never original67. Dennett's is that the distinction is less principled than it looks: intentional ascriptions are licensed by the predictive success of the intentional stance, and there is no further fact about whether the states 'really' have content13. Both positions load N — they make the ascription turn on an interpreter — and differ about whether that is a deficiency.

The teleosemantic answer is different in kind, and it is the one that gives the question empirical purchase. A frog's bug detector represents bugs, not because anyone reads it that way, but because it was selected for tracking bugs; the function is a historical fact about the mechanism, not an interpretive convenience11,81,82. If that is what original intentionality consists in, then the question about machines becomes: did any selection process give this state the function of carrying information about that thing?

Which is a real question with a real answer. Coelho Mollo and Millière argue that preference fine-tuning establishes world-involving functions explicitly, and that pre-training alone may in limited domains select for internal states with world-involving content — with the surprising corollary that multimodality and embodiment are neither necessary nor sufficient to solve the grounding problem76. Whether they are right is a matter for interpretability and for careful analysis of what training objectives actually select for. It is not a matter for intuition about whether machines are the sort of thing that can mean anything.

Note what this makes irrelevant. Not the size of the model, not the fluency of the output, not whether anyone is impressed. Anscombe's and Bratman's analyses of intention as a distinctive practical attitude — something that structures planning and commits the agent over time, rather than merely representing176,177 — add a further requirement that current systems variously meet and fail, and that is testable. Haugeland's demand that a system must have something at stake for its states to have genuine significance is the strongest version of the sceptical case, and it is a demand about the system's relation to its own commitments rather than about its architecture178.

6 Subject

6.1What constitutes a self?

Locke started this by separating personhood from substance: what makes a later person the same as an earlier one is continuity of consciousness, not persistence of matter179. Parfit's development is the one that matters here, because he showed that the criterion breaks in a specific way and drew the radical conclusion that identity is not what matters180.

Machines make the break cheap. Copying a mind is a thought experiment for humans and a routine operation for a model. The tenth experiment runs one declared history — forty steps of gradual component replacement, a memory wipe at step 25, a copy at step 20, one branch halted at step 30 and restored from a step-29 backup at step 35 — and evaluates seven persistence criteria as pure predicates over the record.

The criteria diverge immediately. Material overlap with the original fails at step 11, on gradual replacement alone, before anything dramatic happens. Connectedness of memory to the original fails at step 25 while continuity of memory — the overlapping-chain criterion — never fails, which is Parfit's distinction between connectedness and continuity appearing on its own180. Unbroken process and self-model divide the two branches in opposite directions. Seven criteria produce five distinct verdict profiles over one history.

The important result is at the fission point. Four of the seven criteria — memory connectedness, memory continuity, unbroken process, self-model — pronounce both post-copy branches identical to the original. Identity is transitive, so that makes the branches identical to each other, which they are not: they diverge, and one is destroyed. The criteria that avoid the contradiction avoid it by tracking a lump of matter or a registration rather than a mind181,182.

This is not an argument that machines have no selves. It is an argument that the concept of numerical identity, applied to systems that can be copied, does not have determinate application — which is Parfit's conclusion arrived at by enumeration. What survives is the family of things identity was supposed to underwrite: continuity of memory, of character, of commitment, in degrees. Those are measurable and they matter.

Two further positions bear on machines directly. Metzinger's self-model theory holds that no self exists; what exists is a self-model whose representational character is transparent to the system running it, so that the system cannot recognise the model as a model183. On that view the question 'does the system have a self?' becomes 'does it run a transparent self-model?', which is a question about architecture and is answerable. Dennett's centre of narrative gravity is the same move in a different register: the self is an abstraction posited to make a stream of behaviour intelligible, real in the way a centre of gravity is real184,185. Dennett's fuller treatment develops the same thought into a general account of what a mind is a model of186. Gallagher's minimal/narrative distinction is the useful taxonomy187, and Strawson's insistence that the minimal self is momentary rather than extended is the sharpest dissent188,189.

6.2What does it mean for a machine to know something?

Start with the standard analysis and its standard failure. Justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge; Gettier's two counterexamples showed a belief can be true, and justified, and true for reasons unrelated to the justification190. The repairs divide into causal191, reliabilist192, modal193,194, virtue-theoretic195, and the knowledge-first move of denying the analysis is possible at all196. Zagzebski's argument that Gettier cases are inescapable for any analysis with the standard structure is the reason this looks less like an unsolved problem than a structural feature197.

The applied form of the question is unusually pressing, because we now measure machine epistemic performance constantly and the measurements are all of the same kind. The sixth experiment shows what that kind cannot see.

Two worlds, each of 200 sites in 20 regions. In each, some sites hold real barns, some hold facades that present a barn face, some hold neither. The agent's belief-forming process is the same in both: if it looks like a barn, believe there is a barn. In the uniform world the facades are spread evenly, three to a region. In the clustered world the same sixty facades are concentrated: half the regions are fake-barn county, half are honest.

The two worlds are indistinguishable by every aggregate statistic. Same number of beliefs formed: 180. Same number true: 120. Same accuracy: 0.6667. An agent that reports its own historical hit rate as its confidence is perfectly calibrated in both, so the calibration curves are identical too198.

They differ in the modal structure of the true beliefs. In the uniform world every belief has local safety two-thirds; in the clustered world thirty of the 120 true beliefs are formed in circumstances where the same process would deliver a falsehood in two cases out of three. Those thirty beliefs are true by luck, in Goldman's original fake-barn sense199,200.

And then the sting. Sensitivity — Nozick's condition, that the belief would not be held if it were false193 — ranks the two worlds in the opposite order: 120 true beliefs fail it in the uniform world against 30 in the clustered one. Two criteria that both refine reliabilism, both motivated by the same cases, disagree about which world contains more knowledge.

The consequences for how machine epistemic standing is currently reported are direct. Accuracy on a benchmark is a measure of the truth ratio of a process in the conditions the benchmark samples. It says nothing about whether the truths would survive perturbation, and nothing about the conditions the process will meet in deployment. Calibration is better and is still an aggregate over the same sample. Kalai and colleagues' analysis of why language models hallucinate is relevant here in an unexpected way: their claim is that the evaluation regime rewards guessing over acknowledging uncertainty, which is to say that the metric selects for a truth ratio and is indifferent to whether the truths are safe201,202.

There is also a position that rejects the whole project. Williamson's knowledge-first programme holds that knowledge is a mental state in its own right, not a compound of belief plus extras, and that the analytic project has the order of explanation backwards196. If that is correct, the question 'what would make it true that this system knows?' does not decompose into conditions on belief, justification and luck at all, and the experiment above is a demonstration about the failure of the decomposition rather than a contribution to it. That is a fair reading of it, and it does not change what the experiment shows about the metrics: whatever knowledge is, accuracy on a sample is not a measure of it.

There remains the position that knowledge is not an internal state at all but a standing in a practice — an entitlement to assert, with the answerability that goes with it. On that view a system that cannot be held to its assertions cannot know anything, whatever its internal states14. This is a claim about accountability rather than representation, and it is worth separating from the others precisely because it will otherwise be smuggled in as though it were one of them.

This is not an accident: they are the two bases that make contact with facts about a system that can be investigated from outside, one by looking at the mechanism and one by looking at where the mechanism came from.

Second, the plates that load P are almost exactly the plates whose separation field says 'nothing'. That is the clearest structural fact in the ledger, and it is why the phenomenality questions behave differently from the rest.

Third, the plates that load N are not therefore idle. A position that makes an ascription turn on a community's entitlements is not thereby untestable; it is testable against the practice, and it is revisable by changing the practice. That is a different mode of settlement, not the absence of one.

The ledger also supports a use it was not designed for, which is diagnostic. Take any claim about a machine mind you encounter in the wild, find the plate it belongs to, and read off the tell. In practice the tells cluster: an argument that shifts between two plates in the same band without noticing, an argument that answers a plate in one band with evidence appropriate to another, and an argument whose separation field says 'nothing' being conducted as though new results would settle it. Three failure modes account for most of what is said publicly about these systems, and all three become visible the moment the determination base is named.

7.2Which band each question falls in

The article's substantive claim can now be stated in the form that makes it refutable.

Four of the fourteen questions are empirical in the ordinary sense: an observation would settle them and we can say which. Whether general intelligence requires a body, on the causal reading, is being tested by the transfer of text-and-image-trained systems to control. Whether it can exist without an affective control signal is answered in the negative by any budgeted search experiment. Whether determinate action requires something with the formal role of a preference ordering is answered in the affirmative by enumeration. And what intelligence is becomes empirical the moment a measure is fixed — the difficulty there is that fixing the measure is itself a choice, not a discovery.

Five are mixed. Understanding, concepts, intentions, knowledge and selfhood all have a substantial empirical core — counterfactual competence, systematic generalisation, selection history, modal robustness, continuity of memory — surrounded by a residue that is settled by what we take the word to mean. The right response is not to lament the residue but to do the empirical core properly and be explicit about where it stops.

Five are constitutive. Substrate independence, syntax and semantics, whether intelligence requires consciousness, whether an AGI could be conscious, and whether it could have moral status. For each of these the article has given a specific reason why no observation separates the answers, and in three cases the reason is a computation: implementation grades that quantify over runs that did not happen, an integration measure that varies across a behaviourally uniform class, a criterion space in which every separating predicate names the substrate.

Calling those five constitutive is not calling them meaningless203,204. It is saying that progress on them comes from deciding well under uncertainty, with attention to what turns on the decision — which, for moral status at least, is a great deal. It is also saying that anyone who claims an experiment has settled one of the five has changed the subject, and that the change of subject is usually from the constitutive question to a neighbouring empirical one that shares its vocabulary.

7.3Against this article

Four objections deserve answers, and one of them may be right.

That the framework smuggles in a verificationist premise. If a question is called constitutive because no observation separates the answers, and constitutive questions are then treated differently from empirical ones, has the article not assumed that meaning is settled by verification? No: the constitutive questions are treated as real questions with real answers throughout, and the recommendation for them is precaution and explicit decision, not silence. But the objection has force against a stronger version of the thesis than the one defended here, and anyone who wants that stronger version does need to answer it.

That the five bases are not exhaustive or not exclusive. Correct on the second count, deliberately: positions load several. On the first, I know of no candidate ground for these ascriptions that is not one of the five or a combination, but the framework would be improved by a sixth if one exists, and falsified by a position that fits none.

That the experiments prove less than they are made to carry. This is the objection I take most seriously, and it should be stated precisely. Every experiment here is a result about a small, exhaustively enumerated formal system. The inference from 'behaviour underdetermines organisation among 5,832 three-state machines' to 'behaviour underdetermines organisation in general' is an inference, and the experiment does not license it by itself. What the experiments establish is that the underdetermination is not an artefact of complexity or ignorance — it is present in the simplest case where every fact is available. That is worth something, and it is less than a theorem about the general case.

That the fact tables do the work. Most sharply for the moral status experiment: the search finds no criterion excluding the machine, but the search operates over declared facts, and the declaration that an agentic system has preferences is doing enormous work. This is right, and it is the intended result rather than a defect. The point of the experiment is to relocate the argument from the conclusion to the premises. Anyone who wants the machine excluded now knows exactly what they must deny, and must deny it in a way that does not also exclude the infant, the comatose patient, or the octopus.

What would refute the article's central claim: a demonstration that one of the five constitutive questions is separated by an observation — an experiment whose outcome the rival answers do not both predict. Not a rhetorical advantage, not an intuition pump, not a capability result that makes one answer feel less plausible. An observation. If someone produces one, the question moves band and the framework has done its job by making the movement visible.

7.4Where the ladder stops

The argument of this article can be put in four sentences.

Every question about whether a machine understands, feels, wants, knows or matters is a question about what would make the ascription true. There are five candidate answers, and a position is characterised by which of them it loads. Where rival positions load different bases, no observation separates them, and no capability result ever will. Where they load the same base, the disagreement is usually tractable and is usually being worked on by people who do not describe themselves as philosophers.

What follows is not quietism. Four of the fourteen questions are open empirical problems and the article has said which experiments bear on them. Five more have empirical cores that are being actively advanced. The remaining five require decision rather than discovery, and one of those five — whether these systems can be wronged — will be decided by default if it is not decided deliberately, because deployment does not wait for adjudication.

The honest position on that last group is not confidence in either direction. It is a credence, held explicitly, with the base it rests on named, and revised when the base is challenged rather than when a new benchmark is announced. That is an unglamorous recommendation. It is also, as far as I can tell, the only one the structure of these disputes actually supports.

The ladder was numbered so that it could be climbed. What it reaches is not an answer to any of the fourteen questions. It is a clear view of which of them have answers of the kind we have been looking for, and which have been asking us for something else all along.

"""determination_lab, part 1: organisation, implementation, reference.

Stdlib only. Fully deterministic: no RNG seeds, no sampling, no wall-clock.
Every quantity is an exhaustive count over a finitely specified space.
"""
from itertools import product, permutations, combinations

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# E1  Multiple realisation: how much does behaviour fix organisation?
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Space: Moore machines with N states, input alphabet {0,1}, output alphabet
# {0,1}, start state 0.  delta: N x 2 -> N, lambda: N -> {0,1}.
# Behaviour: the output emitted after reading each input string of length <= L.

def e1(N=3, L=5):
    states = range(N)
    deltas = list(product(states, repeat=2 * N))      # N^(2N)
    lams = list(product((0, 1), repeat=N))            # 2^N

    # all input strings of length 0..L, in a fixed order
    words = [()]
    frontier = [()]
    for _ in range(L):
        nxt = []
        for w in frontier:
            for a in (0, 1):
                nxt.append(w + (a,))
        words.extend(nxt)
        frontier = nxt

    # For a given delta, the state reached by each word is fixed.  Precompute.
    def reached(delta):
        out = []
        for w in words:
            q = 0
            for a in w:
                q = delta[2 * q + a]
            out.append(q)
        return tuple(out)

    classes = {}
    reach_cache = {}
    for d in deltas:
        r = reached(d)
        reach_cache[d] = r
        for lam in lams:
            sig = tuple(lam[q] for q in r)
            classes.setdefault(sig, []).append((d, lam))

    total = len(deltas) * len(lams)
    sizes = sorted((len(v) for v in classes.values()), reverse=True)
    singletons = sum(1 for s in sizes if s == 1)

    # minimum number of states needed to realise each behaviour, by re-running
    # the enumeration for smaller N and recording which signatures appear
    def sigs_for(n):
        st = range(n)
        got = set()
        for d in product(st, repeat=2 * n):
            r = []
            for w in words:
                q = 0
                for a in w:
                    q = d[2 * q + a]
                r.append(q)
            for lam in product((0, 1), repeat=n):
                got.add(tuple(lam[q] for q in r))
        return got

    s1 = sigs_for(1)
    s2 = sigs_for(2)
    need = {1: len(s1), 2: len(s2 - s1), 3: len(set(classes) - s2)}

    # Non-isomorphic realisations of the single most-realised behaviour.
    # Isomorphism = permutation of states fixing the start state 0.
    perms = [p for p in permutations(range(N)) if p[0] == 0]
    big_sig = max(classes, key=lambda k: len(classes[k]))
    members = set(classes[big_sig])
    orbits = set()
    seen = set()
    for m in classes[big_sig]:
        if m in seen:
            continue
        d, lam = m
        orb = set()
        for p in perms:
            nd = [0] * (2 * N)
            for q in range(N):
                for a in (0, 1):
                    nd[2 * p[q] + a] = p[d[2 * q + a]]
            nl = [0] * N
            for q in range(N):
                nl[p[q]] = lam[q]
            orb.add((tuple(nd), tuple(nl)))
        seen |= orb
        orbits.add(min(orb))

    # how many behaviours are realised by a machine that is NOT minimal, i.e.
    # the same behaviour is available with fewer states
    redundant = len(set(classes) & s2)

    return {
        "N": N, "L": L, "words": len(words),
        "machines": total,
        "behaviours": len(classes),
        "largest_class": sizes[0],
        "median_class": sizes[len(sizes) // 2],
        "singletons": singletons,
        "need1": need[1], "need2": need[2], "need3": need[3],
        "big_class_members": len(members),
        "big_class_orbits": len(orbits),
        "reachable_with_fewer": redundant,
    }


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# E2  What does it take for a physical system to implement a computation?
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# A "physical" device P: 12 states, two inputs, one observable binary output.
# Target computations: every 3-state Moore machine over the same alphabets.
# Four grades of the implementation relation, from Putnam's to Chalmers'.

def _phys():
    """A 12-state device built as an explicit product: a three-state core whose
    state the output reads off, times a four-tick counter the output ignores.
    The device therefore does have a determinate computational profile -- the
    question the grades ask is which grade of the implementation relation is
    capable of noticing it."""
    core_d = {(0, 0): 1, (0, 1): 2, (1, 0): 2, (1, 1): 0, (2, 0): 0, (2, 1): 1}
    core_l = {0: 0, 1: 1, 2: 1}
    delta, lam, idx = {}, {}, {}
    for m in range(3):
        for c in range(4):
            idx[(m, c)] = m * 4 + c
    for (m, c), p in idx.items():
        for a in (0, 1):
            delta[(p, a)] = idx[(core_d[(m, a)], (c + 1) % 4)]
        lam[p] = core_l[m]
    return delta, lam


def e2(T=12, N=3, MAXRUN=12):
    Pd, Pl = _phys()
    full_inputs = tuple(1 if (t * t + t) % 3 else 0 for t in range(MAXRUN))

    machines = []
    for d in product(range(N), repeat=2 * N):
        for lam in product((0, 1), repeat=N):
            machines.append((d, lam))
    total = len(machines)

    # GRADE 4 -- Chalmers' combinatorial state automaton.  The mapping from
    # physical state TYPES must mirror the transitions for EVERY input, not
    # only the one that happened.  A homomorphism check; run-independent.
    def grade4_ok(m):
        d, lam = m
        phi = {0: 0}
        stack = [0]
        while stack:
            p = stack.pop()
            if lam[phi[p]] != Pl[p]:
                return False
            for a in (0, 1):
                p2 = Pd[(p, a)]
                q2 = d[2 * phi[p] + a]
                if p2 in phi:
                    if phi[p2] != q2:
                        return False
                else:
                    phi[p2] = q2
                    stack.append(p2)
        return True
    grade4 = sum(1 for m in machines if grade4_ok(m))

    rows = []
    for R in range(0, MAXRUN + 1):
        inputs = full_inputs[:R]
        traj = [0]
        for a in inputs:
            traj.append(Pd[(traj[-1], a)])
        record = [Pl[p] for p in traj]
        visited = len(set(traj))

        # GRADE 2 -- outputs fixed in advance, physical states taken
        # time-indexed (a maximal state description includes the clock), so
        # the state mapping is free.  Only the output record must match.
        g2 = 0
        # GRADE 3 -- the mapping is from physical state TYPES, but only the
        # actual run has to be mirrored.
        g3 = 0
        for (d, lam) in machines:
            q, o, ok3 = 0, [lam[0]], True
            phi = {}
            for t, p in enumerate(traj):
                if ok3:
                    if phi.get(p, q) != q or lam[q] != Pl[p]:
                        ok3 = False
                    else:
                        phi[p] = q
                if t < R:
                    q = d[2 * q + inputs[t]]
                    o.append(lam[q])
            if o == record:
                g2 += 1
            if ok3:
                g3 += 1
        rows.append((R, visited, g2, g3))

    return {
        "T": T, "N": N, "maxrun": MAXRUN,
        "machines": total,
        "grade1": total,
        "grade4": grade4,
        "rows": rows,
        "g2_full": rows[-1][2], "g3_full": rows[-1][3],
        "counterfactual_runs": 2 ** MAXRUN,
    }


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# E3  Newman's problem: how many world-facts does it take to fix reference?
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Domain D of n objects; the world contains one real relation R*.  A purely
# structural theory of the world -- however complete -- is satisfied by every
# relation isomorphic to R*.  Anchoring means fixing, non-structurally, that
# some particular pair does or does not stand in R.

def _world(n=7):
    """A fixed digraph on n labelled nodes, given by an explicit rule."""
    R = set()
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            if i != j and (i * i + 3 * j) % 7 < 2:
                R.add((i, j))
    return frozenset(R)


def e3(n=7):
    Rstar = _world(n)
    dom = list(range(n))
    perms = list(permutations(dom))

    def apply(p, R):
        return frozenset((p[i], p[j]) for (i, j) in R)

    aut = [p for p in perms if apply(p, Rstar) == Rstar]
    copies = {apply(p, Rstar) for p in perms}

    # "Just more theory": any further purely structural axiom is already
    # entailed by the complete structural description, so the admissible set
    # is unchanged.  Verified by testing three independent structural axioms.
    def deg_profile(R):
        outd = sorted(sum(1 for (i, j) in R if i == k) for k in dom)
        ind = sorted(sum(1 for (i, j) in R if j == k) for k in dom)
        return (outd, ind)

    def tri_count(R):
        return sum(1 for a in dom for b in dom for c in dom
                   if (a, b) in R and (b, c) in R and (c, a) in R)

    def recip_count(R):
        return sum(1 for (i, j) in R if (j, i) in R)

    axioms = [deg_profile, tri_count, recip_count]
    after_axioms = {R for R in copies
                    if all(f(R) == f(Rstar) for f in axioms)}

    # Anchoring.  Each anchor is one de re fact about a named pair: either
    # (a,b) is in R, or it is not.  Anchors are taken in a fixed order.
    pairs = [(i, j) for i in dom for j in dom if i != j]
    ladder = []
    live = set(copies)
    used = 0
    for (a, b) in pairs:
        if len(live) == 1:
            break
        fact = (a, b) in Rstar
        live = {R for R in live if ((a, b) in R) == fact}
        used += 1
        ladder.append((used, (a, b), fact, len(live)))

    return {
        "n": n,
        "domain_relations": 2 ** (n * (n - 1)),
        "aut": len(aut),
        "structural_copies": len(copies),
        "after_three_axioms": len(after_axioms),
        "anchors_to_unique": used,
        "ladder": ladder,
        "arcs": len(Rstar),
    }

"""determination_lab, part 2: grounding, architecture, knowledge."""
from itertools import product, combinations
from math import log2

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# E4  The grounding floor of a closed dictionary
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Every word is defined using other words in the same vocabulary.  A word
# becomes available once every word in its definition is available.  A
# GROUNDING SET is a set of words made available from outside the dictionary;
# it is adequate if its closure is the whole vocabulary.

def _dictionary(N, d):
    """Deterministic definitional digraph: definition of word i uses d words."""
    defs = {}
    for i in range(N):
        used, k = [], 1
        while len(used) < d:
            j = (i * 37 + k * 101 + (i * k) % 13) % N
            if j != i and j not in used:
                used.append(j)
            k += 1
        defs[i] = tuple(sorted(used))
    return defs


def _closure(defs, S, used_in=None, need0=None):
    """Counter-based propagation: a word becomes available when its last
    unmet definer becomes available."""
    N = len(defs)
    if used_in is None:
        used_in = {i: [] for i in range(N)}
        for w, ds in defs.items():
            for x in ds:
                used_in[x].append(w)
        need0 = {w: len(set(ds)) for w, ds in defs.items()}
    need = dict(need0)
    known = set()
    stack = []
    for w in S:
        if w not in known:
            known.add(w)
            stack.append(w)
    while stack:
        x = stack.pop()
        for w in used_in[x]:
            if w in known:
                continue
            need[w] -= 1
            if need[w] == 0:
                known.add(w)
                stack.append(w)
    return known


def _index(defs):
    N = len(defs)
    used_in = {i: [] for i in range(N)}
    for w, ds in defs.items():
        for x in ds:
            used_in[x].append(w)
    return used_in, {w: len(set(ds)) for w, ds in defs.items()}


def _sccs(defs, N):
    """Tarjan on the graph definiens -> definiendum."""
    adj = {i: [] for i in range(N)}
    for w, ds in defs.items():
        for x in ds:
            adj[x].append(w)
    index, low, onstk, stk, out = {}, {}, set(), [], []
    counter = [0]
    for root in range(N):
        if root in index:
            continue
        work = [(root, 0)]
        while work:
            v, pi = work[-1]
            if pi == 0:
                index[v] = low[v] = counter[0]
                counter[0] += 1
                stk.append(v)
                onstk.add(v)
            recurse = False
            for i in range(pi, len(adj[v])):
                w = adj[v][i]
                if w not in index:
                    work[-1] = (v, i + 1)
                    work.append((w, 0))
                    recurse = True
                    break
                elif w in onstk:
                    low[v] = min(low[v], index[w])
            if recurse:
                continue
            if low[v] == index[v]:
                comp = []
                while True:
                    w = stk.pop()
                    onstk.discard(w)
                    comp.append(w)
                    if w == v:
                        break
                out.append(comp)
            work.pop()
            if work:
                u = work[-1][0]
                low[u] = min(low[u], low[v])
    return out


def _source_scc_bound(defs, N):
    """Lower bound: every SCC with no incoming edge from another SCC must
    contain at least one externally grounded word."""
    comp_of = {}
    comps = _sccs(defs, N)
    for ci, comp in enumerate(comps):
        for v in comp:
            comp_of[v] = ci
    incoming = [False] * len(comps)
    for w, ds in defs.items():
        for x in ds:
            if comp_of[x] != comp_of[w]:
                incoming[comp_of[w]] = True
    return sum(1 for ci in range(len(comps)) if not incoming[ci]), len(comps)


def _greedy(defs, N):
    ui, n0 = _index(defs)
    S = set()
    known = _closure(defs, S, ui, n0)
    while len(known) < N:
        best, gain = None, -1
        for w in range(N):
            if w in known:
                continue
            g = len(_closure(defs, known | {w}, ui, n0)) - len(known)
            if g > gain:
                best, gain = w, g
        S.add(best)
        known = _closure(defs, S, ui, n0)
    return S


def _exact(defs, N, cap):
    for k in range(1, cap + 1):
        for S in combinations(range(N), k):
            if len(_closure(defs, S)) == N:
                return set(S)
    return None


def e4():

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# E5  Same behaviour, different architecture, different integration
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# A system is four binary units.  Each unit's next value is op(u_a, u_b) for
# an op in {AND, OR, XOR} and an unordered pair of units (self-pairs allowed).
# 30 rules per unit, 30^4 = 810,000 systems, enumerated exhaustively.
# BEHAVIOUR: units 0 and 1 are the interface.  Set them to each of four values
# with the internal units cleared, run three steps, record the interface each
# step.  Systems with the same record are behaviourally indistinguishable to
# any observer confined to the interface.

NU = 4
PAIRS = [(a, b) for a in range(NU) for b in range(a, NU)]
OPS = ("AND", "OR", "XOR")
_ALL_RULES = [(op, a, b) for op in OPS for (a, b) in PAIRS]


def _rule_table(rule):
    op, a, b = rule
    t = []
    for s in range(1 << NU):
        x = (s >> a) & 1
        y = (s >> b) & 1
        t.append((x & y) if op == "AND" else (x | y) if op == "OR" else (x ^ y))
    return tuple(t)


# Distinct unit rules: AND(a,a) and OR(a,a) both copy a, and XOR(a,a) is the
# constant 0 whatever a is, so the 30 syntactic rules collapse to fewer
# distinct functions.  Deduplicate so that every enumerated system is a
# distinct dynamics.
_seen, RULES, RTAB = {}, [], []
for r in _ALL_RULES:
    t = _rule_table(r)
    if t not in _seen:
        _seen[t] = len(RULES)
        RULES.append(r)
        RTAB.append(t)
NR = len(RULES)


def _behaviour(sysrules):
    t0, t1, t2, t3 = (RTAB[sysrules[0]], RTAB[sysrules[1]],
                      RTAB[sysrules[2]], RTAB[sysrules[3]])
    sig = []
    for iface in range(4):
        s = iface
        for _ in range(3):
            s = t0[s] | (t1[s] << 1) | (t2[s] << 2) | (t3[s] << 3)
            sig.append(s & 3)
    return tuple(sig)


def _transition(sysrules):
    t = [RTAB[r] for r in sysrules]
    return [sum(t[u][s] << u for u in range(NU)) for s in range(1 << NU)]


def _ei(trans, subset):
    """Effective information of a subsystem: mutual information between a
    uniformly perturbed current state of the subset and its next state, with
    the complement injected independently and uniformly.  This is Tononi's
    effective information, not IIT's current phi; the argument below turns on
    behaviour-invariance, not on the choice of measure."""
    comp = [u for u in range(NU) if u not in subset]
    k = len(subset)
    if k == 0:
        return 0.0
    nsub, ncomp = 1 << k, 1 << len(comp)
    joint = {}
    for si in range(nsub):
        base = 0
        for bit, u in enumerate(subset):
            if (si >> bit) & 1:
                base |= 1 << u
        for ci in range(ncomp):
            s = base
            for bit, u in enumerate(comp):
                if (ci >> bit) & 1:
                    s |= 1 << u
            nxt = trans[s]
            o = 0
            for bit, u in enumerate(subset):
                if (nxt >> u) & 1:
                    o |= 1 << bit
            joint[(si, o)] = joint.get((si, o), 0) + 1
    tot = nsub * ncomp
    px = 1.0 / nsub
    py = {}
    for (si, o), c in joint.items():
        py[o] = py.get(o, 0) + c
    mi = 0.0
    for (si, o), c in joint.items():
        pxy = c / tot
        mi += pxy * log2(pxy / (px * (py[o] / tot)))
    return mi


_BIPART = []
for _k in range(1, NU // 2 + 1):
    for _A in combinations(range(NU), _k):
        _B = tuple(u for u in range(NU) if u not in _A)
        if _k == NU - _k and _A[0] != 0:
            continue
        _BIPART.append((_A, _B, min(_k, NU - _k)))


def _phi(trans):
    whole = _ei(trans, tuple(range(NU)))
    best = None
    for A, B, norm in _BIPART:
        v = (whole - (_ei(trans, A) + _ei(trans, B))) / norm
        if best is None or v < best[0]:
            best = (v, A)
    return whole, max(best[0], 0.0), best[1]


def _feedforward(sysrules):
    """True if the dependency digraph has no cycle at all -- no unit depends,
    directly or transitively, on itself."""
    dep = {}
    for u in range(NU):
        op, a, b = RULES[sysrules[u]]
        dep[u] = {a, b} if op != "XOR" or a != b else set()
    reach = {u: set(dep[u]) for u in range(NU)}
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        for u in range(NU):
            new = set(reach[u])
            for v in list(reach[u]):
                new |= reach[v]
            if new != reach[u]:
                reach[u], changed = new, True
    return not any(u in reach[u] for u in range(NU))

# E6  Accuracy, calibration, and safety come apart
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# A world is 200 sites in 20 regions of 10.  Each site holds a real barn, a
# facade, or neither.  The agent's process: if a site presents a barn face,
# believe there is a barn here.  Two worlds are built with the SAME number of
# real barns and facades -- hence the same global accuracy and the same
# calibration curve -- but different spatial distributions.

REGIONS, PER = 20, 10


def _uniform_world():
    """Every region alike: three facades, six barns, one open field."""
    sites = []
    for r in range(REGIONS):
        for i in range(PER):
            sites.append("FACADE" if i < 3 else "BARN" if i < 9 else "FIELD")
    return sites


def _clustered_world():
    """The same 60 facades and 120 barns, but half the regions are fake-barn
    county (six facades, three barns) and half are honest (nine barns)."""
    sites = []
    for r in range(REGIONS):
        for i in range(PER):
            if r % 2 == 0:
                sites.append("FACADE" if i < 6 else "BARN" if i < 9 else "FIELD")
            else:
                sites.append("BARN" if i < 9 else "FIELD")
    return sites


def _stats(sites):
    believes = [i for i, s in enumerate(sites) if s in ("BARN", "FACADE")]
    true_ = [i for i in believes if sites[i] == "BARN"]
    acc = len(true_) / len(believes)

    def safety(i):
        r = i // PER
        nb = [j for j in range(r * PER, (r + 1) * PER)
              if sites[j] in ("BARN", "FACADE")]
        return sum(1 for j in nb if sites[j] == "BARN") / len(nb)

    safeties = {i: safety(i) for i in believes}
    unsafe_true = [i for i in true_ if safeties[i] < 0.5]
    per_region = []
    for r in range(REGIONS):
        nb = [j for j in range(r * PER, (r + 1) * PER)
              if sites[j] in ("BARN", "FACADE")]
        if nb:
            per_region.append(sum(1 for j in nb if sites[j] == "BARN") / len(nb))
    return {
        "sites": len(sites),
        "beliefs": len(believes),
        "true": len(true_),
        "accuracy": acc,
        "unsafe_true": len(unsafe_true),
        "unsafe_true_pct": 100.0 * len(unsafe_true) / len(true_),
        "safety_min": min(safeties.values()),
        "safety_max": max(safeties.values()),
        "region_min": min(per_region),
        "region_max": max(per_region),
        "mean_safety_of_true": sum(safeties[i] for i in true_) / len(true_),
    }


def e6():
    U, C = _stats(_uniform_world()), _stats(_clustered_world())

    # Sensitivity: in the nearest world where there is no barn at this site,
    # does the agent still believe there is?  For a site whose facade-twin sits
    # in the same region, yes.  Count the beliefs that fail sensitivity.
    def insensitive(sites):
        n = 0
        for i, s in enumerate(sites):
            if s != "BARN":
                continue
            r = i // PER
            if any(sites[j] == "FACADE"
                   for j in range(r * PER, (r + 1) * PER)):
                n += 1
        return n

    iu, ic = insensitive(_uniform_world()), insensitive(_clustered_world())

    # Calibration: the agent reports its own historical hit rate as its
    # confidence.  Both worlds produce the same number, so the calibration
    # curve is identical and perfectly calibrated in each.
    return {
        "uniform": U, "clustered": C,
        "same_accuracy": abs(U["accuracy"] - C["accuracy"]) < 1e-12,
        "insensitive_uniform": iu, "insensitive_clustered": ic,
        "conf": U["accuracy"],
    }

# E7  What a preference relation buys, and what its absence costs
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Options are n alternatives.  A tournament is a complete asymmetric relation
# on them -- an agent that always has a strict view about any two options.
# Enumerate all of them and ask which support an action at all.

def _tournaments(n):
    pairs = list(combinations(range(n), 2))
    for bits in product((0, 1), repeat=len(pairs)):
        rel = set()
        for (i, j), b in zip(pairs, bits):
            rel.add((i, j) if b else (j, i))
        yield frozenset(rel)


def _transitive(rel, n):
    for a in range(n):
        for b in range(n):
            for c in range(n):
                if (a, b) in rel and (b, c) in rel and (a, c) not in rel:
                    return False
    return True


def _cycles(rel, n):
    """Shortest directed cycle length, or 0 if acyclic."""
    for L in range(3, n + 1):
        for combo in combinations(range(n), L):
            for perm in permutations(combo):
                if perm[0] != min(perm):
                    continue
                if all((perm[i], perm[(i + 1) % L]) in rel for i in range(L)):
                    return L
    return 0


def _condorcet(rel, n):
    for a in range(n):
        if all((a, b) in rel for b in range(n) if b != a):
            return True
    return False


def _choice_defined(rel, n):
    """On how many non-empty subsets does the relation single out a winner?"""
    ok = 0
    subs = 0
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        for S in combinations(range(n), k):
            subs += 1
            best = [a for a in S if all((a, b) in rel for b in S if b != a)]
            if len(best) == 1:
                ok += 1
    return ok, subs


def e7(EPS=0.01):
    out = {}
    for n in (4, 5):
        ts = list(_tournaments(n))
        trans = [t for t in ts if _transitive(t, n)]
        cyc = [t for t in ts if not _transitive(t, n)]
        cond = [t for t in ts if _condorcet(t, n)]
        cyclens = {}
        pump_total = 0
        for t in cyc:
            L = _cycles(t, n)
            cyclens[L] = cyclens.get(L, 0) + 1
            pump_total += L
        # choice coverage
        cov_trans = _choice_defined(trans[0], n)
        worst_cyc = min(_choice_defined(t, n)[0] for t in cyc)
        # the indifferent agent: the empty relation
        empty_cov = _choice_defined(frozenset(), n)
        out[n] = {

        }

# E8  An affect-like signal as the price of deciding at all
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# A decision tree of branching b and depth d.  Leaf value is a deterministic
# function of the path.  An exhaustive evaluator scores every leaf.  A tagged
# evaluator probes each child cheaply -- one leaf drawn from its subtree by a
# fixed rule -- and descends only into the top k.

B, D = 6, 6


def _leaf_value(path):
    v = 0
    for i, c in enumerate(path):
        v = (v * 31 + (c + 1) * (i * i + 7)) % 100003
    return v


def _probe_path(path):
    """A fixed, cheap representative leaf of the subtree below `path`."""
    p = list(path)
    while len(p) < D:
        p.append((sum(p) + len(p) * 3) % B)
    return tuple(p)


def _best_below(path, memo):
    if path in memo:
        return memo[path]
    if len(path) == D:
        r = (_leaf_value(path), path)
    else:
        r = max(_best_below(path + (c,), memo) for c in range(B))
    memo[path] = r
    return r


def e8():

    best_v, best_p = _best_below((), memo)
    leaves = B ** D

    rows = []

            if len(path) == D:

                return (_leaf_value(path), path)
            tagged = []
            for c in range(B):
                cost[0] += 1
                tagged.append((_leaf_value(_probe_path(path + (c,))), c))
            tagged.sort(reverse=True)
            return max(search(path + (c,)) for _, c in tagged[:k])

        v, p = search(())

        "gradual replacement": chain["gradual"],
        "memory connectedness": len(s["mem"] & o_mem) / len(o_mem) >= 0.5,
        "memory continuity": chain["psych"],
        "unbroken process": s["unbroken"],
        "self-model": s["model"] == origin["model"],
        "institutional token": s["token"],
    }

7.6Where to go next

Four entry points, chosen because each makes the structure of a dispute visible rather than taking a side in it.

  • On implementation and computation. Chalmers' 1996 paper and Piccinini's book are the two ends of the live disagreement about what it is for a physical system to compute; read them together and the substrate question stops being about silicon.
  • On machine consciousness. The Butlin et al. indicator report is the most disciplined attempt to make the question tractable, and its own methodological caveats are the best short statement of why it is hard. Pair it with the Cogitate adversarial result.
  • On content. Millikan for the original teleosemantic move, Coelho Mollo and Millière for the version aimed at systems trained on text, and Newman's 1928 review for the result that constrains all of it.
  • On moral status. Birch's The Edge of Sentience for the precautionary framework, Schwitzgebel for the dilemma that arises when status is genuinely uncertain, and Jaworska and Tannenbaum's survey for the map of grounds.
