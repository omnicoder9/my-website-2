Abstract

Knowledge representation and reasoning (KR&R) is the part of artificial intelligence concerned with encoding knowledge about the world in a form a program can manipulate, and with the inference procedures that derive what is implicit in that encoding [1,2]. This article surveys the field's main formalisms in roughly the order they appeared. It starts from propositional and first-order logic, moves through semantic networks, frames, production rules and expert systems, and then covers the description logics behind modern ontologies and knowledge graphs. It ends with the reasoning patterns that classical logic handles poorly: induction, abduction, defaults and common sense. Every section pairs the theory with a short, runnable Python program (standard library only; outputs shown were produced by running the code) and closes with exercises and worked solutions.

1What knowledge representation is for

A program that diagnoses infections, answers questions about films, or configures computer hardware needs to hold facts about its domain and to act on consequences of those facts that nobody wrote down explicitly. Brachman and Levesque define knowledge representation as "the field of study concerned with using formal symbols to represent a collection of propositions believed by some putative agent", and reasoning as "the formal manipulation of the symbols representing a collection of believed propositions to produce representations of new ones" [1]. The two are inseparable: a representation is only as useful as the inferences it supports, and an inference procedure is only as good as the representation it runs on.

Davis, Shrobe and Szolovits argued that a knowledge representation plays five distinct roles at once [3]:

  1. A surrogate: a stand-in inside the reasoner for things in the world, which is always imperfect, so some conclusions will be wrong.
  2. A set of ontological commitments: a decision about what to see in the world (objects? events? substances?) and what to ignore.
  3. A fragmentary theory of intelligent reasoning: each formalism carries a view of which inferences are sanctioned and which are recommended.
  4. A medium for efficient computation: the representation determines what can be computed quickly.
  5. A medium of human expression: people must be able to write, read and correct it.

The fourth role produces the central tension of the field. Levesque and Brachman showed that the more a language can say, the harder it becomes to reason with it: there is a fundamental tradeoff between expressiveness and tractability [4]. First-order logic can say almost anything but its validity problem is undecidable; a taxonomy of classes says very little but can be queried in linear time. Most of the formalisms in this article are best understood as different points on that curve, chosen for different jobs.

Two further distinctions run through the whole article. A declarative representation states what is true and leaves the "how" to a general inference procedure; a procedural one embeds the knowledge in code that knows how to use it [2]. And reasoning can be sound (it never derives something false from true premises), complete (it derives everything that follows), both, or, deliberately, neither, as with the default and commonsense reasoning of Sections 9 and 10.

2A short history

The idea that reasoning can be reduced to the manipulation of symbols is old. Aristotle's syllogistic, in the Prior Analytics, catalogued valid argument forms such as "all A are B; all B are C; therefore all A are C" [11], and the tree of genus and species later drawn by Porphyry is the ancestor of every class hierarchy in this article [12]. Linnaeus applied the same nested-class idea to living things in Systema Naturae (1735) [13]. In 1854 George Boole gave the algebra of propositions [14], and in 1879 Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift introduced quantifiers and the predicate calculus that became first-order logic [15]. Gödel proved first-order logic complete in 1930 [16]; Church and Turing proved in 1936 that its validity problem is undecidable [9,10].

Artificial intelligence inherited these results. Newell and Simon's Logic Theorist (1956) proved theorems from Principia Mathematica by heuristic search [17]. Three years later John McCarthy's "Programs with common sense" proposed the Advice Taker, a program that would represent its knowledge as logical sentences and could be told new facts rather than reprogrammed. It is usually taken as the founding document of logic-based KR [18]. Robinson's resolution principle (1965) gave a single, machine-oriented inference rule for first-order logic [19], and it led to Prolog in the early 1970s [20,21].

A second tradition came from psychology and language. Quillian's semantic memory (1968) stored word meanings as a network of linked nodes [22]; Minsky's frames (1974) packaged stereotyped situations into slot-and-filler structures [23]. In 1969 McCarthy and Hayes introduced the situation calculus and named the frame problem [24]. The 1970s and 1980s were the era of expert systems: DENDRAL for mass spectrometry [25], MYCIN for infectious disease [26] and R1/XCON for computer configuration [27]. A 1980 special issue of Artificial Intelligence launched nonmonotonic reasoning with default logic, circumscription and non-monotonic logic [28–30]. KL-ONE turned frames into a formal class language and seeded the description logics [31], and Cyc began its attempt to encode common sense by hand [32].

From the 1990s the emphasis moved to sharing and scale. Gruber defined an ontology as "an explicit specification of a conceptualization" [33]; the Semantic Web proposal [34] produced the W3C standards RDF and OWL [35,36]; and in 2012 Google announced its Knowledge Graph [37]. Current research asks how symbolic knowledge and large language models can be combined [38].

3Propositional and first-order logic

Logic is the reference point against which every other formalism is measured, because it comes with a precise account of what follows from what [1,2]. A logic has three parts: a syntax (which strings are well-formed sentences), a semantics (what makes a sentence true in a model, a possible world) and a proof theory (rules that derive sentences from sentences).

3.1 Propositional logic

Propositional logic has atomic propositions (P, Rain, B21) combined with the connectives ¬, ∧, ∨, → and ↔. A model assigns true or false to every atom, and the truth of compound sentences follows from truth tables. The key semantic notion is entailment:

Definition: entailmentKB ⊨ α  iff  every model in which KB is true also makes α true.
Equivalently: KB ⊨ α  iff  KB ∧ ¬α is unsatisfiable.

The second line is what makes logic computable in practice: to prove α, show that adding its negation leads to contradiction. A proof procedure ⊢ is sound if KB ⊢ α implies KB ⊨ α, and complete if the converse holds. Checking entailment by enumerating models is sound and complete but costs 2n for n atoms; propositional satisfiability (SAT) was the first problem proved NP-complete [6]. In practice, solvers descended from the Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland procedure handle very large instances [2,39,40].

The program below is the model-checking algorithm, applied to a fragment of the "wumpus world" used throughout Russell and Norvig [2]: a breeze in a square means a pit in an adjacent square.

Listing: s1_prop.pyPython 3 · standard library
from itertools import product

def symbols(f):
    """Collect proposition symbols from a formula given as nested tuples."""
    if isinstance(f, str):
        return {f}
    return set().union(*(symbols(a) for a in f[1:]))

def holds(f, m):
    """Evaluate formula f in model m (dict symbol -> bool)."""
    if isinstance(f, str):
        return m[f]
    op, *args = f
    if op == 'not': return not holds(args[0], m)
    if op == 'and': return all(holds(a, m) for a in args)
    if op == 'or':  return any(holds(a, m) for a in args)
    if op == '->':  return (not holds(args[0], m)) or holds(args[1], m)
    if op == '<->': return holds(args[0], m) == holds(args[1], m)
    raise ValueError(op)

def entails(kb, query):
    """KB |= query iff query is true in every model of KB (truth-table method)."""
    syms = sorted(symbols(kb) | symbols(query))
    for values in product([False, True], repeat=len(syms)):
        m = dict(zip(syms, values))
        if holds(kb, m) and not holds(query, m):
            return False, m          # counter-model found
    return True, None

# Wumpus-style example: breeze in [1,2] iff pit in [1,1] or [1,3] ...
kb = ('and',
      ('<->', 'B11', ('or', 'P12', 'P21')),
      ('<->', 'B21', ('or', 'P11', 'P22', 'P31')),
      ('not', 'P11'), ('not', 'B11'), 'B21')
print(entails(kb, ('not', 'P12')))   # (True, None)
print(entails(kb, 'P22'))            # (False, {...counter-model...})
(True, None)

The first query succeeds: no model of the knowledge base has a pit in [1,2]. The second fails, and the program returns a counter-model in which the pit is in [3,1] instead of [2,2]. A counter-model is more useful than a bare "no", because it shows what the knowledge base leaves open.

Resolution

Robinson's resolution rule [19] works on sentences in conjunctive normal form (CNF), a conjunction of clauses, each a disjunction of literals. From two clauses containing complementary literals, it derives a clause with both removed:

Resolution rule(ℓ ∨ C₁), (¬ℓ ∨ C₂)  ⟹  (C₁ ∨ C₂)

Resolution is refutation-complete: if KB ∧ ¬α is unsatisfiable, repeated resolution will eventually derive the empty clause □ [2,19].

Listing: s1b_res.pyPython 3 · standard library
def resolve(c1, c2):
    """All resolvents of two clauses (frozensets of literals like 'P' / '~P')."""
    out = []
    for lit in c1:
        comp = lit[1:] if lit.startswith('~') else '~' + lit
        if comp in c2:
            out.append(frozenset((c1 - {lit}) | (c2 - {comp})))
    return out

def refutes(clauses):
    """Saturate by resolution; True if the empty clause is derived."""
    clauses = set(map(frozenset, clauses))
    while True:
        new = set()
        pairs = [(a, b) for a in clauses for b in clauses if a != b]
        for a, b in pairs:
            for r in resolve(a, b):
                if not r:
                    return True
                new.add(r)
        if new <= clauses:
            return False
        clauses |= new

# KB in CNF: (~P | Q), (~Q | R), P.  Query R: add the negation ~R.
print(refutes([{'~P', 'Q'}, {'~Q', 'R'}, {'P'}, {'~R'}]))   # True: KB |= R
print(refutes([{'~P', 'Q'}, {'P'}, {'~R'}]))                 # False

3.2 First-order logic

Propositional logic cannot say "every parent of a parent is a grandparent" without one sentence per pair of people. First-order logic (FOL) adds terms that denote objects (constants, variables, functions such as mother(x)), predicates that denote relations, and the quantifiers ∀ and ∃ [2,15]. A model now consists of a domain of objects and an interpretation of every symbol over that domain.

Examples∀x ∀y ∀z (Parent(x,y) ∧ Parent(y,z) → Grandparent(x,z))
∀x (Bird(x) → ∃y (Mother(y,x)))
∀x (Student(x) → ∃c (Course(c) ∧ Takes(x,c)))

Three results fix what can be expected of first-order reasoning. Gödel's completeness theorem: there are proof systems in which every entailed sentence has a proof [16]. The Church–Turing theorem: no algorithm decides, for every sentence, whether it is valid [9,10]. Together these make FOL entailment semi-decidable: a prover will confirm every true entailment eventually, but may run forever on a non-entailment [2].

Unification and Horn clauses

To lift resolution to FOL, Robinson introduced unification: finding the most general substitution θ that makes two expressions identical [19]. Knows(John, x) and Knows(y, Mother(y)) unify with θ = {y/John, x/Mother(John)}. A Horn clause has at most one positive literal, so it can be read as a rule body → head. Horn logic is the basis of Prolog, which reads such clauses as a program run by backward chaining [20,21], and of Datalog, which forbids function symbols so that forward chaining always terminates [2].

The code below implements unification with the occurs check and a forward-chaining Datalog engine that applies every rule to every matching combination of facts until nothing new appears (a fixpoint).

Listing: s2_fol.pyPython 3 · standard library
def is_var(t):
    return isinstance(t, str) and t.startswith('?')

def unify(x, y, s):
    """Return a substitution extending s that makes x and y equal, or None."""
    if s is None:
        return None
    if x == y:
        return s
    if is_var(x):
        return unify_var(x, y, s)
    if is_var(y):
        return unify_var(y, x, s)
    if isinstance(x, tuple) and isinstance(y, tuple) and len(x) == len(y):
        for a, b in zip(x, y):
            s = unify(a, b, s)
        return s
    return None

def unify_var(v, t, s):
    if v in s:
        return unify(s[v], t, s)
    if is_var(t) and t in s:
        return unify(v, s[t], s)
    if occurs(v, t, s):
        return None                       # occurs check
    return {**s, v: t}

def occurs(v, t, s):
    if v == t:
        return True
    if is_var(t) and t in s:
        return occurs(v, s[t], s)
    return isinstance(t, tuple) and any(occurs(v, a, s) for a in t)

def subst(s, t):
    if is_var(t):
        return subst(s, s[t]) if t in s else t
    if isinstance(t, tuple):
        return tuple(subst(s, a) for a in t)
    return t

def match_all(premises, facts, s):
    """Yield every substitution satisfying all premises against the fact base."""
    if not premises:
        yield s
        return
    first, rest = premises[0], premises[1:]
    for f in facts:
        s2 = unify(first, f, dict(s))
        if s2 is not None:
            yield from match_all(rest, facts, s2)

def forward_chain(facts, rules):
    """Naive forward chaining for definite clauses (Datalog): run to fixpoint."""
    facts = set(facts)
    while True:
        new = set()
        for premises, conclusion in rules:
            for s in match_all(premises, facts, {}):
                c = subst(s, conclusion)
                if c not in facts:
                    new.add(c)
        if not new:
            return facts
        facts |= new

facts = {('parent', 'abe', 'homer'), ('parent', 'homer', 'bart'),
         ('parent', 'homer', 'lisa'), ('parent', 'mona', 'homer')}
rules = [

    ([('parent', '?x', '?y'), ('ancestor', '?y', '?z')], ('ancestor', '?x', '?z')),

Note the second line of output: the substitution is returned in triangular form (?x maps to mother(?y), and ?y to john); applying subst resolves it to mother(john). Forward chaining is data-driven: it derives every consequence, whether or not it is needed. Backward chaining, used by Prolog and by MYCIN in Section 6, is goal-driven: it starts from the query and works back to the facts [2].

Exercises for Section 3

3.1Using a truth table (or the entails function), decide whether (P → Q) ∧ (Q → R) ⊨ (P → R).

Solution

Yes. The conclusion P → R is false only in rows where P is true and R is false. In such a row, if Q is false then P → Q is false; if Q is true then Q → R is false. Either way the premises are false, so no model makes the premises true and the conclusion false. This is the hypothetical syllogism. In code: entails(('and', ('->','P','Q'), ('->','Q','R')), ('->','P','R')) returns (True, None).

3.2Translate into FOL: "Every student who takes a course taught by a professor admires some professor."

Solution

∀s ∀c ∀p (Student(s) ∧ Course(c) ∧ Takes(s,c) ∧ Professor(p) ∧ Teaches(p,c) → ∃q (Professor(q) ∧ Admires(s,q)))

A common mistake is to write ∃q (Professor(q) ∧ …) outside the universal quantifiers, which would claim a single professor admired by every such student. The sentence is also ambiguous in English: on the reading "admires the professor who teaches the course", replace the consequent with Admires(s,p).

3.3Find the most general unifier of P(x, f(y), z) and P(g(w), f(a), w). Then explain why Knows(John, x) and Knows(x, Elizabeth) do not unify, and how renaming fixes it.

Solution

θ = {x/g(w), y/a, z/w}. Applying θ gives P(g(w), f(a), w) on both sides. For the second pair, x would have to be both John and Elizabeth, so unification fails. If the two sentences come from different clauses, their variables are independent, so standardise apart: rename to Knows(x₁, Elizabeth), which unifies with θ = {x/Elizabeth, x₁/John} [2].

3.4The sibling rule in the Datalog program derives sibling(bart, bart). Add a built-in inequality test so that it does not.

Solution

Treat neq as a built-in that is evaluated under the current substitution, not matched against stored facts:

4Ontologies, taxonomies and knowledge graphs

An agent that shares knowledge with other agents needs to agree with them on vocabulary. Gruber defined an ontology as "an explicit specification of a conceptualization" [33], and Studer, Benjamins and Fensel refined this to "a formal, explicit specification of a shared conceptualization" [41]. Each word does work. Conceptualization means an abstract model of some part of the world; explicit means the concepts and constraints are written down; formal means a machine can read them; shared means a community agrees to them.

4.1 From taxonomy to ontology

A taxonomy is the simplest kind of ontology: a set of categories ordered by the subclass ("is-a") relation, as in Linnaean classification [13] or WordNet's hypernym hierarchy, where dog is a kind of canine, which is a kind of carnivore [42]. Subclass is transitive, so a taxonomy supports inheritance: whatever is true of all carnivores is true of all dogs [2].

An ontology adds properties (relations between classes), constraints on them (domain, range, cardinality), and axioms such as disjointness ("nothing is both a plant and an animal") or definitions ("a parent is a person with at least one child"). The Gene Ontology, for example, organises terms for biological processes, molecular functions and cellular components, linked by is_a and part_of relations, and is used to annotate genes across species [43]. Large clinical terminologies such as SNOMED CT are built on description logic, the formalism of Section 7 [5,44].

4.2 Knowledge graphs and the Semantic Web stack

A knowledge graph is "a graph of data intended to accumulate and convey knowledge of the real world, whose nodes represent entities of interest and whose edges represent relations between these entities" [45]. The W3C's Resource Description Framework (RDF) standardises the data model: every statement is a triple (subject, predicate, object) whose terms are IRIs (global identifiers), literals or blank nodes [35]. RDF Schema (RDFS) adds a small vocabulary (rdfs:subClassOf, rdfs:domain, rdfs:range, rdfs:subPropertyOf) with a formal semantics that licenses new triples [46]. OWL, the Web Ontology Language, adds the richer description-logic constructors [36], and SPARQL is the query language [47].

Large public knowledge graphs include DBpedia, extracted from Wikipedia infoboxes [48], and Wikidata, a collaboratively edited graph launched by the Wikimedia Foundation in 2012 [49]. When Google announced its Knowledge Graph in 2012, it described it as containing more than 500 million objects and 3.5 billion facts about them [37].

RDFS entailment can be computed by forward chaining a handful of rules to a fixpoint ("materialisation"), exactly as in the Datalog engine of Section 3. The program below implements six of the W3C's RDFS entailment rules [46] and a one-pattern query matcher.

Listing: s3_kg.pyPython 3 · standard library
TYPE, SUBCLASS, DOMAIN, RANGE = 'rdf:type', 'rdfs:subClassOf', 'rdfs:domain', 'rdfs:range'
SUBPROP = 'rdfs:subPropertyOf'

def rdfs_closure(triples):
    """Materialise a subset of the RDFS entailment rules (rdfs2,3,5,7,9,11)."""
    g = set(triples)
    while True:
        new = set()
        for (s, p, o) in g:
            for (s2, p2, o2) in g:
                if p == SUBCLASS and p2 == SUBCLASS and o == s2:
                    new.add((s, SUBCLASS, o2))            # rdfs11: transitivity
                if p == TYPE and p2 == SUBCLASS and o == s2:
                    new.add((s, TYPE, o2))                # rdfs9: type inheritance
                if p == SUBPROP and p2 == SUBPROP and o == s2:
                    new.add((s, SUBPROP, o2))             # rdfs5
                if p2 == SUBPROP and p == s2:
                    new.add((s, o2, o))                   # rdfs7
                if p2 == DOMAIN and p == s2:
                    new.add((s, TYPE, o2))                # rdfs2
                if p2 == RANGE and p == s2:
                    new.add((o, TYPE, o2))                # rdfs3
        if new <= g:
            return g
        g |= new

def query(g, pattern):
    """Match one triple pattern; terms starting with '?' are variables."""
    for t in g:
        b = {}
        if all((q == v) or (q.startswith('?') and b.setdefault(q, v) == v)
               for q, v in zip(pattern, t)):
            yield b

kg = {
    ('ex:MarieCurie', 'ex:wonPrize', 'ex:NobelPhysics1903'),
    ('ex:wonPrize', DOMAIN, 'ex:Laureate'),
    ('ex:wonPrize', RANGE, 'ex:Award'),
    ('ex:wonPrize', SUBPROP, 'ex:receivedHonour'),
    ('ex:Laureate', SUBCLASS, 'ex:Person'),
    ('ex:Person', SUBCLASS, 'ex:Agent'),
    ('ex:MarieCurie', 'ex:bornIn', 'ex:Warsaw'),
}
closed = rdfs_closure(kg)
print(sorted(b['?c'] for b in query(closed, ('ex:MarieCurie', TYPE, '?c'))))
print(list(query(closed, ('?who', 'ex:receivedHonour', '?what'))))
print(len(kg), '->', len(closed), 'triples')
['ex:Agent', 'ex:Laureate', 'ex:Person']
[{'?who': 'ex:MarieCurie', '?what': 'ex:NobelPhysics1903'}]

Six triples were inferred, including the rdfs:subPropertyOf consequence that Marie Curie receivedHonour the 1903 prize. Note what RDFS does not do: rdfs:domain is not a constraint that rejects bad data, it is a rule that adds a type. Asserting ex:Warsaw ex:wonPrize ex:X would make Warsaw a Laureate rather than raising an error. OWL behaves the same way under its open-world semantics, which is a frequent source of surprise (Section 9 returns to this).

4.3 Statistical views of knowledge graphs

Knowledge graphs are always incomplete, so a large body of work learns to predict missing edges. TransE, for example, embeds each entity and relation as a vector and trains so that h + r ≈ t for every true triple (h, r, t) [50]. Such embeddings support link prediction but give up the crisp entailment guarantees of RDFS and OWL. Hogan et al. survey the deductive and inductive sides together [45], and Pan et al. map the ways knowledge graphs and large language models are being combined: graphs to ground and check a model's output, and models to build and complete graphs [38].

Exercises for Section 4

4.1Given the triples (Rex type Dog), (Dog subClassOf Mammal), (Mammal subClassOf Animal), (owns range Pet) and (alice owns Rex), list every triple RDFS adds.

Solution

Four new triples: (Dog subClassOf Animal) by transitivity (rdfs11); (Rex type Mammal) and (Rex type Animal) by type inheritance (rdfs9); (Rex type Pet) from the range of owns (rdfs3). Running rdfs_closure on these five triples returns exactly nine. Note that nothing is inferred about alice, because owns has no declared domain.

4.2Name two kinds of knowledge an ontology language like OWL can state that a pure taxonomy cannot, and give an example of each.

Solution

Any two of: disjointness (Plant and Animal share no instances); definitions with necessary and sufficient conditions (Parent ≡ Person ⊓ ∃hasChild.Person), which let a reasoner classify individuals and classes automatically; cardinality restrictions (a Bicycle has exactly two wheels); property characteristics such as transitivity of partOf or hasParent being the inverse of hasChild [36]. A taxonomy can express only the subclass relation.

5Semantic networks and frames

5.1 Semantic networks

Quillian's model of semantic memory represented each concept as a node and each relation as a labelled link, so that the meaning of a word was the whole structure reachable from its node [22]. Collins and Quillian tested a hierarchical version experimentally. They predicted that properties are stored at the most general node they apply to ("can breathe" at animal, "can fly" at bird, "can sing" at canary) and that verifying a sentence takes longer the more links must be traversed. Their subjects' reaction times rose with hierarchical distance, as predicted [51].

Semantic networks were attractive because they were easy to draw and made retrieval of related facts fast. Their weakness, set out in Woods's paper "What's in a link", was that the notation had no agreed semantics: the same arrow might mean "is a subclass of", "is an instance of", "typically has" or "every instance has" [52]. Two systems with identical-looking networks could draw different conclusions. Brachman's response, KL-ONE, gave every link type a precise meaning and is the direct ancestor of description logics [7,31].

5.2 Frames

Minsky proposed that when one meets a new situation, one "selects from memory a structure called a frame", a remembered framework "to be adapted to fit reality by changing details as necessary" [23]. A frame is a data structure for a stereotyped object or situation: a set of slots, each with facets such as a value, a default, a type restriction, or a procedure ("demon") that runs when the value is needed or added. Frames are linked in an inheritance hierarchy, and a slot without a local value inherits one from its parent.

Frame languages such as KRL [53] and the commercial systems surveyed by Fikes and Kehler [54] made this concrete. Schank and Abelson's scripts applied the same idea to event sequences, such as going to a restaurant: enter, be seated, order, eat, pay, leave [55]. class Frame:
    """A Minsky-style frame: named slots with values, defaults and procedural attachments."""
    registry = {}

    def __init__(self, name, is_a=None, **slots):
        self.name, self.is_a, self.slots = name, is_a, {}
        for k, v in slots.items():
            self.slots[k] = {'value': v}
        Frame.registry[name] = self

    def default(self, slot, value):
        self.slots.setdefault(slot, {})['default'] = value
        return self

    def if_needed(self, slot, fn):
        self.slots.setdefault(slot, {})['if_needed'] = fn
        return self

    def get(self, slot, origin=None):
        """Look up a slot: own value, then if-needed demon, then default, then inherit."""
        origin = origin or self
        facets = self.slots.get(slot, {})
        if 'value' in facets:
            return facets['value']
        if 'if_needed' in facets:
            return facets['if_needed'](origin)
        if 'default' in facets:
            return facets['default']
        if self.is_a:
            return Frame.registry[self.is_a].get(slot, origin)
        return None

A production system represents knowledge as condition–action rules ("productions") that operate on a working memory of facts. 6.2 Certainty factors

Medical evidence is rarely conclusive, so MYCIN attached a certainty factor (CF) in [−1, 1] to every rule and fact [61]. The CF of a conclusion from one rule is the rule's CF times the CF of its premises, where a conjunction takes the minimum. When two rules support the same conclusion, their CFs combine:

def combine(a, b):
    """MYCIN parallel combination of two certainty factors in [-1, 1]."""
    if a >= 0 and b >= 0:
        return a + b * (1 - a)
    if a < 0 and b < 0:
        return a + b * (1 + a)
    return (a + b) / (1 - min(abs(a), abs(b)))

class Consultation:
    def __init__(self, answers):

        self.known, self.trace = {}, []

        """Backward chaining: establish goal's certainty from rules or by asking."""
        if goal in self.known:
            return self.known[goal]
        if goal in ASKABLE:
            self.known[goal] = self.answers.get(goal, 0.0)
            return self.known[goal]
        total = 0.0
        for rid, premises, concl, rule_cf in RULES:
            if concl != goal:
                continue
            premise_cf = min(self.cf(p, depth + 1) for p in premises)   # AND = min

                total = combine(total, contribution)
                self.trace.append(f"{rid}: {' & '.join(premises)} -> {concl} "
                                  f"(CF {contribution:.2f})")
        self.known[goal] = total
        return total

7Description logics

Description logics (DLs) are a family of decidable fragments of first-order logic designed for one job: defining classes ("concepts") and the relations between them, and reasoning about which classes contain which [7]. They descend from KL-ONE [31] and are the logical basis of OWL [36]. A DL knowledge base has two parts. The TBox (terminological box) holds axioms about concepts, such as Mother ≡ Woman ⊓ ∃hasChild.Person. The ABox (assertional box) holds facts about individuals, such as Woman(mary) and hasChild(mary, tom).

7.1 The basic language ALC

ALC, introduced by Schmidt-Schauß and Smolka [62], builds complex concepts from atomic concepts A and roles r:

Existential restriction∃r.C{x | ∃y: (x,y) ∈ rI ∧ y ∈ CI}∃y (r(x,y) ∧ C(y))
Value restriction∀r.C{x | ∀y: (x,y) ∈ rI → y ∈ CI}∀y (r(x,y) → C(y))

Adding further constructors gives the letters in DL names: H for role hierarchies, O for nominals (concepts with one named member), I for inverse roles, Q for qualified number restrictions (≥ 2 hasChild.Female), R for complex role inclusions, and S for ALC plus transitive roles. OWL 2 DL corresponds to SROIQ [36,63].

7.2 Reasoning services

A DL reasoner offers a small set of standard services, all of which reduce to one: concept satisfiability [7].

  • Satisfiability: can concept C have any instances?
  • Subsumption: is every C a D (C ⊑ D)? True iff C ⊓ ¬D is unsatisfiable.
  • Classification: compute the full subsumption hierarchy of all named concepts.
  • Consistency: does the ABox have a model together with the TBox?
  • Instance checking: is individual a an instance of C? True iff adding ¬C(a) makes the ABox inconsistent.

7.3 Tableau algorithms

The standard decision procedure for expressive DLs is the tableau algorithm [7,62]. To test whether C is satisfiable, it tries to build a model. It puts C in negation normal form (negation only in front of atomic concepts), places it in the label of a root individual, and applies expansion rules until no rule applies or a clash (A and ¬A in one label) appears:

  • ⊓-rule: if C ⊓ D is in a label, add C and D.
  • ⊔-rule: if C ⊔ D is in a label, add C or D. This is a choice point, so branch and backtrack.
  • ∃-rule: if ∃r.C is in x's label, create a new r-successor y with C in its label.
  • ∀-rule: if ∀r.C is in x's label and y is an r-successor, add C to y's label.

If some branch ends without a clash, it describes a model and C is satisfiable. The program below is a compact tableau for ALC with an empty TBox. It applies the ∀-rule at the moment each successor is created, which is equivalent for this language.

# Tableau satisfiability for ALC concepts (empty TBox).
# Concepts: 'A' | ('not', C) | ('and', C, D) | ('or', C, D) | ('some', r, C) | ('all', r, C)

def nnf(c):
    """Push negation inward to negation normal form."""
    if isinstance(c, str):
        return c
    op = c[0]
    if op == 'not':
        d = c[1]
        if isinstance(d, str):
            return c
        if d[0] == 'not':  return nnf(d[1])
        if d[0] == 'and':  return ('or',  nnf(('not', d[1])), nnf(('not', d[2])))
        if d[0] == 'or':   return ('and', nnf(('not', d[1])), nnf(('not', d[2])))
        if d[0] == 'some': return ('all',  d[1], nnf(('not', d[2])))
        if d[0] == 'all':  return ('some', d[1], nnf(('not', d[2])))
    if op in ('and', 'or'):
        return (op, nnf(c[1]), nnf(c[2]))
    return (op, c[1], nnf(c[2]))

def clash(label):
    return any(('not', c) in label for c in label if isinstance(c, str))

def sat(label):
    """Is the set of NNF concepts `label` (one individual) satisfiable?"""
    label = set(label)
    # deterministic AND-rule to saturation
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        for c in list(label):
            if isinstance(c, tuple) and c[0] == 'and' and not {c[1], c[2]} <= label:
                label |= {c[1], c[2]}
                changed = True
    if clash(label):
        return False
    # non-deterministic OR-rule: branch (this is where exponential search lives)
    for c in label:
        if isinstance(c, tuple) and c[0] == 'or' and c[1] not in label and c[2] not in label:
            return sat(label | {c[1]}) or sat(label | {c[2]})
    # EXISTS-rule: each successor gets C plus every ALL-restriction on the same role
    for c in label:
        if isinstance(c, tuple) and c[0] == 'some':
            r, filler = c[1], c[2]
            succ = {filler} | {d[2] for d in label
                               if isinstance(d, tuple) and d[0] == 'all' and d[1] == r}
            if not sat(succ):
                return False
    return True

def satisfiable(c):
    return sat({nnf(c)})

def subsumes(d, c):
    """C is subsumed by D iff C AND NOT D is unsatisfiable."""
    return not satisfiable(('and', c, ('not', d)))

7.4 Complexity and practical reasoners

DLs made the expressiveness–tractability tradeoff of Section 1 precise. Brachman and Levesque showed in 1984 that adding a single constructor to a tractable frame language makes subsumption co-NP-hard [64]. Satisfiability of ALC concepts is PSPACE-complete [62], and EXPTIME-complete with a general TBox [7]; for SROIQ it is N2EXPTIME-complete [8]. Despite these worst cases, highly optimised reasoners such as HermiT classify large real ontologies routinely [65]. At the other end, the lightweight logic EL (only ⊓, ∃ and ⊤) has polynomial subsumption even with a general TBox. It is expressive enough for large medical terminologies such as SNOMED CT and is the basis of the OWL 2 EL profile [5,36].

8Deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning

The American philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce distinguished three kinds of inference with a famous example about a bag of beans [66]. Each combines two of three statements, a rule, a case and a result, to infer the third:

8.1 Deduction

Deduction derives conclusions that must be true if the premises are. Everything in Sections 3, 4 and 7 is deductive: model checking, resolution, forward chaining, RDFS materialisation and tableau reasoning are all sound procedures for logical entailment [2]. Deduction never adds information that was not implicit in the premises; it makes it explicit.

8.2 Induction

Induction generalises from instances to rules. Hume pointed out that no finite set of observations can logically guarantee a universal law [67], so inductive conclusions are always defeasible. In AI, induction is the core of machine learning. In KR its most direct form is inductive logic programming (ILP), which learns first-order Horn clauses from examples and background knowledge [68]. Quinlan's FOIL, for instance, learns relational definitions by greedily adding literals to a clause until it covers positive examples and excludes negatives [69]. The simplest illustration is Mitchell's Find-S algorithm, which computes the most specific conjunctive hypothesis consistent with the positive examples [70].

8.3 Abduction

Abduction is inference to an explanation: given a theory T and an observation O, find a hypothesis H such that T ∪ H ⊨ O and T ∪ H is consistent. Usually one wants H to be minimal or most plausible. Diagnosis, plan recognition and language interpretation are all naturally abductive [71]. Poole's Theorist framework treats both defaults and diagnoses as hypotheses selected from a fixed set [72], and abductive logic programming extends Prolog with abducible predicates and integrity constraints [73]. Abduction is deductively unsound: it is the fallacy of affirming the consequent, made useful by preferring the best explanation.

9Nonmonotonic reasoning and default logic

Classical logic is monotonic: if KB ⊨ α then KB ∪ {β} ⊨ α for any β. Learning more never retracts a conclusion. Everyday reasoning is not like this. Told that Tweety is a bird, one concludes Tweety flies; told also that Tweety is a penguin, one withdraws that conclusion. Formalising such defeasible reasoning was the subject of the 1980 special issue of Artificial Intelligence [28–30].

9.1 Closed worlds and negation as failure

The simplest nonmonotonic rule is the closed-world assumption (CWA): if an atomic fact is not entailed, assume it is false [74]. Databases rely on it; if a flight is not in the timetable, there is no such flight. Prolog's negation as failure implements a procedural version: \+ p succeeds if the attempt to prove p finitely fails [75]. The semantic web takes the opposite open-world view: absence of a triple means "unknown", which is why OWL reasoners do not conclude that a person with no recorded children has none [36].

9.2 Reiter's default logic

Reiter's default logic [28] extends a set W of first-order sentences with default rules of the form

Default ruleα : β₁, …, βₙ / γ     read: "if α is believed, and each βᵢ is consistent with what is believed, conclude γ"
Example:  Bird(x) : Flies(x) / Flies(x)

α is the prerequisite, the βᵢ are the justifications and γ is the consequent. A default is normal if it has a single justification equal to its consequent, as in the example. The meaning of a default theory (D, W) is given by its extensions: sets of beliefs E that contain W, are closed under logical consequence, and are closed under the defaults whose justifications are consistent with E itself. Formally, E is an extension iff E = Γ(E), where Γ(S) is the smallest deductively closed set containing W and closed under the defaults whose justifications are consistent with S [28]. Because E appears on both sides, extensions are fixed points, not something computed by a single forward pass.

Key properties, all from Reiter's paper: a theory may have one, several or no extensions; every normal theory has at least one; and normal theories are semi-monotonic, so adding defaults never destroys an existing extension [28].

For normal defaults and a propositional W of literals and Horn rules, extensions can be found by trying the defaults in every order, applying each one whose justification is still consistent. The program does exactly that.

# Literals are strings; '-p' is the negation of 'p'. W is a set of literals plus
# Horn implications; a normal default  pre : just / just  is (pre, just).
def neg(l):

def close(facts, implications):
    facts = set(facts)

    while changed:
        changed = False
        for body, head in implications:
            if set(body) <= facts and head not in facts:
                facts.add(head)
                changed = True
    return facts

    exts = set()
    for order in permutations(defaults):
        E = close(W, implications)
        progress = True
        while progress:
            progress = False
            for pre, just in order:
                if pre in E and just not in E and consistent(close(E | {just}, implications)):
                    E = close(E | {just}, implications)
                    progress = True
                    break
        exts.add(frozenset(E))
    return exts

9.3 Other formalisms

  • Circumscription (McCarthy): minimise the extension of chosen predicates, typically an "abnormality" predicate Ab, so that ∀x (Bird(x) ∧ ¬Ab(x) → Flies(x)) yields Flies(Tweety) unless Tweety is known to be abnormal [29].
  • Non-monotonic and autoepistemic logic: McDermott and Doyle introduced a modal operator for "is consistent" [30]; Moore reinterpreted it as reasoning about one's own beliefs, "if I believed P, I would know it" [76].
  • Answer set programming: Gelfond and Lifschitz's stable model semantics gives logic programs with negation as failure a declarative, nonmonotonic meaning [77]. ASP has become a practical problem-solving paradigm with efficient solvers [78].
  • Truth maintenance systems record why each belief is held, so that when a premise is withdrawn every belief that depended on it can be retracted. Doyle's justification-based TMS and de Kleer's assumption-based TMS are the classic designs [79,80].

Exercises for Section 9

9.1Find all extensions of W = {a} with normal defaults a : b / b, b : c / c and a : ¬c / ¬c.

Solution

Two extensions. If a : ¬c / ¬c fires first, ¬c blocks b : c / c, giving Th({a, b, ¬c}). Otherwise a : b / b gives b, then b : c / c gives c, which blocks a : ¬c / ¬c, giving Th({a, b, c}). Calling extensions({'a'}, [], [('a','b'), ('b','c'), ('a','-c')]) returns both: [['a','b','c'], ['-c','a','b']]. Note that b is in both, so a skeptical reasoner concludes b but neither c nor ¬c.

9.2Show that the naive CWA can make a consistent knowledge base inconsistent, using KB = {P(a) ∨ P(b)}.

Solution

KB entails neither P(a) nor P(b), so the CWA adds ¬P(a) and ¬P(b). Together with P(a) ∨ P(b) this is inconsistent. Reiter showed that the CWA is consistent for Horn databases but not in general, which motivated refinements such as the generalised CWA and circumscription [29,74].

10Commonsense reasoning

McCarthy's 1959 paper proposed that "a program has common sense if it automatically deduces for itself a sufficiently wide class of immediate consequences of anything it is told and what it already knows" [18]. Sixty years later this remains one of the hardest problems in AI. Davis and Marcus identify several reasons: commonsense knowledge is vast, mostly unstated because everyone knows it, spread over many domains (time, space, physics, intentions, social conventions), and used in plausible rather than strictly valid reasoning [81].

10.1 Reasoning about action and the frame problem

The situation calculus describes a changing world with situations (snapshots), fluents (properties that vary between situations) and actions that map one situation to the next [24]. McCarthy and Hayes immediately ran into the frame problem: besides saying what an action changes, one must also say what it leaves unchanged. Painting a block does not move it, loading a gun does not change its colour, and with m actions and n fluents the number of such "frame axioms" grows as m × n [2,24].

The natural fix is a nonmonotonic law of inertia: fluents persist unless something changes them. Hanks and McDermott showed with the Yale shooting problem that naively minimising change gives the wrong answer [82]. A gun is loaded, the agent waits, then fires at Fred. The intended model has Fred dead. But an equally minimal model has the gun mysteriously unloading during the wait, so Fred survives: each model contains exactly one "abnormal" change. Solutions that prefer later changes, or that minimise only unexplained changes, recover the intended result [82,83].

The event calculus of Kowalski and Sergot handles inertia directly: a fluent holds at time t if an event initiated it earlier and no event has terminated ("clipped") it since [84]. Mueller develops it into a full framework for commonsense reasoning [83]. The program below implements the core axiom for the Yale scenario.

Listing: s9_ec.pyPython 3 · standard library
# A tiny discrete event calculus: fluents persist (inertia) unless an event
# terminates them. This is the commonsense "law of inertia" that solves the
# frame problem for this domain without writing one frame axiom per fluent.
INITIATES = {('load', 'loaded'), ('wake', 'awake')}
TERMINATES = {('shoot', 'alive', 'loaded'),       # (event, fluent, precondition)
              ('shoot', 'loaded', None), ('sleep', 'awake', None)}

def holds_at(fluent, t, initially, narrative):
    """HoldsAt(f, t): f held initially or was initiated, and was not clipped since."""
    state = set(initially)
    for time in range(t):
        for ev in narrative.get(time, []):
            ends = {f for e, f, pre in TERMINATES if e == ev and (pre is None or pre in state)}
            starts = {f for e, f in INITIATES if e == ev}
            state = (state - ends) | starts
    return fluent in state

# Yale shooting scenario (Hanks & McDermott, 1987): load, wait, shoot.
narrative = {0: ['load'], 1: ['wait'], 2: ['shoot']}
for t in range(4):
    print(t, {f: holds_at(f, t, {'alive'}, narrative) for f in ('alive', 'loaded')})
0 {'alive': True, 'loaded': False}
1 {'alive': True, 'loaded': True}
2 {'alive': True, 'loaded': True}
3 {'alive': False, 'loaded': False}

10.2 Building commonsense knowledge bases

Hayes's "naive physics manifesto" called for axiomatising everyday knowledge of liquids, solids, space and time at the level people use it, rather than at the level of physics textbooks [85]. The largest attempt to do this by hand is Cyc, begun by Lenat in 1984: a very large knowledge base of assertions in a higher-order logical language, organised into "microtheories" that allow locally consistent but globally conflicting assumptions [32]. A different approach crowdsources knowledge in looser form: ConceptNet is a multilingual graph of everyday relations such as UsedFor, CapableOf and AtLocation [86], and ATOMIC collects if–then knowledge about everyday events and people's intentions and reactions [87]. COMET then trained a transformer language model on such graphs to generate new commonsense inferences for unseen events [88].

10.3 Measuring common sense

Levesque, Davis and Morgenstern proposed the Winograd Schema Challenge as an alternative to the Turing test: pairs of sentences that differ in one word, where resolving a pronoun requires commonsense knowledge [89]. In "The trophy doesn't fit in the brown suitcase because it is too big", it is the trophy; change big to small and it becomes the suitcase. The schemas are built so that word statistics alone should not answer them. Large language models now score highly on such benchmarks, but Davis and Marcus's broader concern still applies: high benchmark scores do not show that a system has a reliable, inspectable model of the world, which is what symbolic commonsense representations aim to provide [38,81].

Exercises for Section 10

10.1Modify the event-calculus narrative so that the agent also performs shoot at time 0, before loading. Predict the output, then explain why the precondition on TERMINATES matters.

Solution

With {0: ['shoot', 'load'], 1: ['wait'], 2: ['shoot']}, the first shot does nothing to alive because loaded does not hold at time 0, so the output is unchanged: alive through t = 2, dead at t = 3. Without the precondition, shooting an unloaded gun would kill, which is why effect axioms in the event calculus are conditional on fluents holding when the event happens [83,84].

10.2For the trophy-and-suitcase schema, state the commonsense knowledge needed to resolve it in each variant.

Solution

For X to fit inside Y, X must be smaller than Y. "Doesn't fit because it is too big": the explanation must name the object whose size is the problem for an object being put inside, which is the trophy. "Too small" picks out the container, the suitcase. The reasoner needs a containment relation, a size comparison, and the knowledge that a "because" clause explains the failure [89].

11Knowledge acquisition and inference engines

11.1 The knowledge acquisition bottleneck

The expert-systems era showed that the hardest part of building a knowledge-based system was not the inference engine but getting the knowledge in. Feigenbaum called this the knowledge acquisition bottleneck: the slow, error-prone process by which a knowledge engineer interviews an expert and turns their answers into rules [57,59]. Experts often cannot articulate what they know, and what they say is not always what they do.

Responses to the bottleneck fall into four groups:

  • Interactive tools that let experts edit knowledge directly. Davis's TEIRESIAS let a physician watch MYCIN fail on a case, trace the faulty conclusion back to a missing or wrong rule, and enter a correction, checked against the model of what rules usually look like [90]. Protégé, developed at Stanford from the 1980s, became the most widely used ontology editor [91].
  • Methodologies. CommonKADS treats knowledge engineering as model construction, separating the task, the domain knowledge and the inference structure [41,92].
  • Incremental, case-driven acquisition. Ripple-down rules let an expert correct one misclassified case at a time, adding an exception rule only in the context where the error occurred, so the knowledge base is never restructured [93].
  • Machine learning and extraction. Induction (Section 8) learns rules from data; ILP learns first-order theories [68]; DBpedia and NELL extract facts from text and the web at scale [48,94]; and language models are now used to propose candidate triples for knowledge graphs [38,88].

11.2 Inference engines

An inference engine is the general procedure that applies stored knowledge to a problem. The engines in this article fall into a few families:

11.3 Production-system engines and conflict resolution

A forward-chaining production system runs a recognise–act cycle: match all rules against working memory to form the conflict set; select one instantiation using a conflict-resolution strategy; act by executing its action; repeat [2,56]. Common strategies are refraction (do not fire the same instantiation twice), recency (prefer rules matching the newest facts) and specificity (prefer rules with more conditions), which lets a specific exception rule pre-empt a general one.

Listing: s10_engine.pyPython 3 · standard library
# A forward-chaining production system: recognise-act cycle with an agenda,
# refraction, and conflict resolution by specificity then recency.
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Rule:
    name: str
    conditions: frozenset          # all must be in working memory
    action: str                    # fact to assert
    blockers: frozenset = frozenset()  # negated conditions: must be absent

RULES = [
    Rule('engine-cranks', frozenset({'key_turned'}), 'cranks',
         blockers=frozenset({'battery_flat'})),
    Rule('no-crank', frozenset({'key_turned', 'lights_dim'}), 'battery_flat'),
    Rule('needs-fuel', frozenset({'cranks', 'fuel_gauge_empty'}), 'refuel'),
    Rule('starts', frozenset({'cranks'}), 'engine_runs',
         blockers=frozenset({'fuel_gauge_empty', 'battery_flat'})),
    Rule('jump-start', frozenset({'battery_flat'}), 'advise_jump_start'),
]

def run(wm, rules, trace=True):
    wm = {f: i for i, f in enumerate(wm)}     # fact -> time stamp (for recency)
    fired, clock = set(), len(wm)
    while True:
        # MATCH: build the conflict set of satisfied, not-yet-fired instantiations
        agenda = [r for r in rules
                  if r.conditions <= wm.keys() and not (r.blockers & wm.keys())
                  and r.name not in fired and r.action not in wm]
        if not agenda:
            return wm
        # SELECT: most specific rule first, then the one matching the newest fact
        agenda.sort(key=lambda r: (len(r.conditions) + len(r.blockers),
                                   max(wm[c] for c in r.conditions)), reverse=True)
        rule = agenda[0]
        # ACT
        fired.add(rule.name)
        wm[rule.action] = clock
        clock += 1
        if trace:
            print(f"fire {rule.name:14s} => {rule.action}   "
                  f"(conflict set: {[r.name for r in agenda]})")

run(['key_turned', 'lights_dim'], RULES)
fire no-crank       => battery_flat   (conflict set: ['no-crank', 'engine-cranks'])
fire jump-start     => advise_jump_start   (conflict set: ['jump-start'])

Both no-crank and engine-cranks match at the start and tie on specificity (two tests each, counting the negated condition). Recency breaks the tie in favour of no-crank, which matches the newer fact lights_dim. Its conclusion, battery_flat, then blocks engine-cranks, so the system advises a jump start instead of predicting that the engine runs.

The Rete algorithm

Naive matching re-tests every rule against every fact on every cycle. Forgy observed that working memory changes only slightly between cycles, and designed the Rete network to exploit this [95]. Rules are compiled into a dataflow network: alpha nodes test single facts against individual conditions, and beta (join) nodes combine partial matches across conditions while checking that shared variables agree. Each node stores the partial matches that pass it, so when a fact is added or removed only the affected tokens flow through the network. Conditions shared by several rules share nodes. The cost of each cycle therefore depends on how much working memory changed, not on its total size [2,95]. Rete was developed for the OPS family of languages, in which R1/XCON was written [27,95].

Exercises for Section 11

11.1A help-desk system has 2,000 rules and a working memory of 50,000 facts about a site's equipment, and it is asked one question at a time ("why is printer P7 offline?"). Should its engine forward-chain or backward-chain? Why?

Solution

Backward chaining. Each query concerns one goal, and forward chaining would derive conclusions about all 50,000 facts, nearly all irrelevant. Backward chaining explores only the rules whose conclusions bear on the question. Forward chaining suits monitoring tasks, where new data arrives continuously and every consequence may matter [2].

11.2Run the production system with initial working memory ['key_turned'] only. Which rules fire, in what order, and what is the final working memory?

Solution

engine-cranks fires (cranks), then starts (engine_runs), because neither blocker is present. needs-fuel never matches. The final working memory is ['cranks', 'engine_runs', 'key_turned'], which is what the code returns.

12Synthesis

The formalisms in this article are answers to the same question, made at different points on the expressiveness–tractability curve and for different users.

FormalismMain strengthMain limitationTypical reasoning
Propositional logicSimple; mature SAT solversNo objects or quantifiersModel checking, DPLL, resolution
First-order logicGeneral, precise semanticsSemi-decidableResolution, unification
Semantic networks / framesIntuitive, object-centred, fast inheritanceInformal semantics; ad hoc defaultsInheritance, demons
Production rulesModular, explainable, easy to authorRule interaction; brittle at edgesForward/backward chaining, Rete
Description logics / OWLDecidable, standardised, automatic classificationLimited rules; open-world surprisesTableau, consequence-based
Knowledge graphsScale, integration, simple data modelIncomplete; shallow semanticsMaterialisation, queries, embeddings
Default logic, ASP, circumscriptionDefeasible conclusions and exceptionsMultiple extensions; high complexityFixed points, stable models

Three themes recur. First, semantics matters: semantic networks were displaced by description logics not because the pictures were wrong but because the links had no agreed meaning [31,52]. Second, knowledge is the bottleneck: the engines have been good enough for decades, and the open problem is acquiring, maintaining and checking large bodies of knowledge [59,81]. Third, symbolic and statistical methods are converging: knowledge graphs are completed with embeddings, commonsense graphs train neural models, and language models are grounded and checked against curated knowledge [38,50,88]. For further study, Brachman and Levesque [1] is the standard KR&R textbook, Russell and Norvig [2] places it within AI as a whole, and the Description Logic Handbook [7] is the reference on ontology reasoning.
