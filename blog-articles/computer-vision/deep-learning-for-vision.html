Deep Learning for Vision

From the perceptron to the vision transformer: how neural networks learn from pixels, why convolutions and attention work, and how to build, train and shrink image models that hold up in practice.

21 sections in four parts24 executed NumPy listings + a PyTorch recipe67 exercises with solutions193 Vancouver references

A photograph is a grid of numbers. A 224 × 224 colour image holds 150,528 of them, and nothing in those numbers says "dog" or "stop sign". Deep learning closes that gap by stacking simple, differentiable functions and adjusting millions of their parameters by gradient descent until the stack maps pixels to the answers we want [1,2]. Two families of architectures dominate image work. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) build in the assumption that useful patterns are local and can appear anywhere [3]; vision transformers treat an image as a set of patches and let every patch exchange information with every other [4]. Either way, what the model learns depends as much on the data, the loss and the optimiser as on the architecture.

The article has four parts that build on each other. Part I covers the neural-network machinery shared by every model. Part II covers convolutional networks. Part III covers vision transformers and their hybrids with CNNs. Part IV covers training and deployment: data, losses, imbalance, schedules and compression.

Part I · Neural network foundations

§1Perceptrons, multilayer networks and activation functions

The perceptron

Rosenblatt's perceptron [9] is a single unit that computes a weighted sum of its inputs and thresholds it: ŷ = sign(w·x + b). Geometrically, w·x + b = 0 is a hyperplane, and the unit reports which side of it an input lies on. The learning rule is equally simple. Visit the training examples one at a time; whenever an example (x, y) with y ∈ {−1, +1} is misclassified, move the hyperplane towards it with w ← w + η·y·x, b ← b + η·y. If the data can be separated by some hyperplane with margin γ and all inputs lie within radius R of the origin, the rule makes at most (R/γ)² mistakes before it stops [10].

The catch is the word separable. No single hyperplane separates the XOR pattern (true when exactly one input is on), and Minsky and Papert's analysis of what single-layer perceptrons cannot compute cooled interest in the approach for years [11]. Listing 1 reproduces both halves of the story: the rule converges on AND and cycles forever on XOR, while two layers of the same threshold units solve XOR by computing OR and AND and combining them.

Listing 1. The perceptron learning rule on AND and XOR, and a hand-wired two-layer network.Python · NumPy · executed
import numpy as np

def train_perceptron(X, y, epochs=20, lr=1.0):
    """Rosenblatt's learning rule. Labels y are in {-1, +1}."""
    w, b = np.zeros(X.shape[1]), 0.0
    for epoch in range(epochs):
        mistakes = 0
        for xi, yi in zip(X, y):
            if yi * (xi @ w + b) <= 0:          # wrong side of (or on) the boundary
                w += lr * yi * xi
                b += lr * yi
                mistakes += 1
        if mistakes == 0:
            return w, b, epoch + 1               # converged
    return w, b, None                            # never separated the data

X = np.array([[0, 0], [0, 1], [1, 0], [1, 1]], dtype=float)
for name, y in {"AND": [-1, -1, -1, 1], "XOR": [-1, 1, 1, -1]}.items():
    w, b, epochs = train_perceptron(X, np.array(y, dtype=float))
    pred = np.sign(X @ w + b).astype(int)
    print(f"{name}: w={w}, b={b:+.0f}, epochs to converge={epochs}, predictions={pred}")

# A two-layer network with hand-set weights solves XOR: h1 = OR, h2 = AND, out = h1 AND NOT h2
step = lambda z: (z > 0).astype(int)
W1 = np.array([[1, 1], [1, 1]]); b1 = np.array([-0.5, -1.5])
w2 = np.array([1, -2]);           b2 = -0.5
print("2-layer XOR:", step(step(X @ W1 + b1) @ w2 + b2))

On XOR the weights return to zero after every epoch: the four updates cancel exactly, so the predictions are sign(0) = 0 everywhere. A two-layer network has no such limitation, but its hand-set weights raise the real question of the next section: how do we learn the weights of hidden units when no label says what they should output?

Multilayer networks

A multilayer perceptron (MLP) composes affine maps with element-wise non-linearities:

h⁽¹⁾ = φ(W⁽¹⁾x + b⁽¹⁾), h⁽ˡ⁾ = φ(W⁽ˡ⁾h⁽ˡ⁻¹⁾ + b⁽ˡ⁾), ŷ = W⁽ᴸ⁾h⁽ᴸ⁻¹⁾ + b⁽ᴸ⁾

The non-linearity φ is essential: without it the composition of affine maps is itself affine, and depth buys nothing. With it, a single hidden layer of sufficient width can approximate any continuous function on a compact set to arbitrary accuracy [12,13]. The universal-approximation theorems say nothing about how many units are needed or whether gradient descent will find the weights; in practice deeper networks represent many functions of interest far more efficiently than shallow ones [1], which is why image models have tens to hundreds of layers.

Activation functions

The choice of φ shapes how gradients flow. The logistic sigmoid σ(z) = 1/(1+e−z) and tanh were standard until around 2010, but both saturate: for large |z| their derivatives approach zero. Backpropagation multiplies one such derivative per layer, so gradients reaching early layers can shrink exponentially with depth, the vanishing-gradient problem [14,15]. The rectified linear unit, ReLU(z) = max(0, z), has derivative exactly 1 for z > 0, does not saturate on the positive side, is cheap to compute, and made deep supervised networks markedly easier to train [16–18]. Its weakness is that a unit whose input is always negative receives no gradient and can "die"; leaky ReLU keeps a small slope (e.g. 0.01) for z < 0. Transformers and many recent CNNs use smooth variants: GELU, z·Φ(z) with Φ the standard normal CDF [19], and SiLU/Swish, z·σ(z) [20].

Exercises
  1. Show that a perceptron cannot represent XOR: assume weights w₁, w₂, b exist and derive a contradiction from the four required inequalities.
    Solution

    We need b ≤ 0 (input 00 → negative), w₂ + b > 0 (01 → positive), w₁ + b > 0 (10 → positive) and w₁ + w₂ + b ≤ 0 (11 → negative). Adding the middle two gives w₁ + w₂ + 2b > 0, so w₁ + w₂ + b > −b ≥ 0, contradicting the last inequality.

  2. Why does stacking linear layers without activations not increase expressive power? What does a 10-layer linear network compute?
    Solution

    W₂(W₁x + b₁) + b₂ = (W₂W₁)x + (W₂b₁ + b₂), which is again affine. Any depth collapses to a single affine map Ax + c. (Deep linear networks still have non-convex training dynamics, which makes them a useful object of theory, but they cannot represent more functions.)

  3. With He initialisation, what standard deviation do the weights of a 3 × 3 convolution with 64 input channels get? Why is the fan-in 576 and not 64?
    Solution

    Each output value is a weighted sum over 3·3·64 = 576 inputs, so nin = 576 and σ = √(2/576) ≈ 0.059. The fan-in counts every input that feeds one output, spatial taps included.

  4. A ReLU network is a piecewise-linear function of its input. Explain why, and say what determines the "pieces".
    Solution

    For a fixed input, each ReLU is either active (identity) or inactive (zero), so the network reduces to a product of masked affine maps, which is affine. The set of inputs that produce the same on/off pattern is a convex polytope (an intersection of half-spaces defined by the pre-activations), and the network is affine on each such region.

§2Forward propagation and backpropagation

Training needs the gradient of a scalar loss with respect to every parameter. Backpropagation computes all of them in one backward sweep whose cost is a small constant multiple of the forward pass [22]. It is reverse-mode automatic differentiation applied to the network's computation graph [23]: the forward pass stores the intermediate values each layer needs, and the backward pass applies the chain rule from the loss back to the input, one layer at a time.

The three rules you need

For a mini-batch stored as rows of X, a linear layer computes Z = XW + b. Given the upstream gradient G = ∂L/∂Z, matrix calculus gives:

∂L/∂W = Xᵀ G ∂L/∂b = Σ_rows G ∂L/∂X = G Wᵀ

An element-wise activation multiplies the upstream gradient by its derivative (for ReLU, by the 0/1 mask of positive inputs). And for softmax followed by cross-entropy, the combined gradient with respect to the logits has the remarkably simple form p − onehot(y), where p is the softmax output [1]. These three rules are the whole of Listing 3, a 70-line framework whose layers each implement forward and backward. Numerical stability matters even here: the softmax subtracts the row maximum before exponentiating, which leaves the result unchanged mathematically but prevents overflow [24].

Listing 3. A minimal deep-learning library: layers with forward/backward methods and a stable softmax cross-entropy.Python · NumPy · executed
import numpy as np

class Layer:
    training = True
    def params(self):  return []          # list of (parameter array, gradient array) pairs
    def forward(self, x):  raise NotImplementedError
    def backward(self, g): raise NotImplementedError   # g = dLoss/dOutput; returns dLoss/dInput

class Linear(Layer):
    def __init__(self, n_in, n_out, rng):
        self.W = rng.normal(0.0, np.sqrt(2.0 / n_in), (n_in, n_out))   # He initialisation
        self.b = np.zeros(n_out)
        self.dW, self.db = np.zeros_like(self.W), np.zeros_like(self.b)
    def forward(self, x):
        self.x = x                                # cache the input for the backward pass
        return x @ self.W + self.b
    def backward(self, g):
        self.dW[...] = self.x.T @ g               # dL/dW = x^T g
        self.db[...] = g.sum(axis=0)              # dL/db = sum over the batch
        return g @ self.W.T                       # dL/dx = g W^T
    def params(self):
        return [(self.W, self.dW), (self.b, self.db)]

class ReLU(Layer):
    def forward(self, x):
        self.mask = x > 0
        return x * self.mask
    def backward(self, g):
        return g * self.mask

class Sequential(Layer):
    def __init__(self, *layers): self.layers = list(layers)
    def forward(self, x):
        for layer in self.layers: x = layer.forward(x)
        return x
    def backward(self, g):
        for layer in reversed(self.layers): g = layer.backward(g)
        return g
    def params(self):
        return [p for layer in self.layers for p in layer.params()]
    def train(self, mode=True):
        for layer in self.layers: layer.training = mode
        return self

def softmax(z):
    z = z - z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)          # shift for numerical stability
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def cross_entropy(logits, y):
    """Mean softmax cross-entropy and its gradient with respect to the logits."""
    n = len(y)
    z = logits - logits.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    log_p = z - np.log(np.exp(z).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))   # log-softmax
    loss = -log_p[np.arange(n), y].mean()
    grad = np.exp(log_p)
    grad[np.arange(n), y] -= 1.0                  # dL/dz = softmax(z) - onehot(y)
    return loss, grad / n

Checking gradients

Hand-written backward passes are easy to get subtly wrong, and a wrong gradient often still trains, just badly. The standard check compares each analytic derivative with a central finite difference, (L(θ+ε) − L(θ−ε))/2ε, and reports the relative error. Values around 10−7 in double precision indicate a correct implementation; values above about 10−3 almost always indicate a bug [1].

Listing 4. Gradient checking by central finite differences.Python · NumPy · executed

def grad_check(model, x, y, eps=1e-6, n_checks=20, seed=0):
    """Compare backprop gradients with central finite differences."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    loss, g = cross_entropy(model.forward(x), y)
    model.backward(g)
    worst = 0.0
    for p, dp in model.params():
        analytic = dp.copy()
        for _ in range(n_checks // 2):
            i = tuple(rng.integers(s) for s in p.shape)
            old = p[i]
            p[i] = old + eps; lp, _ = cross_entropy(model.forward(x), y)
            p[i] = old - eps; lm, _ = cross_entropy(model.forward(x), y)
            p[i] = old
            numeric = (lp - lm) / (2 * eps)
            rel = abs(numeric - analytic[i]) / max(1e-12, abs(numeric) + abs(analytic[i]))
            worst = max(worst, rel)
    return worst

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    net = Sequential(Linear(64, 32, rng), ReLU(), Linear(32, 10, rng))
    x, y = rng.normal(size=(8, 64)), rng.integers(0, 10, size=8)
    print(f"max relative error, backprop vs finite differences: {grad_check(net, x, y):.2e}")

Training end to end

Listing 5 loads the digits dataset (1,797 grey-scale images of 8 × 8 pixels, ten classes [6]), splits it 60/20/20 into training, validation and test sets with stratification so every split has the same class proportions, and trains a 64–128–10 MLP with plain mini-batch stochastic gradient descent. The fit function is the training loop every later listing reuses: shuffle, slice a mini-batch, forward, loss, backward, update.

Listing 5. Data splits, the reusable training loop, and a first MLP on the digits.Python · NumPy · executed
# Uses code from Listing 3 (run them first, or paste them above this one).
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

def digits_splits(seed=0):
    """8x8 handwritten digits (1,797 images): 60% train / 20% validation / 20% test, stratified."""
    d = load_digits()
    X, y = d.images.astype(np.float64) / 16.0, d.target       # pixel values 0..16 -> [0, 1]
    X_tmp, X_te, y_tmp, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=seed)
    X_tr, X_va, y_tr, y_va = train_test_split(X_tmp, y_tmp, test_size=0.25, stratify=y_tmp, random_state=seed)
    return (X_tr, y_tr), (X_va, y_va), (X_te, y_te)

class SGD:
    def __init__(self, params, lr): self.params, self.lr = params, lr
    def step(self):
        for p, g in self.params: p -= self.lr * g

def accuracy(model, X, y):
    model.train(False)
    acc = (model.forward(X).argmax(1) == y).mean()
    model.train(True)
    return acc

def fit(model, opt, X, y, epochs=30, batch=32, seed=0, loss_fn=cross_entropy, schedule=None, augment=None):
    """Mini-batch training loop. Returns per-epoch mean training loss."""
    rng, history, step = np.random.default_rng(seed), [], 0
    for epoch in range(epochs):
        order, losses = rng.permutation(len(X)), []
        for i in range(0, len(X), batch):
            idx = order[i:i + batch]
            xb = X[idx] if augment is None else augment(X[idx], rng)
            if schedule is not None: opt.lr = schedule(step)
            loss, g = loss_fn(model.forward(xb), y[idx])   # forward pass + loss
            model.backward(g)                               # backward pass fills .grad arrays
            opt.step()                                      # gradient-based update
            losses.append(loss); step += 1
        history.append(float(np.mean(losses)))
    return history

if __name__ == "__main__":
    (X_tr, y_tr), (X_va, y_va), (X_te, y_te) = digits_splits()
    flat = lambda X: X.reshape(len(X), -1)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    mlp = Sequential(Linear(64, 128, rng), ReLU(), Linear(128, 10, rng))
    hist = fit(mlp, SGD(mlp.params(), lr=0.1), flat(X_tr), y_tr, epochs=30)
    print(f"train/val/test sizes: {len(X_tr)}/{len(X_va)}/{len(X_te)}")
    print(f"loss: epoch 1 {hist[0]:.3f} -> epoch 30 {hist[-1]:.4f}")
    print(f"accuracy  train {accuracy(mlp, flat(X_tr), y_tr):.3f}  val {accuracy(mlp, flat(X_va), y_va):.3f}"
          f"  test {accuracy(mlp, flat(X_te), y_te):.3f}")

Exercises
  1. Derive ∂L/∂z for L = −log softmax(z)y and confirm it equals p − onehot(y).
    Solution

    L = −zy + log Σk ezk. Then ∂L/∂zj = −[j = y] + ezj/Σkezk = pj − [j = y]. With a mean over a batch of n examples, each row is divided by n, as in cross_entropy.

  2. Add a Tanh layer to Listing 3 and verify it with Listing 4. What must forward cache?
    Solution
    class Tanh(Layer):
    def forward(self, x):
        self.y = np.tanh(x)            # cache the output: d tanh/dx = 1 - tanh(x)^2
        return self.y
    def backward(self, g):
        return g * (1 - self.y ** 2)

    Replacing ReLU() with Tanh() in the gradient-check network should give a relative error of the same order as ReLU's; anything above about 10−5 points to a bug.

  3. Why is reverse mode (backpropagation) preferred to forward-mode differentiation for training networks?
    Solution

    Forward mode computes the derivative of every output with respect to one input direction per pass, so a network with P parameters would need P passes. Reverse mode computes the derivative of one scalar output (the loss) with respect to all inputs in a single backward pass. Training has one scalar loss and millions of parameters, so reverse mode wins by a factor of roughly P [23]. The price is memory: activations must be stored for the backward pass.

  4. The gradient check uses ε = 10−6. What goes wrong with ε = 10−12, and with ε = 10−1?
    Solution

    With ε = 10−12 the difference L(θ+ε) − L(θ−ε) is dominated by floating-point rounding (relative precision about 10−16 on a loss of order 1), so the estimate is noise. With ε = 10−1 the truncation error of the central difference, O(ε²·L‴), is large, and for ReLU networks a step that size often crosses a kink, giving a wrong slope.

§3Loss functions and gradient-based optimisation

A loss function turns "how wrong is the model?" into a differentiable number. For classification, the standard choice is the cross-entropy between the one-hot label and the softmax output, which is the negative log-likelihood of the correct class under the model [1]. Regression targets such as box coordinates typically use L2, L1 or the Huber loss. Part IV returns to vision-specific losses (§17); this section is about minimising whatever loss we choose.

Stochastic gradient descent

The empirical risk is an average over N training examples, so its gradient is too. Computing it exactly costs a full pass over the data per step. Stochastic gradient descent replaces it with the average over a random mini-batch of B examples, an unbiased but noisy estimate, and takes many cheap steps instead [25,26]:

θ ← θ − η · (1/B) Σ_{i∈batch} ∇_θ ℓ(f_θ(xᵢ), yᵢ)

The learning rate η is the most important hyperparameter in deep learning. Too small and progress is slow; too large and the loss oscillates or diverges. Listing 6's first two rows show the effect: after 20 epochs, η = 0.01 leaves the training loss at 0.66, while η = 0.1 reaches 0.10.

Momentum and adaptive methods

Momentum keeps an exponentially decaying sum of past gradients and steps along it, which damps oscillation across steep, narrow valleys and accelerates progress along shallow directions [27,28]. Nesterov momentum evaluates the correction at the look-ahead point, a modification with better theoretical rates on convex problems [28,29]. Adam keeps running estimates of each parameter's gradient mean m and uncentred variance v, corrects both for their zero initialisation, and scales each step by m̂/(√v̂ + ε), so every parameter gets its own effective learning rate [30]. AdamW applies weight decay directly to the weights instead of adding it to the gradient, because in Adam an L2 term in the gradient is rescaled by 1/√v̂ and no longer acts like decay [31].

# Uses code from Listing 5 (run them first, or paste them above this one).
class Momentum:
    """Heavy-ball momentum (PyTorch convention): v <- mu*v + g ; p <- p - lr*v."""
    def __init__(self, params, lr, mu=0.9, nesterov=False):
        self.params, self.lr, self.mu, self.nesterov = params, lr, mu, nesterov
        self.v = [np.zeros_like(p) for p, _ in params]
    def step(self):
        for (p, g), v in zip(self.params, self.v):
            v *= self.mu; v += g
            p -= self.lr * (g + self.mu * v if self.nesterov else v)

class Adam:
    """Adam with bias correction; weight_decay > 0 gives decoupled decay (AdamW)."""
    def __init__(self, params, lr=1e-3, betas=(0.9, 0.999), eps=1e-8, weight_decay=0.0):
        self.params, self.lr, (self.b1, self.b2), self.eps, self.wd = params, lr, betas, eps, weight_decay
        self.m = [np.zeros_like(p) for p, _ in params]
        self.v = [np.zeros_like(p) for p, _ in params]
        self.t = 0
    def step(self):
        self.t += 1
        for (p, g), m, v in zip(self.params, self.m, self.v):
            m *= self.b1; m += (1 - self.b1) * g             # first moment (mean of g)
            v *= self.b2; v += (1 - self.b2) * g * g         # second moment (uncentred variance)
            m_hat = m / (1 - self.b1 ** self.t)              # bias correction for the zero init
            v_hat = v / (1 - self.b2 ** self.t)
            if self.wd: p -= self.lr * self.wd * p           # AdamW: decay applied outside the adaptive scaling
            p -= self.lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + self.eps)

Exercises
  1. For the quadratic L(θ) = ½λθ², for which learning rates does plain gradient descent converge? What does this say about loss surfaces with very different curvatures in different directions?
    Solution

    The update is θ ← (1 − ηλ)θ, which converges if |1 − ηλ| < 1, i.e. 0 < η < 2/λ. With curvatures λmax ≫ λmin, η must stay below 2/λmax for stability, so progress along the flat direction, at rate (1 − ηλmin) per step, is slow. Momentum and adaptive per-parameter scaling both address this ill-conditioning.

  2. Adam's bias correction divides m by (1 − β₁ᵗ). What is the size of the very first Adam step for a parameter, regardless of the gradient's magnitude?
    Solution

    At t = 1, m̂ = g and v̂ = g², so the step is η·g/(|g| + ε) ≈ η·sign(g). Every parameter moves by about η on the first step, which is why Adam is relatively insensitive to gradient scale and why a warm-up is often used with it (§19).

  3. Explain why L2 regularisation and weight decay are equivalent for SGD but not for Adam.
    Solution

    For SGD, adding (λ/2)‖θ‖² to the loss adds λθ to the gradient, and the update θ ← θ − η(g + λθ) = (1 − ηλ)θ − ηg is exactly multiplicative decay. In Adam the λθ term enters m and v and is divided by √v̂, so parameters with large historical gradients are decayed less. AdamW restores uniform decay by subtracting ηλθ outside the adaptive scaling [31].

§4Regularisation, dropout and normalisation

Deep networks have enough capacity to memorise their training sets, including sets with randomly shuffled labels [33]. Regularisation is anything that biases training towards solutions that generalise. The main tools for vision are weight decay, dropout, data augmentation (§5), early stopping on the validation loss, and, as a side effect, normalisation layers.

Weight decay

Weight decay shrinks every weight towards zero at each step, which penalises large weights and is equivalent to an L2 penalty for SGD [34]. Typical values are 10−4 to 5 × 10−4 for CNNs trained with SGD and 0.05 to 0.3 for ViTs trained with AdamW [35,36]. It is common to exempt biases and normalisation parameters from decay.

Dropout

Dropout zeroes each activation independently with probability p during training [37]. A network cannot rely on any single unit, which discourages co-adapted features; the procedure can also be read as training an exponential ensemble of thinned networks that share weights. The "inverted" form used by all modern frameworks divides the surviving activations by 1 − p at training time, so the expected activation is unchanged and inference uses the full network without rescaling. In convolutional layers, dropping individual pixels of a feature map is weak because neighbouring pixels are highly correlated; DropBlock drops contiguous regions instead [38], and stochastic depth drops entire residual blocks [39], which is the default regulariser in modern ViT and ConvNeXt recipes [35,40].

Normalisation layers

Batch normalisation (BN) standardises each channel using the mean and variance of the current mini-batch, then applies a learnable scale γ and shift β [41]. It allows much higher learning rates and made very deep CNNs practical. Its original motivation, reducing "internal covariate shift", has been questioned; later analysis attributes much of its benefit to a smoother loss landscape [42]. At inference BN uses running averages of the statistics collected during training, so its behaviour differs between modes, a frequent source of bugs (forgetting model.eval()) and of trouble with very small batches. Layer normalisation computes statistics over the features of each example instead, so it is independent of the batch; it is the standard in transformers [43,44]. Group normalisation normalises over groups of channels within each example and works well for detection and segmentation, where per-GPU batches are often only one or two images [45].

class Dropout(Layer):
    """Inverted dropout: scale kept units by 1/(1-p) at train time so inference needs no change."""
    def __init__(self, p, seed=0): self.p, self.rng = p, np.random.default_rng(seed)
    def forward(self, x):
        if not self.training or self.p == 0: return x
        self.mask = (self.rng.random(x.shape) >= self.p) / (1 - self.p)
        return x * self.mask
    def backward(self, g):
        return g * self.mask if self.training and self.p > 0 else g

class BatchNorm(Layer):
    """Batch normalisation over axis 0 (features last). Running stats are used at inference."""
    def __init__(self, n, momentum=0.1, eps=1e-5):
        self.gamma, self.beta = np.ones(n), np.zeros(n)
        self.dgamma, self.dbeta = np.zeros(n), np.zeros(n)
        self.run_mean, self.run_var, self.mom, self.eps = np.zeros(n), np.ones(n), momentum, eps
    def forward(self, x):
        if self.training:
            mu, var = x.mean(0), x.var(0)
            self.run_mean = (1 - self.mom) * self.run_mean + self.mom * mu
            self.run_var  = (1 - self.mom) * self.run_var  + self.mom * var * len(x) / (len(x) - 1)
        else:
            mu, var = self.run_mean, self.run_var
        self.inv_std = 1.0 / np.sqrt(var + self.eps)
        self.xhat = (x - mu) * self.inv_std
        return self.gamma * self.xhat + self.beta
    def backward(self, g):
        self.dgamma[...] = (g * self.xhat).sum(0)
        self.dbeta[...] = g.sum(0)
        gx = g * self.gamma
        # gradient through the batch mean and variance (all samples interact)
        return self.inv_std * (gx - gx.mean(0) - self.xhat * (gx * self.xhat).mean(0))
    def params(self):
        return [(self.gamma, self.dgamma), (self.beta, self.dbeta)]

class SGDW(SGD):
    """SGD with momentum and L2 weight decay added to the gradient (applied to all parameters for brevity)."""
    def __init__(self, params, lr, mu=0.9, weight_decay=0.0):
        super().__init__(params, lr); self.mu, self.wd = mu, weight_decay
        self.v = [np.zeros_like(p) for p, _ in params]
    def step(self):
        for (p, g), v in zip(self.params, self.v):
            v *= self.mu; v += g + self.wd * p
            p -= self.lr * v

Exercises
  1. Why does inverted dropout divide by 1 − p during training, and what would go wrong at test time if it did not?
    Solution

    With keep probability 1 − p, the expected value of a dropped-out activation is (1 − p)·a. Dividing by 1 − p restores E[output] = a, so the next layer sees inputs of the same scale at training and test time. Without the rescaling, test-time activations (no dropout) would be 1/(1 − p) times larger than anything seen in training; the original paper avoided this by scaling the weights by 1 − p at test time instead [37].

  2. A detection model is fine-tuned with 2 images per GPU and batch norm. Validation accuracy is poor and erratic. Give two explanations and two fixes.
    Solution

    Batch statistics from two images are very noisy, so training-mode normalisation is noisy, and the running averages used at test time poorly match what the network saw while training. Fixes: freeze BN (use the pre-trained statistics and affine parameters as constants), use synchronised BN across GPUs to enlarge the effective batch, or replace BN with group normalisation [45].

  3. Batch norm's backward pass in Listing 7 contains the terms gx.mean(0) and xhat * (gx * xhat).mean(0). What would be wrong with returning just g * gamma * inv_std?
    Solution

    That expression treats the batch mean and variance as constants. They are functions of every example in the batch, so changing one input shifts the normalisation of all the others. The two extra terms are the gradient flowing through μ and σ²; without them the gradient check fails, and the update ignores the fact that adding a constant to all inputs of a channel has no effect on the output.

§5Data augmentation, transfer learning and fine-tuning

Data augmentation

Data augmentation generates new training examples by transforming existing ones with operations that should not change the label [48]. It is the most direct way to tell a model which variations it must ignore. The classic ImageNet recipe used random crops and horizontal flips, plus colour perturbations [18]. Later methods widened the menu: Cutout erases random squares [49]; mixup trains on convex combinations of two images and of their labels [50]; CutMix pastes a patch of one image into another and mixes the labels in proportion to area [51]; AutoAugment searches for augmentation policies [52], and RandAugment reduces that search to two hyperparameters, the number and magnitude of random operations [53]; AugMix mixes chains of augmentations to improve robustness to corruptions [54]. Strong augmentation is a large part of why ViTs can be trained on ImageNet-1k alone [35,36].

The rule that matters most is that augmentations must preserve the label for your task. Horizontal flips are harmless for ImageNet but change the meaning of text, of left and right in medical images, and of some traffic signs. Rotations by 180° turn a 6 into a 9.

Transfer learning and fine-tuning

Features learned on a large dataset are often useful for a different task. Early layers of ImageNet CNNs learn generic edge and colour detectors, and features become more task-specific with depth [55]. Three ways to reuse a pre-trained network are common:

  • Linear probe (feature extraction). Freeze the backbone and train only a new classifier on its features. Cheap, and robust when labels are very scarce.
  • Full fine-tuning. Initialise from the pre-trained weights and train everything, usually with a learning rate 10–100× smaller than from scratch, often with a larger rate for the new head. ImageNet models that are more accurate tend to transfer better when fine-tuned [56].
  • Linear probe, then fine-tune. Fitting the head first avoids large random-head gradients distorting the pre-trained features, which can improve out-of-distribution accuracy [57]. Parameter-efficient variants such as LoRA train small low-rank updates instead of all weights [58].

Transfer is not guaranteed to help. With enough target data and training time, models trained from scratch can match ImageNet-pre-trained ones on COCO detection; pre-training mainly speeds convergence and helps most when target data is scarce [59]. Large pre-training corpora and self-supervised backbones have since made frozen features remarkably strong [60,61].

Part II · Convolutional neural networks

§6Convolutional layers, pooling and receptive fields

An MLP treats an image as an unordered vector: shift the picture one pixel and every input to the first layer changes. It is also wasteful. Mapping a 224 × 224 × 3 image to 64 feature maps of the same size with a fully connected layer would need about 4.8 × 1011 weights. Convolutional layers rest on two assumptions about images: useful patterns are local (an edge depends on a few neighbouring pixels), and they are stationary (an edge detector useful in one corner is useful everywhere). So each output unit connects only to a small window of the input, and every position uses the same weights. The same mapping then needs 3 × 3 × 3 × 64 = 1,728 weights plus 64 biases [1,3,62]. The architecture descends from Fukushima's neocognitron [63], itself inspired by Hubel and Wiesel's simple and complex cells in the visual cortex [64].

The operation

A convolutional layer with Cin input channels and Cout output channels holds Cout kernels of shape Cin × k × k. Each output value is a dot product between one kernel and the k × k × Cin input window beneath it, plus a bias:

y[o, i, j] = b[o] + Σ_c Σ_u Σ_v W[o, c, u, v] · x[c, i·s + u·d − p, j·s + v·d − p]

Here s is the stride, p the zero padding and d the dilation (§9). Deep-learning libraries actually compute this cross-correlation, without flipping the kernel; since the kernel is learned, the distinction does not matter. The spatial output size along each axis is [65]:

H_out = ⌊ (H + 2p − d·(k − 1) − 1) / s ⌋ + 1

Weight sharing makes convolution translation-equivariant: shifting the input shifts the feature map by the same amount (up to border effects and stride). A 1 × 1 convolution is a per-pixel fully connected layer across channels and is used everywhere to change channel counts cheaply [66].

        self.b = np.zeros(c_out) if bias else None
        self.k, self.s, self.p, self.d, self.g = k, stride, padding, dilation, groups
        self.dW = np.zeros_like(self.W); self.db = None if self.b is None else np.zeros_like(self.b)

    def _cols(self, xp, Ho, Wo):
        k, s, d = self.k, self.s, self.d
        # cols[n, c, u, v, i, j] = xp[n, c, i*s + u*d, j*s + v*d]
        return np.stack([np.stack([xp[:, :, u*d: u*d + s*(Ho-1) + 1: s, v*d: v*d + s*(Wo-1) + 1: s]
                                   for v in range(k)], 2) for u in range(k)], 2)

    def forward(self, x):
        N, C, H, W_ = x.shape
        k, s, p, d, G = self.k, self.s, self.p, self.d, self.g
        Ho = (H + 2*p - d*(k-1) - 1) // s + 1
        Wo = (W_ + 2*p - d*(k-1) - 1) // s + 1
        self.x_shape = x.shape
        xp = np.pad(x, ((0, 0), (0, 0), (p, p), (p, p)))
        cols = self._cols(xp, Ho, Wo).reshape(N, G, C // G, k, k, Ho, Wo)
        Wg = self.W.reshape(G, -1, C // G, k, k)
        out = np.einsum("ngcuvhw,gocuv->ngohw", cols, Wg, optimize=True).reshape(N, -1, Ho, Wo)
        self.cols, self.out_hw = cols, (Ho, Wo)
        return out if self.b is None else out + self.b[None, :, None, None]

    def backward(self, g):
        N, C, H, W_ = self.x_shape
        k, s, p, d, G = self.k, self.s, self.p, self.d, self.g
        Ho, Wo = self.out_hw
        gg = g.reshape(N, G, -1, Ho, Wo)
        self.dW[...] = np.einsum("ngohw,ngcuvhw->gocuv", gg, self.cols, optimize=True).reshape(self.W.shape)
        if self.b is not None: self.db[...] = g.sum((0, 2, 3))
        dcols = np.einsum("ngohw,gocuv->ngcuvhw", gg, self.W.reshape(G, -1, C // G, k, k), optimize=True)
        dcols = dcols.reshape(N, C, k, k, Ho, Wo)
        dxp = np.zeros((N, C, H + 2*p, W_ + 2*p))
        for u in range(k):                                   # scatter-add each kernel tap back ("col2im")
            for v in range(k):
                dxp[:, :, u*d: u*d + s*(Ho-1) + 1: s, v*d: v*d + s*(Wo-1) + 1: s] += dcols[:, :, u, v]
        return dxp[:, :, p: p + H, p: p + W_]

    def params(self):
        return [(self.W, self.dW)] + ([] if self.b is None else [(self.b, self.db)])

class MaxPool2d(Layer):
    """Non-overlapping k x k max pooling (stride = k); H and W must be divisible by k."""
    def __init__(self, k=2): self.k = k
    def forward(self, x):
        N, C, H, W = x.shape; k = self.k
        xr = x.reshape(N, C, H // k, k, W // k, k)
        out = xr.max(axis=(3, 5))
        self.mask = xr == out[:, :, :, None, :, None]       # route gradient to the arg-max
        return out
    def backward(self, g):
        N, C, Ho, Wo = g.shape; k = self.k
        return (self.mask * g[:, :, :, None, :, None]).reshape(N, C, Ho * k, Wo * k)

class GlobalAvgPool(Layer):
    def forward(self, x): self.shape = x.shape; return x.mean(axis=(2, 3))
    def backward(self, g):
        N, C, H, W = self.shape
        return np.broadcast_to(g[:, :, None, None] / (H * W), self.shape).copy()

class Flatten(Layer):
    def forward(self, x): self.shape = x.shape; return x.reshape(len(x), -1)
    def backward(self, g): return g.reshape(self.shape)

Pooling

Pooling layers summarise a neighbourhood with a fixed function, usually the maximum or the mean, and are usually applied with stride 2 to halve the resolution. They add a small amount of local translation invariance and enlarge the receptive field of later layers at no parameter cost. Global average pooling averages each channel over all positions, turning a C × H × W map into a C-vector; it replaced the large fully connected heads of early CNNs and makes the network accept any input size [66,67]. Many modern networks downsample with stride-2 convolutions instead of pooling.

Receptive fields

The receptive field of a unit is the region of the input that can affect it. For a chain of layers with kernel sizes kl, strides sl and dilations dl, it obeys a simple recursion: with jl the product of all strides so far, rl = rl−1 + dl(kl − 1)·jl−1 [68]. Strides early in the network therefore multiply the growth contributed by every later layer.

def receptive_field(layers):
    """layers: list of (name, kernel, stride, dilation). Returns rows of
    (name, receptive field r, cumulative stride j) using r_l = r_{l-1} + (k_eff - 1) * j_{l-1}."""
    r, j, rows = 1, 1, []
    for name, k, s, d in layers:
        k_eff = d * (k - 1) + 1                 # a dilated kernel spans d*(k-1)+1 input positions
        r += (k_eff - 1) * j
        j *= s
        rows.append((name, r, j))
    return rows

Exercises
  1. A 512 × 512 input passes through a 7 × 7 convolution with stride 2 and padding 3, then a 3 × 3 max-pool with stride 2 and padding 1. What is the output size?
    Solution

    Convolution: ⌊(512 + 6 − 6 − 1)/2⌋ + 1 = ⌊511/2⌋ + 1 = 256. Pooling: ⌊(256 + 2 − 2 − 1)/2⌋ + 1 = 128. This is the ResNet stem, which reduces resolution by 4 before the first residual stage.

  2. Count the parameters and multiply–accumulate operations (MACs) of a 3 × 3 convolution from 128 to 256 channels on a 56 × 56 output, with bias.
    Solution

    Parameters: 3·3·128·256 + 256 = 295,168. MACs: one 1,152-term dot product per output value, and 256·56·56 = 802,816 output values, so 1,152 × 802,816 ≈ 0.92 GMACs (bias additions are usually ignored).

  3. Is a CNN with global average pooling translation-invariant? Consider stride and padding.
    Solution

    Not exactly. Stride and pooling subsample, so a one-pixel shift of the input generally does not correspond to a shift of the strided feature maps, and aliasing can change the output substantially [70]. Zero padding also leaks absolute position. Global average pooling makes the final representation invariant to shifts of the last feature map, but that holds only for shifts by multiples of the total stride (32 for ResNet-50). Blurring before subsampling (anti-aliasing) restores much of the invariance [71].

  4. Use the receptive-field recursion to show that n stacked 3 × 3 convolutions with stride 1 have receptive field 2n + 1.
    Solution

    With all strides 1, jl = 1, so each layer adds k − 1 = 2: rn = 1 + 2n.

§7Feature maps and hierarchical feature extraction

Each output channel of a convolutional layer is a feature map: the response of one learned filter at every position. A CNN alternates convolutions with downsampling, so as depth increases the spatial resolution falls, the number of channels rises, and each unit's receptive field widens. The result is a hierarchy. Visualisations of trained ImageNet networks show first-layer filters that resemble oriented edge and colour-opponent detectors, middle layers that respond to textures and repeated patterns, and deep layers that respond to object parts and whole objects [72,73]. Network dissection quantified this by matching units to labelled concepts and found that object and part detectors become more common with depth [74]. The same ordering, from generic to specific, is what makes early layers transferable (§5) [55].

Exercises
  1. In Listing 11, the first convolution has 80 parameters and the second 1,168. Verify these numbers and explain why later layers dominate the parameter count in most CNNs.
    Solution

    Conv 1: 8 × (1·3·3) + 8 = 80. Conv 2: 16 × (8·3·3) + 16 = 1,168. Parameters scale with Cin·Cout, and channel counts usually double each time the resolution halves, so the deepest stages hold most of the weights even though they process the fewest positions. Compute, which scales with H·W·Cin·Cout, stays roughly constant per stage under the halve-resolution, double-channels rule.

  2. Replace Flatten(), Linear(64, 10) in Listing 11 with GlobalAvgPool(), Linear(16, 10). What do you expect for clean accuracy and for shifted accuracy?
    Solution

    Global pooling discards where in the 2 × 2 map a feature fired, so the head is invariant to shifts of that map; shifted accuracy should rise towards clean accuracy. But on 8 × 8 digits position carries useful information (a stroke at the top versus the bottom distinguishes 7 from L-shaped 4s), and the head has far fewer inputs, so clean accuracy may drop slightly.   3. Feature visualisations show what maximally activates a unit. Why can they be misleading as explanations of a unit's role?
    Solution

    A unit may respond to several unrelated patterns (polysemanticity), and the maximising input can be far from natural images. Combining optimisation-based visualisations with dataset examples across the activation range, as Olah and colleagues recommend, gives a fuller picture [73].

VGG: depth with small kernels

VGG networks use only 3 × 3 convolutions, in stages separated by 2 × 2 max-pooling, with channel counts 64–128–256–512–512 [77]. Two stacked 3 × 3 layers have a 5 × 5 receptive field and three have 7 × 7, but with fewer parameters (27C² instead of 49C² for the 7 × 7 case) and more non-linearities. The VGG team's ILSVRC 2014 entry, an ensemble, reached 7.3% top-5 error, and VGG-16 remained a popular backbone for its simplicity, but 124 M of its 138 M parameters sit in the three fully connected layers.

ResNet: learning residuals

He and colleagues observed a degradation problem: deeper plain networks had higher training error than shallower ones (a 56-layer plain network on CIFAR-10 trained worse than a 20-layer one), so the issue was optimisation, not overfitting [67]. Their fix lets each block learn a residual: y = x + F(x). If the identity is close to optimal, the block only needs to push F towards zero, and gradients reach early layers through the identity path unchanged, since ∂y/∂x = I + ∂F/∂x [81]. A 152-layer ResNet ensemble won ILSVRC 2015 with 3.57% top-5 error [67]. ResNet-50 and deeper use a bottleneck block: a 1 × 1 convolution reduces channels by 4, a 3 × 3 convolution works in the narrow space, and a 1 × 1 convolution expands back.

class Residual(Layer):
    """y = x + F(x). The identity path carries the gradient straight through: dL/dx = g + F'(x)^T g."""
    def __init__(self, f): self.f = f
    def forward(self, x): return x + self.f.forward(x)
    def backward(self, g): return g + self.f.backward(g)
    def params(self): return self.f.params()

def deep_mlp(n_blocks, residual, rng, width=64):
    blocks = []
    for _ in range(n_blocks):                    # each block = two Linear layers
        if residual:
            last = Linear(width, width, rng); last.W[...] = 0     # zero-init: every block starts as identity
            blocks.append(Residual(Sequential(Linear(width, width, rng), ReLU(), last)))
        else:
            blocks += [Linear(width, width, rng), ReLU(), Linear(width, width, rng), ReLU()]
    return Sequential(Linear(64, width, rng), ReLU(), *blocks, Linear(width, 10, rng))

MobileNet: cheap convolutions

MobileNets target phones and embedded devices. MobileNetV1 replaces each standard convolution with a depthwise separable one (§9), cutting computation by roughly 8–9× for 3 × 3 kernels, and adds width and resolution multipliers to trade accuracy for speed [83]. MobileNetV2 introduces the inverted residual with linear bottleneck shown in Figure 9 [78]. MobileNetV3 was found partly by hardware-aware neural architecture search, adds squeeze-and-excitation channel attention [84] and uses the cheaper hard-swish activation [79,85].

EfficientNet: balanced scaling

Networks can be made larger in three ways: deeper (more layers), wider (more channels) or at higher input resolution. Tan and Le showed that scaling any single dimension saturates quickly, and proposed compound scaling: depth, width and resolution are multiplied by αφ, βφ and γφ for a single coefficient φ, with α·β²·γ² ≈ 2 so that FLOPs roughly double per unit of φ. A grid search on the baseline gave α = 1.2, β = 1.1, γ = 1.15 [80]. The baseline, EfficientNet-B0, was itself found by architecture search and is built from MBConv (inverted residual) blocks with squeeze-and-excitation. FLOPs are not latency, however: depthwise convolutions have low arithmetic intensity on GPUs, and EfficientNetV2 replaced some of them with fused regular convolutions to train faster [86].

§9Dilated and depthwise-separable convolutions

Dilated (atrous) convolution

A dilated convolution with rate d spaces its kernel taps d pixels apart, so a 3 × 3 kernel covers a (2d + 1) × (2d + 1) window with the same nine weights. Stacking layers with exponentially increasing dilation grows the receptive field exponentially while keeping full resolution, which is what dense-prediction tasks such as semantic segmentation need [88]. DeepLab used atrous convolution to remove the last downsampling steps of a classification backbone and added atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP), parallel branches with different rates, to capture context at several scales [89,90].

Dilation has a pitfall. Stacking layers with the same rate samples the input on a sparse lattice, the gridding effect: a unit never sees most of the pixels inside its receptive field. Choosing rates without a common factor, such as 1, 2, 3, fills the gaps [91]. Listing 14 shows both cases.

Depthwise-separable convolution

A standard convolution filters spatially and mixes channels in one step. A depthwise-separable convolution factorises the two [83,92,93]: a depthwise k × k convolution applies one filter per input channel (a grouped convolution with groups = Cin), then a pointwise 1 × 1 convolution mixes channels. For Cin = M, Cout = N and kernel size k, the cost ratio relative to a standard convolution is [83]:

(M·k²·H·W + M·N·H·W) / (M·N·k²·H·W) = 1/N + 1/k²

§10Feature pyramids and multiscale architectures

Objects in images appear at very different sizes. A pedestrian may span 20 pixels or 500. Classical vision handled this with image pyramids, processing the image at several resolutions [94]. That works for CNNs too, but multiplies the cost. A CNN already computes a pyramid internally, its stages at strides 4, 8, 16 and 32, but the levels are semantically unequal: early high-resolution maps are good at localisation but carry weak semantics, and deep low-resolution maps the reverse. SSD made predictions from several backbone levels directly [95]; the high-resolution levels then lacked the semantics to recognise small objects well.

The Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) fixes this with a top-down pathway [96]. Starting from the coarsest map, it repeatedly upsamples by 2 (nearest neighbour) and adds a 1 × 1-projected copy of the backbone map at the same resolution (the lateral connection), then smooths each sum with a 3 × 3 convolution. Every output level P2–P5 then has the same channel count (256 in the paper) and strong semantics, and detectors assign objects to levels according to their size. Variants add a further bottom-up path (PANet) [97] or repeated, learned-weight bidirectional fusion (BiFPN in EfficientDet) [98]. Segmentation networks follow the same principle: U-Net's decoder upsamples and concatenates encoder features at each scale [99], and HRNet keeps a high-resolution branch throughout and exchanges information across parallel resolutions [100].

def upsample2x(x):
    return x.repeat(2, axis=2).repeat(2, axis=3)               # nearest-neighbour

class FPN:
    """Top-down pathway with lateral 1x1 connections (Lin et al., 2017).
    Inputs: backbone maps C2..C5 at strides 4, 8, 16, 32. Outputs: P2..P5, all with `d` channels."""
    def __init__(self, in_channels, d, rng):
        self.lateral = [Conv2d(c, d, 1, rng) for c in in_channels]
        self.smooth = [Conv2d(d, d, 3, rng, padding=1) for _ in in_channels]
    def forward(self, feats):
        lat = [l.forward(f) for l, f in zip(self.lateral, feats)]
        outs = [lat[-1]]                                           # start from the coarsest level
        for f in reversed(lat[:-1]):
            outs.insert(0, f + upsample2x(outs[0]))                # semantic top-down + spatial lateral
        return [s.forward(o) for s, o in zip(self.smooth, outs)]   # 3x3 reduces upsampling aliasing

  1. Why does FPN add the lateral and top-down maps while U-Net concatenates encoder and decoder features? Name one consequence of each choice.
    Solution

    Addition requires equal channel counts (hence the 1 × 1 lateral projections) and keeps every level at d channels, which lets one detection head be shared across levels. Part III · Vision transformers

§11Image patches and embeddings

The transformer was designed for sequences of word tokens [44]. Its central operation, self-attention, compares every token with every other, so its cost grows with the square of the sequence length. A 224 × 224 image has 50,176 pixels; attention over individual pixels would need about 2.5 × 109 pairwise scores per head and layer. The Vision Transformer (ViT) sidesteps this by cutting the image into non-overlapping P × P patches and treating each patch as a token [4]. With P = 16, a 224 × 224 image becomes a 14 × 14 grid of 196 tokens.

Each patch is flattened to a vector of P²·C = 768 values (for RGB) and mapped by a learned linear layer to the model width D. This patch embedding is mathematically identical to a convolution with kernel size and stride both equal to P, which is how libraries implement it. ViT then prepends a learnable [class] token, whose final state feeds the classifier, and adds a position embedding to every token (§12) [4]. Many later models drop the class token and average-pool the output tokens instead.

§12Self-attention and positional encodings

Scaled dot-product attention

Self-attention lets each token gather information from all others, weighted by relevance. Each token's vector x is projected to a query q = xWQ, a key k = xWK and a value v = xWV. The output for token i is a weighted average of all values, with weights given by a softmax over the similarities between its query and every key [44]:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q Kᵀ / √d_k ) V

Dividing by √dk keeps the dot products at unit scale: for random vectors with unit-variance entries, q·k has variance dk, and large logits would push the softmax into saturation, where its gradients vanish. Multi-head attention runs h attentions in parallel on D/h-dimensional slices and concatenates the results, so different heads can attend to different relations at the same cost as one full-width head. A transformer block combines attention with a per-token MLP (expansion ratio 4 in ViT), each wrapped in a residual connection with LayerNorm.

Why positions must be added

Attention treats its input as a set: permuting the tokens permutes the outputs in the same way and changes nothing else. Without extra information, a ViT cannot tell a patch in the top-left corner from one in the bottom-right. Position information is injected in several ways:

  • Fixed sinusoids of geometrically spaced frequencies, added to the tokens [44]. Their 2-D version, half the channels encoding the row and half the column, is used by MoCo v3 and MAE [102,103].
  • Learned absolute embeddings, one vector per position, as in the original ViT. Its authors found that 2-D-aware variants gave no significant improvement over learned 1-D embeddings, because the model learns the 2-D structure itself [4].
  • Relative position biases add a learned term to each attention logit that depends only on the offset between the two tokens [104]. Swin uses one per head, indexed by the 2-D offset inside a window [105].
  • Rotary embeddings (RoPE) rotate pairs of query and key channels by angles proportional to position, so that their dot product depends only on the relative offset [106]. 2-D variants for ViTs extrapolate well to resolutions not seen in training [107].

def multi_head_self_attention(x, Wqkv, Wo, n_heads, bias=None):
    """x: (N, T, D). Wqkv: (D, 3D). Wo: (D, D). bias: optional (heads, T, T) added to the logits."""
    N, T, D = x.shape
    dh = D // n_heads
    q, k, v = np.split(x @ Wqkv, 3, axis=-1)
    split = lambda t: t.reshape(N, T, n_heads, dh).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3)       # (N, h, T, dh)
    q, k, v = split(q), split(k), split(v)
    logits = q @ k.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2) / np.sqrt(dh)                         # (N, h, T, T)
    if bias is not None: logits = logits + bias
    A = softmax(logits.reshape(-1, T)).reshape(N, n_heads, T, T)              # rows sum to 1
    out = (A @ v).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(N, T, D)                      # concatenate heads
    return out @ Wo, A

def sincos_2d(h, w, D, base=10000.0):
    """Fixed 2-D sine-cosine position embedding: half the channels encode the row, half the column."""
    def sincos_1d(pos, d):
        omega = 1.0 / base ** (np.arange(d // 2) / (d // 2))
        ang = pos[:, None] * omega[None]
        return np.concatenate([np.sin(ang), np.cos(ang)], axis=1)
    rows, cols = np.meshgrid(np.arange(h), np.arange(w), indexing="ij")
    return np.concatenate([sincos_1d(rows.ravel(), D // 2), sincos_1d(cols.ravel(), D // 2)], axis=1)

def rope(x, pos, base=10000.0):
    """Rotary position embedding for 1-D positions: rotate channel pairs by angle pos * omega_i."""
    d = x.shape[-1]
    omega = 1.0 / base ** (np.arange(d // 2) / (d // 2))
    ang = pos[:, None] * omega
    x1, x2 = x[..., 0::2], x[..., 1::2]
    out = np.empty_like(x)
    out[..., 0::2] = x1 * np.cos(ang) - x2 * np.sin(ang)
    out[..., 1::2] = x1 * np.sin(ang) + x2 * np.cos(ang)
    return out

    QKV: 768·2,304 + 2,304 = 1,771,776. Output projection: 768·768 + 768 = 590,592. MLP: 768·3,072 + 3,072 + 3,072·768 + 768 = 4,722,432. Two LayerNorms: 4·768 = 3,072. Total 7,087,872. Twelve blocks give 85.05 M; adding the 742,656 embedding parameters from Listing 16, a final LayerNorm (1,536) and the 1,000-class head (769,000) gives 86,567,656, exactly torchvision's count for ViT-B/16 [76].

  2. Show that self-attention without position information is permutation-equivariant: if P is a permutation matrix, Attention(PX) = P·Attention(X).
    Solution

    Q, K and V become PQ, PK and PV. The logits become PQKᵀPᵀ, i.e. rows and columns are permuted together. Row-wise softmax commutes with this, giving P·A·Pᵀ. Multiplying by PV gives P·A·PᵀP·V = P·A·V, since PᵀP = I. Per-token MLPs and LayerNorm are also equivariant, so the whole encoder is.

  3. Why does the RoPE score in Listing 17 depend only on m − n? Use the 2-D rotation R(θ).
    Solution

    Each channel pair of q at position m is rotated by R(mω) and each pair of k at position n by R(nω). Their dot product is qᵀR(mω)ᵀR(nω)k = qᵀR((n − m)ω)k, because rotations compose by adding angles and R(θ)ᵀ = R(−θ). Summing over pairs with different frequencies keeps the dependence on n − m only [106].

§13Vision Transformer (ViT) and Swin Transformer

ViT

The original ViT applies a standard transformer encoder to patch tokens with minimal image-specific design [4]. Its key empirical finding concerned data. Trained on ImageNet-1k alone (1.3 M images), ViTs underperformed ResNets of comparable size; pre-trained on ImageNet-21k (14 M) or the proprietary JFT-300M [108] and then fine-tuned, they matched or exceeded the best CNNs while needing less pre-training compute. The authors' explanation is inductive bias. A CNN builds in locality and translation equivariance; a ViT has to learn them from data, which costs examples but leaves it free to learn global interactions where they help.

Two lines of work then made ViTs practical without huge private datasets. DeiT trained them on ImageNet-1k alone with strong augmentation (RandAugment, mixup, CutMix), stochastic depth, AdamW and long schedules, and added distillation from a CNN teacher through a dedicated token [35]. "How to train your ViT?" showed systematically that augmentation and regularisation can substitute for roughly an order of magnitude more data [36]. At the other extreme, ViTs scale smoothly to billions of parameters when data allows [109]. Analyses of trained models find that ViTs have more uniform representations across layers than ResNets and that some heads attend globally even in early layers [110].

Swin Transformer

A plain ViT keeps one resolution throughout (a 14 × 14 grid for ViT-B/16) and attends globally in every layer. Both are awkward for dense prediction: detectors and segmenters want multiscale feature maps (§10), and global attention at high resolution is expensive (§15). The Swin Transformer addresses both [105]:

  • Hierarchy. It starts from 4 × 4 patches (stride 4) and, between stages, patch merging concatenates each 2 × 2 group of tokens and projects 4C channels to 2C. Stages run at strides 4, 8, 16 and 32, exactly like a ResNet, so Swin can replace a CNN backbone under an FPN.
  • Window attention. Self-attention is computed only within non-overlapping M × M windows (M = 7). Cost becomes linear in the number of tokens.
  • Shifted windows. Alternate blocks shift the window grid by ⌊M/2⌋ tokens, so information crosses window borders. The shift is implemented as a cyclic roll of the feature map plus an attention mask, which keeps the number of windows constant.
  • Relative position bias. Each head adds a learned bias indexed by the relative offset of two tokens inside a window.

Swin-T (C = 96, stage depths 2-2-6-2) has 28 M parameters and needs 4.5 GFLOPs at 224 px, comparable to ResNet-50 and ConvNeXt-T; torchvision's checkpoint reaches 81.5% top-1 [76]. Swin V2 adapted the design for larger models and resolutions [111].

def window_partition(x, M):
    """(H, W, C) -> (num_windows, M*M, C)"""
    H, W, C = x.shape
    return x.reshape(H // M, M, W // M, M, C).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3, 4).reshape(-1, M * M, C)

def shifted_window_mask(H, W, M, s):
    """Attention mask for shifted windows after a cyclic shift by s = M // 2 (Liu et al., 2021).
    Tokens that were not neighbours before the roll get -inf so they cannot attend to each other."""
    region = np.zeros((H, W, 1))
    cnt = 0

    return np.where(same, 0.0, -np.inf)                        # (nW, M*M, M*M)

Convolutions supply locality, translation equivariance and efficiency at high resolution; attention supplies content-dependent, global interactions. Exercises
  1. Why does it make computational sense to put attention in the late, low-resolution stages rather than the early ones?
    Solution

    Global attention costs O(N²) in the number of tokens N. At stride 4 a 224-px image has 3,136 positions; at stride 32 it has 49. The attention-score term is (3,136/49)² = 4,096 times cheaper in the last stage, while convolutions cost O(N) and handle the many early tokens efficiently.

  2. ConvNeXt is a pure CNN. In what sense is it still a "hybrid"?
    Solution

    It contains no attention, but its macro design (patchify stem, stage ratios similar to Swin, inverted bottlenecks, large depthwise kernels, fewer activations and normalisations, LayerNorm, GELU) and its training recipe (AdamW, long schedules, heavy augmentation, stochastic depth) were adopted from vision transformers [40]. It hybridises design knowledge rather than layers.

§15Computational cost and attention scaling

For a ViT with N tokens and width D, each encoder block costs about 12ND² multiply–accumulates (MACs) in its linear layers (4ND² for the Q, K, V and output projections, 8ND² for the MLP with expansion 4) plus 2N²D for the two attention products QKᵀ and A·V. The linear part grows linearly with N, the attention part quadratically. At 224 px, ViT-B/16 has N = 197 and attention is a small share; but N grows with the square of the resolution, so at high resolution attention dominates both compute and memory, since the N × N attention matrix must normally be materialised per head [4,121].

Ways to tame attention

  • Restrict who attends to whom. Local windows (Swin [105], ViTDet [120]), sliding neighbourhoods [119], or attention over spatially reduced keys and values (PVT [118]).
  • Reduce the number of tokens. Larger patches, hierarchical merging, or merging similar tokens on the fly, as in Token Merging, which raised the throughput of large ViTs by about 2× with little loss of accuracy and no retraining [122].
  • Approximate the softmax. Kernel-based linear attention rewrites softmax(QKᵀ)V so that it can be computed in O(N) [121,123], usually at some cost in accuracy.
  • Compute exact attention more cleverly. FlashAttention tiles the computation so the N × N matrix never leaves fast on-chip memory, computing the softmax incrementally. The arithmetic is unchanged, but memory becomes linear in N and wall-clock time drops because memory traffic, not arithmetic, was the bottleneck [124–126]. This is now the default in major frameworks.

A final caution about efficiency numbers: FLOPs, parameter counts and measured throughput frequently disagree, and a model that is cheaper on one is not necessarily faster on another or on different hardware [87]. Always measure latency on the target device (§20).

Exercises
  1. Using the per-block formula, at what token count N does the attention term 2N²D equal the linear term 12ND² for D = 768? What image size is that for ViT-B/16?
    Solution

    2N²D = 12ND² gives N = 6D = 4,608 tokens. With 16-px patches that is √4,608 ≈ 68 patches per side, about 1,086 × 1,086 pixels. Listing 19 shows attention at 47% of block compute at 1,024 px, consistent with this crossover just above that size.

  2. FlashAttention performs the same number of FLOPs as standard attention. Why is it faster?
    Solution

    Standard attention writes the N × N logits and probabilities to GPU main memory (HBM) and reads them back, which for long sequences takes longer than the arithmetic itself. FlashAttention keeps tiles of Q, K and V in on-chip SRAM, computes partial softmaxes with running maxima and sums, and writes only the output, cutting HBM traffic dramatically [124]. For the backward pass it recomputes attention tiles instead of storing them, trading cheap FLOPs for expensive memory traffic.

  3. A segmentation model needs features at stride 4 from 1,024 × 1,024 images. Why is a plain ViT with global attention at that stride impractical, and what would you use instead?
    Solution

    Stride 4 gives 256² = 65,536 tokens; one attention matrix per head then has 4.3 × 10⁹ entries. Use a hierarchical backbone (Swin or a CNN) with an FPN-style decoder, or a plain ViT at stride 16 with windowed attention plus an upsampling pyramid as in ViTDet [105,120].

Part IV · Training and model optimisation

§16Dataset construction, labelling and train/validation/test splits

A model can only be as good as its data, and in applied vision most of the effort, and most of the failures, sit in the dataset. Practitioners consistently under-invest in data work, and data problems compound downstream into what Sambasivan and colleagues call data cascades [127].

Construction

  • Define the label space first. Write down the classes, their boundaries and the hard cases (is a reflection of a car a car? a partially visible one?), and the unit of annotation (image label, box, mask, keypoint). Large benchmarks such as ImageNet and COCO publish their taxonomies and annotation protocols [128,129].
  • Sample the deployment distribution. Cover the cameras, lighting, seasons, geographies and populations the model will meet. A dataset that is convenient to collect is rarely the one the model will see.
  • Document it. A datasheet records why and how the data was collected, consent and licensing, known gaps and recommended uses [130].

Labelling

Annotation is a measurement process with its own errors. Good practice includes written guidelines with visual examples, a qualification task, redundant labels on a subset to measure inter-annotator agreement, hidden gold-standard items to monitor quality, and adjudication of disagreements [131]. Active learning prioritises the unlabelled images on which the current model is most uncertain or most informative, to spend the labelling budget where it helps most [132]. Even curated benchmarks contain errors: an estimated average of at least 3.3% of test labels across ten widely used datasets are wrong, including about 6% of the ImageNet validation set [133], and re-annotation of ImageNet with multiple labels per image changed which models looked best [134]. Confident learning finds likely errors by comparing each given label with out-of-sample predicted probabilities [135].

Splits

The training set fits parameters; the validation set selects hyperparameters, architectures and checkpoints; the test set estimates performance once, after all choices are frozen. Reusing the test set for decisions makes its estimate optimistic. Three properties matter more than the exact ratios:

  • Independence at the right level. If several images share a source (the same patient, video, scene, camera, product or writer), split by source, not by image. Otherwise near-identical frames land on both sides and the test score measures memorisation. This is a form of leakage [136].
  • No duplicates across splits. Near-duplicate images are common in scraped data; the CIFAR test sets, for example, contain near-duplicates of training images [137].
  • Representativeness. Stratify by class (and by other important attributes) so that small splits still contain rare cases, and consider a time-based split when the deployment will see future data. Even a carefully replicated test set can show substantial accuracy drops: ImageNet models lost 11–14 points on the re-collected ImageNetV2 test set [138].

  1. A skin-lesion dataset has 10,000 images from 2,000 patients, several images per lesion. Design the split and say what a random image-level split would overestimate.
    Solution

    Split by patient (e.g. GroupShuffleSplit or StratifiedGroupKFold with patient ID as the group), stratified by diagnosis so rare classes appear in every split, and if possible also check acquisition sites.   2. Why must out-of-fold (cross-validated) predictions be used to flag label errors, rather than the predictions of a model trained on all the data?
    Solution

    A model that has trained on an example has partly memorised its (possibly wrong) label, so it assigns high probability to it and the error is hidden. Out-of-fold predictions come from a model that never saw the example, so they reflect what the rest of the data implies [135].

  3. You tuned 40 hyperparameter configurations on the validation set and the best reached 91.0%. Why is that number optimistic, and what should you report?
    Solution

    Selecting the maximum of 40 noisy estimates biases it upwards (winner's curse): the best configuration was partly lucky on this particular validation sample. Report the selected model's accuracy on the untouched test set, ideally with a confidence interval, and mention the number of configurations tried.

§17Cross-entropy, focal loss, contrastive loss and regression losses

Cross-entropy and label smoothing

Cross-entropy is the default for classification (§3). Label smoothing replaces the one-hot target with (1 − ε)·onehot + ε/K, which stops the model driving logits towards infinity to reach probability 1 [139]. It usually improves accuracy and calibration slightly, but it erases information about which wrong classes are similar, so teachers trained with label smoothing make worse distillation teachers [140]. Multi-label tasks (several labels may be true) use an independent sigmoid with binary cross-entropy per class instead of a softmax.

Focal loss

One-stage detectors classify on the order of 100,000 candidate locations per image, almost all of them easy background. Their many small losses swamp the few informative ones. Focal loss multiplies binary cross-entropy by (1 − pt)γ, where pt is the predicted probability of the true class, so well-classified examples contribute almost nothing [141]:

FL(p_t) = −α_t · (1 − p_t)^γ · log(p_t) (γ = 2, α = 0.25 in RetinaNet)

Contrastive losses

Contrastive losses learn an embedding in which similar items are close and dissimilar ones far apart. The original pairwise form pulls positive pairs together and pushes negative pairs apart until they exceed a margin [142]; the triplet loss compares an anchor with one positive and one negative, as in FaceNet [143]. Modern self-supervised and multimodal learning mostly uses InfoNCE: treat the matching pair as the correct class among a batch of candidates and apply cross-entropy to similarities divided by a temperature τ [144]. SimCLR uses two augmented views of each image as the positive pair and the rest of the batch as negatives [145]; MoCo keeps a queue of negatives from a slowly updated momentum encoder [146]; supervised contrastive learning treats all same-class images as positives [147]; CLIP applies the same symmetric loss to image–text pairs [148].

Regression losses

For coordinates and other continuous targets, L2 loss penalises large errors heavily and is sensitive to outliers; L1 is robust but has a constant gradient that does not shrink near the optimum. The Huber loss [149], introduced to detection as "smooth L1" [150], is quadratic for small errors and linear for large ones. For bounding boxes, losses that act on the overlap directly align training with the evaluation metric. Plain 1 − IoU gives no gradient when boxes do not overlap; Generalised IoU subtracts the fraction of the smallest enclosing box not covered by the union, so it still reports how far apart disjoint boxes are [151], and Distance-IoU adds a centre-distance term for faster convergence [152]. For segmentation masks, the Dice loss optimises overlap and is robust to the heavy foreground–background imbalance of small structures [153].

    p_t = np.where(targets == 1, p, 1 - p)
    alpha_t = np.where(targets == 1, alpha, 1 - alpha)
    log_pt = -np.logaddexp(0, -np.where(targets == 1, logits, -logits))   # stable log(p_t)
    return -(alpha_t * (1 - p_t) ** gamma * log_pt)

def info_nce(z1, z2, tau=0.1):
    """Symmetric InfoNCE / NT-Xent-style loss: row i of z1 should match row i of z2 among all rows."""
    z1 = z1 / np.linalg.norm(z1, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    z2 = z2 / np.linalg.norm(z2, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    sim = z1 @ z2.T / tau                                   # (B, B) cosine similarities / temperature
    idx = np.arange(len(z1))
    return -(log_softmax(sim)[idx, idx].mean() + log_softmax(sim.T)[idx, idx].mean()) / 2

def smooth_l1(pred, target, beta=1.0):
    d = np.abs(pred - target)
    return np.where(d < beta, 0.5 * d ** 2 / beta, d - 0.5 * beta)

def giou(a, b):
    """Generalised IoU for boxes (x1, y1, x2, y2) (Rezatofighi et al., 2019). Loss = 1 - GIoU."""
    iw = max(0.0, min(a[2], b[2]) - max(a[0], b[0])); ih = max(0.0, min(a[3], b[3]) - max(a[1], b[1]))
    inter = iw * ih
    area = lambda r: (r[2] - r[0]) * (r[3] - r[1])
    union = area(a) + area(b) - inter
    hull = (max(a[2], b[2]) - min(a[0], b[0])) * (max(a[3], b[3]) - min(a[1], b[1]))
    return inter / union, inter / union - (hull - union) / hull

    Solution

    As τ → 0 the softmax becomes a hard maximum: the loss focuses entirely on the hardest negative, which is unstable and sensitive to false negatives. Practical values (0.05–0.2 in SimCLR-style training, or learned, as in CLIP) trade off hard-negative emphasis against stability [145,148].

§18Class imbalance and hard-example mining

Real data is rarely balanced. Natural images follow long-tailed distributions (LVIS has over 1,000 categories, many with only a handful of training examples [154]), and defect, disease and anomaly detection often have positive rates below 1%. A classifier trained by plain empirical risk minimisation on such data is biased towards frequent classes, and overall accuracy hides poor performance on rare ones; report per-class recall, balanced accuracy or precision–recall instead.

Remedies

  • Re-sampling. Oversample rare classes (or undersample frequent ones). In a systematic study on CNNs, oversampling was the most consistently effective remedy and did not cause the overfitting it causes in classical models [155]. LVIS introduced repeat-factor sampling, which repeats images containing rare categories [154].
  • Re-weighting. Weight each class's loss by inverse frequency or by the inverse "effective number of samples", (1 − βⁿ)/(1 − β), which accounts for diminishing returns from near-duplicate examples [156].
  • Margins and logit adjustment. LDAM enforces larger margins for rare classes [157]. Logit adjustment subtracts τ·log(prior) from the logits, either after training or inside the loss, which is consistent for minimising balanced error [158].
  • Decoupling. Learn the representation with ordinary sampling, then re-balance only the classifier [159].

Hard-example mining

A related problem is that most examples are easy. Classical detectors trained on a bootstrapped set of negatives that the current model got wrong [160,161]. Online hard example mining (OHEM) does this within each mini-batch: compute the loss on all candidates, then back-propagate only the highest-loss ones [162]. Focal loss (§17) is the soft version, down-weighting easy examples instead of discarding them [141].

def weighted_ce(class_w):
    """Cross-entropy where each example is weighted by the weight of its true class."""
    def loss_fn(logits, y):
        n = len(y)
        z = logits - logits.max(1, keepdims=True)
        log_p = z - np.log(np.exp(z).sum(1, keepdims=True))
        w = class_w[y]
        grad = np.exp(log_p); grad[np.arange(n), y] -= 1
        return -(w * log_p[np.arange(n), y]).sum() / w.sum(), grad * w[:, None] / w.sum()
    return loss_fn

def ohem_ce(keep=0.25):
    """Online hard example mining: back-propagate only the highest-loss fraction of each batch."""
    def loss_fn(logits, y):
        n = len(y)
        z = logits - logits.max(1, keepdims=True)
        log_p = z - np.log(np.exp(z).sum(1, keepdims=True))
        per_ex = -log_p[np.arange(n), y]
        hard = np.argsort(per_ex)[-max(1, int(keep * n)):]
        grad = np.zeros_like(logits)
        grad[hard] = np.exp(log_p[hard]); grad[hard, y[hard]] -= 1
        return per_ex[hard].mean(), grad / len(hard)
    return loss_fn

§19Learning-rate schedules and optimisers

A constant learning rate is rarely best. Large steps early make fast progress and help escape poor regions; small steps late let the parameters settle into a minimum. Common schedules are:

  • Step decay. Divide the rate by 10 at fixed epochs; the original ResNet recipe divided it when the error plateaued [67].
  • Cosine annealing. Decay smoothly along half a cosine to (nearly) zero, optionally with warm restarts [163]. Standard for ViTs and modern CNN recipes [35,46].
  • Warm-up. Ramp the rate up linearly over the first few epochs. Goyal and colleagues combined a 5-epoch warm-up with the linear scaling rule (multiply the rate by k when the batch grows by k) to train ResNet-50 with batches of 8,192 images in one hour without loss of accuracy [82]. Adam-family optimisers and transformers are particularly sensitive to large early updates and almost always use warm-up.
  • Cyclical and one-cycle. Vary the rate between bounds; the one-cycle policy rises once to a high peak and then anneals far below the start, enabling fast "super-convergence" in some settings [164,165].

§20Quantisation, pruning, distillation and inference optimisation

A trained model is usually larger and slower than deployment allows. Four families of techniques reduce cost, and they combine.

Quantisation

Quantisation stores weights, and often activations, with fewer bits. Uniform affine quantisation maps a real value x to an integer q = clip(round(x/s) + z, qmin, qmax) with scale s and zero point z, and approximates x ≈ s(q − z) [170,171]. Weights are usually quantised symmetrically (z = 0) and per output channel, because channels' ranges differ; activations usually per tensor, with ranges estimated on a small calibration set. Eight-bit post-training quantisation typically loses little accuracy for CNNs, cuts model size by 4× and enables integer arithmetic [171,172]. At 4 bits and below, quantisation-aware training, which simulates rounding in the forward pass and passes gradients straight through it [173], or smarter rounding such as AdaRound [174], is usually needed [175]. FP8 formats offer a floating-point alternative on recent accelerators [176].

Pruning

Pruning removes weights or whole structures. The idea is old: Optimal Brain Damage removed weights according to a second-order estimate of their importance [177]. Magnitude pruning, removing the smallest weights and fine-tuning the rest, can remove most weights of large CNNs without loss of accuracy [178,179], and pruning gradually during training works better than pruning once [180]. Unstructured sparsity reduces storage but only speeds up inference with sparse kernels or hardware support, such as the 2:4 pattern (two zeros in every four weights) accelerated by NVIDIA Ampere GPUs [181]. Structured pruning removes whole filters or channels and yields a smaller dense network that is faster everywhere [182]. The lottery-ticket hypothesis observes that dense networks contain sparse subnetworks that train well from their original initialisation [183]. A survey of the field cautions that many pruning papers are hard to compare because of inconsistent baselines and metrics [184].

Knowledge distillation

Distillation trains a small student to imitate a large teacher [185,186]. The student matches the teacher's softened probabilities, softmax(z/T) with temperature T > 1, which reveal how the teacher ranks the wrong classes ("dark knowledge"); the KL term is multiplied by T² to keep its gradient on the same scale as the hard-label loss. Variants match intermediate features [187] or use a distillation token (DeiT) [35]. Distillation works best with a patient, consistent teacher: long schedules and the same augmented view shown to teacher and student [188].

def quantize(w, bits=8, per_channel=False):
    """Symmetric uniform quantisation: q = clip(round(w / s), -(2^(b-1)-1), 2^(b-1)-1); returns s * q."""
    qmax = 2 ** (bits - 1) - 1
    absmax = np.abs(w).max(axis=0, keepdims=True) if per_channel else np.abs(w).max()   # per output unit
    s = absmax / qmax
    return np.clip(np.round(w / s), -qmax, qmax) * s

def kd_loss(teacher_logits, labels, T=4.0, alpha=0.9):
    """Hinton et al. (2015): alpha * T^2 * KL(teacher_T || student_T) + (1 - alpha) * CE(labels).
    The 'targets' passed in by fit() are example indices, used to look up labels and teacher logits."""
    def loss_fn(logits, idx):
        pt, ps = softmax(teacher_logits[idx] / T), softmax(logits / T)
        kl = (pt * (np.log(pt + 1e-12) - np.log(ps + 1e-12))).sum(1).mean()
        ce, g_ce = cross_entropy(logits, labels[idx])
        g_kd = T * (ps - pt) / len(idx)                    # gradient of T^2 * KL with respect to the logits
        return alpha * T * T * kl + (1 - alpha) * ce, alpha * g_kd + (1 - alpha) * g_ce
    return loss_fn

    fit(teacher, Adam(teacher.params(), lr=1e-3), Xtr, y_tr, epochs=40)

    n_w = sum(w.size for w in W_fp)
    print(f"teacher fp32: {n_w:,} weights = {4 * n_w / 1024:.0f} KiB, test acc {accuracy(teacher, Xte, y_te):.3f}")

    # ---- 1. Post-training quantisation of weights (and activations) ----
    for bits, pc in [(8, False), (8, True), (4, False), (4, True), (3, False), (3, True)]:
        for l, w in zip(weights, W_fp): l.W[...] = quantize(w, bits, pc)
        print(f"weights int{bits} {'per-channel' if pc else 'per-tensor '}: test acc {accuracy(teacher, Xte, y_te):.3f}"
              f"   size {bits * n_w / 8 / 1024:.0f} KiB")
    for l, w in zip(weights, W_fp): l.W[...] = quantize(w, 8, True)
    a, highs = Xtr[:100], []                                                      # calibration batch
    teacher.train(False)
    for layer in teacher.layers:
        a = layer.forward(a)
        if isinstance(layer, ReLU): highs.append(a.max())                         # observed activation range
    qnet = Sequential(teacher.layers[0], teacher.layers[1], FakeQuantAct(highs[0]),
                      teacher.layers[2], teacher.layers[3], FakeQuantAct(highs[1]), teacher.layers[4])
    print(f"weights int8 per-channel + activations uint8 (100-image calibration): test acc {accuracy(qnet, Xte, y_te):.3f}")
    for l, w in zip(weights, W_fp): l.W[...] = w                                  # restore fp32 weights

    # ---- 2. Global magnitude pruning, with and without fine-tuning ----
    for sparsity in (0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 0.98):
        thresh = np.quantile(np.abs(np.concatenate([w.ravel() for w in W_fp])), sparsity)
        masks = [(np.abs(w) > thresh).astype(float) for w in W_fp]
        for l, w, m in zip(weights, W_fp, masks): l.W[...] = w * m

        fit(teacher, opt, Xtr, y_tr, epochs=5)
        print(f"prune {sparsity:.0%} of weights: test acc {acc0:.3f} before fine-tuning, "
              f"{accuracy(teacher, Xte, y_te):.3f} after 5 epochs")
        for l, w in zip(weights, W_fp): l.W[...] = w

    res = {"student alone": [], "student + distillation": []}

            else:

            res[name].append(accuracy(s, Xte, y_te))

    for name, a in res.items():
        print(f"{name:23s} ({n_s} params): test acc {np.mean(a):.3f} ± {np.std(a):.3f} (5 seeds)")

Inference optimisation

Beyond the model itself, deployment speed depends on how the computation is executed:

  • Graph-level fusion. Fold batch normalisation into the preceding convolution at inference time (W′ = γW/σ, b′ = γ(b − μ)/σ + β), and fuse element-wise operations into neighbouring kernels to save memory traffic.
  • Compilers and runtimes. TVM [189], PyTorch 2's torch.compile [190], ONNX Runtime [191] and TensorRT [192] select kernels, fuse operators and apply quantised arithmetic for the target hardware.
  • System choices. Input resolution (compute scales with its square), batch size (throughput versus latency), memory layout, and exact attention kernels for transformers [124].
  • Measure on the target. FLOPs and parameter counts are proxies; latency, throughput, memory and energy measured on the deployment device are the quantities that matter [87].
Exercises
  1. Derive the batch-norm folding formulas: given y = γ(Wx + b − μ)/√(σ² + ε) + β, find W′ and b′ with y = W′x + b′.
    Solution

    Let s = γ/√(σ² + ε) per output channel. Then y = s·Wx + s(b − μ) + β, so W′ = s·W (scale each output channel's kernel) and b′ = s(b − μ) + β. This is exact at inference, where μ and σ² are fixed running statistics.

  2. A ResNet-50 has 25.6 M parameters. Estimate its size in fp32, int8, and int8 with 50% unstructured sparsity stored as a bitmask.
    Solution

    fp32: 25.6 M × 4 B ≈ 102 MB (97.5 MiB). int8: 25.6 MB. With 50% sparsity: 12.8 M non-zero bytes plus a 1-bit mask per weight (3.2 MB), about 16 MB, before any entropy coding. Unstructured sparsity saves storage, but speed-ups need sparse kernels.

  3. In Listing 24, why does per-channel quantisation help most at 3 bits and hardly at all at 8 bits?
    Solution

    With 8 bits the grid is fine (255 levels), so even a range stretched by the largest channel leaves small rounding error everywhere. With 3 bits there are only 7 levels; a per-tensor scale set by the largest channel leaves channels with small weights on just one or two levels, often rounding them to zero. Per-channel scales give every output unit its own 7 levels.

  4. The distillation loss in Listing 24 multiplies the KL term by T². Show why, by computing the gradient of KL(softmax(t/T) ‖ softmax(z/T)) with respect to z.
    Solution

    The gradient of the KL with respect to z is (softmax(z/T) − softmax(t/T))/T, so it shrinks by 1/T as the temperature rises (and, for small logit differences, the difference of the softmaxes shrinks by roughly another 1/T). Multiplying by T² keeps the soft-target gradients at a magnitude comparable to the hard-label cross-entropy across temperatures [186]; Listing 24's g_kd = T * (ps - pt) is exactly T² times that gradient.

§21Putting it together: a PyTorch fine-tuning recipe

The NumPy listings show the mechanisms; real projects use a framework that provides automatic differentiation, GPU kernels and pre-trained weights. The listing below fine-tunes an ImageNet-pre-trained ResNet-50 from torchvision [8,193] on an image-folder dataset, and uses most of the techniques in this article: augmentation (§5), transfer with a lower backbone learning rate (§5), label smoothing (§17), class-balanced sampling (§18), AdamW with warm-up and cosine decay (§19), mixed precision (§19), checkpoint selection on the validation set and a single final test evaluation (§16), and batch-norm folding plus export for inference (§20). The same structure works for a ViT or Swin backbone by changing the model constructor and the head attribute.

The API calls used (torchvision.transforms.v2, torch.amp.autocast/GradScaler, nn.CrossEntropyLoss(label_smoothing=…), WeightedRandomSampler, LambdaLR, torch.onnx.export) are standard, but test it on a small subset before a long run.
Recipe. Fine-tuning a pre-trained ResNet-50 with the techniques from Parts I–IV.Python · PyTorch · not executed
import math, torch, torch.nn as nn
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader, WeightedRandomSampler
from torch.fx.experimental.optimization import fuse
from torchvision import datasets, models
from torchvision.transforms import v2 as T

device = "cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu"
weights = models.ResNet50_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V2
mean, std = [0.485, 0.456, 0.406], [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]          # ImageNet normalisation

train_tf = T.Compose([T.RandomResizedCrop(224, scale=(0.25, 1.0)), T.RandomHorizontalFlip(),   # §5: check flips are label-safe!
                      T.RandAugment(num_ops=2, magnitude=9),
                      T.ToImage(), T.ToDtype(torch.float32, scale=True), T.Normalize(mean, std)])
eval_tf = T.Compose([T.Resize(256), T.CenterCrop(224),
                     T.ToImage(), T.ToDtype(torch.float32, scale=True), T.Normalize(mean, std)])

# §16: data/train, data/val, data/test must be split by source (patient, video, ...) beforehand
train_ds = datasets.ImageFolder("data/train", train_tf)
val_ds = datasets.ImageFolder("data/val", eval_tf)
test_ds = datasets.ImageFolder("data/test", eval_tf)

# §18: sample each class equally often (oversampling the tail)
counts = torch.bincount(torch.tensor(train_ds.targets))
sample_w = (1.0 / counts.float())[train_ds.targets]
sampler = WeightedRandomSampler(sample_w, num_samples=len(train_ds), replacement=True)
train_dl = DataLoader(train_ds, batch_size=64, sampler=sampler, num_workers=8, pin_memory=True, drop_last=True)
val_dl = DataLoader(val_ds, batch_size=128, num_workers=8)
test_dl = DataLoader(test_ds, batch_size=128, num_workers=8)

# §5: transfer learning - new head, smaller learning rate for the pre-trained backbone
model = models.resnet50(weights=weights)
model.fc = nn.Linear(model.fc.in_features, len(train_ds.classes))
model = model.to(device, memory_format=torch.channels_last)
head = list(model.fc.parameters())
backbone = [p for n, p in model.named_parameters() if not n.startswith("fc.")]
decay = lambda ps: [p for p in ps if p.ndim > 1]                  # no weight decay on biases / BN params
no_decay = lambda ps: [p for p in ps if p.ndim <= 1]
optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW([
    {"params": decay(backbone), "lr": 1e-4, "weight_decay": 0.05},
    {"params": no_decay(backbone), "lr": 1e-4, "weight_decay": 0.0},
    {"params": head, "lr": 1e-3, "weight_decay": 0.0}])

# §19: linear warm-up, then cosine decay, stepped every iteration
epochs = 30
total, warmup = epochs * len(train_dl), 2 * len(train_dl)
def lr_lambda(step):
    if step < warmup: return (step + 1) / warmup
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * (step - warmup) / max(1, total - warmup)))
scheduler = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR(optimizer, lr_lambda)

criterion = nn.CrossEntropyLoss(label_smoothing=0.1)              # §17
use_amp = device == "cuda"
scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda", enabled=use_amp)            # §19: mixed precision

@torch.no_grad()
def evaluate(loader):
    model.eval()                                                  # BN uses running statistics (§4)
    per_class_correct = torch.zeros(len(train_ds.classes)); per_class_n = torch.zeros(len(train_ds.classes))
    for x, y in loader:
        x = x.to(device, memory_format=torch.channels_last)
        with torch.autocast(device_type=device, dtype=torch.float16, enabled=use_amp):
            pred = model(x).argmax(1).cpu()
        per_class_n += torch.bincount(y, minlength=len(per_class_n)).float()
        per_class_correct += torch.bincount(y[pred == y], minlength=len(per_class_n)).float()
    return (per_class_correct / per_class_n.clamp(min=1)).mean().item()   # balanced accuracy (§18)

best = 0.0
for epoch in range(epochs):
    model.train()
    for x, y in train_dl:
        x, y = x.to(device, memory_format=torch.channels_last, non_blocking=True), y.to(device, non_blocking=True)
        with torch.autocast(device_type=device, dtype=torch.float16, enabled=use_amp):
            loss = criterion(model(x), y)                         # forward pass (§2)
        optimizer.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
        scaler.scale(loss).backward()                             # backward pass (§2)
        scaler.unscale_(optimizer)
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
        scaler.step(optimizer); scaler.update(); scheduler.step()
    val_bal_acc = evaluate(val_dl)
    if val_bal_acc > best:                                        # §16: select on validation only
        best = val_bal_acc
        torch.save(model.state_dict(), "best.pt")
    print(f"epoch {epoch + 1}: val balanced acc {val_bal_acc:.4f}")

model.load_state_dict(torch.load("best.pt"))
print(f"test balanced accuracy (reported once): {evaluate(test_dl):.4f}")

# §20: fold BN into convolutions and export for an inference runtime (ONNX Runtime, TensorRT, ...)
model = model.float().eval().to("cpu", memory_format=torch.contiguous_format)
fused = fuse(model)                                             # conv+BN folding (§20)
torch.onnx.export(fused, torch.randn(1, 3, 224, 224), "model.onnx",
                  input_names=["image"], output_names=["logits"], dynamic_axes={"image": {0: "batch"}})

Quantising the exported model to int8 is then a job for the inference toolchain (for example ONNX Runtime's static quantisation with a calibration set, or TensorRT's int8 mode) [191,192]. Always re-measure accuracy on the validation set after each compression step, and latency on the target device.

Exercises
  1. The recipe selects checkpoints by balanced accuracy on the validation set but trains with a class-balanced sampler and label smoothing. Which of these choices would you change for a multi-label task, and how?
    Solution

    Replace the softmax cross-entropy with nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss (one sigmoid per label, optionally with focal weighting or per-class pos_weight), drop the single-label class-balanced sampler (an image can belong to several classes) in favour of repeat-factor sampling by rarest label [154], and evaluate with per-class average precision (mAP) instead of balanced accuracy.

  2. Adapt the recipe to fine-tune torchvision.models.swin_t. What changes besides the constructor?
    Solution

    The classifier attribute is model.head instead of model.fc; Swin has LayerNorm instead of BN, so the BN-folding step does nothing; transformers are usually fine-tuned with a slightly higher backbone learning rate (or layer-wise learning-rate decay) and stronger regularisation (stochastic depth via the constructor's stochastic_depth_prob) [35,105]. Memory use is higher, so a smaller batch or gradient accumulation may be needed.

  3. Why does the recipe call scaler.unscale_ before clipping gradients?
    Solution

    With loss scaling, the gradients held in .grad are multiplied by the current scale factor. Clipping must compare the true gradient norm with the threshold, so the gradients are unscaled first; scaler.step then knows not to unscale them again and still skips the step if any gradient is infinite or NaN [169].

§22Summary

  • Foundations. Networks are compositions of affine maps and non-linearities, trained by backpropagation and stochastic gradient methods. Non-saturating activations, careful initialisation, normalisation and residual connections are what make depth trainable.
  • CNNs build in locality and weight sharing, which makes them parameter-efficient and translation-equivariant (though not invariant). Their evolution from VGG to ResNet, MobileNet, EfficientNet and ConvNeXt is a story of easier optimisation, cheaper operations and balanced scaling.
  • Vision transformers treat images as sequences of patch tokens and learn global interactions through attention, at a cost quadratic in the number of tokens. Hierarchical and windowed designs (Swin) and hybrids recover the multiscale, local efficiency of CNNs.
  • Training and deployment decide whether any architecture works in practice: clean, leakage-free splits; losses that match the task and its imbalance; schedules and optimisers matched to the model; and compression measured on the target hardware.

Every NumPy listing in this article is self-contained given the listings it names, and every printed output came from running it. Changing a seed, a learning rate or a dataset size and re-running is the fastest way to build intuition for the claims above.
