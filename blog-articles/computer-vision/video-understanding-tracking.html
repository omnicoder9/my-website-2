Tracking Through Time: Video Understanding and Multi-Object Tracking

A video is a stack of images plus one more axis, and that axis brings its own problems: decoding it efficiently, keeping the same name on the same object from frame to frame, recognising what people are doing, finding when it happens, and doing all of it before the next frame arrives.

00What time adds

An image model answers what and where. A video model must also answer which one (is the person in frame 200 the same person as in frame 100?), what is happening (a motion pattern spread over many frames, such as "opening a door"), and when (the start and end of an event inside a long recording). Each of these needs information that no single frame contains.

Time also changes the engineering. A 30 frames-per-second (fps) camera produces 108,000 frames an hour, almost all of them nearly identical to their neighbours, and stored not as images but as a compressed bitstream in which most frames are described as changes relative to other frames [1,2]. Reading the right frames cheaply is a design problem in its own right. Online systems face a hard deadline: a result about frame t that arrives after frame t+5 has already been captured describes a world that has moved on [3].

This article follows the data from the codec to the decision. Sections 01–06 cover decoding and sampling, single-object tracking, multi-object tracking, Bayesian state estimation (Kalman and particle filters), data association with the Hungarian algorithm and appearance embeddings, and the life cycle of a track through occlusion and re-identification. Sections 07–10 cover temporal models (temporal convolutions, recurrent networks, video transformers), action recognition and temporal localization, summarization and anomaly detection, and real-time processing. Every code listing was run for this article and its actual output is printed beneath it; Section 11 lists what was and was not verified.

01Video decoding, frame sampling and temporal preprocessing

How a video file is stored

A video file is a container (MP4, MKV, WebM) holding one or more compressed streams. Modern codecs such as H.264/AVC and H.265/HEVC exploit the fact that neighbouring frames are similar. An I-frame (intra-coded) is compressed on its own, like a still image. A P-frame is predicted from earlier frames using motion-compensated blocks plus a residual, and a B-frame may be predicted from both earlier and later frames [1,2]. The run of frames from one I-frame to the next is the group of pictures (GOP). Because B-frames reference future frames, the order in which frames are decoded differs from the order in which they are shown, so every packet carries a decode timestamp (DTS) and a presentation timestamp (PTS) [4].

Two consequences matter for vision work. First, random access is expensive: to obtain frame 250 of a stream whose last keyframe is frame 240, the decoder must decode frames 240–249 as well. Second, frame index is not time. Many recordings, especially from phones and screen capture, have variable frame rate, so the time of frame k must be read from its PTS rather than computed as k/fps [4,5].

Decoded frames are usually in a YUV format with subsampled chroma (yuv420p: one U and one V sample per 2×2 block of pixels). Broadcast-style video uses limited range, with luma from 16 to 235 rather than 0 to 255 [6]. A converter that assumes the wrong range or the wrong colour matrix (BT.601 versus BT.709) produces images that look washed out or slightly tinted, which is a quiet source of train–serve mismatch when training frames were extracted by one tool and production frames by another.

Choosing which frames to read

A model rarely needs every frame. The sampling strategy is part of the model and must be identical at training and test time, apart from deliberate randomness used as augmentation.

  • Segment sampling. Temporal Segment Networks split the video into K equal segments and draw one frame (or short snippet) from each, at a random offset during training and at the centre during testing. Scores from the segments are combined by a consensus function such as averaging, which lets a 2D image network model a whole video at the cost of K frames [7].
  • Dense clips. 3D convolutional networks such as C3D take a short clip of consecutive frames (16 in C3D) [8]; I3D inflates 2D ImageNet filters into 3D and was trained on the Kinetics dataset [9,11]. At test time several clips are sampled across the video and their predictions averaged.
  • Two-rate sampling. SlowFast runs a slow pathway on few frames with many channels (for appearance) and a fast pathway on α times more frames with a fraction β of the channels (for motion); the paper's defaults are α = 8 and β = 1/8 [10].
  • Motion inputs. Two-stream networks add a second network that sees stacked optical flow fields [12]. Classical dense flow (for example Farnebäck's polynomial expansion) and learned flow such as RAFT are the usual sources [13,14]; computing flow is often more expensive than the recognition network that consumes it.

Decoding efficiently

The listing below implements segment and dense sampling and decodes the chosen frames in one forward pass. OpenCV's grab() demuxes and decodes a frame without converting it to BGR, and retrieve() converts only the frames we keep [5]. Clips are returned as (T, H, W, 3) and transposed to (C, T, H, W), the layout 3D CNNs expect.

sampling.py — segment and dense sampling, single-pass decodingPython 3.13 · NumPy · OpenCV · ffmpeg
"""Frame sampling for video models: segment (TSN-style) and dense clip sampling,
decoded with OpenCV by sequential reading (no random seeks)."""
import json, subprocess
import numpy as np
import cv2

def probe(path):
    """Container/stream metadata via ffprobe (fps, frame count, codec, GOP-relevant info)."""
    out = subprocess.run(
        ["ffprobe", "-v", "error", "-select_streams", "v:0", "-count_packets",
         "-show_entries", "stream=codec_name,width,height,avg_frame_rate,nb_read_packets,pix_fmt",
         "-of", "json", path], capture_output=True, text=True, check=True).stdout
    s = json.loads(out)["streams"][0]
    num, den = map(int, s["avg_frame_rate"].split("/"))
    return dict(codec=s["codec_name"], w=s["width"], h=s["height"], pix_fmt=s["pix_fmt"],
                fps=num / den, n_frames=int(s["nb_read_packets"]))

def segment_indices(n_frames, n_segments, train=True, rng=None):
    """TSN sampling: split video into equal segments, take one frame per segment.
    Random offset within each segment when training, centre when testing."""
    edges = np.linspace(0, n_frames, n_segments + 1)
    if train:
        rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
        idx = edges[:-1] + rng.random(n_segments) * np.diff(edges)
    else:
        idx = (edges[:-1] + edges[1:]) / 2
    return np.minimum(idx.astype(int), n_frames - 1)

def dense_clip_indices(n_frames, clip_len, stride, start):
    """Dense clip: clip_len frames spaced `stride` apart starting at `start` (3D-CNN style)."""
    idx = start + stride * np.arange(clip_len)
    return np.clip(idx, 0, n_frames - 1)  # pad short videos by repeating the last frame

def read_frames(path, indices, size=(224, 224)):
    """Decode once, front to back, keeping only the wanted frames.
    Sequential grab() is cheaper than seeking: a seek must restart decoding at the
    previous keyframe."""
    wanted = {}
    for k, i in enumerate(indices):
        wanted.setdefault(int(i), []).append(k)
    out = [None] * len(indices)
    cap = cv2.VideoCapture(path)
    last = max(wanted)
    for i in range(last + 1):
        if not cap.grab():          # demux + decode, no colour conversion
            break
        if i in wanted:
            ok, bgr = cap.retrieve()  # colour-convert only the frames we keep
            rgb = cv2.cvtColor(cv2.resize(bgr, size, interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA), cv2.COLOR_BGR2RGB)
            for k in wanted[i]:
                out[k] = rgb
    cap.release()
    return np.stack(out)  # (T, H, W, 3) uint8

With short GOPs, seeking wins because each seek decodes only a few frames. With a 300-frame GOP (one keyframe per 10 s, common for streaming and surveillance encoders) every seek re-decodes from the start, and seeking was about 3.5 times slower than one sequential pass. For training pipelines that sample many clips from long videos, a common remedy is to re-encode the dataset once with short GOPs, or to pre-extract frames, trading disk for decode time. In production, hardware decoders such as NVIDIA NVDEC move the decode off the CPU entirely [15].

Temporal preprocessing checklist

  • Resample by timestamp, not index, when the model expects a fixed frame rate.
  • Apply the same spatial augmentation (crop, flip, colour jitter) to every frame of a clip; per-frame random crops create artificial jitter that a temporal model will learn.
  • Normalize with the statistics of the pretrained backbone, and keep the colour range and matrix consistent between training extraction and production decoding [6].
  • Pad short videos (repeat the last frame or loop) and record that you did; in the listing np.clip repeats the last frame.
  • Remember that horizontal flips change the meaning of some classes ("swiping left" becomes "swiping right" in Something-Something) [16].

Exercise 1.1 — Frames versus time

A phone video is 12.0 s long and its container reports 30 fps, but ffprobe counts 331 packets, and the PTS gaps between frames vary from 25 ms to 45 ms. You need a 16-frame clip that covers exactly the interval 4.0–6.0 s. Why is dense_clip_indices(…, start=120, stride=4) wrong, and what should you do instead?

Show solution

The video has variable frame rate: 331 frames in 12 s is an average of 27.6 fps, not 30, and the spacing is uneven. Index 120 is therefore not at 4.0 s, and a stride of 4 frames does not correspond to a fixed time step. Read each frame's PTS (for example with ffprobe -show_frames or CAP_PROP_POS_MSEC during sequential reading [4,5]), compute the 16 target times 4.0 + 2.0·i/15 s, and for each pick the decoded frame with the nearest PTS. Alternatively, convert once to constant frame rate with ffmpeg and record that conversion as part of the dataset.

Exercise 1.2 — How much does a seek cost?

A surveillance stream is encoded with one keyframe every 4 s at 25 fps and no B-frames. Your sampler seeks to 8 uniformly random frames per clip. On average, how many frames does the decoder decode per wanted frame, and what is the total for one clip? What changes if you request 8 consecutive frames instead?

Show solution

The GOP is 100 frames. A uniformly random target lies on average halfway through its GOP, so the decoder decodes about 50 frames (from the keyframe up to and including the target, ≈ 50.5) per wanted frame, roughly 400 decoded frames for 8 random targets, a 50× overhead. Eight consecutive frames need a single seek (≈ 50 frames on average to reach the first) plus 7 sequential frames, about 57 in total. This is why the dense-clip loader in the listing reads forward from one start point, and why the benchmark above shows seeking losing badly with long GOPs [1].

Exercise 1.3 — Temporal coverage

For a 10 s video at 30 fps, compare the time span covered by (a) TSN with 8 segments, (b) one dense clip of 16 frames at stride 2, (c) the SlowFast slow pathway with 4 frames and temporal stride 16. Which sampling would you expect to do best on a class defined by a long-range order of events, such as "putting something into a box, then closing it"?

Show solution

(a) TSN spans essentially the whole 10 s (300 frames), one frame per 1.25 s segment. (b) The dense clip spans 16 × 2 = 32 frames ≈ 1.07 s. (c) The slow pathway spans 4 × 16 = 64 frames ≈ 2.1 s. For a class whose identity depends on the order of events several seconds apart, sparse sampling across the whole video (a) sees both events; a 1 s dense clip may contain only one of them. The catch is that TSN's average consensus discards order, so a temporal model that keeps order (a TCN, RNN or transformer over the segment features, §07) is needed to exploit it [7,10].

02Single-object tracking

In single-object tracking (SOT, often called visual object tracking) the user draws a box around an arbitrary object in the first frame and the tracker must locate that same object in every later frame. Nothing is known about the object class in advance, so the tracker builds an appearance model online from one example [17]. The problem is hard for the reasons any tracking problem is hard: appearance changes with pose and lighting, the object may be occluded or leave the frame, similar-looking distractors appear, and the camera moves.

Benchmarks score a tracker by the overlap between predicted and true boxes. The success plot gives the fraction of frames whose IoU exceeds a threshold, for thresholds from 0 to 1, and is summarised by its area under the curve; the precision plot gives the fraction of frames whose centre error is below a pixel threshold, commonly reported at 20 pixels [18]. LaSOT added long sequences (1,400 videos, over 3.5 million frames) in which the target disappears and reappears, which exposes trackers that cannot re-detect [19].

Correlation filters

Correlation-filter trackers learn a filter h whose correlation with the target patch produces a sharp peak at the target centre and low values elsewhere. Bolme and colleagues' MOSSE (minimum output sum of squared error) filter solves this in closed form in the Fourier domain. With training patches fi and desired Gaussian-shaped responses gi, and capital letters for 2D Fourier transforms, the filter is [20]

H* = Σi Gi ⊙ Fi*  /  ( Σi Fi ⊙ Fi* + ε )

where ⊙ is element-wise multiplication and * is complex conjugation. Tracking a new frame costs two FFTs and an element-wise product, and the paper reports about 669 frames per second on 2010 hardware. The numerator and denominator are updated with a running average (learning rate η = 0.125 in the paper) so the filter adapts to appearance change. The peak-to-sidelobe ratio (PSR), the peak height minus the mean of the surrounding response divided by its standard deviation, measures confidence: Bolme et al. report PSR typically between 20 and 60 under normal tracking and below about 7 when the target is occluded or tracking fails [20]. Kernelized correlation filters (KCF) generalise the idea with the kernel trick and circulant structure of shifted samples, and add multi-channel features such as HOG [21].

mosse.py — MOSSE tracker with PSR-gated updates and coastingPython 3.13 · NumPy
"""MOSSE correlation-filter single-object tracker (Bolme et al., 2010) in NumPy,
with the peak-to-sidelobe ratio (PSR) used to detect occlusion and freeze updates."""
import numpy as np

def preprocess(patch):
    p = np.log1p(patch.astype(np.float64))                 # compress illumination
    p = (p - p.mean()) / (p.std() + 1e-5)
    h, w = p.shape
    win = np.outer(np.hanning(h), np.hanning(w))           # cosine window reduces FFT edge effects
    return p * win

def gaussian_peak(h, w, sigma=2.0):
    y, x = np.mgrid[:h, :w]
    return np.exp(-((x - w // 2) ** 2 + (y - h // 2) ** 2) / (2 * sigma ** 2))

def psr(resp, exclude=5):
    """Peak-to-sidelobe ratio: (peak - mean(sidelobe)) / std(sidelobe)."""
    py, px = np.unravel_index(resp.argmax(), resp.shape)
    mask = np.ones_like(resp, bool)
    mask[max(py - exclude, 0):py + exclude + 1, max(px - exclude, 0):px + exclude + 1] = False
    side = resp[mask]
    return (resp.max() - side.mean()) / (side.std() + 1e-9)

class MOSSE:
    def __init__(self, frame, box, lr=0.125, eps=1e-3, n_aug=8, pad=2.0, rng=None):
        rng = rng or np.random.default_rng(0)
        self.x, self.y, self.w, self.h = box                # top-left corner and target size
        self.ww, self.wh = int(pad * self.w), int(pad * self.h)   # search window larger than target
        self.lr, self.eps, self.v = lr, eps, np.zeros(2)
        self.G = np.fft.fft2(gaussian_peak(self.wh, self.ww))
        f = self._crop(frame)
        self.A = np.zeros_like(self.G); self.B = np.zeros_like(self.G)
        for i in range(n_aug):                              # small random shifts as extra training samples
            dy, dx = (0, 0) if i == 0 else rng.integers(-2, 3, 2)
            F_ = np.fft.fft2(preprocess(np.roll(f, (dy, dx), (0, 1))))
            G_ = np.fft.fft2(np.roll(gaussian_peak(self.wh, self.ww), (dy, dx), (0, 1)))
            self.A += G_ * np.conj(F_); self.B += F_ * np.conj(F_)

    def _crop(self, frame):
        """Search window centred on the target centre, edge-padded at image borders."""
        cx, cy = int(round(self.x + self.w / 2)), int(round(self.y + self.h / 2))
        P = max(self.ww, self.wh)
        f = np.pad(frame, P, mode="edge")
        return f[cy - self.wh // 2 + P:cy - self.wh // 2 + P + self.wh, cx - self.ww // 2 + P:cx - self.ww // 2 + P + self.ww]

    def update(self, frame, psr_min=7.0):
        F_ = np.fft.fft2(preprocess(self._crop(frame)))
        resp = np.real(np.fft.ifft2((self.A / (self.B + self.eps)) * F_))
        q = psr(resp)
        if q >= psr_min:                                    # confident: move and adapt the filter
            dy, dx = np.array(np.unravel_index(resp.argmax(), resp.shape)) - [self.wh // 2, self.ww // 2]
            self.x += dx; self.y += dy
            self.v = 0.7 * self.v + 0.3 * np.array([dx, dy])
            F_ = np.fft.fft2(preprocess(self._crop(frame)))
            self.A = self.lr * self.G * np.conj(F_) + (1 - self.lr) * self.A
            self.B = self.lr * F_ * np.conj(F_) + (1 - self.lr) * self.B
        else:                                               # occluded or lost: coast, do not learn
            self.x += self.v[0]; self.y += self.v[1]
        return (self.x, self.y, self.w, self.h), q

Siamese and transformer trackers

Deep trackers replace the online-learned filter with a similarity function learned offline on large video datasets. SiamFC embeds the exemplar (the first-frame patch) and a larger search region with the same fully convolutional network and cross-correlates the two feature maps; the location of the maximum is the new target position [22]. SiamRPN adds a region-proposal head so the network also regresses the box size [23]. More recent one-stream transformer trackers such as OSTrack feed exemplar and search-region patches through a single vision transformer, so feature extraction and template–search interaction happen together [24,25].

SOT matters for multi-object tracking in two ways. Some MOT systems run a single-object tracker per target between detector calls, and the confidence signals of SOT (PSR, response peak) are the same signals a multi-object tracker needs to decide whether a track is still visible. OpenCV ships several single-object trackers, including the online multiple-instance-learning tracker (MIL) [26].

Confidence measures are only meaningful if the model has not been trained on the failure; that is why MOSSE freezes updates when PSR is low [20], and why multi-object trackers stop updating a track's appearance when its detection is occluded (§06).

Exercise 2.2 — Why pad the search window?

An early version of mosse.py cropped a search window exactly the size of the 32×32 target. During the 8-frame occlusion the target moved about 20 px, and after it reappeared the tracker never recovered (mean error 72.6 px). Explain why, and why a window twice the target size fixed it.

Show solution

Correlation via the FFT is circular, and the response is only meaningful for shifts smaller than about half the window. A 32-px window can represent displacements of at most ±16 px, and the cosine window further suppresses the edges. A target 20 px away is outside, or at the attenuated edge of, the region the filter can see, so the peak lands on background. A 64-px window doubles the capture range to about ±32 px. The cost is more background in the training patch, which is why trackers combine padding with a cosine window, and why KCF and later trackers add explicit motion models or re-detection [20,21].

Exercise 2.3 — Success and precision

A tracker produces IoUs of 0.82, 0.75, 0.60, 0.40, 0.10, 0.00 and centre errors of 3, 6, 12, 25, 60, 140 px on six frames. Compute the success rate at IoU 0.5 and the precision at 20 px. Why can a tracker look good on precision but poor on success?

Show solution

Success at 0.5: three frames (0.82, 0.75, 0.60) out of six = 0.50. Precision at 20 px: three frames (3, 6, 12) = 0.50. Here they agree, but they measure different things: precision checks only the centre, so a tracker that keeps the centre right but gets the scale wrong (a box far too large around a shrinking target) passes the precision test while failing on IoU. This is one reason benchmarks report the success-plot AUC as the primary score [18,19].

03Multi-object tracking: tracking-by-detection and joint detection-tracking

Multi-object tracking (MOT) must find every object of given classes in every frame and give each one an identity that stays the same for as long as the object is in view. The output is a set of trajectories: for each identity, a box per frame in which it is visible. Unlike SOT, the number of objects is unknown and changes as objects enter and leave, and errors come in three kinds: missed objects, false objects, and identity errors, where two trajectories swap or one trajectory is split in two [27,28].

The MOTChallenge benchmarks (MOT16, MOT17, MOT20) made pedestrian tracking in crowded street scenes the standard testbed, with MOT20 focusing on very dense crowds [29,30]. DanceTrack deliberately uses dancers in similar costumes with fast, non-linear motion, so appearance cues are weak and motion is hard to predict, which rewards different methods than MOT17 does [31].

Tracking-by-detection

The dominant recipe runs an object detector on each frame independently and then links detections over time. Each existing track predicts where its object should be now (§04); a cost is computed between every track and every detection (§05); an assignment solver picks the matches; and unmatched tracks and detections start, pause or end trajectories (§06). SORT showed in 2016 that a Kalman filter plus Hungarian matching on IoU, applied to a strong detector, could be competitive with far more complex methods while running at hundreds of frames per second for the tracking part alone [32]. DeepSORT added an appearance embedding and a matching cascade to survive longer occlusions [33], and ByteTrack showed that low-confidence detections, usually thrown away, recover many occluded objects when matched in a second pass [34]. Later variants refine each component: OC-SORT corrects Kalman drift after occlusion using observations [35], BoT-SORT adds camera-motion compensation [36], and StrongSORT upgrades DeepSORT's detector, embedding and update rules [37].

The strength of tracking-by-detection is modularity: the detector can be replaced as detectors improve, and the tracker needs no training. Its weakness is that the detector sees one frame at a time and cannot use the track history to find an object that is hard to see in this frame.

Joint detection and tracking

Joint methods let one network see more than one frame, or share features between detection and association.

  • Regression-based. Tracktor uses the box-regression head of a Faster R-CNN detector to move each track's previous box onto the object in the current frame, so tracking falls out of detection [38]. CenterTrack feeds the previous frame and a heatmap of previous object centres into a CenterNet-style detector that outputs, for each object, its centre and an offset to its centre in the previous frame [39].
  • Shared embeddings. JDE and FairMOT add a re-identification embedding head to a one-stage detector, so one forward pass produces boxes and appearance features [40,41]. FairMOT argued that anchor-free, high-resolution features avoid the imbalance between the detection and re-ID tasks that hurt earlier joint models [41].
  • Query-based. TrackFormer and MOTR extend the DETR-style set-prediction transformer with track queries: an embedding that detects an object in one frame is carried to the next frame, where it detects the same object again, so identity is preserved by the query rather than by a separate association step [42,43].

Online, offline and the choice in practice

An online tracker decides about frame t using only frames up to t; an offline (batch) tracker may look at the whole video and solve a global problem, for example a min-cost flow over all detections, which helps with long occlusions at the cost of latency [27]. Most deployed systems are online. On MOTChallenge-style pedestrian benchmarks, simple tracking-by-detection with a strong detector has remained highly competitive; ByteTrack, for instance, reported state-of-the-art MOT17 and MOT20 results in 2022 using a YOLOX detector and motion-only association [34]. On DanceTrack, where motion is erratic, methods that model motion better or learn association end to end gain more [31,35,43]. The honest rule is that the detector dominates: a tracker cannot link boxes that were never detected.

04Kalman filters, particle filters and Bayesian state estimation

Every tracker carries a belief about each object's state xt (position, size, velocity) given the measurements z1:t seen so far. The Bayes filter updates this belief recursively in two steps [44,45]:

predict: p(xt | z1:t−1) = ∫ p(xt | xt−1) p(xt−1 | z1:t−1) dxt−1
update: p(xt | z1:t) ∝ p(zt | xt) p(xt | z1:t−1)

The motion model p(xt | xt−1) says how objects move; the measurement model p(zt | xt) says how detections relate to the true state. Prediction spreads the belief out (we become less sure where the object is), and an update with a measurement sharpens it. When a detection is missing, as during an occlusion, the tracker can only predict, and its uncertainty grows; that growing uncertainty is what later tells the association step how far from the prediction a returning object may be found.

The Kalman filter

If the motion and measurement models are linear with Gaussian noise, the belief stays Gaussian and the Bayes filter has an exact closed form, the Kalman filter [46]. With state mean x̂, covariance P, transition F, process noise Q, measurement matrix H and measurement noise R:

x̂− = F x̂    P− = F P FT + Q
S = H P− HT + R    K = P− HT S−1    x̂ = x̂− + K (z − H x̂−)    P = (I − K H) P−

The Kalman gain K weighs the measurement against the prediction: large measurement noise R gives a small gain (trust the prediction), large predicted uncertainty P− gives a large gain (trust the detection). The innovation y = z − H x̂− with covariance S is also the right yardstick for association: the squared Mahalanobis distance d² = yT S−1 y follows a χ² distribution with as many degrees of freedom as the measurement has dimensions, so a detection can be rejected if d² exceeds, say, the 95% quantile, 9.4877 for a 4-D box measurement. DeepSORT uses exactly this gate [33,45].

Box trackers use a constant-velocity model. SORT's state is the box centre, area and aspect ratio plus the velocities of centre and area, with the aspect ratio assumed constant [32]; DeepSORT uses centre, aspect ratio and height with their velocities, and scales the noise with box height so that a far, small pedestrian and a near, large one are treated alike [33]. The listing uses centre, width and height with height-scaled noise, and the numerically safer Joseph form of the covariance update.

kalman.py — constant-velocity Kalman filter for boxes, with χ² gatingPython 3.13 · NumPy
"""Constant-velocity Kalman filter for bounding boxes (DeepSORT-style state)."""
import numpy as np

class KalmanBox:
    """State x = [cx, cy, w, h, vcx, vcy, vw, vh]; measurement z = [cx, cy, w, h].
    Noise is scaled by box height so the filter behaves the same for near and far objects."""
    def __init__(self, z, std_pos=1/20, std_vel=1/160, dt=1.0):
        self.sp, self.sv = std_pos, std_vel
        self.F = np.eye(8); self.F[:4, 4:] = dt * np.eye(4)   # x_t = F x_{t-1}
        self.H = np.eye(4, 8)                                 # z = H x
        h = z[3]
        self.x = np.r_[z, np.zeros(4)].astype(float)
        self.P = np.diag(np.r_[[2*self.sp*h]*4, [10*self.sv*h]*4] ** 2)

    def predict(self):
        h = self.x[3]
        Q = np.diag(np.r_[[self.sp*h]*4, [self.sv*h]*4] ** 2)
        self.x = self.F @ self.x
        self.P = self.F @ self.P @ self.F.T + Q
        return self.x[:4]

    def innovation(self, z):
        R = np.diag([self.sp * self.x[3]] * 4) ** 2
        S = self.H @ self.P @ self.H.T + R                  # innovation covariance
        return z - self.H @ self.x, S

    def mahalanobis2(self, z):
        y, S = self.innovation(z)
        return float(y @ np.linalg.solve(S, y))

    def update(self, z):
        y, S = self.innovation(z)
        K = self.P @ self.H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)            # Kalman gain
        self.x = self.x + K @ y
        I_KH = np.eye(8) - K @ self.H
        R = np.diag([self.sp * self.x[3]] * 4) ** 2
        self.P = I_KH @ self.P @ I_KH.T + K @ R @ K.T       # Joseph form: stays symmetric PSD

CHI2_95_4DOF = 9.4877   # gate: accept z if d^2 < chi2_{0.95}(4)

Nonlinear models: EKF, UKF and particle filters

When motion or measurement is nonlinear (a camera that rotates, a target that turns, a bearing-only sensor), the Gaussian belief is no longer exact. The extended Kalman filter linearises the models around the current estimate with Jacobians; the unscented Kalman filter instead propagates a small set of deterministically chosen sigma points through the nonlinear functions and refits a Gaussian, which is usually more accurate and needs no derivatives [47].

A particle filter drops the Gaussian assumption entirely and represents the belief by N weighted samples. The bootstrap filter of Gordon, Salmond and Smith propagates each particle through the motion model, multiplies its weight by the measurement likelihood, and resamples when the weights become too uneven [48]. Isard and Blake's CONDENSATION brought the idea to visual tracking, where image likelihoods are often multimodal because clutter looks like the target [49]. A standard degeneracy test is the effective sample size Neff = 1 / Σ wi², with resampling when it drops below a threshold such as N/2 [50].

The listing tracks one point among clutter. The likelihood is the key line: with detection probability PD and Poisson clutter of intensity κ per pixel, the probability of this frame's detection set given a particle is proportional to (1 − PD) + PD Σj 𝒩(zj; x, σ²) / κ. The "missed" term keeps particles alive when the target is not detected, and the sum lets every detection near a particle support it without the filter having to decide which detection is the target.

particle.py — bootstrap particle filter with clutter-aware likelihood and systematic resamplingPython 3.13 · NumPy
"""Bootstrap particle filter tracking a point among clutter detections."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
W, H, T, N = 640, 480, 80, 2000             # image size, frames, particles
PD, LAM, SIG = 0.8, 4, 6.0                  # detection prob, clutter per frame, meas. std (px)

# Ground truth: a target that turns (nonlinear path), plus per-frame detection sets
t = np.arange(T)
truth = np.c_[320 + 200*np.sin(t/15), 240 + 150*np.sin(t/25)]
frames, seen = [], []
for k in range(T):
    seen.append(rng.random() < PD)
    zs = [truth[k] + rng.normal(0, SIG, 2)] if seen[-1] else []
    zs += list(rng.uniform([0, 0], [W, H], (rng.poisson(LAM), 2)))      # false alarms
    frames.append(np.array(zs).reshape(-1, 2))

def likelihood(particles, zs):
    """p(Z | x) up to a constant, for one target with detection prob PD and Poisson clutter
    of intensity kappa = LAM / (W*H): either the target was missed, or one z_j came from it.
    This mixture is what lets the particle filter ignore false alarms."""
    kappa = LAM / (W * H)
    L = np.full(len(particles), 1 - PD)
    if len(zs):
        d2 = ((particles[:, None, :2] - zs[None]) ** 2).sum(-1)        # (N, |Z|)
        g = np.exp(-d2 / (2 * SIG**2)) / (2 * np.pi * SIG**2)          # Gaussian p(z_j | x)
        L += PD * g.sum(1) / kappa
    return L

x = np.c_[rng.uniform(0, W, N), rng.uniform(0, H, N), rng.normal(0, 5, (N, 2))]   # [x, y, vx, vy]
w = np.full(N, 1 / N)
est, resamples = [], 0
for k in range(T):
    # 1) predict: constant velocity + random acceleration
    x[:, 2:] += rng.normal(0, 1.5, (N, 2))
    x[:, :2] += x[:, 2:]
    # 2) weight by the likelihood of this frame's detections
    w *= likelihood(x, frames[k]); w /= w.sum()
    est.append(w @ x[:, :2])
    # 3) resample when the effective sample size collapses (systematic resampling)
    ess = 1.0 / np.sum(w**2)
    if ess < N / 2:
        u = (rng.random() + np.arange(N)) / N
        idx = np.minimum(np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(w), u), N - 1)
        x, w = x[idx], np.full(N, 1 / N); resamples += 1

est = np.array(est)
err = np.linalg.norm(est - truth, axis=1)
print(f"mean error after frame 10: {err[10:].mean():.1f} px  (median {np.median(err[10:]):.1f})")
print(f"frames where the target was not detected: {T - sum(seen)}; false alarms: {sum(len(z) for z in frames) - sum(seen)}")
print(f"resampling steps: {resamples}/{T}")
output (actual run)
mean error after frame 10: 6.8 px  (median 4.8)
frames where the target was not detected: 16; false alarms: 314
resampling steps: 50/80

The target was missed in 16 of 80 frames and 314 false alarms were scattered over the image, yet after the first ten frames the mean error was 6.8 px, close to the 6 px measurement noise. A Kalman filter fed the nearest detection would occasionally lock onto clutter; the particle filter does not need a hard decision. The price is compute: 2,000 particles × several detections per frame, and the cost grows quickly with state dimension, which is why box trackers with good detectors almost always use Kalman filters.

If the filter's model is correct, the innovation is Gaussian with covariance S, so d² is a sum of 4 squared independent standard normals after whitening, which is χ²-distributed with 4 degrees of freedom. 05Data association: the Hungarian algorithm and appearance embeddings

After prediction, each frame poses a matching problem: which detection belongs to which track? With M tracks and N detections, build an M×N cost matrix C, where Cij is small when detection j is a plausible continuation of track i, and find the one-to-one assignment that minimises the total cost. Pairs whose cost is too high are forbidden (gated) and stay unmatched; unmatched tracks are candidates for occlusion or termination, unmatched detections for new tracks.

The assignment problem

The linear assignment problem is solved exactly in polynomial time by the Hungarian algorithm, introduced by Kuhn in 1955 and shown by Munkres to run in O(n³) [51,52]. Rectangular problems (M ≠ N) are handled by padding or by algorithms designed for them; SciPy's linear_sum_assignment implements a shortest-augmenting-path method for rectangular matrices described by Crouse [53,54]. For the few dozen objects in a typical frame this takes microseconds, so there is little reason to settle for a greedy match, which can be far from optimal.

import itertools
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment

# IoU between 3 predicted tracks (rows) and 3 detections (columns)
S = np.array([[0.90, 0.70, 0.00],
              [0.75, 0.00, 0.10],
              [0.00, 0.60, 0.65]])
C = 1 - S

def greedy(C, max_cost):
    pairs, used_r, used_c = [], set(), set()
    for i, j in sorted(np.ndindex(*C.shape), key=lambda ij: C[ij]):   # cheapest pair first
        if i not in used_r and j not in used_c and C[i, j] <= max_cost:
            pairs.append((i, j)); used_r.add(i); used_c.add(j)
    return sorted(pairs)

What goes into the cost

  • Overlap. 1 − IoU between the predicted box and the detection. Cheap and effective at high frame rates, where objects move little between frames. SORT uses it alone [32].
  • Motion distance. The Mahalanobis distance from §04 accounts for the track's uncertainty: a track that has coasted through an occlusion has a wide gate, a freshly updated one a tight gate [33].
  • Appearance. The cosine distance between an embedding of the detection crop and the track's stored appearance. This is what allows matching after long gaps, when motion prediction is unreliable [33].

DeepSORT combines motion and appearance as a weighted sum, uses the Mahalanobis distance as a gate, and matches in a cascade that gives priority to tracks seen most recently, so that a long-lost track with a large covariance cannot steal a detection from a track that was updated in the previous frame [33]. ByteTrack's contribution is about which detections enter the matrix: it first matches high-score detections, then matches the remaining tracks to low-score detections using IoU only, because a partly occluded person typically produces a low-confidence box in the right place [34].

Classical radar tracking developed more global alternatives. Multiple hypothesis tracking (MHT) keeps a tree of alternative association hypotheses over several frames and prunes it [55]; joint probabilistic data association (JPDA) avoids a hard decision by weighting all gated measurements by their association probabilities [56]. Both are more expensive than frame-by-frame Hungarian matching and are less common in camera-based tracking, but the particle-filter likelihood in §04 is an example of the same "don't decide too early" idea.

Appearance embeddings

The appearance model is usually a person (or vehicle) re-identification network: a CNN or transformer that maps a crop to a vector, trained so that crops of the same identity are close and crops of different identities far apart. Training uses identity classification on re-ID datasets such as Market-1501, metric-learning losses such as the batch-hard triplet loss, or both [57–59]. At tracking time features are L2-normalised, so the dot product is the cosine similarity. Rather than keeping only the last feature, trackers keep a gallery of recent features (DeepSORT keeps up to 100 per track) or an exponential moving average, which StrongSORT and the listing in §06 use to smooth over single bad crops [33,37].

The assign function in §06 replaces gated costs by 106, solves, and then drops pairs above the threshold. Why not just solve on the raw costs and threshold afterwards? Give a 2×2 example where the two approaches differ.

Show solution

Without the penalty the solver may use a forbidden pair to make the total cheaper, and dropping that pair afterwards strands a match that was valid. Take threshold 0.5 and C = [[0.40, 0.55], [0.00, 0.45]]. On the raw costs the optimum is (0,1) + (1,0) = 0.55, cheaper than (0,0) + (1,1) = 0.85; after thresholding, (0,1) is dropped and only one pair survives. With 0.55 replaced by 106, the solver must take (0,0) + (1,1), and both pairs are valid: two matches instead of one. (We checked both with linear_sum_assignment.) The penalty must be larger than any sum of valid costs, but finite, because SciPy rejects infeasible matrices containing only infinite entries in a row [54].

Exercise 5.3 — Balancing motion and appearance

In a combined cost λ·(1 − IoU) + (1 − λ)·dcos, what value of λ suits (a) a 60 fps traffic camera with distinctive vehicles, (b) a 5 fps feed of a crowded station, (c) dancers in identical costumes?

Show solution

(a) Objects move little between frames and IoU is very reliable; λ near 1 works and saves computing embeddings, as SORT shows [32]. (b) At 5 fps people move far between frames, IoU is often zero for correct pairs, and crowds create ambiguity; appearance must carry most of the weight (λ small), with the Mahalanobis gate to prevent matches across the room [33]. (c) Appearance is nearly useless when costumes match, so it should get low weight; motion modelling becomes the bottleneck, which is why DanceTrack rewards better motion models and learned association [31,35].

06Track management, occlusion handling and re-identification

Association decides matches within one frame. Track management decides what a match or a miss means over time: when a new object becomes a track, how long a track survives without detections, and when a returning object should get its old identity back. Most identity errors in practice come from these rules rather than from the assignment solver.

The track life cycle

  • Birth. An unmatched high-confidence detection starts a tentative track. It is confirmed only after being matched in several consecutive frames (ninit = 3 in DeepSORT), which filters out one-frame false positives [33]. SORT likewise suppresses tracks until they have been matched a minimum number of times [32]. The listing assigns the public identity number only at confirmation, so noise does not consume IDs.
  • Coasting. A confirmed track that is not matched keeps predicting with its motion model and is marked lost. Its growing covariance widens its gate.
  • Death. A lost track is deleted after max_age frames without a match. SORT deletes after a single missed frame, arguing that long-term re-identification is out of scope [32]; DeepSORT keeps tracks for up to 30 frames and relies on appearance to reclaim them [33]; ByteTrack keeps lost tracks for 30 frames by default [34].
  • Re-identification. When a lost track's object reappears, appearance similarity, not position, is what recognises it. Embeddings are updated only from confident, unoccluded detections; the ByteTrack-style second pass in the listing updates position from a low-score box but deliberately leaves the appearance alone, because an occluded crop shows the occluder (see Exercise 2.1 for the same failure in SOT).

A complete tracker

The tracker below puts §04 and §05 together: Kalman prediction, a first association pass of all tracks against high-score detections using IoU or a combined IoU-plus-appearance cost with Mahalanobis and cosine gates, a ByteTrack second pass of the remaining confirmed tracks against low-score boxes, and the life cycle above. It reuses KalmanBox from §04.

tracker.py — tracking-by-detection with two-stage association and life-cycle managementPython 3.13 · NumPy · SciPy
"""Tracking-by-detection: Kalman prediction, Hungarian association with IoU and appearance,
ByteTrack-style second pass on low-score boxes, and track life-cycle management."""
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment
from kalman import KalmanBox, CHI2_95_4DOF
from scene import iou

def xyxy_to_cxcywh(b): return np.r_[(b[:2] + b[2:]) / 2, b[2:] - b[:2]]
def cxcywh_to_xyxy(z): return np.r_[z[:2] - z[2:] / 2, z[:2] + z[2:] / 2]

class Track:
    _next_id = 1
    def __init__(self, box, feat):
        self.kf = KalmanBox(xyxy_to_cxcywh(box))
        self.feat = feat
        self.id = None                   # assigned when confirmed, so ids are not wasted on noise
        self.hits, self.misses = 1, 0
        self.state = "tentative"

    @property
    def box(self): return cxcywh_to_xyxy(self.kf.x[:4])

    def update(self, box, feat, n_init, alpha=0.9):
        self.kf.update(xyxy_to_cxcywh(box))
        if feat is not None:             # exponential moving average of the appearance
            self.feat = alpha * self.feat + (1 - alpha) * feat
            self.feat /= np.linalg.norm(self.feat)
        self.hits += 1; self.misses = 0
        if self.state == "tentative" and self.hits >= n_init:
            self.state, self.id = "confirmed", Track._next_id
            Track._next_id += 1

def assign(cost, max_cost):
    """Hungarian assignment; pairs whose cost exceeds max_cost are treated as unmatched."""
    if cost.size == 0:
        return [], list(range(cost.shape[0])), list(range(cost.shape[1]))
    r, c = linear_sum_assignment(np.where(cost > max_cost, 1e6, cost))
    pairs = [(i, j) for i, j in zip(r, c) if cost[i, j] <= max_cost]
    mi = {i for i, _ in pairs}; mj = {j for _, j in pairs}
    return pairs, [i for i in range(cost.shape[0]) if i not in mi], [j for j in range(cost.shape[1]) if j not in mj]

class Tracker:
    def __init__(self, high=0.6, low=0.1, use_low=True, use_app=True, max_age=30, n_init=3,
                 lam=0.5, max_cos=0.4):
        self.high, self.low, self.use_low, self.use_app = high, low, use_low, use_app
        self.max_age, self.n_init, self.lam, self.max_cos = max_age, n_init, lam, max_cos
        self.tracks = []

    def cost_matrix(self, tracks, boxes, feats, use_app):
        c_iou = 1 - iou(np.array([t.box for t in tracks]).reshape(-1, 4), boxes)
        if not use_app or c_iou.size == 0:
            return c_iou
        c_app = 1 - np.array([t.feat for t in tracks]) @ feats.T          # cosine distance
        cost = self.lam * c_iou + (1 - self.lam) * c_app
        for i, t in enumerate(tracks):                                    # motion + appearance gates
            for j, b in enumerate(boxes):
                if (t.kf.mahalanobis2(xyxy_to_cxcywh(b)) > CHI2_95_4DOF * 4 or c_app[i, j] > self.max_cos):
                    cost[i, j] = np.inf
        return cost

    def step(self, dets):
        for t in self.tracks:
            t.kf.predict()
        boxes = np.array([d[0] for d in dets]).reshape(-1, 4)
        scores = np.array([d[1] for d in dets])
        feats = np.array([d[2] for d in dets]).reshape(len(dets), -1)
        hi = np.where(scores >= self.high)[0]
        lo = np.where((scores >= self.low) & (scores < self.high))[0]

        # Stage 1: all tracks vs high-confidence detections
        tr = self.tracks
        cost = self.cost_matrix(tr, boxes[hi], feats[hi], self.use_app)
        max_cost = 0.7 if not self.use_app else 0.6
        pairs, un_t, un_d = assign(cost, max_cost)
        for i, j in pairs:
            tr[i].update(boxes[hi[j]], feats[hi[j]], self.n_init)

        # Stage 2 (ByteTrack): remaining confirmed tracks vs low-confidence boxes, IoU only
        rest = [tr[i] for i in un_t if tr[i].state == "confirmed"]
        if self.use_low and rest and len(lo):
            pairs2, un2, _ = assign(1 - iou(np.array([t.box for t in rest]), boxes[lo]), 0.5)
            for i, j in pairs2:
                rest[i].update(boxes[lo[j]], None, self.n_init)    # occluded crop: do not touch appearance
            matched2 = {id(rest[i]) for i, _ in pairs2}
        else:
            matched2 = set()

        # Life cycle: unmatched tracks age; tentative ones die at once, confirmed after max_age
        for i in un_t:
            t = tr[i]
            if id(t) in matched2:
                continue
            t.misses += 1
            if t.state == "tentative" or t.misses > self.max_age:
                t.state = "deleted"
        self.tracks = [t for t in tr if t.state != "deleted"]
        for j in un_d:                                              # births from unmatched high boxes
            self.tracks.append(Track(boxes[hi[j]], feats[hi[j]]))
        return [(t.id, t.box) for t in self.tracks if t.state == "confirmed" and t.misses == 0]

Detections have 3 px noise, a 5% random miss rate, and about 0.5 false alarms per frame, mostly at low confidence. Each person has a 64-dimensional identity vector, and a detection's embedding is that vector plus noise, contaminated by the occluder's vector when partly hidden. The setup is deliberately small so you can read it, but it contains the three failure modes that matter: crossings, long occlusions, and motion that changes while the object is unseen.

scene.py — synthetic scene with occlusions, misses, false alarms and toy embeddingsPython 3.13 · NumPy
"""Synthetic MOT scene: four walkers, a crossing with occlusion, missed detections,
low-confidence detections of partly hidden people, false alarms, and toy appearance embeddings."""
import numpy as np

def iou(a, b):
    """IoU between box arrays a (N,4) and b (M,4) in [x1, y1, x2, y2]."""
    a, b = np.asarray(a, float).reshape(-1, 4), np.asarray(b, float).reshape(-1, 4)
    lt = np.maximum(a[:, None, :2], b[None, :, :2]); rb = np.minimum(a[:, None, 2:], b[None, :, 2:])
    inter = np.prod(np.clip(rb - lt, 0, None), axis=2)
    area = lambda r: (r[:, 2] - r[:, 0]) * (r[:, 3] - r[:, 1])
    return inter / (area(a)[:, None] + area(b)[None] - inter + 1e-9)

PILLAR = (440, 520)   # x-range of a static occluder (e.g. a column) in front of walker 3

def make_scene(T=150, seed=0, dim=64):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    t = np.arange(T)
    paths = {  # centre trajectories (cx, cy); boxes are 40 x 100 px
        1: np.c_[40 + 5.0*t, 240 + 0*t],          # walks right
        2: np.c_[640 - 5.0*t, 250 + 0*t],         # walks left, passes behind 1 around t = 60
        3: np.c_[200 + 4.0*np.minimum(t, 70) + 4.0*np.clip(t - 85, 0, None), 110 + 0*t],
           # walks right, pauses behind the pillar for 15 frames, then walks on
        4: np.c_[150 + 3*np.sin(t/7), 380 + 0*t], # loiters
    }
    ident = {k: (v := rng.normal(size=dim)) / np.linalg.norm(v) for k in paths}
    gt, dets = [], []
    for k in range(T):
        boxes = {i: np.r_[p[k] - [20, 50], p[k] + [20, 50]] for i, p in paths.items()}
        gt.append(boxes)
        frame = []
        for i, b in boxes.items():
            # visibility: person 2 is behind person 1
            occ = iou(b, boxes[1])[0, 0] if i == 2 else 0.0
            if i == 3 and PILLAR[0] - 10 < (b[0] + b[2]) / 2 < PILLAR[1] + 10:
                continue                               # behind the pillar
            if occ > 0.45 or rng.random() < 0.05:      # fully hidden, or a random miss
                continue
            score = 0.9 - 1.2 * occ + rng.normal(0, 0.03)  # partial occlusion lowers confidence
            feat = ident[i] + rng.normal(0, 0.5 / np.sqrt(dim), dim)   # same person: cosine ~0.9
            if occ > 0:  # occluded crops leak the occluder's appearance
                feat = (1 - occ) * feat + occ * ident[1]
            frame.append((b + rng.normal(0, 3, 4), float(np.clip(score, 0.05, 0.99)), feat / np.linalg.norm(feat)))
        for _ in range(rng.poisson(0.5)):              # false alarms, usually low score
            c = rng.uniform([30, 60], [610, 420])
            f = rng.normal(size=dim)
            frame.append((np.r_[c - [20, 50], c + [20, 50]], float(rng.uniform(0.1, 0.6)), f / np.linalg.norm(f)))
        dets.append(frame)
    return gt, dets

Measuring tracking quality

The CLEAR MOT metrics match ground truth and hypotheses frame by frame at an IoU threshold and count false negatives (FN), false positives (FP) and identity switches (IDSW), where a ground-truth object's matched hypothesis ID changes. MOTA = 1 − (FN + FP + IDSW) / GT, where GT is the number of ground-truth boxes [28]. Because FN and FP are usually far more numerous than switches, MOTA mostly measures detection. IDF1 instead matches whole trajectories one-to-one across the video and reports the F1 score of correctly identified boxes, IDF1 = 2·IDTP / (2·IDTP + IDFP + IDFN), so it punishes every frame spent under a wrong identity [60]. HOTA, now the main metric on MOTChallenge and DanceTrack, is the geometric mean of a detection accuracy and an association accuracy, averaged over IoU thresholds, so that neither aspect can dominate [30,61]. The listing implements simplified MOTA and IDF1; for published numbers use the official TrackEval implementation referenced by the HOTA paper.

metrics.py — CLEAR-MOT counts, MOTA and IDF1Python 3.13 · NumPy · SciPy
"""CLEAR-MOT (MOTA, ID switches) and IDF1 for box tracks at an IoU threshold of 0.5."""
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment
from scene import iou

def mota(gt, hyp, thr=0.5):
    """gt[k] = {gt_id: box}; hyp[k] = [(hyp_id, box), ...]."""
    fn = fp = idsw = n_gt = 0
    last = {}                                         # gt_id -> hyp_id it was last matched to
    for g, h in zip(gt, hyp):
        gids, gb = list(g), np.array(list(g.values())).reshape(-1, 4)
        hids, hb = [x[0] for x in h], np.array([x[1] for x in h]).reshape(-1, 4)
        n_gt += len(gids)
        S = iou(gb, hb) if len(hids) else np.zeros((len(gids), 0))
        # keep last frame's correspondences if they are still valid, then Hungarian for the rest
        cost = 1 - S
        for a, gi in enumerate(gids):
            if gi in last and last[gi] in hids:
                b = hids.index(last[gi])
                if S[a, b] >= thr: cost[a, b] = -1
        r, c = linear_sum_assignment(np.where(S >= thr, cost, 10)) if S.size else ([], [])
        matches = [(a, b) for a, b in zip(r, c) if S[a, b] >= thr]
        for a, b in matches:
            if gids[a] in last and last[gids[a]] != hids[b]:
                idsw += 1
            last[gids[a]] = hids[b]
        fn += len(gids) - len(matches); fp += len(hids) - len(matches)
    return dict(MOTA=1 - (fn + fp + idsw) / n_gt, FN=fn, FP=fp, IDSW=idsw)

def idf1(gt, hyp, thr=0.5):
    """Global one-to-one matching of whole trajectories that maximises correctly identified boxes."""
    G = sorted({i for g in gt for i in g}); Hs = sorted({x[0] for h in hyp for x in h})
    tp = np.zeros((len(G), len(Hs)))
    for g, h in zip(gt, hyp):
        for hid, hb in h:
            for gid, gb in g.items():
                if iou(gb, hb)[0, 0] >= thr:
                    tp[G.index(gid), Hs.index(hid)] += 1
    r, c = linear_sum_assignment(-tp)
    idtp = tp[r, c].sum()
    n_g = sum(len(g) for g in gt); n_h = sum(len(h) for h in hyp)
    return dict(IDF1=2 * idtp / (n_g + n_h), IDP=idtp / max(n_h, 1), IDR=idtp / n_g)

Exercise 6.3 — Why EMA the appearance?

The track's appearance is updated as f ← α f + (1 − α) fdet with α = 0.9. What goes wrong with α = 0 (always take the newest crop) during the crossing in scene.py? What goes wrong with α = 1?

Show solution

With α = 0 the track's appearance becomes the newest crop. During the crossing the walker's crops are contaminated by the occluder's identity vector, so the track briefly "looks like" the person in front, which invites a swap at the next ambiguous frame. With α = 1 the appearance never adapts, so slow, genuine changes (turning around, lighting) erode the similarity until a returning person fails the cosine gate. A moving average, as in JDE and StrongSORT, sits between the two; skipping updates from low-confidence or overlapping boxes removes the worst contamination [37,40].

07Temporal convolutional networks, recurrent models and video transformers

Tracking keeps identities; recognising what happens requires a model that combines evidence across frames. There are four broad ways to add the time axis to a vision network, and they trade accuracy, cost and the ability to run online.

  1. Pool frame features. Run an image network on sampled frames and average the scores or features, as TSN does [7]. Cheap and strong on appearance-heavy classes, but blind to order.
  2. Convolve over time. 3D convolutions (C3D, I3D) treat time like a third spatial axis [8,9]. R(2+1)D factorises each 3D kernel into a 2D spatial and a 1D temporal convolution, which the authors found easier to optimise [62]. SlowFast uses two pathways at different frame rates [10], X3D grows a small 2D network along time, resolution, width and depth to find efficient trade-offs [63], and the temporal shift module (TSM) shifts a fraction of channels one frame forward or backward, giving 2D networks temporal mixing at essentially zero extra FLOPs [64]. On top of per-frame features, a temporal convolutional network (TCN) applies 1-D convolutions along time.
  3. Recur over time. Recurrent networks such as LSTMs and GRUs carry a hidden state from frame to frame [65,66]. LRCN fed CNN features into an LSTM for activity recognition and video description [67]; ConvLSTM replaces the matrix products with convolutions so the state keeps its spatial layout [68].
  4. Attend over time. Transformers let every token attend to every other token [69]. Video transformers split a clip into space-time patches and must control the quadratic cost of attention over thousands of tokens [70–73].

Temporal convolutional networks

A TCN in the sense of Bai, Kolter and Koltun stacks residual blocks of causal, dilated 1-D convolutions: causal so the output at time t depends only on inputs up to t, dilated (by 1, 2, 4, …) so the receptive field grows exponentially with depth. In their benchmark this generic architecture matched or beat canonical LSTMs and GRUs on a range of sequence tasks while being simpler to train and fully parallel over time [74]. In video, Lea et al. used temporal convolutional encoder–decoders for fine-grained action segmentation [75], and MS-TCN stacks several dilated TCN stages, each refining the previous stage's frame-wise predictions, to reduce over-segmentation [76]. With kernel size k, two convolutions per block, and L blocks with dilations 20…2L−1, the receptive field is

RF = 1 + 2 (k − 1) (2L − 1)

Recurrent models

An RNN's advantage for video is streaming: it summarises the past in a fixed-size state, so each new frame costs the same regardless of how long the video has been running. LSTMs use input, forget and output gates to protect the state from vanishing gradients [65]; GRUs use two gates and fewer parameters [66]. Their disadvantages are sequential training (no parallelism over time) and a limited effective memory in practice, which is why long-range video models usually combine recurrence or convolution with an external memory such as a long-term feature bank [77].

Video transformers

A video transformer turns a clip into tokens, for example 16×16-pixel patches from each of 8 frames (8 × 14 × 14 = 1,568 tokens for 224×224 frames), and applies self-attention [25]. Full joint space-time attention compares every token with every other one, which costs (T·N)² for T frames of N patches. Several designs reduce this:

  • Divided attention (TimeSformer): each block first attends over time between patches at the same spatial position, then over space within a frame, costing T·N·(T + N). The authors found divided attention more accurate than joint attention on their benchmarks at lower cost [70].
  • Factorised encoders and tubelets (ViViT): tokens can be 3D "tubelets" spanning several frames, and the model can run a spatial transformer per frame followed by a temporal transformer over frame embeddings [71].
  • Local windows (Video Swin): attention inside shifted 3D windows, giving linear cost in the number of tokens [72].
  • Multiscale pooling (MViT): pooling attention progressively reduces the number of tokens while increasing channel width, like a CNN pyramid [73].

Transformers need a lot of data. VideoMAE showed that masked-autoencoder pre-training with very high masking ratios (90–95% of tubelet tokens) makes video transformers data-efficient, because redundant neighbouring frames make reconstruction too easy at lower ratios [78].

The listing implements a causal TCN head, a GRU head that can process a stream in chunks, and a divided space-time attention block in PyTorch [79]. It checks three properties that are easy to break: the TCN is causal (perturbing frame 40 changes nothing before frame 40), the GRU gives identical results when the sequence is processed in two chunks with the state carried over, and the attention block preserves the token layout.

temporal_models.py — causal TCN, streaming GRU, divided space-time attentionPython 3.13 · PyTorch 2.15 (CPU)
"""Three temporal heads over per-frame features: a causal dilated TCN, a GRU, and a
divided space-time attention block (TimeSformer-style)."""
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class CausalBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, ch, dilation, k=3, p=0.1):
        super().__init__()
        self.pad = (k - 1) * dilation                      # pad on the left only => causal
        self.conv1 = nn.Conv1d(ch, ch, k, dilation=dilation)
        self.conv2 = nn.Conv1d(ch, ch, k, dilation=dilation)
        self.drop = nn.Dropout(p)
    def forward(self, x):                                  # x: (B, C, T)
        y = self.drop(F.relu(self.conv1(F.pad(x, (self.pad, 0)))))
        y = self.drop(F.relu(self.conv2(F.pad(y, (self.pad, 0)))))
        return F.relu(x + y)                               # residual connection

class TCN(nn.Module):
    """Dilations 1, 2, 4, ... give a receptive field that grows exponentially with depth."""
    def __init__(self, d_in, ch, n_classes, levels=5, k=3):
        super().__init__()
        self.inp = nn.Conv1d(d_in, ch, 1)
        self.blocks = nn.Sequential(*[CausalBlock(ch, 2 ** i, k) for i in range(levels)])
        self.out = nn.Conv1d(ch, n_classes, 1)
        self.receptive_field = 1 + 2 * (k - 1) * (2 ** levels - 1)
    def forward(self, feats):                              # feats: (B, T, D) -> logits (B, T, K)
        return self.out(self.blocks(self.inp(feats.transpose(1, 2)))).transpose(1, 2)

class GRUHead(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_in, hidden, n_classes):
        super().__init__()
        self.gru = nn.GRU(d_in, hidden, batch_first=True)
        self.out = nn.Linear(hidden, n_classes)
    def forward(self, feats, h=None):                      # streaming: pass h back in on the next chunk
        y, h = self.gru(feats, h)
        return self.out(y), h

class DividedSTBlock(nn.Module):
    """Attend over time (same patch across frames), then over space (patches within a frame)."""
    def __init__(self, dim, heads):
        super().__init__()
        self.nt, self.ns, self.nm = nn.LayerNorm(dim), nn.LayerNorm(dim), nn.LayerNorm(dim)
        self.t_attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(dim, heads, batch_first=True)
        self.s_attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(dim, heads, batch_first=True)
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(dim, 4 * dim), nn.GELU(), nn.Linear(4 * dim, dim))
    def forward(self, x):                                  # x: (B, T, N, D) patch tokens
        B, T, N, D = x.shape
        xt = x.permute(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(B * N, T, D)    # sequences along time
        h = self.nt(xt); xt = xt + self.t_attn(h, h, h, need_weights=False)[0]
        xs = xt.reshape(B, N, T, D).permute(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(B * T, N, D)
        h = self.ns(xs); xs = xs + self.s_attn(h, h, h, need_weights=False)[0]
        x = xs.reshape(B, T, N, D)
        return x + self.mlp(self.nm(x))

Exercise 7.2 — Online or not?

MS-TCN uses non-causal (centred) dilated convolutions for action segmentation [76]. Why is that a good choice offline and a problem for a live system that must flag a fall within 0.5 s? How would you adapt it?

Show solution

A centred convolution at frame t uses frames up to t + RF/2, so boundaries are judged with context from both sides, which improves segmentation accuracy when the whole video is available. Live, the output for frame t cannot be computed until RF/2 frames later: with RF = 125 at 15 fps that is about 4 s, eight times the 0.5 s budget. Options: make the convolutions causal (pad left only, as in the listing), or allow a small fixed look-ahead (for example 7 frames ≈ 0.47 s) by padding asymmetrically, and accept the accuracy cost [3,74].

Exercise 7.3 — Token budgets

ViViT's tubelet embedding uses 2×16×16 (time × height × width) tubelets [71]. How many tokens does a 32-frame 224×224 clip produce? T' = 32/2 = 16 temporal positions, N = 14 × 14 = 196 spatial positions, 3,136 tokens. Joint: 3,136² ≈ 9.83 M pairs. Divided: 3,136 × (16 + 196) ≈ 0.665 M pairs, a ratio of about 14.8. Exercise 7.4 — Order matters

A TSN with average consensus scores 72% on Kinetics-like data but only slightly above chance on a dataset whose classes include both "pushing something left to right" and "pushing something right to left". Explain why, and name two ways to fix it.

Show solution

Average pooling is permutation-invariant: reversing the frames gives the same prediction, so classes that differ only in temporal order or direction are indistinguishable. Something-Something was designed around exactly such fine-grained temporal distinctions [16], whereas many Kinetics classes can be recognised from scene and objects in a single frame [11]. Fixes: replace the average with an order-aware model over segment features (TCN, GRU, or temporal transformer), or use a backbone with temporal modelling inside it, such as TSM, a 3D CNN or a video transformer [9,64,70].

Recognition

Recognition is the setting for which the architectures in §07 were mostly designed and compared: two-stream networks [12], 3D CNNs pre-trained on Kinetics [9], SlowFast [10] and video transformers [70–73]. Two caveats matter when reading results. Many Kinetics classes can be recognised from a single frame of scene context, so high accuracy does not imply temporal reasoning [11]; Something-Something was built to require it [16]. And reported accuracy usually involves multi-view testing (several clips × several crops per video, averaged), so the compute per video is a multiple of the per-clip figure.

Temporal localization

Localization finds actions inside long, untrimmed videos where most frames are background. Early methods slid windows of several lengths over the video and classified each one, in a cascade of proposal, classification and localization networks [84]. Boundary-based methods such as BSN predict, for each time step, the probability of being an action start, an end, or inside an action, and pair likely starts and ends into proposals with flexible durations [85]. ActionFormer applies a local-attention transformer to a feature sequence and, at every time step of a multi-scale pyramid, classifies the action and regresses the distances to its start and end, an anchor-free one-stage design analogous to object detectors [86]. Most pipelines run on pre-extracted clip features from a recognition backbone (I3D, SlowFast or a video transformer), because end-to-end training on full untrimmed videos is memory-bound.

Localization is scored like detection, with temporal IoU (tIoU) in place of box IoU. A prediction is a true positive if its tIoU with an unmatched ground-truth segment of the same class exceeds a threshold; average precision (AP) is computed per class from the ranked predictions, and mAP averages over classes. THUMOS'14 results are commonly reported at tIoU 0.3–0.7, and ActivityNet reports the average mAP over thresholds 0.5:0.05:0.95 [81,82]. Overlapping duplicates are removed with 1-D non-maximum suppression before scoring.

localization.py — temporal IoU, 1-D NMS and AP at a tIoU thresholdPython 3.13 · NumPy
"""Temporal action localization utilities: temporal IoU, 1-D NMS, and AP at a tIoU threshold
(the ActivityNet / THUMOS protocol in miniature)."""
import numpy as np

def tiou(seg, segs):
    """Temporal IoU of one segment [s, e] against an array of segments (N, 2)."""
    segs = np.asarray(segs, float).reshape(-1, 2)
    inter = np.clip(np.minimum(seg[1], segs[:, 1]) - np.maximum(seg[0], segs[:, 0]), 0, None)
    union = (seg[1] - seg[0]) + (segs[:, 1] - segs[:, 0]) - inter
    return inter / union

def nms_1d(segs, scores, thr=0.5):
    order, keep = np.argsort(-scores), []
    while order.size:
        i = order[0]; keep.append(i)
        order = order[1:][tiou(segs[i], segs[order[1:]]) < thr]
    return keep

def average_precision(preds, gts, thr):
    """preds: list of (video, start, end, score); gts: dict video -> (M, 2) array. One class."""
    preds = sorted(preds, key=lambda p: -p[3])
    used = {v: np.zeros(len(g), bool) for v, g in gts.items()}
    n_gt = sum(len(g) for g in gts.values())
    tp = np.zeros(len(preds))
    for k, (v, s, e, _) in enumerate(preds):
        if v not in gts or len(gts[v]) == 0: continue
        ov = tiou((s, e), gts[v]); ov[used[v]] = -1          # each GT may be matched once
        j = int(np.argmax(ov))
        if ov[j] >= thr:
            tp[k], used[v][j] = 1, True
    ctp = np.cumsum(tp)
    rec, prec = ctp / n_gt, ctp / np.arange(1, len(preds) + 1)
    prec = np.maximum.accumulate(prec[::-1])[::-1]           # monotone precision envelope
    r = np.r_[0, rec]
    return float(np.sum((r[1:] - r[:-1]) * prec))             # area under the PR step curve

09Video summarization and anomaly detection

Both tasks answer the question "which parts of this video deserve a human's attention?". Summarization picks the most informative or interesting parts for a viewer; anomaly detection picks the parts that do not look like normal footage. Both depend on good temporal segmentation and on scores that are comparable across time.

Shots, keyframes and summaries

Edited video is made of shots, continuous takes separated by cuts or gradual transitions. Classical shot-boundary detection compares colour histograms or other features of consecutive frames and declares a cut where the difference is large; gradual transitions (fades, dissolves) are harder because the change is spread over many frames [87]. Learned detectors such as TransNet V2 are now standard for large collections [88]. A keyframe summary picks representative frames per shot; a video skim picks segments and plays them in order.

Benchmark summarization follows a protocol popularised with SumMe (25 personal videos with human-made summaries) and TVSum (50 videos with frame-level importance scores from 20 annotators each) [89,90]. A model predicts a score per frame; the video is cut into segments (often with kernel temporal segmentation); each segment's value is its total score; and a 0/1 knapsack picks segments to maximise value under a budget of 15% of the video's length. Zhang et al.'s vsLSTM, a bidirectional LSTM that predicts frame importance, established this pipeline for deep models [91,92].

Otani et al. showed that under this protocol, randomly generated importance scores reach F1 scores comparable to published methods, because the segmentation and the knapsack, not the scores, determine much of the result: the knapsack prefers many short segments, and short segments dominate the overlap measure [93]. They recommend rank-correlation measures between predicted and human frame scores instead. The listing reproduces the mechanism in miniature.

"""Shot-based video summarization: histogram cut detection, per-shot scores,
0/1 knapsack under a 15% duration budget, and one keyframe per selected shot."""
import numpy as np
import cv2

def synth_video(rng, shot_lens=(40, 90, 25, 120, 60, 45), size=96):
    """Shots with distinct palettes plus camera noise and a moving square; returns frames and true cuts."""
    frames, cuts = [], np.cumsum(shot_lens)[:-1].tolist()
    for s, n in enumerate(shot_lens):
        base = rng.integers(0, 256, 3)
        for k in range(n):
            f = np.full((size, size, 3), base, np.uint8)
            f = cv2.add(f, rng.integers(0, 25, f.shape, dtype=np.uint8))       # sensor noise
            x = (k * 2) % (size - 20)
            cv2.rectangle(f, (x, 30), (x + 20, 50), (255 - base).tolist(), -1)  # moving object
            frames.append(f)
    return frames, cuts

def hsv_hist(f, bins=(16, 4, 4)):
    h = cv2.calcHist([cv2.cvtColor(f, cv2.COLOR_BGR2HSV)], [0, 1, 2], None, bins, [0, 180, 0, 256, 0, 256])
    return cv2.normalize(h, None).flatten()

def detect_cuts(frames, k=6.0):
    """Cut where the frame-to-frame histogram distance is far above its running median."""
    H = [hsv_hist(f) for f in frames]
    d = np.array([cv2.compareHist(H[i - 1], H[i], cv2.HISTCMP_BHATTACHARYYA) for i in range(1, len(H))])
    med = np.median(d); mad = np.median(np.abs(d - med)) + 1e-6
    return [int(i) + 1 for i in np.where(d > med + k * 1.4826 * mad)[0]], np.array(H)

def knapsack(values, weights, capacity):
    """Exact 0/1 knapsack by dynamic programming over integer weights (frames)."""
    n = len(values); best = np.zeros((n + 1, capacity + 1))
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        w, v = weights[i - 1], values[i - 1]
        best[i] = best[i - 1]
        if w <= capacity:
            best[i, w:] = np.maximum(best[i - 1, w:], best[i - 1, :capacity + 1 - w] + v)
    chosen, c = [], capacity
    for i in range(n, 0, -1):
        if best[i, c] != best[i - 1, c]:
            chosen.append(i - 1); c -= weights[i - 1]
    return sorted(chosen)

Anomaly detection

Video anomaly detection flags events that deviate from normal activity in a scene, such as a person running in a corridor, a vehicle on a pedestrian path, or a fight. Anomalies are rare, diverse and often undefined in advance, so the dominant formulation is one-class: train only on normal footage and score test frames by how poorly they fit [94]. Variants differ in what "fit" means:

  • Reconstruction. An autoencoder trained on normal clips reconstructs them well and anomalies badly; Hasan et al. learned such a model of "temporal regularity" [95].
  • Prediction. Predict the next frame from previous ones and score the prediction error; Liu et al. argued this is a better anomaly signal than reconstruction because anomalies are, by definition, unexpected [96].
  • Density on features. Fit a distribution to features of normal data (from a pre-trained network, or from tracks: speeds, directions, dwell times) and score by negative log-likelihood or Mahalanobis distance. This is what the listing does.
  • Weak supervision. With video-level labels only ("this video contains an anomaly somewhere"), Sultani et al. trained a multiple-instance ranking model that pushes the highest-scoring segment of an anomalous video above the highest of a normal one, and released the UCF-Crime dataset of real surveillance videos [97].

Benchmarks include the UCSD pedestrian datasets and CUHK Avenue for one-class detection [98,99]. The usual metric is frame-level ROC AUC: the probability that a randomly chosen anomalous frame receives a higher score than a randomly chosen normal one. AUC is threshold-free, which is convenient for comparison and inconvenient for deployment, where an operator needs a threshold and a false-alarm rate.

"""One-class video anomaly detection on track-level features: fit a Gaussian to normal footage,
score test frames by Mahalanobis distance, smooth in time, report frame-level ROC AUC."""
import numpy as np

def frame_features(speeds, headings):
    """Per-frame descriptor from the tracks visible in that frame."""
    hist = np.histogram(headings, bins=8, range=(-np.pi, np.pi))[0] + 1e-3
    p = hist / hist.sum()
    return np.array([len(speeds), speeds.mean(), speeds.max(), -(p * np.log(p)).sum()])

def simulate(n_frames, rng, anomalous=()):
    X, y = [], []
    for k in range(n_frames):
        n = rng.poisson(8) + 1
        speeds = np.abs(rng.normal(1.4, 0.35, n))                 # walking, m/s
        heads = rng.choice([0, np.pi], n) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, n)  # a corridor: two main directions
        is_anom = any(a <= k < b for a, b in anomalous)
        if is_anom:                                                # someone running
            speeds = np.r_[speeds, abs(rng.normal(2.4, 0.6))]
            heads = np.r_[heads, rng.choice([0, np.pi]) + rng.normal(0, 0.6)]
        X.append(frame_features(speeds, heads)); y.append(is_anom)
    return np.array(X), np.array(y)

def roc_auc(scores, labels):
    """Probability a random anomalous frame scores above a random normal one (Mann-Whitney U)."""
    order = np.argsort(scores); ranks = np.empty(len(scores)); ranks[order] = np.arange(1, len(scores) + 1)
    pos = labels.astype(bool); n1, n0 = pos.sum(), (~pos).sum()
    return (ranks[pos].sum() - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * n0)

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

Exercise 9.2 — Why not threshold every frame?

An anomaly detector runs at 25 fps on 40 cameras. The per-frame false-alarm rate at the chosen threshold is 0.1%. How many false-alarm frames per hour does the control room see, and what two changes reduce the number of alerts without lowering frame-level recall much?

Show solution

25 × 3,600 × 40 = 3.6 million frames per hour; 0.1% is 3,600 false-alarm frames per hour, one per second. (1) Smooth scores over time (as in the listing) and require the score to stay high for a minimum duration, which removes isolated spikes. (2) Raise alerts per event, merging contiguous alarm frames into one alert with a cool-down, so a 3-second false positive costs one alert instead of 75. Per-camera thresholds calibrated on each camera's own normal footage also help, because "normal" differs by scene [94,98].

Exercise 9.3 — Choose the formulation

A city has 2,000 hours of normal footage from a tunnel camera and 30 short clips of past incidents (stopped vehicles, debris, wrong-way drivers), labelled only as "contains an incident". Which formulation would you start with, and how would you use the 30 clips?

Show solution

Start one-class: model normal traffic (for example track-level speed and heading statistics per lane, or prediction error [96]), because 2,000 hours describe normal behaviour well and the incident types are open-ended. Use the 30 clips as a validation set to choose features, smoothing and thresholds, not for training. If more labelled incidents accumulate, a weakly supervised MIL ranker can be trained on video-level labels [97], and wrong-way driving, which has a crisp definition, is better handled by an explicit rule on track headings than by any anomaly score.

10Real-time processing and latency constraints

An offline benchmark asks how accurate a model is. A live system also asks how old its answer is when it arrives. If processing a frame takes longer than the time between frames, the system must either fall behind or skip frames, and while it computes, the world keeps moving. Li, Wang and Ramanan formalised this as streaming perception: a prediction is scored against the ground truth at the moment the prediction becomes available, not the moment its input frame was captured. Under this evaluation, a faster, less accurate detector can beat a slower, more accurate one, and forecasting (for example with a Kalman filter) to compensate for latency improves results [3].

Throughput, latency and queues

Two numbers describe a pipeline. Throughput is how many frames per second it completes. Latency is the time from capture to result for one frame. They are linked through any queue in between: by Little's law, the average number of frames waiting equals the arrival rate times the average time each spends in the system, L = λW [100]. If frames arrive faster than they are served, L grows without bound and so does W. An unbounded queue in front of a slow model is therefore a latency bug: throughput looks fine, but every result is increasingly stale.

The simulation below makes this concrete. A 30 fps camera feeds a detector that needs 45 ± 8 ms per frame (about 22 fps). Three policies are compared: an unbounded FIFO queue, a one-slot buffer that always holds the newest frame (dropping stale ones), and running the detector on every third frame with a 2 ms Kalman-only update in between.

realtime.py — discrete-event simulation of three frame-scheduling policiesPython 3.13 · NumPy
"""Discrete-event simulation of a 30 fps camera feeding a detector that needs ~45 ms per frame.
Compares an unbounded FIFO queue, a size-1 'latest frame' buffer, and detect-every-k with
cheap Kalman-only updates in between."""
import numpy as np

def simulate(policy, seconds=20, fps=30, det_ms=45, jitter_ms=8, trk_ms=2, k=3, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    arrivals = np.arange(0, seconds * 1000, 1000 / fps)       # capture timestamps (ms)
    busy_until, queue, lat, dropped, n = 0.0, [], [], 0, 0
    for t in arrivals:
        # serve frames that can start before this arrival
        while queue and max(busy_until, queue[0]) <= t:
            ts = queue.pop(0)
            start = max(busy_until, ts)
            heavy = policy != "detect_every_k" or n % k == 0
            cost = (det_ms + rng.normal(0, jitter_ms)) if heavy else trk_ms
            busy_until = start + max(cost, 1); n += 1
            lat.append(busy_until - ts)                      # capture -> result latency
        if policy == "latest" and queue:                     # overwrite the stale frame
            queue[-1] = t; dropped += 1
        else:
            queue.append(t)
    lat = np.array(lat)
    return dict(processed_fps=n / seconds, dropped=dropped, backlog=len(queue),
                p50=np.percentile(lat, 50), p95=np.percentile(lat, 95), max=lat.max())

for pol in ("fifo", "latest", "detect_every_k"):
    r = simulate(pol)
    print(f"{pol:15s} out {r['processed_fps']:5.1f} fps | dropped {r['dropped']:4d} | backlog {r['backlog']:4d} | "
          f"latency p50 {r['p50']:7.1f} ms  p95 {r['p95']:7.1f} ms  max {r['max']:7.1f} ms")
output (actual run)

Over 20 simulated seconds, FIFO processed 22 fps as expected, but 155 frames were still queued at the end and the median result was 2.7 s old. The latest-frame buffer kept the same throughput and dropped 154 frames, but every result was at most 91 ms old. Detecting on every third frame and tracking in between delivered a result for every frame (29.9 fps out) with a 95th-percentile latency of 53 ms. This last pattern, a heavy model on keyframes and something light in between, is general: deep feature flow runs the expensive network on sparse keyframes and propagates its features to intermediate frames with optical flow [101], and production video analytics frameworks such as NVIDIA DeepStream expose a detector "interval" with a tracker filling the skipped frames [102].

Budgeting a live pipeline

  • Write the budget down. At 30 fps the frame period is 33.3 ms. Decode, preprocessing, inference, association, and output must fit in it on average, and the tail (p95, p99) must fit whatever latency the application tolerates.
  • Move decode off the CPU. Hardware decoders (NVDEC on NVIDIA GPUs) decode many streams in parallel and leave frames in GPU memory, avoiding a copy to host and back [15,102].
  • Batch across streams, not across time. Batching frames from many cameras raises throughput; batching consecutive frames of one camera adds latency equal to the batch length.
  • Prefer bounded queues and decide explicitly what to drop. For tracking, dropping frames lowers the effective frame rate, which hurts IoU-based association (§05 Exercise 5.3); a detect-every-k scheme with prediction in between degrades more gracefully.
  • Choose efficient temporal models. TSM adds temporal modelling to a 2D network at essentially no extra FLOPs, X3D targets low-compute regimes, and causal TCNs or GRUs process streams incrementally [63,64,74].
  • Compensate for latency. If results are 60 ms old, forecast the state 60 ms ahead with the tracker's motion model before acting on it [3].

Exercise 10.1 — Little's law

Frames arrive at 30 fps; the model serves 22.2 fps. With an unbounded FIFO starting empty, how many frames are queued after 20 s and roughly how long does the last frame wait? Compare with the simulation output.

Show solution

The backlog grows at 30 − 22.2 = 7.8 frames per second, so about 156 frames after 20 s (the simulation reports 155). The last arriving frame waits for all of them: 156 / 22.2 ≈ 7.0 s, plus its own service time. The simulation's maximum recorded latency (5.2 s) is lower because it only records latencies of frames that finished within the 20 s window; the frames still queued would have waited longer. Little's law, L = λW, makes the same point in steady state: when λ exceeds the service rate there is no steady state, and W grows linearly with time [100].

Exercise 10.2 — Faster but worse?

Detector A has 0.52 AP offline and 120 ms latency; detector B has 0.44 AP and 30 ms latency. Pedestrians move about 1.5 m/s, and a 1 m error makes a box useless at the camera's scale. Which should a live system use, and what would make A competitive?

Show solution

With A, results are at least 120 ms old (and more if frames queue), during which a pedestrian moves about 18 cm, and A can only process about 8 fps, so the next update is another 120 ms away. B is four times fresher and runs every frame. Under streaming evaluation B is likely to win, which matches Li et al.'s finding that streaming accuracy favours faster detectors [3]. A becomes competitive with forecasting (propagate each box by its Kalman velocity to the current time) and with detect-every-k scheduling, where A's accuracy is used on keyframes and a tracker covers the gaps [101,102].

Exercise 10.3 — Where is the budget going?

A 1080p 30 fps pipeline measures: decode on CPU 14 ms, resize and normalize on CPU 6 ms, host-to-GPU copy 3 ms, detector 15 ms, tracker 1 ms. It sustains only 25 fps although no stage exceeds 33 ms. Why, and what is the first fix?

Show solution

The stages run sequentially in one thread, so the per-frame time is their sum, 39 ms ≈ 25.6 fps. Either pipeline the stages (decode frame t+1 while the GPU runs the detector on frame t), which bounds throughput by the slowest stage (15 ms), or remove CPU work: hardware decode with NVDEC and GPU preprocessing eliminate the 14 + 6 + 3 ms on the host [15,102]. Pipelining raises throughput but not single-frame latency; moving work to hardware reduces both.

11Summary, checklist and what was verified

Video adds one axis and three kinds of problems: engineering (compressed, timestamped streams that are cheap to read sequentially and expensive to seek), identity (keeping a consistent name on each object through misses, crossings and occlusions), and temporal semantics (recognising and locating patterns that exist only across frames). The tracking problems are solved mostly by careful probability and bookkeeping on top of a good detector; the semantic problems mostly by temporal architectures trained on large video datasets; and both must respect a latency budget when they run live.
