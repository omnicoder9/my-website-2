Measuring Vision Systems: Evaluation, Reliability and Responsible Deployment

A vision model must be evaluated against the conditions in which it will actually operate. This article works through the metrics, stress tests, data checks, threat models and monitoring that turn a benchmark score into evidence that a system is fit for use.

00Operating conditions first

A single number on a public benchmark answers a narrow question: how well does this model do on images drawn the same way as that benchmark's test split? Deployment asks a different question. The camera, optics, lighting, weather, population of objects and people, and the cost of each kind of mistake all differ from the benchmark. Models trained on one dataset routinely lose accuracy when tested on another collected for the same task [1], and even a carefully replicated test set for ImageNet produced accuracy drops of 11% to 14% for a wide range of models [2].

The automotive industry formalised this idea as the operational design domain (ODD): the operating conditions under which a system is designed to function, including environmental, geographical and time-of-day restrictions [3]. The concept transfers to any vision product. Before choosing metrics, write down the ODD: the sensors, mounting positions, resolutions, frame rates, illumination range, weather, scene types, object sizes, and the populations of people who will appear in front of the camera. Every later section of this article tests one face of that description.

01Classification metrics

Every binary classifier with a threshold produces four counts: true positives (TP), false positives (FP), false negatives (FN) and true negatives (TN). The common metrics are ratios of these counts [4]:

accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + FP + FN + TN) precision = TP / (TP + FP) "when it fires, how often is it right?" recall = TP / (TP + FN) "of the real cases, how many did it find?" F1 = 2 · precision · recall / (precision + recall)

Accuracy hides the minority class. With 5% positives, a model that always answers "negative" scores 95% accuracy and 0% recall. Precision and recall are each blind to one type of error, which is why they are reported as a pair or combined into F1, their harmonic mean [4]. For multi-class problems, compute the per-class values and average them: macro averaging weights each class equally, which exposes poor performance on rare classes; micro averaging pools the counts and is dominated by common classes [4].

Threshold-free metrics: ROC-AUC and precision-recall

Most models output a score, and the threshold is a product decision. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate as the threshold sweeps from high to low [5]. The area under it, ROC-AUC, has a direct interpretation: it is the probability that a randomly chosen positive receives a higher score than a randomly chosen negative, which is the Mann-Whitney U statistic rescaled [6].

ROC-AUC does not change when class prevalence changes, because both of its axes are rates conditioned on the true class. That is a strength for comparing rankers and a weakness for rare-event detection: thousands of false alarms barely move the false positive rate when negatives number in the millions. Precision-recall (PR) curves expose this, and are more informative than ROC curves on imbalanced data [7]. A curve dominates in ROC space if and only if it dominates in PR space, but the areas under the two curves can rank models differently [8].

Uncertainty in the estimate

A test set is a sample, so every metric computed on it has sampling error. With 50 positive images, one extra miss moves recall by two percentage points. Report confidence intervals. The percentile bootstrap is a general method: resample the test set with replacement many times, recompute the metric, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles [9]. When images are correlated (frames from the same video, images from the same patient), resample whole groups rather than single images, or the interval will be too narrow.

02Detection metrics

A detector must say what and where. Intersection over union (IoU), also called the Jaccard index, scores the "where" for a pair of boxes:

IoU(A, B) = area(A ∩ B) / area(A ∪ B) range 0 to 1

A prediction counts as a true positive when its IoU with an unmatched ground-truth box of the same class reaches a threshold. PASCAL VOC used 0.5 [10]. Each ground-truth object can be matched at most once, so a second box on the same object is a false positive. Predictions are processed in descending confidence order, which is what makes the procedure a ranking evaluation [10].

Average precision and mean AP

Sweeping the confidence threshold over the ranked detections traces a precision-recall curve for each class. Average precision (AP) summarises it as the area under an interpolated version of the curve, where precision at each recall is replaced by the maximum precision at any equal or higher recall [10][11]. VOC 2007 sampled this envelope at 11 recall points; later VOC editions integrated over all points [10][11]. Mean AP (mAP) averages AP over classes.

The COCO benchmark changed two things [12]. It averages AP over ten IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05, so the headline number (written AP or AP@[.50:.95]) rewards precise localisation. It also reports AP for small (area below 32² pixels), medium and large (above 96² pixels) objects. The official COCO evaluation samples the precision envelope at 101 recall points [11]. COCO calls this averaged figure "AP" and does not distinguish AP from mAP, which causes confusion when papers are compared.

Comparing numbers

AP@0.5 (VOC style) and AP@[.50:.95] (COCO style) for the same model can differ by 20 points or more. Before comparing two reported figures, check the IoU thresholds, the interpolation, the area ranges, the maximum detections per image, and whether "difficult" or crowd regions were ignored [11].

Raising the IoU threshold to 0.75 turns the loose box on img2 into a false positive as well, which is why AP drops. Generalised IoU (GIoU) extends IoU to non-overlapping boxes so it can be used as a training loss, but evaluation conventionally still uses plain IoU [13].

03Segmentation metrics

Semantic segmentation assigns a class to each pixel, so the confusion matrix is built over pixels. Three summary measures are standard, all defined in the fully convolutional network paper that set the modern benchmark convention [14]: pixel accuracy (fraction of correctly labelled pixels), mean accuracy (per-class recall averaged over classes) and mean IoU (per-class IoU averaged over classes).

The Dice coefficient, introduced in ecology to measure the association between species [15], is the standard overlap measure in medical imaging:

Dice(A, B) = 2 |A ∩ B| / (|A| + |B|) = 2TP / (2TP + FP + FN) IoU(A, B) = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| = TP / (TP + FP + FN) Dice = 2 · IoU / (1 + IoU) so Dice ≥ IoU, equal only at 0 and 1

Dice on pixels is identical to F1 on pixels. Because the two are monotonically related, they always rank a pair of masks the same way for a single class, but averages over images or classes can differ. Pixel accuracy is dominated by large classes such as road, sky and background. Small, safety-relevant classes need per-class IoU or Dice, and boundary-sensitive tasks need a boundary metric as well. The Metrics Reloaded consensus recommends choosing metrics from the properties of the problem (structure size, boundary importance, presence of empty references) rather than by habit [16].

04Tracking metrics

A multi-object tracker must detect objects in every frame and keep a consistent identity for each object over time. Metrics differ in how much weight they put on each of those jobs.

  • MOTA (multiple object tracking accuracy), from the CLEAR MOT metrics, sums misses, false positives and identity switches over all frames and divides by the number of ground-truth objects: MOTA = 1 − (FN + FP + IDSW) / GT [17]. It can be negative. Because detection errors vastly outnumber identity switches in typical scenes, MOTA mostly measures detection.
  • An identity switch (IDSW) occurs when a ground-truth object is matched to a different tracker ID than the one it was last matched to [17].
  • Track fragmentation (Frag) counts the times a ground-truth trajectory is interrupted, that is, goes from tracked to untracked and back [18]. The same work introduced mostly tracked (MT, at least 80% of the trajectory covered) and mostly lost (ML, at most 20% covered) [18].
  • IDF1 finds a single one-to-one mapping between ground-truth identities and tracker identities for the whole sequence, chosen to maximise identity true positives (IDTP), then computes IDF1 = 2·IDTP / (2·IDTP + IDFP + IDFN) [19]. It measures how long the tracker keeps the right identity, which matters for counting, re-identification and dwell time.
  • HOTA (higher order tracking accuracy) separates detection accuracy (DetA) from association accuracy (AssA), computes HOTAα = √(DetAα · AssAα) at each localisation threshold α, and averages over α from 0.05 to 0.95 [20]. Its creators showed it balances detection and association more evenly than MOTA or IDF1, and MOTChallenge now reports it alongside them [20][21].

Per-frame matching uses the Hungarian algorithm, which solves the minimum-cost one-to-one assignment problem exactly [22]. The listing uses point distance for brevity; real benchmarks gate on box IoU.

Output
{'MOTA': 0.85, 'IDF1': 0.737, 'IDSW': 1, 'Frag': 1, 'FP': 0, 'FN': 2}

Which tracking metric to optimise depends on the product. A collision-avoidance system cares mostly about detection in every frame (MOTA, DetA). A retail footfall counter that double-counts every re-acquired shopper cares about identity (IDF1, AssA, IDSW). Report several, and look at raw counts of switches and fragments, not only the composite scores.

05Depth and geometry

Dense depth

Monocular and stereo depth estimators are usually evaluated on valid ground-truth pixels (for example, projected LiDAR returns, which are sparse) with a set of metrics popularised by Eigen and colleagues [23] and adopted by the KITTI benchmark [24]:

AbsRel = mean( |d - d*| / d* ) SqRel = mean( (d - d*)² / d* ) RMSE = sqrt( mean( (d - d*)² ) ) in metres RMSElog = sqrt( mean( (log d - log d*)² ) ) δ < 1.25 = fraction of pixels with max(d/d*, d*/d) < 1.25 (also 1.25², 1.25³)

Here d is the prediction and d* the ground truth. AbsRel and δ are scale-relative, so a 1 m error at 50 m counts less than a 1 m error at 2 m. RMSE in metres is dominated by distant pixels. A system for close-range manipulation and one for highway driving should therefore not be compared on the same single metric. Monocular methods trained without metric supervision are often evaluated after median scaling of each prediction to the ground truth; state whether this was done, because it hides scale errors that matter in deployment.

Reprojection error

For calibration, structure-from-motion and pose estimation, the basic geometric metric is reprojection error: the image distance between an observed feature point and the projection of its estimated 3D point through the estimated camera [25]. Bundle adjustment minimises the sum of squared reprojection errors over all cameras and points [25], and camera calibration reports the residual root-mean-square reprojection error as its quality measure [26].

Output

With 0.3 px of detector noise per axis, the expected 2D RMS error is about 0.42 px, so 0.46 px is consistent with a correct calibration. A 2.5% error in focal length raises it to 3.5 px. A low reprojection error on the calibration images is necessary but not sufficient: it can still hide overfitting if the calibration target did not cover the whole field of view and range of depths. Hold out some views and report the held-out error.

06System performance

A model that is accurate but misses its latency budget, overheats the device or exhausts memory is not deployable. Measure the full pipeline on the target hardware, not the model alone on a workstation GPU.

MeasureDefinitionWhat to report
LatencyTime from frame capture (or request arrival) to result availablep50, p95, p99, and maximum; per stage
ThroughputCompleted inferences per second under sustained loadAt a stated batch size and concurrency
FPSFrames processed per second in a streaming pipelineSustained, after thermal steady state
MemoryPeak resident memory (host and accelerator)Peak, not average; include runtime overhead
Power and energyAverage watts; joules per inference = watts × secondsMeasured at the wall or rail, with idle baseline subtracted

The MLPerf Inference benchmark formalises these trade-offs as scenarios: single-stream (one query at a time, scored by 90th-percentile latency), multi-stream, server (Poisson arrivals with a latency bound) and offline (pure throughput) [27]. Choose the scenario that matches the product: a robot arm is single-stream, a cloud photo-tagging service is server, and overnight video indexing is offline.

Throughput and latency are linked but distinct. Batching raises throughput by amortising fixed costs, but each frame waits for its batch to fill. Little's law, L = λW, relates the mean number of items in a stable system to the arrival rate and mean time in the system [28]; a pipeline receiving 30 frames per second with 100 ms mean latency holds about 3 frames in flight at once. Tail latency matters more than the mean in systems with many components, because the slowest component determines when the whole request finishes [29].

On accelerators, energy is dominated by data movement rather than arithmetic: fetching a value from off-chip DRAM costs orders of magnitude more energy than a multiply-accumulate [30]. Memory footprint and power therefore move together, and quantisation or pruning can reduce energy by more than it reduces operation count. Report energy and the hardware used, following guidance on systematic energy reporting for machine learning [31].

Benchmarking pitfalls
  • GPU work is asynchronous. Without a device synchronise before stopping the timer, you measure only the kernel launch.
  • Exclude warm-up iterations (memory allocation, autotuning, JIT compilation), and run long enough to reach thermal steady state. Embedded devices throttle clocks when hot.
  • Fix input resolution, batch size, precision and power mode, and state them. Results at FP16 with a "max performance" power profile do not predict battery operation.
  • Measure end to end with real decode and pre-processing. Synthetic tensors already in device memory skip the slowest stages.

07Robustness

Deployed cameras see conditions that curated datasets under-sample: night, glare, rain, fog, snow, dirty lenses, motion blur, partial occlusion, compression artefacts, and new camera models with different colour processing. Two families of tests are needed.

Synthetic corruptions

ImageNet-C applies 15 corruption types (noise, blur, weather and digital categories) at five severity levels to the ImageNet validation set [32]. Its summary score, the corruption error (CE), sums a model's top-1 errors over the five severities of a corruption and divides by the same sum for a fixed baseline (AlexNet); mean CE (mCE) averages over corruptions [32]. The same corruptions applied to detection benchmarks (PASCAL-C, COCO-C, Cityscapes-C) reduced detector performance to between 30% and 60% of clean performance [33].

Natural distribution shift

Synthetic corruptions are reproducible but do not cover everything. Real shift includes new geography, new camera hardware, seasonal change and changing populations. Benchmarks such as WILDS collect datasets with natural shifts, including camera-trap images from new locations and satellite imagery from new regions and years, and show large gaps between in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance [34]. A frequent cause is shortcut learning: models exploit features that correlate with the label in the training data, such as background, texture or a hospital-specific marker, and fail when the correlation breaks [35]. Predictive uncertainty also degrades under shift, so a model that is calibrated in distribution can be over-confident out of distribution [36].

The practical recipe: slice the test set by ODD attributes (time of day, weather, camera, site) and report the metric per slice; add synthetic corruptions for conditions you cannot collect; and hold out entire sites or cameras to estimate performance on new installations.

08Data quality

Evaluation is only as trustworthy as the test data. Four problems recur.

Annotation consistency

Label noise in test sets is common: a study of ten widely used benchmarks estimated an average of at least 3.3% label errors, including at least 6% of the ImageNet validation set, enough to change which model ranks first [37]. Measure agreement by having a sample labelled twice. For categorical labels, Cohen's kappa corrects raw agreement for the agreement expected by chance [38]:

κ = (p_o − p_e) / (1 − p_e) p_o = observed agreement, p_e = chance agreement

For boxes and masks, compute IoU between two annotators' outputs for the same object. This inter-annotator IoU is an upper bound on meaningful model precision: demanding IoU 0.95 from a model when humans agree only to 0.85 measures noise.

Leakage

Leakage is the introduction of information into training that would not be available in deployment, producing over-optimistic estimates [39]. In vision the commonest form is near-duplicates across the split: adjacent video frames, burst photos, the same scene from a slightly different angle, or the same patient in multiple scans. Even curated benchmarks are affected: 3.3% of CIFAR-10 and 10% of CIFAR-100 test images have near-duplicates in the training set [40]. The fix is to split by the unit that will be new in deployment (video, camera, site, patient, day), and to search for near-duplicates with perceptual hashes or embedding similarity before freezing the test set.

Imbalance

Class imbalance hurts convolutional network training, and oversampling of minority classes was found to be the most effective general remedy in a systematic study [41]. For evaluation, imbalance means that aggregate metrics are dominated by the majority class: report per-class metrics and macro averages, and make sure the test set has enough examples of each rare class to give a usable confidence interval.

Coverage

Coverage asks whether the test set samples the whole ODD. Build a table of ODD attributes (lighting, weather, camera, object size, demographic group) against the number of test examples in each cell. Empty or tiny cells are untested conditions, and the evaluation report should say so. Datasheets for datasets provide a structured way to record how data was collected, labelled and split, and which conditions it covers [42].

09Security

A vision model is an attack surface. NIST's taxonomy of adversarial machine learning organises attacks by the attacker's goal (availability, integrity, privacy), their knowledge of the model, and the lifecycle stage they target [43].

Adversarial examples (evasion)

Small, deliberately chosen perturbations, often imperceptible to people, can change a network's prediction [44]. Goodfellow and colleagues argued that this follows from locally linear behaviour in high dimensions, and introduced the fast gradient sign method (FGSM), a single step x' = x + ε·sign(∇x L) [45]. Projected gradient descent (PGD) iterates that step and projects back into the allowed ε-ball; it is the standard first-order attack for evaluating and training robust models [46]. Attacks also work in the physical world: printed stickers on a stop sign caused misclassification in drive-by tests [47].

Evaluating robustness correctly is hard. Many proposed defences only masked gradients and were broken by adapted attacks [48]. Use strong, parameter-free attack suites such as AutoAttack [49] together with optimisation attacks like Carlini-Wagner [50], report the threat model (norm and ε), and where a guarantee is required, use certified methods such as randomised smoothing, which proves an L2 robustness radius for each prediction [51].

Output

eps=2/255  FGSM acc=1.000  PGD acc=1.000
eps=4/255  FGSM acc=1.000  PGD acc=1.000
eps=6/255  FGSM acc=0.950  PGD acc=0.950
eps=8/255  FGSM acc=0.428  PGD acc=0.428

On a linear model, FGSM is already the optimal L∞ attack, so PGD adds nothing; on deep networks PGD is substantially stronger [46]. A perturbation of at most 8/255 per pixel, eight intensity levels on an 8-bit scale, takes the model from 100% to 43% accuracy. Exercise 13 shows why: the change in the logit grows with the input dimension.

Data poisoning and backdoors

An attacker who can insert training examples can implant a backdoor: BadNets showed that a network trained on data with a small trigger pattern performs normally on clean inputs but outputs an attacker-chosen class whenever the trigger is present, including in a traffic-sign detector [52]. Clean-label attacks achieve targeted misclassification with correctly labelled poison images, so label auditing alone does not detect them [53]. Defences start with provenance: know where every training image came from, restrict who can add data, and evaluate on trusted held-out data that never passed through the open pipeline.

Model extraction and privacy attacks

Prediction APIs leak the model. Extraction attacks reconstruct functionally equivalent models from query responses [54], and Knockoff Nets copied the functionality of black-box image classifiers by querying them with natural images from other distributions [55]. Membership inference determines whether a given record was in the training set [56], and model inversion has recovered recognisable face images from a face-recognition model's confidence scores [57]. Mitigations include returning labels instead of full probability vectors, rounding scores, rate-limiting and per-client query monitoring; PRADA detects extraction by testing whether the distribution of a client's successive queries deviates from natural behaviour [58].

10Privacy and ethics

Biometric privacy and consent

Faces, gait and other body measurements identify people. The EU General Data Protection Regulation defines biometric data as personal data resulting from specific technical processing of physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics that allows or confirms unique identification, such as facial images (Article 4(14)), and prohibits processing it for unique identification unless a listed exception, such as explicit consent, applies (Article 9) [59]. The EU AI Act prohibits, among other practices, building facial-recognition databases through untargeted scraping of images from the internet or CCTV, emotion recognition in workplaces and schools except for medical or safety reasons, and real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement outside narrow exceptions (Article 5) [60]. In the United States, Illinois' Biometric Information Privacy Act requires informed written consent before collecting biometric identifiers such as face geometry and gives individuals a private right of action [61].

Engineering practices follow from this: minimise collection (detect a person without identifying them where the task allows), blur or crop faces and licence plates at the edge before storage, set retention limits, record the lawful basis and consent for every dataset, and keep evaluation data under the same controls as training data.

Demographic performance disparities

Aggregate accuracy can conceal large differences between groups of people. Gender Shades audited three commercial gender classifiers and found error rates up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women against at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men [62]. NIST's evaluation of 189 face-recognition algorithms found false positive rates that varied by factors of 10 to more than 100 across demographic groups for many algorithms [63].

Disaggregated evaluation is the remedy: report error rates for each relevant group and intersection, with confidence intervals so that small groups are not over- or under-interpreted. Which error rates must be equal depends on the harm. Equality of opportunity, for example, requires equal true positive rates across groups [64]. For face verification, false matches and false non-matches cause different harms (wrongful identification versus denied access), so both are reported separately. The Wilson score interval behaves well for the small counts typical of minority groups [65].

Model cards are a standard format for publishing this kind of disaggregated result together with the intended use and out-of-scope uses [66], and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework places such measurement within a broader process of governing, mapping, measuring and managing AI risk [67].

11Deployment monitoring

Machine learning systems degrade silently: inputs change, upstream components change, and no exception is raised [68]. Production readiness rubrics include monitoring of input distributions, prediction distributions and model staleness as first-class tests [69].

Drift detection

Dataset shift has several forms: covariate shift (input distribution changes), prior or label shift (class frequencies change), and concept shift (the relation between input and label changes) [70]. Ground truth is rarely available in real time, so monitors watch proxies:

  • Input statistics such as brightness, contrast, blur and resolution, compared with a reference window using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test [71] or the population stability index (PSI) [72].
  • Embedding distributions from the model or a pretrained encoder, compared with a kernel two-sample test such as maximum mean discrepancy [73]. An empirical study found that two-sample tests on dimension-reduced representations, including the model's own softmax outputs, were effective shift detectors [74].
  • Prediction distributions: the class mix and confidence histogram. Black-box shift estimation uses the confusion matrix and the predicted label distribution to detect and correct label shift [75].
  • Per-input novelty: low maximum softmax probability is a simple baseline signal for misclassified and out-of-distribution inputs [76].

Confidence calibration

A model is calibrated when its confidence matches its accuracy: of all predictions made with 80% confidence, 80% should be correct. Modern deep networks are often over-confident; temperature scaling, which divides the logits by one scalar T fitted on validation data, is a simple and effective fix and does not change the predicted class [77]. Expected calibration error (ECE) bins predictions by confidence and averages the gap between accuracy and confidence, weighted by bin size [78][77]. Recalibrate after any shift, because calibration fitted in one domain degrades in another [36].

Failure analysis

When metrics move, the next question is which errors grew. For detectors, error-diagnosis toolkits break the AP gap into error types: TIDE separates classification, localisation, combined, duplicate, background and missed-object errors and reports the AP each would recover if fixed [79]; earlier work analysed sensitivity to object characteristics such as occlusion, size and viewpoint [80]. Combine this with slicing by ODD attributes, and route a sample of low-confidence and drift-flagged production frames to human review. Confirmed failures become new test cases, which closes the loop in Figure 1.

12The evaluation report

Pulling the sections together, a release-ready evaluation report for a vision model should answer each of the following. Model cards [66] and datasheets [42] give templates for publishing the answers.

  • The ODD is written down, and each test set is mapped to the part of the ODD it covers, with known gaps listed.
  • Test sets are split by the unit that will be new in deployment, checked for near-duplicates, and audited for label quality with a measured agreement figure.
  • Task metrics follow the benchmark convention exactly (IoU thresholds, interpolation, area ranges) and carry confidence intervals.
  • Metrics are reported per class and per ODD slice, not only in aggregate.
  • Robustness is measured under synthetic corruptions and on at least one held-out site, camera or time period.
  • Error rates are disaggregated by relevant demographic groups wherever people are in view, with intervals.
  • Latency (p50, p99), throughput, peak memory and energy per inference are measured end to end on target hardware at steady state.
  • The threat model is stated, and robustness to the relevant attacks is measured with strong attacks; the training-data provenance is documented.
  • Biometric data processing has a documented lawful basis and consent, with minimisation and retention rules.
  • Calibration is measured and corrected, and production monitors for input drift, prediction drift, calibration and latency are in place with named owners.

13Exercises and solutions

Exercises are grouped by section. Work them before opening the solution; several have numerical answers that you can check with the listings above.

01Defect inspection

A visual inspection model screens 10,000 parts, of which 100 are defective. It flags 150 parts, and 80 of the flagged parts are defective. Compute accuracy, precision, recall and F1. Then compute the accuracy of a model that never flags anything.

Solution

TP = 80, FP = 70, FN = 20, TN = 9,830. Accuracy = 9,910 / 10,000 = 0.991. Precision = 80/150 = 0.533. Recall = 80/100 = 0.80. F1 = 2(0.533)(0.80)/(1.333) = 0.64. The never-flag model has accuracy 9,900/10,000 = 0.990, only 0.1 points lower, while missing every defect. Accuracy is the wrong headline metric here.

01ROC-AUC by hand

Positive scores are {0.9, 0.7, 0.4}; negative scores are {0.8, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1}. Compute ROC-AUC using its probabilistic interpretation.

Solution

There are 3 × 4 = 12 positive-negative pairs. 0.9 beats all 4 negatives; 0.7 beats 3 (not 0.8); 0.4 beats 3. AUC = (4 + 3 + 3) / 12 = 10/12 = 0.833 [6]. Running roc_auc([1,1,1,0,0,0,0], [.9,.7,.4,.8,.3,.2,.1]) from Listing 1 gives the same value.

02How far can a box drift?

A ground-truth box is 100×100 pixels. A prediction of the same size is shifted by d pixels both horizontally and vertically. (a) What is the IoU when d = 20? (b) What is the largest d that still counts as a true positive at IoU 0.5 and at 0.75? (c) Repeat (b) at 0.5 for a 20×20 box.

Solution

(a) Intersection = 80² = 6,400; union = 10,000 + 10,000 − 6,400 = 13,600; IoU = 0.471, a false positive at the VOC threshold.

(b) IoU = (100−d)² / (20,000 − (100−d)²). Setting IoU ≥ t gives (100−d)² ≥ 20,000t/(1+t). At t = 0.5, d ≤ 18.4 px. At t = 0.75, d ≤ 7.4 px.

(c) For a 20 px box the same algebra gives d ≤ 3.7 px. Small objects are much harder under fixed IoU thresholds, which is why COCO reports AP for small objects separately [12].

02Average precision from a ranked list

One class has 4 ground-truth objects. Detections sorted by confidence are: TP, FP, TP, TP, FP. Compute the all-point interpolated AP.

Solution

Cumulative (recall, precision): (0.25, 1.00), (0.25, 0.50), (0.50, 0.67), (0.75, 0.75), (0.75, 0.60). The interpolated envelope is 1.00 for recall in (0, 0.25] and 0.75 for recall in (0.25, 0.75], then 0 because recall never reaches 1. AP = 0.25 × 1.00 + 0.50 × 0.75 = 0.625. Note that the envelope lifts the 0.50 and 0.67 points up to 0.75 [10][11].

03Dice and IoU

(a) A mask has IoU 0.6 with its reference. What is its Dice coefficient? (b) Prove that Dice ≥ IoU for any pair of masks.

Solution

(a) Dice = 2(0.6)/(1 + 0.6) = 0.75.

(b) Let J = IoU in [0, 1]. Dice − J = 2J/(1+J) − J = J(1 − J)/(1 + J) ≥ 0, with equality only at J = 0 or J = 1. Because the mapping is monotonic, Dice and IoU rank masks identically for a single structure; the gap is largest at J = √2 − 1 ≈ 0.41.

03Choosing a segmentation metric

Listing 3 reports pixel accuracy 0.98 and mIoU 0.59 for a model that never detects potholes. A road-maintenance customer asks which single number to put in the contract. What do you recommend?

Solution

Neither. Pixel accuracy is dominated by road pixels and would reward the useless model. Mean IoU mixes in classes the customer does not care about. Report per-class IoU or Dice for the pothole class, plus an object-level measure (for example, the fraction of potholes larger than a minimum area that are detected with IoU above a threshold), since the customer counts potholes, not pixels. This follows the Metrics Reloaded advice to choose metrics from the problem's properties, such as small structures and object-level decisions [16].

04Tracking metrics by hand

One person is visible for 10 frames. The tracker outputs ID 1 in frames 1–4, nothing in frames 5–6, and ID 2 in frames 7–10. There are no false positives. Compute FN, IDSW, Frag, MOTA and IDF1.

Solution

FN = 2, IDSW = 1 (frame 7 matches ID 2 after the last match was ID 1), Frag = 1. MOTA = 1 − (2 + 0 + 1)/10 = 0.70 [17]. For IDF1, the best one-to-one mapping assigns the person to ID 2 or ID 1 (4 frames each), so IDTP = 4. There are 10 ground-truth detections and 8 tracker detections, so IDF1 = 2(4)/(10 + 8) = 0.44 [19]. IDF1 penalises the identity break much more heavily than MOTA does.

05Depth metrics by hand

Ground-truth depths (m) are {2, 4, 10, 20}; predictions are {2.2, 3.6, 11, 25}. Compute AbsRel, RMSE and δ < 1.25. Which pixel dominates each metric?

Solution

Relative errors: 0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0.25, so AbsRel = 0.1375. Squared errors: 0.04, 0.16, 1, 25, so RMSE = √(26.2/4) = 2.56 m. Ratios max(d/d*, d*/d): 1.10, 1.11, 1.10, 1.25; the last is not strictly below 1.25, so δ = 0.75. The 20 m pixel dominates RMSE (25 of the 26.2 sum) and decides δ; AbsRel weights all four more evenly. This is why the three metrics are reported together [23].

06Batching against a deadline

A camera delivers 30 FPS. The model takes 25 ms at batch size 1 and 60 ms at batch size 4. The product needs each result within 100 ms of capture. (a) Does each configuration keep up with the camera? (b) Which meets the latency requirement? (c) If the accelerator draws 15 W on average while running, what is the energy per frame at batch 1?

Solution

(a) Batch 1: 1/0.025 = 40 FPS ≥ 30, keeps up. Batch 4: 4/0.060 = 66.7 FPS, keeps up with headroom.

(b) With batch 4, the first frame waits for three more frames at 33.3 ms intervals (100 ms) and then 60 ms of inference: 160 ms worst case, which fails. Batch 1 gives about 25 ms plus pre- and post-processing, which passes. Higher throughput does not mean lower latency; choose the MLPerf single-stream view for this product [27].

(c) 15 W × 0.025 s = 0.375 J per frame (for the inference itself; measure the whole pipeline in practice).

07Corruption error

Under fog, your model's top-1 error at severities 1–5 is {0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40}. The baseline's errors are {0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 0.50, 0.60}. Compute the fog corruption error. What does a CE below 1 mean?

Solution

CE = (0.10 + 0.15 + 0.20 + 0.30 + 0.40) / (0.20 + 0.30 + 0.40 + 0.50 + 0.60) = 1.15/2.00 = 0.575 [32]. A CE below 1 means the model makes fewer errors under this corruption than the baseline. It does not mean the model is robust in absolute terms: an error of 40% at severity 5 may still be unacceptable. ImageNet-C also defines a relative CE, which subtracts clean error, to measure degradation rather than total error [32].

08Leakage in video data

A team has 50 surveillance videos. With a random 80/20 frame split, test accuracy is 97%. With a split by video, it is 81%. Which number goes in the report, and what else would you check?

Solution

Report 81%. The random split puts near-identical neighbouring frames on both sides, which is leakage: it measures recall of seen scenes rather than generalisation to new footage [39][40]. Also check whether multiple videos come from the same camera or site (if so, split by camera or site to estimate performance at new installations), give a confidence interval that resamples videos rather than frames, and slice the 81% by condition to see where the gap comes from.

08Annotator agreement

Two annotators label 100 images as "occluded" or "not occluded". Both say yes on 40, both say no on 40, A says yes and B says no on 10, and A says no and B says yes on 10. Compute Cohen's kappa.

Solution

Observed agreement po = 80/100 = 0.80. Each annotator says yes on 50%, so chance agreement pe = 0.5 × 0.5 + 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.50. κ = (0.80 − 0.50)/(1 − 0.50) = 0.60 [38]. The "occluded" definition likely needs a clearer guideline (for example, a minimum hidden fraction) before the labels are used as ground truth.

09Why small perturbations work

For a linear score w·x with 784 inputs and mean |wi| = 0.1, what is the largest change in the score an L∞ perturbation of ε = 8/255 can cause? How does this scale with image size?

Solution

The perturbation η = ε·sign(w) changes the score by ε·Σ|wi| = ε·‖w‖1 = (8/255) × 784 × 0.1 ≈ 2.46. The change grows linearly with the number of inputs while the per-pixel change stays fixed, which is the linearity explanation for adversarial examples [45]. A 224×224×3 image has 150,528 inputs, about 190 times more.

09Hardening a public API

You expose a face-attribute classifier through a public API that returns the full softmax vector. List three risks and a mitigation for each.

Solution
  • Model extraction from query responses [54][55]: return only the top label (or rounded scores), rate-limit, and monitor per-client query distributions [58].
  • Model inversion recovering training faces from confidence scores [57]: avoid returning fine-grained confidences, and do not train on data you are not permitted to expose.
  • Membership inference revealing who was in the training set [56]: reduce overfitting, limit output detail, and consider differentially private training for sensitive data.

Separately, evasion attacks on the API's users are possible with transfer attacks, so evaluate robustness with strong attacks if decisions depend on the output [49].

10Is the disparity real?

A face-verification system misses 12 of 100 genuine pairs for group C and 40 of 1,000 for group A. Compute 95% Wilson intervals for both miss rates. Is the difference likely to be sampling noise?

Solution

Group C: 0.120, interval [0.070, 0.198]. Group A: 0.040, interval [0.030, 0.054] [65]. The intervals do not overlap, so the difference is very unlikely to be noise (non-overlap of 95% intervals is a conservative test). Collect more group C data to narrow its interval, and investigate the cause (image quality, under-representation in training) before release [63]. Use wilson_ci from Listing 10 to check.

11Expected calibration error

A model's predictions fall in three confidence bins: 500 predictions with mean confidence 0.95 and accuracy 0.80; 300 with confidence 0.75 and accuracy 0.70; 200 with confidence 0.55 and accuracy 0.55. Compute ECE. Is the model over- or under-confident, and how would you correct it?

Solution

ECE = 0.5 × |0.80 − 0.95| + 0.3 × |0.70 − 0.75| + 0.2 × 0 = 0.075 + 0.015 = 0.09 [78]. Confidence exceeds accuracy, so the model is over-confident. Fit a temperature T > 1 on held-out data from the deployment domain [77], and re-check after any distribution shift [36].

11Population stability index

A brightness monitor uses four quantile bins of the reference week, so the reference proportions are {0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25}. This week the proportions are {0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40}. Compute PSI. What could have caused it, and what should happen next?

Solution

PSI = Σ (c − r) ln(c/r) = (−0.15)ln(0.4) + (−0.05)ln(0.8) + (0.05)ln(1.2) + (0.15)ln(1.6) = 0.137 + 0.011 + 0.009 + 0.071 = 0.228 [72]. Frames have become brighter: a seasonal change, a new camera exposure setting, a lens cleaning, or a firmware update. Next: break the alert down by camera to see if it is local; check the model's confidence distribution and a labelled sample for accuracy and calibration changes; and decide whether to recalibrate, add data, or fix the camera configuration. Drift in an input statistic is a reason to look, not proof of a performance drop [74].
