A photograph flattens the world: every point along a ray through the lens lands on the same pixel, so depth is lost at the moment of capture. 3D computer vision is the collection of methods that win that dimension back. Some use geometry alone, intersecting rays from two or more viewpoints. Some use learned priors about what scenes usually look like. Most modern systems use both [1,2].

The article moves from measurement to representation. Sections 00 to 05 recover depth, align 3D measurements and estimate camera motion. Sections 06 to 08 describe how a reconstructed scene is stored and rendered. Sections 09 to 11 cover learning on 3D data, filling in what sensors missed, and rendering views that were never captured. Every code listing is self-contained Python and was run to produce the output shown in its comments.

How to read the codeListings use NumPy and SciPy for geometry, OpenCV for feature tracking and PyTorch for learned models. They favour clarity over speed: production systems use the same mathematics inside libraries such as Open3D, PCL and COLMAP [3–5].

00Geometry you need first

Four ideas recur in every later section: the pinhole camera, rigid-body transforms, homogeneous coordinates and epipolar geometry. Hartley and Zisserman remain the standard reference for all four [1].

The pinhole camera

A camera with intrinsic matrix K maps a 3D point Xc = (X, Y, Z) in its own coordinate frame to pixel (u, v) by perspective division:

u = fx·X/Z + cx,    v = fy·Y/Z + cy,    or   λ[u v 1]T = K [R | t] [Xw 1]T

Here fx, fy are focal lengths in pixels and (cx, cy) is the principal point. The extrinsics R ∈ SO(3) and t ∈ ℝ³ move a world point into the camera frame. The 3×4 product P = K[R | t] is the camera projection matrix. Intrinsics, and the lens distortion that the pure pinhole model ignores, are usually estimated with a planar checkerboard using Zhang's method [6].

Rigid transforms and homogeneous coordinates

Camera poses, scan alignments and SLAM trajectories are elements of the special Euclidean group SE(3): a rotation R and a translation t, often packed into a 4×4 matrix T. Appending a 1 to a point makes the transform a single matrix product and lets transforms compose by multiplication. Rotations have three degrees of freedom; optimisers parameterise small updates with an axis-angle vector ω and the exponential map R = exp([ω]×), given in closed form by Rodrigues' formula [7].

Epipolar geometry

Two calibrated views of the same point satisfy the epipolar constraint x₂T E x₁ = 0 in normalised coordinates, where the essential matrix E = [t]×R encodes their relative pose [8]. In pixel coordinates the same relation holds for the fundamental matrix F = K₂−T E K₁−1. F has seven degrees of freedom and E has five. Given a point in one image, the constraint restricts its match in the other image to a line, the epipolar line. That reduction of a 2D search to a 1D search is what makes stereo matching and multi-view reconstruction tractable [1].

Exercise 0.2 — Counting degrees of freedom

Why does the essential matrix have five degrees of freedom when R and t together have six? Why does the eight-point algorithm need eight correspondences when F has only seven degrees of freedom?

Show solution

E = [t]×R is defined only up to scale: multiplying t by any non-zero constant leaves the constraint x₂TEx₁ = 0 unchanged. Two views cannot reveal the length of the baseline, so 3 (rotation) + 3 (translation) − 1 (scale) = 5. For F, the eight-point algorithm solves the linear system A·vec(F) = 0, which has 9 unknowns up to scale and therefore needs 8 equations. It ignores the rank-2 constraint det F = 0 during the solve and enforces it afterwards by zeroing the smallest singular value. A seven-point solver that uses the cubic constraint directly exists and gives up to three solutions [1,9].

01Depth estimation

A depth map stores, for every pixel, the distance along the optical axis to the visible surface. It can be measured actively (structured light, time of flight, LiDAR) or inferred from images. This section covers the passive, image-based routes: stereo, monocular geometry cues and learned estimators.

Stereo depth

After rectification, two cameras share an image plane and their epipolar lines are horizontal rows. A point at column xL in the left image appears at xR = xL − d in the right image, where d is the disparity. Similar triangles give depth directly:

Z = f·B / d      and      ∂Z/∂d = −Z² / (f·B)

where f is the focal length in pixels and B the baseline in metres [1,2]. The second expression is the most important practical fact about stereo: depth error grows with the square of distance. Doubling range quadruples the error for the same sub-pixel matching accuracy, which is why long-range stereo rigs use wide baselines.

Scharstein and Szeliski decomposed classical stereo into four steps that still organise the field: matching-cost computation, cost aggregation, disparity optimisation and refinement [10]. Local methods aggregate costs over a window and pick the lowest cost per pixel (winner-take-all). Global methods add a smoothness term and optimise over the whole image. Semi-global matching (SGM) approximates the 2D optimisation with dynamic programming along 8 or 16 directions and became the default in robotics and automotive systems because it is accurate and parallelises well [11].

The listing below implements the simplest member of the taxonomy: sum-of-absolute-differences cost, box-filter aggregation and winner-take-all selection, followed by the disparity-to-depth conversion.

stereo.py — block matching and disparity-to-depthNumPy, SciPy
import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import uniform_filter

def block_match(left, right, max_disp=32, win=5):
    """Winner-take-all SAD block matching on rectified grayscale float images."""
    H, W = left.shape
    cost = np.full((max_disp, H, W), 1e3, dtype=np.float32)
    for d in range(max_disp):
        ad = np.full((H, W), 1e3, dtype=np.float32)
        ad[:, d:] = np.abs(left[:, d:] - right[:, :W - d])   # x_R = x_L - d
        cost[d] = uniform_filter(ad, size=win)               # aggregate over window
    return cost.argmin(axis=0).astype(np.float32)          # winner-take-all

def disparity_to_depth(disp, f_px, baseline_m, eps=1e-6):
    """Z = f * B / d  (Hartley & Zisserman, ch. 9 / Szeliski ch. 12)."""
    return np.where(disp > 0, f_px * baseline_m / np.maximum(disp, eps), 0.0)

# synthetic test: random texture shifted by a known disparity of 7 px
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
right = rng.random((60, 120)).astype(np.float32)
left = np.roll(right, 7, axis=1)               # left pixel x sees right pixel x-7
d = block_match(left, right, max_disp=16)
print("median disparity:", np.median(d[:, 20:-20]))
Z = disparity_to_depth(d, f_px=700.0, baseline_m=0.12)
print("median depth (m):", round(float(np.median(Z[:, 20:-20])), 2))

# Output:
# median disparity: 7.0
# median depth (m): 12.0

Learned stereo

Deep stereo networks replace hand-crafted matching costs with learned features and keep the geometric structure. GC-Net builds a 4D cost volume (height × width × disparity × features) by concatenating left and shifted right features, filters it with 3D convolutions and reads out disparity with a differentiable soft-argmin [12]. PSMNet adds spatial pyramid pooling for context [13]. RAFT-Stereo avoids a full 3D cost-volume filter: it iteratively updates a disparity field using recurrent units that look up a multi-level correlation pyramid, which generalises better across datasets [14].

Monocular depth

A single image carries depth cues that humans use without thinking: perspective convergence, texture gradients, occlusion, relative size of familiar objects, shading and atmospheric haze. Saxena et al. learned to combine such cues with a Markov random field [15]. Eigen et al. showed that a multi-scale CNN could regress dense depth directly. Because absolute scale is ambiguous from one image, they introduced a scale-invariant loss on log depth [16]:

L = (1/n) Σi gi² − (λ/n²) (Σi gi)²,    gi = log ŷi − log yi

With λ = 1 the loss ignores any global scale factor, because a scale change adds the same constant to every gi; the authors trained with λ = 0.5 as a compromise.

Self-supervised and foundation-scale depth

Ground-truth depth is expensive. Self-supervised methods use view synthesis as the training signal. Godard et al. train on stereo pairs: predict disparity from the left image, warp the right image into the left view, and penalise the photometric difference [17]. Zhou et al. extend the idea to monocular video by jointly predicting depth and camera ego-motion [18]. Monodepth2 made this practical with a per-pixel minimum reprojection loss that handles occlusion and an auto-mask that ignores pixels moving with the camera [19]. Monocular self-supervision still recovers depth only up to scale.

The current state of the art trains on mixtures of many datasets. MiDaS showed that a loss invariant to per-image scale and shift lets a model learn from datasets with incompatible depth units, giving strong zero-shot transfer [20]. Depth Anything scales this recipe with about 62 million automatically pseudo-labelled unlabelled images [21]. Marigold fine-tunes a pretrained latent diffusion image generator to produce affine-invariant depth, exploiting the scene prior learned by image generation [22].

Evaluating depth

The metrics introduced by Eigen et al. remain standard [16]: absolute relative error AbsRel = mean(|ŷ − y|/y), root-mean-square error, and threshold accuracy δ1 = fraction of pixels with max(ŷ/y, y/ŷ) < 1.25. For scale-ambiguous predictors, papers first align prediction and ground truth, using a median ratio for up-to-scale depth or a least-squares scale and shift for affine-invariant depth [20]. The KITTI benchmark supplies LiDAR ground truth for outdoor driving scenes [23].

02Point clouds

A point cloud is an unordered set of 3D points, optionally with colour, intensity or normals. It is the native output of LiDAR and of depth cameras, and the common currency between reconstruction stages. Point clouds are easy to produce and to transform, but they have no connectivity, uneven density and no notion of inside or outside, so most processing starts by building a spatial index such as a k-d tree or voxel grid [3,4].

From depth map to points

Inverting the pinhole model lifts every pixel with known depth to 3D: X = Z·K−1[u v 1]T [1]. Transforming by the camera-to-world pose places all frames in a common coordinate system.

backproject.py — depth map to point cloud and backNumPy
import numpy as np

def backproject(depth, K):
    """Lift a depth map (H,W) in metres to an (N,3) point cloud in the camera frame."""
    H, W = depth.shape
    u, v = np.meshgrid(np.arange(W), np.arange(H))
    z = depth.ravel()
    valid = z > 0
    pix = np.stack([u.ravel(), v.ravel(), np.ones(H * W)])[:, valid]   # homogeneous pixels
    rays = np.linalg.inv(K) @ pix                                       # K^-1 [u v 1]^T
    return (rays * z[valid]).T                                           # X = z * K^-1 x

def project(X, K):
    x = (K @ X.T).T
    return x[:, :2] / x[:, 2:3]

K = np.array([[525.0, 0, 319.5], [0, 525.0, 239.5], [0, 0, 1]])
depth = np.full((480, 640), 2.0)               # fronto-parallel wall 2 m away
P = backproject(depth, K)
print(P.shape, P[:, 2].min(), P[:, 2].max())
uv = project(P[:5], K)
print(np.round(uv, 3))                         # reprojects to pixels (0,0),(1,0),...

# Output:
# (307200, 3) 2.0 2.0
# [[0. 0.] [1. 0.] [2. 0.] [3. 0.] [4. 0.]]

Processing

Four operations appear in nearly every pipeline. Voxel downsampling replaces all points inside each grid cell by their centroid, which equalises density and bounds cost. Statistical outlier removal discards points whose mean distance to their k nearest neighbours is unusually large, removing flying pixels at depth discontinuities. Normal estimation fits a plane to each neighbourhood by principal component analysis; the eigenvector with the smallest eigenvalue is the normal, and the ratio of that eigenvalue to the sum measures local curvature [4]. Clustering groups points into connected components of a radius graph, a special case of DBSCAN [24].

pc_ops.py — downsampling, normals, outlier removal, Euclidean clusteringNumPy, SciPy
import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree

def voxel_downsample(P, voxel):
    """Replace all points in each occupied voxel by their centroid."""
    keys = np.floor(P / voxel).astype(np.int64)
    _, inv, counts = np.unique(keys, axis=0, return_inverse=True, return_counts=True)
    out = np.zeros((len(counts), 3)); np.add.at(out, inv.ravel(), P)
    return out / counts[:, None]

def estimate_normals(P, k=16):
    """PCA normals: eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue of the k-NN covariance."""
    _, idx = cKDTree(P).query(P, k)
    N = P[idx] - P[idx].mean(1, keepdims=True)          # (n,k,3) centred neighbourhoods
    C = np.einsum("nki,nkj->nij", N, N) / k
    w, v = np.linalg.eigh(C)                             # ascending eigenvalues
    curvature = w[:, 0] / w.sum(1)                       # "surface variation"
    return v[:, :, 0], curvature

def remove_statistical_outliers(P, k=20, std_ratio=2.0):
    d, _ = cKDTree(P).query(P, k + 1)
    m = d[:, 1:].mean(1)
    return P[m < m.mean() + std_ratio * m.std()]

def euclidean_clusters(P, radius, min_size=20):
    """Connected components of the radius graph (the core of DBSCAN with minPts=1)."""
    tree = cKDTree(P); labels = -np.ones(len(P), int); c = 0
    for s in range(len(P)):
        if labels[s] >= 0: continue
        stack = [s]; labels[s] = c
        while stack:
            for j in tree.query_ball_point(P[stack.pop()], radius):
                if labels[j] < 0: labels[j] = c; stack.append(j)
        c += 1
    sizes = np.bincount(labels)
    labels[sizes[labels] < min_size] = -1                # tiny clusters -> noise
    return labels

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
    sphere = rng.normal(size=(5000, 3)); sphere /= np.linalg.norm(sphere, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    noisy = np.vstack([sphere, rng.uniform(-3, 3, (100, 3))])
    clean = remove_statistical_outliers(noisy)
    down = voxel_downsample(clean, 0.1)
    n, curv = estimate_normals(down)
    radial = down / np.linalg.norm(down, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    print("kept", len(clean), "->", len(down), "after voxel grid;  mean |n·r| =", np.abs((n * radial).sum(1)).mean().round(3))
    blobs = np.vstack([rng.normal(c, 0.1, (300, 3)) for c in ([0, 0, 0], [2, 0, 0], [0, 2, 0])])
    lab = euclidean_clusters(blobs, 0.25)
    print("clusters found:", len(set(lab) - {-1}))

# kept 5004 -> 1411 after voxel grid;  mean

Registration

Registration finds the rigid transform that aligns one scan to another. If correspondences are known, the optimal rotation has a closed form: centre both sets, take the SVD of the 3×3 cross-covariance H = UΣVT and set R = V·diag(1, 1, det(VUT))·UT, the determinant guarding against reflections [25]. The Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm alternates this solve with re-estimating correspondences as nearest neighbours [26]. Each iteration cannot increase the mean squared error, so ICP converges monotonically, but only to a local minimum: it needs a good initial guess.

Point-to-plane ICP minimises the distance from each source point to the tangent plane at its match instead of to the match itself. Surfaces can then slide along themselves without penalty, and convergence on smooth scenes is much faster [27]. Generalized-ICP models both clouds as locally planar Gaussian distributions and unifies the two variants [28].

When the initial pose is unknown, global registration matches local descriptors such as Fast Point Feature Histograms (FPFH), which summarise the angles between normals in a neighbourhood [29], and then estimates the transform robustly. RANSAC samples three correspondences, fits a transform and counts inliers [30]. Fast Global Registration replaces sampling with a robust objective optimised by graduated non-convexity [31], and TEASER++ gives certifiable solutions even when the large majority of correspondences are outliers [32]. The usual recipe is global registration for a coarse pose followed by ICP for refinement.

icp.py — closed-form rigid alignment and point-to-point ICPNumPy, SciPy
import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree

def best_rigid_transform(A, B):
    """Least-squares R,t with B ≈ R A + t (Arun et al. 1987, SVD / Kabsch)."""
    ca, cb = A.mean(0), B.mean(0)
    H = (A - ca).T @ (B - cb)
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(H)
    D = np.diag([1, 1, np.sign(np.linalg.det(Vt.T @ U.T))])   # guard against reflections
    R = Vt.T @ D @ U.T
    return R, cb - R @ ca

def icp(src, dst, iters=50, tol=1e-8, max_dist=np.inf):
    """Point-to-point ICP (Besl & McKay 1992). Returns 4x4 T mapping src -> dst."""
    tree = cKDTree(dst)
    T = np.eye(4)
    cur = src.copy()
    prev = np.inf
    for _ in range(iters):
        dist, idx = tree.query(cur)
        m = dist < max_dist                     # reject far correspondences
        R, t = best_rigid_transform(cur[m], dst[idx[m]])
        cur = cur @ R.T + t
        step = np.eye(4); step[:3, :3] = R; step[:3, 3] = t
        T = step @ T
        err = np.mean(dist[m] ** 2)
        if abs(prev - err) < tol:
            break
        prev = err
    return T, err

def rot(axis, deg):
    a = np.deg2rad(deg); axis = np.asarray(axis, float) / np.linalg.norm(axis)
    Kx = np.array([[0, -axis[2], axis[1]], [axis[2], 0, -axis[0]], [-axis[1], axis[0], 0]])
    return np.eye(3) + np.sin(a) * Kx + (1 - np.cos(a)) * Kx @ Kx   # Rodrigues

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
    dst = rng.normal(size=(2000, 3)) * [1.0, 0.6, 0.3]
    R_true, t_true = rot([0.2, 1, 0.1], 12), np.array([0.10, -0.05, 0.20])
    src = (dst - t_true) @ R_true                    # src = R^T (dst - t)
    src += rng.normal(scale=0.005, size=src.shape)
    T, err = icp(src, dst)
    print("rotation error (deg):", np.rad2deg(np.arccos((np.trace(T[:3,:3].T @ R_true) - 1) / 2)).round(3))
    print("translation error  :", np.linalg.norm(T[:3, 3] - t_true).round(4), " mse:", err.round(6))

# rotation error (deg): 0.007

Segmentation

Geometric segmentation splits a cloud into primitives and objects. Man-made scenes are dominated by planes, so a common first step is RANSAC plane fitting: sample three points, form the plane through them, count points within a distance threshold, keep the best, then refine by least squares on the inliers [30]. Removing the dominant plane (a floor or table) often leaves objects as separate clusters that Euclidean clustering can pick out [4]. Semantic segmentation, which assigns a class to every point, is learned; Section 09 covers the networks [33].

ransac_plane.py — dominant plane extractionNumPy
import numpy as np

def ransac_plane(P, thresh=0.01, iters=500, seed=0):
    """Fit n·x + d = 0 with RANSAC (Fischler & Bolles 1981). Returns (n, d, inlier mask)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    best = (None, None, np.zeros(len(P), bool))
    for _ in range(iters):
        p0, p1, p2 = P[rng.choice(len(P), 3, replace=False)]
        n = np.cross(p1 - p0, p2 - p0)
        if np.linalg.norm(n) < 1e-12:
            continue                              # degenerate (collinear) sample
        n /= np.linalg.norm(n)
        d = -n @ p0
        inl = np.abs(P @ n + d) < thresh
        if inl.sum() > best[2].sum():
            best = (n, d, inl)
    # refine on all inliers: smallest singular vector of centred inliers
    Q = P[best[2]]; c = Q.mean(0)
    n = np.linalg.svd(Q - c)[2][-1]
    return n, -n @ c, np.abs(P @ n - n @ c) < thresh

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
floor = np.c_[rng.uniform(-2, 2, (3000, 2)), rng.normal(0, 0.003, 3000)]   # z≈0 plane
clutter = rng.uniform([-1, -1, 0.05], [1, 1, 1.0], (1000, 3))               # objects
P = np.vstack([floor, clutter])
n, d, inl = ransac_plane(P)
print("normal:", np.round(np.abs(n), 3), " inliers:", inl.sum(), "of", len(P))

# Output:
# normal: [0. 0. 1.]  inliers: 2997 of 4000

Using n·(ω × p) = (p × n)·ω, each correspondence contributes one row [pi × ni, ni] and right-hand side −ni·(pi − qi) to a 6-unknown system [27]. Convert ω back to an exact rotation before applying it so errors do not accumulate.

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree
from c09_pc_ops import estimate_normals
from c03_icp import rot

def point_to_plane_icp(src, dst, dst_normals, iters=20):
    """Linearised point-to-plane ICP (Chen & Medioni 1992; small-angle R ≈ I + [w]x)."""
    tree = cKDTree(dst); T = np.eye(4); cur = src.copy()
    for _ in range(iters):
        _, idx = tree.query(cur)
        q, n = dst[idx], dst_normals[idx]
        A = np.c_[np.cross(cur, n), n]             # d/d[w,t] of n·(p + w×p + t - q)

        R = rot([w0, w1, w2], np.rad2deg(np.linalg.norm([w0, w1, w2]))) if np.any([w0, w1, w2]) else np.eye(3)
        cur = cur @ R.T + t
        S = np.eye(4); S[:3, :3] = R; S[:3, 3] = t; T = S @ T
    return T

03Structure from motion and multi-view stereo

Structure from motion (SfM) recovers camera poses and a sparse 3D point set from an unordered collection of images. Multi-view stereo (MVS) then uses those poses to compute dense geometry. Together they turn a set of photographs into a textured model, and they produce the camera poses that neural scene representations in Sections 07 and 08 require [2].

Features and matching

SfM starts with local features detected at repeatable locations and described by vectors that tolerate viewpoint and illumination change. SIFT detects scale-space extrema of a difference-of-Gaussians pyramid and describes each with gradient-orientation histograms [35]. ORB is a binary alternative that is far cheaper to compute and match [36]. Learned detectors and descriptors such as SuperPoint [37], and learned matchers such as SuperGlue and LightGlue that reason jointly over both images with attention [38,39], are now common in difficult conditions. Matches are filtered with Lowe's ratio test and verified geometrically by RANSAC on the fundamental or essential matrix [30,35].

Two-view geometry

For a pair of images, the essential matrix from the five-point algorithm [34] or the fundamental matrix from the normalised eight-point algorithm [9] gives the relative pose. Matched points are then triangulated: each image point gives two linear equations in the homogeneous 3D point, and the solution is the null vector of the stacked system (the direct linear transform) [1].

two_view.py — normalised eight-point algorithm and DLT triangulationNumPy
import numpy as np

def normalize(x):
    """Hartley normalisation: centroid to origin, mean distance sqrt(2)."""
    c = x.mean(0); s = np.sqrt(2) / np.mean(np.linalg.norm(x - c, axis=1))
    T = np.array([[s, 0, -s * c[0]], [0, s, -s * c[1]], [0, 0, 1]])
    return (np.c_[x, np.ones(len(x))] @ T.T), T

def eight_point(x1, x2):
    """Fundamental matrix with x2^T F x1 = 0 (Longuet-Higgins 1981; Hartley 1997)."""
    p1, T1 = normalize(x1); p2, T2 = normalize(x2)
    A = np.einsum("ni,nj->nij", p2, p1).reshape(len(p1), 9)
    F = np.linalg.svd(A)[2][-1].reshape(3, 3)
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(F); F = U @ np.diag([S[0], S[1], 0]) @ Vt   # enforce rank 2
    F = T2.T @ F @ T1
    return F / F[2, 2]

def triangulate(P1, P2, x1, x2):
    """Linear (DLT) triangulation, Hartley & Zisserman sec. 12.2."""
    X = []
    for (u1, v1), (u2, v2) in zip(x1, x2):
        A = np.stack([u1 * P1[2] - P1[0], v1 * P1[2] - P1[1],
                      u2 * P2[2] - P2[0], v2 * P2[2] - P2[1]])
        Xh = np.linalg.svd(A)[2][-1]
        X.append(Xh[:3] / Xh[3])
    return np.array(X)

def proj(P, X):
    x = np.c_[X, np.ones(len(X))] @ P.T
    return x[:, :2] / x[:, 2:]

Incremental SfM and bundle adjustment

Incremental SfM, as in Photo Tourism and COLMAP, initialises from a well-conditioned image pair and then repeatedly registers a new image by solving the Perspective-n-Point problem against already-triangulated points, triangulates new points, and refines everything with bundle adjustment [5,40]. Building Rome in a Day showed that the approach scales to city-sized internet photo collections [41]. Bundle adjustment minimises total reprojection error over all cameras Pj and points Xi:

min{Pj}, {Xi} Σ(i,j)∈obs ρ( ‖ xij − π(Pj, Xi) ‖² )

where π is the projection function and ρ a robust loss such as Huber. The problem is solved with Levenberg–Marquardt. Its normal equations have a block structure: each residual involves one camera and one point, so the point block is block-diagonal and can be eliminated cheaply with the Schur complement, leaving a small reduced camera system [42]. The reconstruction is only defined up to a similarity transform (rotation, translation and scale), a seven-parameter gauge freedom that must be fixed or handled by the solver [1,42].

Multi-view stereo

Given calibrated cameras, MVS estimates dense geometry by testing photo-consistency: a hypothesised surface point should look the same in every image that sees it. Plane sweeping tests a family of fronto-parallel depth planes by warping neighbouring images into the reference view and recording matching cost per depth [44]. PMVS grows oriented patches outward from sparse seeds [45]. COLMAP's MVS estimates a depth and normal per pixel with PatchMatch-style random search and propagation [46], jointly choosing which source views to trust for each pixel, then fuses consistent depth maps into a point cloud [43]. MVSNet brought learning to this stage: it warps deep features from source views onto sweep planes, builds a variance-based cost volume and regularises it with 3D convolutions [47]. A watertight mesh is commonly extracted from the fused oriented points by screened Poisson surface reconstruction, which solves for an indicator function whose gradient matches the normals [48].

Feed-forward reconstruction

Recent models skip explicit matching and optimisation for their first estimate. DUSt3R regresses, for a pair of uncalibrated images, a pointmap: the 3D point seen by every pixel, expressed in the first camera's frame. Camera intrinsics, relative pose and dense depth then follow from simple post-processing [49]. VGGT extends this to many views at once: a single large transformer predicts cameras, depth maps, pointmaps and point tracks in one forward pass, in under a second for typical inputs [50]. These models are often used to initialise or replace classical SfM when images are few or texture is poor; classical bundle adjustment remains the reference for accuracy at scale.

You run SfM twice on the same photos and get two reconstructions whose reprojection errors are identical but whose coordinates differ. Explain, and say how you would compare them.

Applying any similarity transform S (rotation, translation and uniform scale, 7 DOF) to all points, with the inverse applied to all camera poses, leaves every projection unchanged. 04Visual odometry

Visual odometry (VO) estimates a camera's motion incrementally from an image sequence. Nistér et al. coined the term by analogy with wheel odometry and demonstrated real-time monocular and stereo systems [52]. Scaramuzza and Fraundorfer's two-part tutorial is the standard introduction [53]. VO differs from SfM in that frames arrive in order and must be processed causally and in real time; it differs from SLAM in that it optimises only recent poses and does not try to recognise previously visited places, so its error grows without bound (drift).

Feature-based pipeline

A minimal monocular VO loop detects corners [54], tracks them into the next frame with pyramidal Lucas–Kanade optical flow [55], estimates the essential matrix with the five-point algorithm inside RANSAC [30,34], and decomposes it into a rotation and a unit-length translation. Of the four possible decompositions, only one places the triangulated points in front of both cameras (the cheirality check) [1]. Chaining the relative poses gives the trajectory.

vo.py — monocular frame-to-frame visual odometry with OpenCVOpenCV 4.x, NumPy
import numpy as np, cv2

def relative_pose(pts1, pts2, K):
    """Monocular two-frame VO step: essential matrix (5-point + RANSAC) then cheirality check."""
    E, mask = cv2.findEssentialMat(pts1, pts2, K, method=cv2.RANSAC, prob=0.999, threshold=1.0)
    _, R, t, mask = cv2.recoverPose(E, pts1, pts2, K, mask=mask)
    return R, t.ravel(), mask.ravel().astype(bool)   # ||t|| = 1: scale is unobservable

def track(prev_gray, gray, prev_pts):
    """Pyramidal Lucas-Kanade optical flow (Lucas & Kanade 1981)."""
    nxt, st, _ = cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK(prev_gray, gray, prev_pts, None,
                                          winSize=(21, 21), maxLevel=3)
    ok = st.ravel() == 1
    return prev_pts[ok], nxt[ok]

def run_vo(frames, K, scale_fn=lambda i: 1.0):
    """Frame-to-frame VO loop. frames: iterable of grayscale uint8 images."""
    R_w, t_w, traj = np.eye(3), np.zeros(3), [np.zeros(3)]
    frames = iter(frames); prev = next(frames)
    pts = cv2.goodFeaturesToTrack(prev, 1500, 0.01, 8)
    for i, img in enumerate(frames, 1):
        p0, p1 = track(prev, img, pts)
        R, t, inl = relative_pose(p0, p1, K)          # maps frame i-1 coords -> frame i coords
        t_w = t_w + scale_fn(i) * (R_w @ (-R.T @ t))  # camera centre moves by -R^T t
        R_w = R_w @ R.T
        traj.append(t_w.copy())
        pts = p1[inl].reshape(-1, 1, 2)
        if len(pts) < 500:                            # replenish features
            pts = cv2.goodFeaturesToTrack(img, 1500, 0.01, 8)
        prev = img
    return np.array(traj)

Scale and drift

Monocular VO recovers translation direction but not length, and the scale of each step is only consistent with its neighbours through shared triangulated points. Small errors therefore accumulate as scale drift. Stereo VO avoids the problem because the known baseline fixes scale [53]. Other sources of scale are an inertial measurement unit (Section 05), wheel odometry or a known geometric quantity such as camera height above the road.

Direct methods

Direct methods skip keypoints and minimise photometric error over image intensities, using pixels with sufficient gradient, including edges that have no distinctive corner. Direct Sparse Odometry (DSO) jointly optimises poses, inverse depths of a sparse set of pixels and a photometric calibration model (exposure time, vignetting and camera response) in a sliding window [56]. Direct methods are accurate in low-texture scenes but sensitive to rolling shutter, auto-exposure and large inter-frame motion.

Evaluation

The KITTI odometry benchmark reports average translational error as a percentage of distance travelled, computed over subsequences of 100 to 800 m, and rotational error in degrees per metre [23]. The TUM RGB-D benchmark defines absolute trajectory error (ATE, after aligning the estimated and true trajectories) and relative pose error (RPE, drift over a fixed interval) [51].

05Visual and visual-inertial SLAM

Simultaneous localisation and mapping (SLAM) estimates a sensor's trajectory and a map of the environment at the same time, and uses the map to correct the trajectory when places are revisited. Cadena et al. describe a modern SLAM system as a front end that turns raw sensor data into constraints (feature tracks, loop closures) and a back end that performs maximum a posteriori (MAP) inference over those constraints [57].

From filters to factor graphs

Early visual SLAM used extended Kalman filters. MonoSLAM tracked a sparse set of landmarks with a single camera in real time [58], but the filter's cost grows quadratically with map size. PTAM separated tracking and mapping into parallel threads and used keyframe bundle adjustment for the map, showing that optimisation could run in real time [59]. Modern back ends express the problem as a factor graph: variables are poses and landmarks, and each factor is a measurement likelihood. Under Gaussian noise, MAP estimation becomes nonlinear least squares [60]:

X* = argminX Σk ‖ hk(Xk) − zk ‖²Σk

solved with Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt by sparse solvers such as g2o, which exploit the fact that each factor touches only a few variables [61].

Visual SLAM systems

ORB-SLAM runs three threads (tracking, local mapping and loop closing) over ORB features, and uses a bag-of-binary-words place recognition index [62] to detect loops and relocalise after tracking failure [63]. ORB-SLAM3 adds visual-inertial operation, fisheye cameras and a multi-map atlas that merges maps when a previously mapped area is re-entered [64]. LSD-SLAM showed that direct, semi-dense SLAM could build large-scale consistent maps with a monocular camera, with explicit handling of scale drift through Sim(3) pose-graph optimisation [65]. DROID-SLAM is a learned system: it iteratively updates dense optical flow with a recurrent network and feeds the result into a differentiable dense bundle adjustment layer, achieving strong robustness across monocular, stereo and RGB-D input [66].

Visual-inertial SLAM

An IMU measures angular velocity and specific force at hundreds of hertz. It complements a camera well: it is unaffected by texture or motion blur, it makes metric scale observable through accelerometer readings, and it makes roll and pitch observable through gravity. Its integrated estimates drift quickly, which camera measurements correct [64,68]. Global position and yaw remain unobservable, so visual-inertial odometry has four unobservable directions [68].

Filter-based fusion is exemplified by the multi-state constraint Kalman filter (MSCKF), which keeps a window of past camera poses in the state and uses feature tracks as constraints between them without adding landmarks to the state [67]. Optimisation-based fusion uses IMU preintegration: the hundreds of IMU samples between two keyframes are summarised into a single relative-motion factor (ΔR, Δv, Δp) that does not depend on the absolute state at the first keyframe, so it need not be re-integrated each time the optimiser updates that state. First-order corrections handle bias updates [69]. VINS-Mono combines preintegration with a sliding-window optimiser, a robust initialisation procedure and loop closure [68].

imu_preintegration.py — preintegrated IMU factor and state predictionNumPy
import numpy as np

def skew(w): return np.array([[0, -w[2], w[1]], [w[2], 0, -w[0]], [-w[1], w[0], 0]])
def so3_exp(phi):
    a = np.linalg.norm(phi)
    if a < 1e-9: return np.eye(3) + skew(phi)
    K = skew(phi / a); return np.eye(3) + np.sin(a) * K + (1 - np.cos(a)) * K @ K

def preintegrate(gyro, acc, dt, bg=np.zeros(3), ba=np.zeros(3)):
    """IMU preintegration between two keyframes (Forster et al. 2017, eqs. 33-35).
    Returns relative rotation, velocity and position increments that do not depend
    on the absolute state, so they need not be recomputed when the optimiser moves it."""
    dR, dv, dp = np.eye(3), np.zeros(3), np.zeros(3)
    for w, a in zip(gyro, acc):
        a_c = a - ba
        dp = dp + dv * dt + 0.5 * dR @ a_c * dt ** 2
        dv = dv + dR @ a_c * dt
        dR = dR @ so3_exp((w - bg) * dt)
    return dR, dv, dp

def predict(R_i, v_i, p_i, dR, dv, dp, T, g=np.array([0, 0, -9.81])):
    """Combine preintegrated terms with the state at keyframe i to predict keyframe j."""
    return R_i @ dR, v_i + g * T + R_i @ dv, p_i + v_i * T + 0.5 * g * T ** 2 + R_i @ dp

# 1 s at 200 Hz: yaw rate 0.5 rad/s, body accelerating forward at 1 m/s^2, accelerometer sees -g too
n, dt = 200, 1 / 200
gyro = np.tile([0, 0, 0.5], (n, 1)); acc = np.tile([1.0, 0, 9.81], (n, 1))
dR, dv, dp = preintegrate(gyro, acc, dt)
R, v, p = predict(np.eye(3), np.zeros(3), np.zeros(3), dR, dv, dp, n * dt)
print("yaw (rad):", np.arctan2(R[1, 0], R[0, 0]).round(3), " speed:", np.linalg.norm(v).round(3),
      " z drift:", p[2].round(6))

Exercise 5.2 — What an IMU can and cannot observe

Explain why a monocular camera plus IMU can recover metric scale, roll and pitch, but not absolute yaw or position.

Show solution

The accelerometer measures metric acceleration (plus gravity). Double-integrating it gives metric displacement over short intervals, which pins the scale of the visual trajectory. Gravity is a known vertical reference, so tilt relative to it (roll and pitch) is observable. Rotating the whole scene and trajectory about the gravity axis, or translating it, changes no measurement, so yaw and the three position coordinates form the four unobservable directions [68,69]. Estimators that ignore this can become overconfident along those directions.

Exercise 5.3 — Why preintegrate?

06Volumetric representations

Point clouds say where surfaces were sampled. Volumetric representations say something about every location in a region: whether it is occupied, how far it is from the nearest surface, or how much light it emits and absorbs. That makes them natural for fusing many noisy measurements, for collision checking and for extracting watertight surfaces.

Voxel grids

A voxel grid divides space into regular cells, each storing a value. Grids make neighbourhood queries trivial and suit 3D convolutions, which is why the first deep networks for 3D shape, 3D ShapeNets and VoxNet, used binary occupancy grids [70,71]. Their weakness is memory: cost grows with the cube of resolution while surfaces occupy only a thin shell. Sparse structures address this. Octrees subdivide only occupied regions [72], and voxel hashing stores small dense blocks only near surfaces in a hash table, enabling room- and building-scale real-time fusion [73].

Occupancy grids

Occupancy grids, introduced by Elfes for sonar-based mapping, store the probability that each cell is occupied [74]. Each range measurement updates the cells its beam passes through (evidence of free space) and the cell where it ends (evidence of occupancy). Storing probabilities as log-odds l = log(p/(1 − p)) turns the Bayesian update into addition [75]:

lt(m) = lt−1(m) + log [ p(m | zt) / (1 − p(m | zt)) ] − l0

OctoMap stores the grid in an octree and clamps log-odds to bounds so that the map can still change when the world does [72]. Occupancy grids remain the standard representation for robot navigation and planning.

import numpy as np

L_OCC, L_FREE, L_MIN, L_MAX = np.log(0.7 / 0.3), np.log(0.4 / 0.6), -2.0, 3.5

def bresenham(x0, y0, x1, y1):
    """Integer grid cells on the segment (x0,y0)->(x1,y1)."""
    cells = []; dx, dy = abs(x1 - x0), -abs(y1 - y0)
    sx, sy = (1 if x0 < x1 else -1), (1 if y0 < y1 else -1); err = dx + dy
    while True:
        cells.append((x0, y0))
        if x0 == x1 and y0 == y1: return cells
        e2 = 2 * err
        if e2 >= dy: err += dy; x0 += sx
        if e2 <= dx: err += dx; y0 += sy

def update(logodds, origin, hits):
    """Inverse-sensor-model update in log-odds form (Elfes 1989; Thrun et al. 2005, ch. 9)."""
    for hx, hy in hits:
        ray = bresenham(*origin, hx, hy)
        for cx, cy in ray[:-1]: logodds[cy, cx] += L_FREE     # cells the beam passed through
        logodds[hy, hx] += L_OCC                              # cell where the beam ended
    np.clip(logodds, L_MIN, L_MAX, out=logodds)               # clamping (as in OctoMap)

Signed distance fields and TSDF fusion

A signed distance field (SDF) stores, at each point, the distance to the nearest surface, positive outside and negative inside. The surface is the zero level set, and the gradient of an exact SDF has unit length and points along the surface normal. Curless and Levoy showed that range scans can be fused by averaging truncated signed distances, computed along each sensor ray and clamped to a band ±μ around the surface, with per-voxel weights [76]:

D(v) ← (W(v)·D(v) + w·d) / (W(v) + w),     W(v) ← W(v) + w

Averaging many noisy observations reduces noise, and because every observation of free space carves the volume, the method handles occlusion naturally. KinectFusion ran this integration on a GPU at sensor frame rate, tracking the camera by ICP against a ray-cast of the current model, and launched real-time dense RGB-D reconstruction [77]. A triangle mesh is extracted from the zero crossing with marching cubes, which looks up a triangulation for each cube according to the signs at its eight corners [78].

tsdf.py — TSDF fusion of eight synthetic depth maps and marching-cubes meshingNumPy, scikit-image
import numpy as np
from skimage.measure import marching_cubes

class TSDFVolume:
    """Weighted TSDF fusion (Curless & Levoy 1996; KinectFusion, Newcombe et al. 2011)."""
    def __init__(self, origin, voxel, dims, trunc):
        self.o, self.v, self.trunc = np.asarray(origin, float), voxel, trunc
        self.tsdf = np.ones(dims, np.float32); self.w = np.zeros(dims, np.float32)
        idx = np.stack(np.meshgrid(*[np.arange(n) for n in dims], indexing="ij"), -1).reshape(-1, 3)
        self.centres = self.o + (idx + 0.5) * voxel

    def integrate(self, depth, K, C2W, max_w=64.0):
        W2C = np.linalg.inv(C2W)
        Xc = self.centres @ W2C[:3, :3].T + W2C[:3, 3]
        z = Xc[:, 2]; z_safe = np.where(z > 1e-6, z, 1.0)
        u = np.round(K[0, 0] * Xc[:, 0] / z_safe + K[0, 2]).astype(int)
        v = np.round(K[1, 1] * Xc[:, 1] / z_safe + K[1, 2]).astype(int)
        H, Wd = depth.shape
        ok = (z > 0) & (u >= 0) & (u < Wd) & (v >= 0) & (v < H)
        d = np.zeros_like(z); d[ok] = depth[v[ok], u[ok]]
        sdf = d - z                                         # projective signed distance along the ray
        ok &= (d > 0) & (sdf > -self.trunc)                 # skip voxels far behind the surface
        f = np.clip(sdf / self.trunc, -1, 1)
        T, Wt = self.tsdf.reshape(-1), self.w.reshape(-1)
        T[ok] = (Wt[ok] * T[ok] + f[ok]) / (Wt[ok] + 1)     # running weighted average
        Wt[ok] = np.minimum(Wt[ok] + 1, max_w)

    def mesh(self):
        verts, faces, normals, _ = marching_cubes(self.tsdf, level=0.0)   # Lorensen & Cline 1987
        return self.o + (verts + 0.5) * self.v, faces

def render_sphere_depth(K, C2W, H, W, centre, r):
    """Ray-cast a depth map of a sphere (stand-in for a depth sensor)."""
    u, v = np.meshgrid(np.arange(W), np.arange(H))
    dirs = np.stack([(u - K[0, 2]) / K[0, 0], (v - K[1, 2]) / K[1, 1], np.ones_like(u, float)], -1)
    dw = dirs @ C2W[:3, :3].T; o = C2W[:3, 3]
    oc = o - centre; a = (dw ** 2).sum(-1); b = 2 * (dw * oc).sum(-1); c = oc @ oc - r * r
    disc = b * b - 4 * a * c
    s = np.where(disc > 0, (-b - np.sqrt(np.maximum(disc, 0))) / (2 * a), 0)
    return s                                                # dirs have z = 1, so s is the z-depth

def look_at(eye, target):
    z = target - eye; z /= np.linalg.norm(z)
    x = np.cross(z, [0, -1, 0]); x /= np.linalg.norm(x); y = np.cross(z, x)
    M = np.eye(4); M[:3, :3] = np.c_[x, y, z]; M[:3, 3] = eye; return M

07Neural radiance fields

A neural radiance field (NeRF) represents a scene as a continuous function FΘ(x, d) → (σ, c) that maps a 3D position and viewing direction to a volume density σ and an emitted colour c. Mildenhall et al. fit this function, an MLP, to a set of posed photographs by rendering it differentiably and minimising the difference to the captured pixels. The result produced novel views of a quality that was a step change for complex real scenes [81].

Volume rendering

The colour of a camera ray r(t) = o + td is the classical volume rendering integral [82,83]:

C(r) = ∫ T(t) σ(r(t)) c(r(t), d) dt,    T(t) = exp(−∫tnt σ(r(s)) ds)

T(t) is the transmittance: the probability that light travels from t to the camera unblocked. NeRF approximates the integral with N samples using the quadrature rule [81,83]:

Ĉ = Σi Ti αi ci,    αi = 1 − exp(−σiδi),    Ti = Πj<i(1 − αj)

This is alpha compositing: each sample is a semi-transparent layer. Every operation is differentiable, so the photometric loss ‖Ĉ − C‖² can be back-propagated to the network weights. The same weights wi = Tiαi give an expected depth Σwiti for free.

volume_render.py — positional encoding and the NeRF quadratureNumPy
import numpy as np

def positional_encoding(x, L=10):
    """gamma(p) = (sin(2^k pi p), cos(2^k pi p))_{k<L}, applied per coordinate (Mildenhall et al. 2020)."""
    freqs = (2.0 ** np.arange(L)) * np.pi
    xf = x[..., None] * freqs                            # (..., D, L)
    return np.concatenate([x, np.sin(xf).reshape(*x.shape[:-1], -1),
                           np.cos(xf).reshape(*x.shape[:-1], -1)], -1)

def volume_render(sigma, rgb, t):
    """Quadrature of the volume rendering integral (Kajiya & Von Herzen 1984; Max 1995).
    sigma: (R,S) densities, rgb: (R,S,3), t: (R,S) sample depths along each ray."""
    delta = np.diff(t, axis=-1, append=1e10)             # distance between samples
    alpha = 1.0 - np.exp(-sigma * delta)                 # opacity of each segment
    T = np.cumprod(np.concatenate([np.ones_like(alpha[:, :1]), 1 - alpha[:, :-1] + 1e-10], -1), -1)
    w = T * alpha                                         # contribution weights
    return (w[..., None] * rgb).sum(1), (w * t).sum(1), w   # colour, expected depth, weights

Positional encoding and view dependence

MLPs fed raw coordinates are biased toward low-frequency functions, a phenomenon called spectral bias [84]. NeRF maps each coordinate through sinusoids of increasing frequency before the network, γ(p) = (sin 2⁰πp, cos 2⁰πp, …, sin 2L−1πp, cos 2L−1πp), with L = 10 for position and L = 4 for direction [81]. Tancik et al. explained the effect through the neural tangent kernel and generalised it to random Fourier features [85]. Density depends only on position, while colour also depends on viewing direction, which lets NeRF model specular highlights but keeps geometry consistent across views [81].

import torch, torch.nn as nn

class TinyNeRF(nn.Module):
    """Minimal NeRF MLP: (gamma(x), gamma(d)) -> (sigma, rgb)."""
    def __init__(self, Lx=10, Ld=4, W=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.Lx, self.Ld = Lx, Ld
        dx, dd = 3 + 6 * Lx, 3 + 6 * Ld
        self.trunk = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(dx, W), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(W, W), nn.ReLU(),
                                   nn.Linear(W, W), nn.ReLU())
        self.sigma = nn.Linear(W, 1)
        self.color = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(W + dd, W // 2), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(W // 2, 3), nn.Sigmoid())

    @staticmethod
    def pe(x, L):
        f = (2.0 ** torch.arange(L, device=x.device)) * torch.pi
        xf = (x[..., None] * f).flatten(-2)
        return torch.cat([x, xf.sin(), xf.cos()], -1)

    def forward(self, x, d):
        h = self.trunk(self.pe(x, self.Lx))
        sigma = torch.relu(self.sigma(h)).squeeze(-1)   # density depends on position only
        rgb = self.color(torch.cat([h, self.pe(d, self.Ld)], -1))  # colour is view dependent
        return sigma, rgb

def render_rays(model, o, d, near=2.0, far=6.0, S=64, perturb=True):
    t = torch.linspace(near, far, S, device=o.device).expand(o.shape[0], S).clone()
    if perturb:                                          # stratified sampling
        t = t + torch.rand_like(t) * (far - near) / S
    x = o[:, None] + t[..., None] * d[:, None]           # (R,S,3)
    sigma, rgb = model(x, d[:, None].expand_as(x))
    delta = torch.cat([t[:, 1:] - t[:, :-1], torch.full_like(t[:, :1], 1e10)], -1)
    alpha = 1 - torch.exp(-sigma * delta)
    T = torch.cumprod(torch.cat([torch.ones_like(alpha[:, :1]), 1 - alpha + 1e-10], -1), -1)[:, :-1]
    w = T * alpha
    return (w[..., None] * rgb).sum(1), (w * t).sum(1)

    return c2w[:3, 3].expand_as(d).reshape(-1, 3), (d / d.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)).reshape(-1, 3)

Making NeRF fast and robust

The original NeRF evaluated a large MLP hundreds of times per ray and took a day or more to train a scene on one GPU [81]. Later work moved capacity from the network into explicit data structures. Plenoxels optimise a sparse voxel grid of densities and spherical-harmonic colour coefficients with no neural network at all [86]. TensoRF factorises the radiance field into compact low-rank tensor components [87]. Instant-NGP stores trainable features in a multiresolution hash table queried by a tiny MLP, cutting training to seconds or minutes [88]. Mip-NeRF 360 handles unbounded real scenes with a non-linear scene contraction, anti-aliased conical frustum sampling and a distortion regulariser [89]. NeuS replaces density with an SDF-derived density, so the same volume rendering yields a clean zero-level-set surface [90].

083D Gaussian splatting

3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) represents a scene explicitly as millions of anisotropic 3D Gaussians and renders them with a fast rasteriser rather than by marching rays through a network. Kerbl et al. showed that it matches the quality of the best NeRF variants on standard benchmarks while training in tens of minutes and rendering at 1080p in real time (at least 30 frames per second) [91].

The representation

Each Gaussian has a mean μ, a covariance Σ, an opacity o and a view-dependent colour represented by spherical-harmonic coefficients (degree 3, i.e. 16 coefficients per colour channel). To keep Σ positive semi-definite during gradient descent, it is factorised as Σ = R S ST RT with a scale vector S and a rotation stored as a unit quaternion [91]. The Gaussians are initialised from the sparse SfM points that COLMAP produces anyway [5].

Projection and rasterisation

For rendering, each 3D Gaussian is projected to a 2D Gaussian on the image using the local affine approximation from EWA splatting [92]: Σ′ = J W Σ WT JT, where W is the world-to-camera rotation and J the Jacobian of the perspective projection at the Gaussian's centre. The screen is split into 16 × 16-pixel tiles; Gaussians are assigned to the tiles they overlap, sorted by depth with a single GPU radix sort, and alpha-composited front to back per pixel with the same formula NeRF uses, C = Σi ci αi Πj<i(1 − αj) [91]. Here αi is the opacity times the 2D Gaussian evaluated at the pixel.

Optimisation and densification

All Gaussian parameters are optimised with stochastic gradient descent against the training images using a combination of L1 and D-SSIM loss. Every few hundred iterations, adaptive density control clones small Gaussians in under-reconstructed regions, splits large Gaussians in regions of high variance, and removes Gaussians whose opacity has dropped near zero [91]. The listing below implements the forward pass for a handful of Gaussians without tiling, so each step is visible.

splat.py — projecting and compositing 3D GaussiansNumPy
import numpy as np

def quat_to_R(q):
    w, x, y, z = q / np.linalg.norm(q)
    return np.array([[1-2*(y*y+z*z), 2*(x*y-w*z), 2*(x*z+w*y)],
                     [2*(x*y+w*z), 1-2*(x*x+z*z), 2*(y*z-w*x)],
                     [2*(x*z-w*y), 2*(y*z+w*x), 1-2*(x*x+y*y)]])

def project_gaussian(mu, scale, q, W2C, fx, fy, cx, cy):
    """3D Gaussian -> 2D screen-space Gaussian (Kerbl et al. 2023, eqs. 5-6; Zwicker et al. 2001).
    Sigma = R S S^T R^T;  Sigma' = J W Sigma W^T J^T."""
    R = quat_to_R(q); S = np.diag(scale)
    Sigma = R @ S @ S.T @ R.T
    t = W2C[:3, :3] @ mu + W2C[:3, 3]                     # camera-space mean
    J = np.array([[fx / t[2], 0, -fx * t[0] / t[2] ** 2],  # Jacobian of perspective projection
                  [0, fy / t[2], -fy * t[1] / t[2] ** 2]])
    cov2d = J @ W2C[:3, :3] @ Sigma @ W2C[:3, :3].T @ J.T
    cov2d += 0.3 * np.eye(2)                               # low-pass filter used by the reference code
    mean2d = np.array([fx * t[0] / t[2] + cx, fy * t[1] / t[2] + cy])
    return mean2d, cov2d, t[2]

def rasterize(gaussians, H, W, W2C, f):
    """Front-to-back alpha compositing of depth-sorted splats (no tiling, for clarity)."""
    img = np.zeros((H, W, 3)); T = np.ones((H, W))
    ys, xs = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W] + 0.5
    proj = [(project_gaussian(g["mu"], g["scale"], g["q"], W2C, f, f, W / 2, H / 2), g) for g in gaussians]
    for (m, C, z), g in sorted(proj, key=lambda p: p[0][2]):   # sort by depth
        Ci = np.linalg.inv(C); dx, dy = xs - m[0], ys - m[1]
        power = -0.5 * (Ci[0, 0] * dx * dx + 2 * Ci[0, 1] * dx * dy + Ci[1, 1] * dy * dy)
        alpha = np.minimum(0.99, g["opacity"] * np.exp(power))
        alpha[alpha < 1 / 255] = 0
        img += (T * alpha)[..., None] * g["color"]
        T *= 1 - alpha
    return img, T

093D deep learning

Convolutional networks assume a regular grid. 3D data usually arrives as an unordered, irregular point set, so 3D deep learning has developed three families of architecture: networks that consume points directly, networks that voxelise and use sparse convolutions, and transformers that apply attention to local point neighbourhoods. Guo et al. survey the field [33].

PointNet-style architectures

A network on point sets must give the same answer for any ordering of its input. PointNet achieves this by applying a shared MLP to every point independently and aggregating with a symmetric function, max pooling, into a global feature [94]. Qi et al. proved that this form can approximate any continuous set function, and showed that the global feature is determined by a small subset of critical points. Small learned alignment networks (T-nets) predict a transform that canonicalises the input and the features. For segmentation, the global feature is concatenated back onto each point's local feature.

PointNet has no notion of local neighbourhoods. PointNet++ applies PointNet hierarchically: farthest-point sampling picks centroids, ball queries gather neighbours, and a mini-PointNet summarises each neighbourhood, with multi-scale grouping to cope with uneven density [95]. DGCNN builds a k-nearest-neighbour graph in feature space at each layer and applies an edge convolution over it, so the graph itself changes as features are learned [96]. KPConv defines convolution weights at kernel points in 3D space and interpolates them to the actual neighbours, giving a true point convolution [97].

    def __init__(self, k):
        super().__init__(); self.k = k
        self.f = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv1d(k, 64, 1), nn.BatchNorm1d(64), nn.ReLU(),
                               nn.Conv1d(64, 128, 1), nn.BatchNorm1d(128), nn.ReLU(),
                               nn.Conv1d(128, 512, 1), nn.BatchNorm1d(512), nn.ReLU())
        self.head = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(512, 256), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(256, k * k))
        nn.init.zeros_(self.head[-1].weight); nn.init.zeros_(self.head[-1].bias)   # start at identity
    def forward(self, x):                                   # x: (B,k,N)
        g = self.f(x).max(-1).values
        return torch.eye(self.k, device=x.device) + self.head(g).view(-1, self.k, self.k)

class PointNetCls(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_classes=40):
        super().__init__()
        self.t_in, self.t_feat = TNet(3), TNet(64)
        self.mlp1 = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv1d(3, 64, 1), nn.BatchNorm1d(64), nn.ReLU())
        self.mlp2 = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv1d(64, 128, 1), nn.BatchNorm1d(128), nn.ReLU(),
                                  nn.Conv1d(128, 1024, 1), nn.BatchNorm1d(1024))
        self.cls = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(1024, 512), nn.BatchNorm1d(512), nn.ReLU(), nn.Dropout(0.3),
                                 nn.Linear(512, 256), nn.BatchNorm1d(256), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(256, n_classes))
    def forward(self, pts):                                 # pts: (B,N,3)
        x = pts.transpose(1, 2)
        x = torch.bmm(self.t_in(x), x)                      # align input
        x = self.mlp1(x)
        A = self.t_feat(x); x = torch.bmm(A, x)             # align features
        g = self.mlp2(x).max(-1).values                     # symmetric function -> order invariance
        reg = ((A @ A.transpose(1, 2) - torch.eye(64)) ** 2).sum((1, 2)).mean()  # orthogonality penalty
        return self.cls(g), reg

Sparse convolutions

Voxelising a point cloud lets standard convolution machinery work, but a dense 3D grid wastes almost all computation on empty space. Sparse convolutional networks store only active voxels in a hash table and compute outputs only where needed. Graham et al. introduced submanifold sparse convolution, which produces outputs only at sites that were already active. Ordinary sparse convolution dilates the active set at every layer and quickly destroys sparsity [98]. The Minkowski Engine generalised sparse tensors and convolutions to arbitrary dimensions, including 4D space-time [99]. In LiDAR object detection, VoxelNet learned voxel features with a PointNet-style layer [100], SECOND made the backbone sparse and fast [101], and PointPillars collapsed the vertical axis into pillars so that a 2D CNN could do the rest [102].

sparse_conv.py — hash-map sparse convolution, regular versus submanifoldNumPy
import numpy as np

def sparse_conv3d(coords, feats, weight, submanifold=True):
    """Naive sparse 3D convolution over a hash map of active voxels.
    coords: (N,3) int voxel indices, feats: (N,Cin), weight: (3,3,3,Cin,Cout).
    Submanifold mode (Graham et al. 2018) only writes outputs at already-active sites,
    so sparsity does not 'dilate' layer after layer."""
    table = {tuple(c): i for i, c in enumerate(coords)}       # coordinate -> row
    offsets = [(i, j, k) for i in (-1, 0, 1) for j in (-1, 0, 1) for k in (-1, 0, 1)]
    if submanifold:
        out_coords = coords
    else:                                                       # regular sparse conv dilates the set
        out_coords = np.unique((coords[:, None] + np.array(offsets)[None]).reshape(-1, 3), axis=0)
    out = np.zeros((len(out_coords), weight.shape[-1]))
    for o, c in enumerate(out_coords):
        for (di, dj, dk) in offsets:
            r = table.get((c[0] + di, c[1] + dj, c[2] + dk))
            if r is not None:                                   # only multiply where input exists
                out[o] += feats[r] @ weight[di + 1, dj + 1, dk + 1]
    return out_coords, out

3D transformers

Self-attention is a set operator, which makes it a natural fit for points. Point Transformer applies vector self-attention within local k-nearest-neighbour neighbourhoods, with a learned positional encoding of relative coordinates [103]. Point Transformer V3 argues that scale matters more than intricate attention design: it serialises points along space-filling curves so that neighbours become contiguous in memory, then applies efficient patch attention over the serialised sequence. That gives a much larger receptive field at lower cost, and strong results across indoor and outdoor segmentation and detection benchmarks [104].

Exercise 9.1 — Why max and not sum?

Both max and sum pooling are symmetric. Give one reason PointNet's authors preferred max pooling, and one situation where sum or mean pooling would behave differently.

Show solution

Max pooling makes the global feature depend on only a subset of critical points, so the output is robust to added interior points, to missing non-critical points and to moderate noise; the authors found it worked better empirically than sum or attention-based alternatives [94]. Sum pooling scales with the number of points, so the same shape sampled at 1,024 or 2,048 points would give different features unless normalised; mean pooling removes that but averages away distinctive local evidence.

10Shape completion

Real scans are incomplete. Occlusion hides the back of every object, reflective and dark surfaces return no depth, and a limited set of viewpoints always leaves gaps. Shape completion predicts the missing geometry from what was observed, using priors learned from large shape collections such as ShapeNet [105].

Approaches

Classical methods fill small holes by smooth interpolation, for example Poisson reconstruction [48], or by exploiting symmetry. Learned methods can hallucinate large missing parts. 3D-EPN encodes a partial TSDF with a 3D CNN, predicts a coarse complete shape, and refines it using nearest neighbours retrieved from a shape database [106]. PCN works on points directly: a PointNet-style encoder produces a global code, and a decoder first produces a coarse cloud and then folds a 2D grid around each coarse point to produce a dense output [107]. PoinTr recasts completion as set-to-set translation with a geometry-aware transformer over point proxies [108]. Implicit models such as DeepSDF and Occupancy Networks complete shapes by inferring a latent code from the partial input and decoding a continuous surface [79,80].

Metrics

Completion is evaluated by comparing predicted and true point sets. The Chamfer distance averages, in both directions, the squared distance from each point to its nearest neighbour in the other set [109]:

CD(A, B) = (1/|A|) Σa∈A minb∈B ‖a − b‖² + (1/|B|) Σb∈B mina∈A ‖a − b‖²

Earth mover's distance requires a one-to-one matching and is more sensitive to density but costlier. Tatarchenko et al. showed that averaged distances can hide important errors and recommended the F-score at a distance threshold τ: the harmonic mean of precision (fraction of predicted points within τ of the truth) and recall (fraction of true points within τ of the prediction) [110].

completion.py — Chamfer distance and a PCN-style completion networkPyTorch 2.x
import torch, torch.nn as nn

def chamfer(a, b):
    """Symmetric Chamfer distance between point sets a:(B,N,3), b:(B,M,3) (Fan et al. 2017)."""
    d = torch.cdist(a, b) ** 2
    return d.min(2).values.mean(1) + d.min(1).values.mean(1)

class TinyPCN(nn.Module):
    """PCN-style encoder-decoder (Yuan et al. 2018): partial cloud -> global code -> full cloud."""
    def __init__(self, n_out=1024, code=512):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc1 = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv1d(3, 128, 1), nn.ReLU(), nn.Conv1d(128, 256, 1))
        self.enc2 = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv1d(512, 512, 1), nn.ReLU(), nn.Conv1d(512, code, 1))
        self.dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(code, 1024), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(1024, n_out * 3))
        self.n_out = n_out
    def forward(self, partial):                             # (B,N,3)
        f = self.enc1(partial.transpose(1, 2))               # per-point features
        g = f.max(-1, keepdim=True).values                   # global feature
        f = torch.cat([f, g.expand_as(f)], 1)                # concat global to each point
        code = self.enc2(f).max(-1).values
        return self.dec(code).view(-1, self.n_out, 3)

def sphere(n): x = torch.randn(n, 3); return x / x.norm(dim=1, keepdim=True)

for step in range(301):

    partial = torch.stack([s[s[:, 2] > 0.0][:512] for s in full])               # keep top half only
    loss = chamfer(net(partial), full).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if step % 100 == 0: print(f"step {step:3d} chamfer {loss.item():.4f}")

11Novel-view synthesis

Novel-view synthesis renders a scene from a viewpoint where no camera stood. Adelson and Bergen formalised what a viewer could ever see as the plenoptic function: the radiance along every ray through every point in space, at every wavelength and time [111]. Every view synthesis method samples or models some slice of that function.

Image-based rendering

Light-field rendering and the Lumigraph treated the problem as resampling: in free space, radiance is constant along a ray, so a 4D set of rays captured on a dense camera grid can be interpolated to form new views without any geometry [112,113]. Sparse captures require geometry. Depth-image-based rendering warps each source pixel into the target view using its depth, which leaves holes wherever the new view sees surfaces that were hidden (disocclusions) and stretches texture at depth edges. Local Light Field Fusion bridged the two approaches by turning each input image into a multiplane image and blending nearby ones, and derived how densely views must be captured for a given quality [114].

dibr.py — forward warping with a z-buffer, and the holes it leavesNumPy
import numpy as np

def warp_to_new_view(img, depth, K, T_src_to_tgt):
    """Depth-image-based rendering: forward-warp pixels into a new camera with a z-buffer.
    Holes (disocclusions) stay empty; filling them is what learned NVS models do."""
    H, W = depth.shape
    u, v = np.meshgrid(np.arange(W), np.arange(H))
    X = (np.linalg.inv(K) @ np.stack([u.ravel(), v.ravel(), np.ones(H * W)])) * depth.ravel()
    Xt = T_src_to_tgt[:3, :3] @ X + T_src_to_tgt[:3, 3:4]
    x = K @ Xt; z = x[2]
    ut, vt = np.round(x[0] / z).astype(int), np.round(x[1] / z).astype(int)
    ok = (z > 0) & (ut >= 0) & (ut < W) & (vt >= 0) & (vt < H)
    out = np.zeros_like(img); zbuf = np.full((H, W), np.inf)
    order = np.argsort(-z[ok])                                  # far to near: near points win
    for i in np.flatnonzero(ok)[order]:
        zbuf[vt[i], ut[i]] = z[i]; out[vt[i], ut[i]] = img.reshape(-1, img.shape[-1])[i]
    return out, np.isinf(zbuf)

H, W = 60, 80
K = np.array([[80, 0, 40], [0, 80, 30], [0, 0, 1.0]])
depth = np.full((H, W), 5.0); depth[20:40, 30:50] = 2.0         # a box in front of a wall
img = np.zeros((H, W, 3)); img[..., 2] = 1; img[20:40, 30:50] = [1, 0, 0]
T = np.eye(4); T[0, 3] = -0.3                                   # camera moves 0.3 m to the right
out, holes = warp_to_new_view(img, depth, K, T)
print("hole pixels:", holes.sum(), f"({100*holes.mean():.1f} % of image)")

# hole pixels: 440 (9.2 % of image)

Scene-specific neural rendering

NeRF and 3D Gaussian splatting (Sections 07 and 08) are today's dominant answers when tens to hundreds of posed images of a static scene are available: they optimise a full 3D representation per scene and render any viewpoint inside the captured volume [81,91]. Their quality degrades outside the convex hull of the input cameras and with sparse views, because the per-scene optimisation has no prior to fall back on.

Generalisable and generative view synthesis

Generalisable models learn priors across scenes. pixelNeRF conditions a radiance field on image features from one or a few input views, so it can render new views without per-scene optimisation [115]. Generative models go further and invent unseen content. Zero-1-to-3 fine-tunes a large image diffusion model to generate an object from a new camera viewpoint given one image and a relative pose [116]. DreamFusion optimises a NeRF so that its renderings score well under a text-conditioned 2D diffusion model, producing 3D objects from text alone [117]. Feed-forward geometry models such as DUSt3R and VGGT supply camera poses and dense geometry from few images and are increasingly used to initialise splatting or NeRF from sparse captures [49,50].

Evaluation

View synthesis is evaluated on held-out views with PSNR (pixel fidelity), SSIM (local structure) [118] and LPIPS, a learned perceptual distance that correlates better with human judgements than either [119]. Reporting all three is standard practice in NeRF and 3DGS papers [81,91]. Generative methods that invent content are additionally judged on multi-view consistency, since a plausible but inconsistent result cannot be turned into a coherent 3D asset.
