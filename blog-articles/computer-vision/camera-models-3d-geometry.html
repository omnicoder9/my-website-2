Every algorithm that measures the world from images rests on a model of how the camera forms them. The model says which ray of light each pixel records. Once rays are known, geometry takes over: two rays from different viewpoints meet at a 3D point, and rays to points of a known object fix where the camera must be. The standard references for this material are Hartley and Zisserman's Multiple View Geometry, Szeliski's Computer Vision: Algorithms and Applications and Ma, Soatto, Košecká and Sastry's An Invitation to 3-D Vision [1,2,3]. This article follows their notation where they agree.

The sections build on each other. Sections 01 to 04 set up the camera, the frames it lives in and the algebra (rotations and homogeneous coordinates) used everywhere after. Section 05 shows how the camera's parameters are measured. Sections 06 to 08 work with two or more views: epipolar geometry, stereo depth and triangulation. Sections 09 and 10 recover the pose of a camera or an object from a single image. Section 11 collects the conventions that cause most real-world bugs. The companion article Seeing in Depth takes these foundations into dense stereo, structure from motion, SLAM and neural scene representations.

01Camera models

A camera model is a function from 3D points to 2D image points. Real lenses are complicated optical systems, but for geometry a few idealised models cover nearly every case. They differ in one thing: whether, and how, the image of a point depends on its depth.

The pinhole camera

A pinhole camera admits light through a single point, the centre of projection or optical centre C. Each scene point X sends one ray through C, and that ray meets the image plane at x. A thin lens focused at infinity forms the same geometry, with the image plane at the focal length behind the lens, which is why the pinhole model describes ordinary cameras well once lens distortion is removed [1,5]. The physical image forms behind C, upside down; it is mathematically equivalent and more convenient to put a virtual image plane at distance f in front of C, where the image is upright [1].

Place the camera frame at C with the Z axis along the optical axis. By similar triangles, a point (X, Y, Z) lands at

x = f X / Z,    y = f Y / Z

on the image plane. This is perspective projection. Dividing by Z is what makes distant objects small, makes parallel lines converge to vanishing points, and destroys depth: every point on the ray, (λX, λY, λZ) for any λ > 0, lands on the same image point [1,2].

Orthographic, weak-perspective and affine cameras

An orthographic camera projects along parallel rays: (X, Y, Z) maps to (sX, sY) for a fixed scale s, independent of depth. It is the limit of a pinhole camera whose centre recedes to infinity while the focal length grows to keep the scale fixed, exactly the dolly zoom in Figure 0 [1]. Telecentric lenses used in metrology approximate it physically.

A weak-perspective (scaled orthographic) camera divides every point by the same average depth Z₀ instead of its own depth: x = f X / Z₀. It is accurate when the object's depth extent ΔZ is small relative to Z₀, because then each point's Z is close to Z₀. Aloimonos analysed orthographic, weak-perspective and paraperspective approximations and the conditions under which each holds [6]. The general affine camera is any 3×4 projection whose last row is (0, 0, 0, 1); it preserves parallelism and ratios of lengths along a line, and it is the model behind the Tomasi–Kanade factorisation method for structure from motion [1,7].

Listing 1.1 projects the eight corners of a 1 m cube placed 5 m away with all three models. The near and far faces separate under perspective and coincide under orthography, and the weak-perspective error drops about a hundredfold when the cube moves ten times further away, because the error scales roughly with (X/Z)(ΔZ/Z).

Listing 1.1 — Perspective, orthographic and weak-perspective projectionNumPy
import numpy as np

# Corners of a 1 m cube centred 5 m in front of the camera (camera frame, metres)
cube = np.array([[x, y, z] for x in (-.5, .5) for y in (-.5, .5) for z in (4.5, 5.5)])
f = 800.0                      # focal length in pixels
cx, cy = 320.0, 240.0          # principal point

def perspective(X):
    return np.c_[f * X[:, 0] / X[:, 2] + cx, f * X[:, 1] / X[:, 2] + cy]

def orthographic(X, s=f / 5.0):  # s: pixels per metre (fixed scale, no depth division)
    return np.c_[s * X[:, 0] + cx, s * X[:, 1] + cy]

def weak_perspective(X):         # divide by the *average* depth Z0 instead of each Z
    Z0 = X[:, 2].mean()
    return np.c_[f * X[:, 0] / Z0 + cx, f * X[:, 1] / Z0 + cy]

P, O, W = perspective(cube), orthographic(cube), weak_perspective(cube)
np.set_printoptions(precision=1, suppress=True)
print("near face, perspective :", P[cube[:, 2] == 4.5][:2])
print("far face,  perspective :", P[cube[:, 2] == 5.5][:2])
print("near face, orthographic:", O[cube[:, 2] == 4.5][:2])
print("far face,  orthographic:", O[cube[:, 2] == 5.5][:2])
print("max |persp - weak| px  : %.1f" % np.abs(P - W).max())
# Move the cube 10x further away: weak perspective error shrinks with depth/size ratio
far = cube + [0, 0, 45]
print("same, cube at 50 m     : %.2f" % np.abs(perspective(far) - weak_perspective(far)).max())

Beyond the pinhole

Fisheye and catadioptric (mirror plus lens) cameras can see more than 180°, which no pinhole camera can: a ray at 90° to the optical axis would land at infinity on the image plane. They are described instead by a mapping from ray angle θ to image radius r. Kannala and Brandt's generic model writes r as an odd polynomial in θ and covers conventional, wide-angle and fisheye lenses; it underlies OpenCV's cv2.fisheye module [4,8]. Usenko, Demmel and Cremers' double-sphere model is a closed-form alternative with a cheap analytic inverse [9]. Everything later in this article applies to such cameras once pixels are converted to unit ray directions.

Exercise 1.1 — From millimetres to pixels

A 50 mm lens is mounted on a full-frame sensor 36 mm wide that records 6000 pixels across. What is the focal length in pixels, and what is the horizontal field of view?

Show solution

Pixel pitch is 36 / 6000 = 0.006 mm, so f = 50 / 0.006 ≈ 8333 px. The half-width of the sensor subtends atan(18 / 50), so the field of view is 2·atan(0.36) ≈ 39.6°. Equivalently, FOV = 2·atan(W / 2f) with W = 6000 px and f = 8333 px [1,2].

Exercise 1.2 — The dolly zoom

A subject stands 5 m from a camera with f = 800 px; a wall is 15 m behind the subject. The camera moves back to 10 m from the subject. What focal length keeps the subject the same size, and how much larger does the wall look than before?

Show solution

Image size is proportional to f / Z. Keeping the subject fixed needs f / 10 = 800 / 5, so f = 1600 px. The wall was at 20 m with magnification 800 / 20 = 40 and is now at 25 m with 1600 / 25 = 64, so it looks 1.6 times larger. Backing away while zooming in compresses depth and pushes the image toward orthographic projection, as Figure 0 shows.

Exercise 1.3 — When is weak perspective good enough?

In Listing 1.1 the worst weak-perspective error is 8.9 px for the cube at 5 m and 0.08 px at 50 m. Derive the first-order error and explain the ratio.

Show solution

Write Z = Z₀ + δ with |δ| ≤ ΔZ/2. Then f X / Z − f X / Z₀ ≈ −(f X / Z₀)(δ / Z₀). The first factor is the point's image offset from the principal point, which falls as 1/Z₀ for a fixed object; the second is the relative depth spread, which also falls as 1/Z₀. The error therefore scales as 1/Z₀²; moving ten times further away shrinks it about 100 times (8.9 → 0.089 px, matching 0.08 to rounding). A common rule of thumb is to use weak perspective only when the depth extent is under about a tenth of the distance and the object is near the image centre [1,6].

02Coordinate systems

Projecting a point involves four coordinate systems. Mixing them up is the most common bug in geometric vision code, so it is worth being explicit about each [1,2].

  • World coordinates (Xw, Yw, Zw), in metres: a fixed frame chosen for the application, such as a calibration board corner, a robot base or a map origin.
  • Camera coordinates (Xc, Yc, Zc), in metres: origin at the optical centre, Zc along the optical axis. In OpenCV's convention x points right and y points down in the image, so Zc points forward into the scene [4].
  • Normalised image coordinates (xn, yn) = (Xc/Zc, Yc/Zc), unitless: the image on a virtual plane at distance 1. These are the coordinates of a calibrated camera, and the essential matrix (Section 06) lives here.
  • Pixel coordinates (u, v): column and row on the sensor, origin at the top-left. In OpenCV the centre of the top-left pixel is (0, 0) [4].

The steps between them are a rigid transform, a perspective division and an affine map:

Xc = R Xw + t  →  xn = Xc/Zc, yn = Yc/Zc  →  u = fx xn + s yn + cx,  v = fy yn + cy

The last step is the intrinsic matrix K. fx and fy are the focal length in units of pixel width and pixel height (they differ if pixels are not square), (cx, cy) is the principal point where the optical axis meets the sensor, and s is a skew term that is zero for essentially all modern sensors [1,10]. R and t, the extrinsics, map world to camera coordinates. Note what t is: the world origin expressed in camera coordinates. The camera's centre in world coordinates is C = −RTt.

K = [ fx   s   cx ;  0   fy   cy ;  0   0   1 ],     λ ũ = K [R | t] X̃w = P X̃w

Listing 2.1 walks one point through the chain for a camera placed 2 m behind the world origin and looking along world +Y. The rows of R are the camera's x, y and z axes written in world coordinates, which is the quickest way to build R by hand.

Listing 2.1 — World → camera → normalised → pixelNumPy
import numpy as np

# World -> camera -> normalised image -> pixel, written out step by step.
K = np.array([[800., 0, 320], [0, 800., 240], [0, 0, 1]])
# Camera 2 m behind the world origin, looking along world +Y; world Z is "up".
# Rows of R are the camera axes expressed in world coordinates:
#   x_cam = world +X (right), y_cam = world -Z (down), z_cam = world +Y (forward)
R = np.array([[1, 0, 0], [0, 0, -1], [0, 1, 0]], float)
C = np.array([0., -2., 1.])          # camera centre in world coordinates
t = -R @ C                           # so that X_c = R X_w + t

Xw = np.array([0.3, 1.0, 1.5])       # a world point (metres)
Xc = R @ Xw + t                      # camera frame (metres)
xn = Xc[:2] / Xc[2]                  # normalised image coordinates (unitless)
uv = (K @ np.r_[xn, 1])[:2]          # pixel coordinates
print("X_c =", Xc, " x_n =", xn.round(4), " (u,v) =", uv.round(2))

# Same thing as one 3x4 projection matrix acting on a homogeneous point
P = K @ np.c_[R, t]
x = P @ np.r_[Xw, 1]
print("P X =", (x[:2] / x[2]).round(2))

# OpenCV camera: x right, y down, z forward.  OpenGL/Blender camera: x right, y up, z backward.
cv_to_gl = np.diag([1., -1., -1.])
print("same point in OpenGL camera frame:", cv_to_gl @ Xc)

Competing camera conventions

Not every library agrees with OpenCV. OpenGL, Blender and many graphics engines put the camera looking down its −Z axis with y up; converting a camera-frame point between the two conventions flips the y and z axes, as the last line of Listing 2.1 shows [11]. In ROS, an _optical frame suffix denotes the OpenCV-style z-forward, x-right, y-down frame, while the camera body frame follows the robotics x-forward, y-left, z-up convention [12]. When a pose looks "almost right but upside down" or a point cloud appears behind the camera, a convention mismatch is the first suspect.

Exercise 2.1 — Pixel centres

Library A puts the centre of the top-left pixel at (0, 0); library B puts the top-left corner of that pixel at (0, 0), so pixel centres are at half-integers. A camera calibrated in A has cx = 319.5. What is cx in B?

Show solution

B's coordinates are A's plus 0.5, so cx = 320.0 (and likewise for cy). Focal lengths are unchanged. A half-pixel error in the principal point is invisible in a casual check but biases pose estimates; record the convention alongside every calibration file.

Exercise 2.2 — Resizing and cropping change K

A camera with fx = fy = 800, cx = 320, cy = 240 (OpenCV pixel-centre convention) records 640×480 images. (a) The images are cropped to the region starting at column 100, row 40. (b) Separately, the full images are downscaled by a factor of 2. Give K in each case.

Show solution

(a) Cropping only moves the origin: cx = 220, cy = 200, focal lengths unchanged. (b) Scaling multiplies focal lengths by 0.5, giving f = 400. For the principal point, convert to corner coordinates, scale, and convert back: c' = (c + 0.5)·0.5 − 0.5, so cx = 159.75, cy = 119.75. Using c/2 = 160 instead is a quarter-pixel error that grows with stronger downscaling.

Exercise 2.3 — Where is the camera?

Show that the camera centre in world coordinates is C = −RTt, and that the camera's optical axis in world coordinates is the third row of R.

Show solution

The camera centre has camera coordinates (0, 0, 0), so 0 = R C + t and C = −R−1t = −RTt because R is orthogonal. The optical axis is the camera's z direction e₃ = (0, 0, 1) in camera coordinates; a direction transforms by R alone, so in world coordinates it is RTe₃, the third column of RT, which is the third row of R. In Listing 2.1 that row is (0, 1, 0): the camera looks along world +Y [1].

03Rigid-body transformations

Cameras and objects move without bending, so the transforms between their frames are rigid: they preserve distances and handedness. A rigid transform is a rotation followed by a translation, X' = R X + t. The set of all such transforms is the special Euclidean group SE(3), and the rotations alone form the special orthogonal group SO(3) [3,13].

Rotation matrices

A rotation matrix R is a 3×3 matrix with RTR = I and det R = +1. Its columns are the rotated coordinate axes, so they are orthonormal. Orthogonality alone allows det R = −1, which is a reflection; the determinant condition excludes it [3]. Although R has nine entries, the six orthonormality constraints leave three degrees of freedom. Several minimal or near-minimal parameterisations exist, each with a weakness:

RepresentationNumbersStrengthWeakness
Euler angles (e.g. roll, pitch, yaw)3Intuitive for vehiclesTwelve possible axis orders; gimbal lock where two axes align [13]
Axis–angle / rotation vector ω3Minimal; natural for small updates; OpenCV's rvecNot unique at θ = π; wraps at 2π [4,14]
Unit quaternion q4No singularities; cheap composition; smooth interpolation (slerp) [15]q and −q are the same rotation; must stay unit length
Rotation matrix R9Applies directly to points; composes by multiplicationDrifts from orthogonality under repeated arithmetic

No three-number parameterisation of SO(3) is free of singularities, which is why optimisers keep a full rotation (a matrix or a quaternion) and apply small three-parameter corrections to it. Zhou and colleagues showed the same issue matters for neural networks that regress rotations, and proposed continuous six-dimensional representations [14,16].

Rodrigues' formula and the exponential map

The rotation vector ω = θ k encodes a rotation by angle θ about unit axis k. Let [k]× be the skew-symmetric matrix with [k]×v = k × v. Rodrigues' formula, published in 1840, gives the matrix in closed form [17]:

R = exp([ω]×) = I + sin θ [k]× + (1 − cos θ) [k]×²

The inverse (the logarithm) recovers θ from trace R = 1 + 2 cos θ and the axis from the skew-symmetric part of R. In Lie-group language, ω lives in the tangent space so(3), and exp and log move between it and SO(3). The same machinery extends to SE(3) with a six-vector of rotation and translation, which is how pose optimisers in calibration, PnP refinement and bundle adjustment parameterise their updates [13,14,18].

SE(3) and composing frames

Pack R and t into a 4×4 matrix and append a 1 to each point; then the rigid transform is a single matrix product (Section 04 explains why this works):

T = [ R   t ; 0T   1 ],    T−1 = [ RT   −RTt ; 0T   1 ]

A disciplined naming scheme removes most frame bugs. Write Tab for the transform that takes coordinates in frame b to coordinates in frame a, so Xa = Tab Xb. Then subscripts chain like fractions: Tac = Tab Tbc, and Tba = Tab−1. In this notation the extrinsic matrix [R | t] is Tcw (world to camera), and the camera's pose in the world is Twc [13]. Matrix multiplication does not commute, and Listing 3.1 shows two transforms giving different results in the two orders.

Listing 3.1 — Rodrigues' formula, SE(3) inverse, quaternions, re-orthonormalisationNumPy, OpenCV
import numpy as np, cv2

def hat(w):
    return np.array([[0, -w[2], w[1]], [w[2], 0, -w[0]], [-w[1], w[0], 0]])

def rodrigues(w):
    th = np.linalg.norm(w)
    if th < 1e-12:
        return np.eye(3) + hat(w)                 # first-order approximation
    k = hat(w / th)
    return np.eye(3) + np.sin(th) * k + (1 - np.cos(th)) * k @ k

def log_so3(R):
    th = np.arccos(np.clip((np.trace(R) - 1) / 2, -1, 1))
    if th < 1e-12:
        return np.zeros(3)
    return th / (2 * np.sin(th)) * np.array([R[2, 1] - R[1, 2], R[0, 2] - R[2, 0], R[1, 0] - R[0, 1]])

w = np.array([0.2, -0.5, 0.9])
R = rodrigues(w)
print("R^T R = I ?", np.allclose(R.T @ R, np.eye(3)), " det R = %.6f" % np.linalg.det(R))
print("matches cv2.Rodrigues:", np.allclose(R, cv2.Rodrigues(w)[0]))
print("log(exp(w)) =", log_so3(R).round(6))

def se3(R, t):
    T = np.eye(4); T[:3, :3] = R; T[:3, 3] = t; return T

def se3_inv(T):
    R, t = T[:3, :3], T[:3, 3]
    return se3(R.T, -R.T @ t)                     # cheaper and more exact than np.linalg.inv

T_wc = se3(R, [1.0, 2.0, 0.5])                    # camera -> world
T_cw = se3_inv(T_wc)                              # world -> camera
print("T_cw @ T_wc = I ?", np.allclose(T_cw @ T_wc, np.eye(4)))

# Composition is not commutative
A, B = se3(rodrigues([0, 0, np.pi / 2]), [1, 0, 0]), se3(np.eye(3), [0, 1, 0])
print("A@B translation:", (A @ B)[:3, 3].round(3), " B@A translation:", (B @ A)[:3, 3].round(3))

# Unit quaternion (w, x, y, z) from the same axis-angle, and back to a matrix
def quat_to_R(q):
    qw, qx, qy, qz = q / np.linalg.norm(q)
    return np.array([[1-2*(qy*qy+qz*qz), 2*(qx*qy-qz*qw), 2*(qx*qz+qy*qw)],
                     [2*(qx*qy+qz*qw), 1-2*(qx*qx+qz*qz), 2*(qy*qz-qx*qw)],
                     [2*(qx*qz-qy*qw), 2*(qy*qz+qx*qw), 1-2*(qx*qx+qy*qy)]])

th = np.linalg.norm(w); q = np.r_[np.cos(th / 2), np.sin(th / 2) * w / th]
print("q =", q.round(4), "-> same R:", np.allclose(quat_to_R(q), R),
      "; -q -> same R:", np.allclose(quat_to_R(-q), R))

# Re-orthonormalise a drifted matrix with the SVD (nearest rotation in Frobenius norm)
noisy = R + 1e-3 * np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal((3, 3))
U, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(noisy)
Rfix = U @ np.diag([1, 1, np.linalg.det(U @ Vt)]) @ Vt
print("orthogonality error before %.1e, after %.1e" % (np.abs(noisy.T @ noisy - np.eye(3)).max(),
                                                         np.abs(Rfix.T @ Rfix - np.eye(3)).max()))

Exercise 3.1 — Invert a pose by hand

R is a 90° rotation about z, R = [0 −1 0; 1 0 0; 0 0 1], and t = (1, 2, 3). Find the camera centre C = −RTt and verify that T−1 maps it back to the origin.

Show solution

RT = [0 1 0; −1 0 0; 0 0 1], so RTt = (2, −1, 3) and C = (−2, 1, −3). Check: R C + t = (0·(−2) − 1·1 + 0, 1·(−2) + 0 + 0, −3) + (1, 2, 3) = (−1, −2, −3) + (1, 2, 3) = (0, 0, 0). Inverting with RT and −RTt is cheaper and more accurate than a general 4×4 inverse.

Exercise 3.2 — Averaging rotations

Five noisy estimates of the same camera orientation are available as rotation matrices. Why is their element-wise mean not a valid answer, and what should you do instead?

Show solution

SO(3) is not closed under addition: the mean of rotation matrices is generally not orthogonal (average a rotation and its inverse about the same axis and you get a symmetric matrix with det < 1). Two standard fixes: (1) take the element-wise mean and project it back to SO(3) with the SVD step of Listing 3.1, which gives the chordal L2 mean; or (2) average unit quaternions after flipping each into the same hemisphere as the first (q ≃ −q), then normalise. For widely spread rotations, iterate in the tangent space: map each rotation to log(R̄TRi), average the vectors, and update R̄ ← R̄ exp(mean) [14,19].

Exercise 3.3 — The logarithm near 180°

The log_so3 function in Listing 3.1 divides by sin θ. What goes wrong for a rotation of exactly 180°, and how can the axis be recovered?

Show solution

At θ = π, sin θ = 0 and the skew part R − RT is zero, so the formula gives 0/0. But then R = I + 2[k]×² = 2kkT − I, so (R + I)/2 = kkT. Any non-zero column of R + I is proportional to k; normalise it. The sign of k is arbitrary because rotating by +π and −π about k is the same rotation, the non-uniqueness listed in the table above. Robust implementations, including OpenCV's Rodrigues, special-case angles near π [4,14].

04Homogeneous coordinates and projective geometry

Perspective projection divides by depth, which is not a linear operation in ordinary coordinates. Homogeneous coordinates make it linear. A 2D point (x, y) is represented by any 3-vector (wx, wy, w) with w ≠ 0; to go back, divide by the last coordinate. Two homogeneous vectors that differ only by scale represent the same point. The idea comes from 19th-century projective geometry and is the language of the projective plane ℙ² and projective space ℙ³ [1,21].

With this representation the whole pinhole camera becomes one matrix product, λ ũ = P X̃, and a rigid transform becomes the 4×4 matrix T of Section 03. Three further benefits follow.

Points at infinity and vanishing points

A homogeneous vector with w = 0, such as (dx, dy, dz, 0), cannot be divided back to a finite point. It is a point at infinity and represents a direction. Its image, P (d, 0)T = K R d, is the vanishing point of all 3D lines with direction d: parallel rails meet at one image point because they share one point at infinity. The camera translation drops out, so vanishing points depend only on orientation and intrinsics, which is why they are used to calibrate cameras and estimate orientation from man-made scenes [1].

Lines, and the duality of join and meet

A line in the plane, ax + by + c = 0, is also a homogeneous 3-vector l = (a, b, c), and point x lies on l exactly when lTx = 0. The line through two points is their cross product, l = x₁ × x₂, and the intersection of two lines is also a cross product, x = l₁ × l₂. Two parallel lines meet at a point with w = 0, so "parallel lines meet at infinity" stops being a figure of speech [1].

Homographies and the hierarchy of transformations

An invertible 3×3 matrix H acting on homogeneous points, x' ≃ H x, is a homography (projective transformation). It has eight degrees of freedom (nine entries less one for scale) and maps lines to lines. Two images are related exactly by a homography when they view a plane, or when the camera only rotates about its centre, in which case H = K R K−1 [1,2]. Homographies are the top of a hierarchy; each lower level adds constraints and preserves more:

TransformationDOF (2D)Matrix formPreserves
Euclidean (rigid)3[R t; 0 1]Lengths, angles, areas

A homography is estimated from four or more point correspondences with the direct linear transform (DLT): each correspondence x' × (H x) = 0 gives two linear equations in the nine entries of H, and the solution is the singular vector of the stacked system with the smallest singular value. The method dates to Abdel-Aziz and Karara's photogrammetry work [1,22]. Hartley showed that translating and scaling the points first, so they are centred at the origin with average distance √2, makes the linear system far better conditioned [23]. Listing 4.1 implements both the projective-plane operations and a normalised DLT, which agrees with cv2.findHomography to sub-pixel transfer error.

def normalise(pts):

def homography_dlt(src, dst):

        A.append([0, 0, 0, -x, -y, -1, v * x, v * y, v])
    H = np.linalg.svd(np.array(A))[2][-1].reshape(3, 3)
    H = np.linalg.inv(Tb) @ H @ Ta
    return H / H[2, 2]

Exercise 4.4 — Why four points?

Why does a homography need at least four correspondences, and why must no three of them be collinear?

Show solution

H has eight degrees of freedom and each correspondence gives two equations, so four points give the eight needed. If three of the four source points are collinear, then their images must also be collinear (homographies preserve collinearity), and the three give only five independent constraints instead of six, so H is underdetermined. In practice one uses many points, the normalised DLT for initialisation, and RANSAC to reject mismatches (Section 06) [1,24].

05Camera calibration

Calibration measures the intrinsics (fx, fy, cx, cy and optionally skew) and the lens distortion coefficients of a camera, so that every pixel can be turned into an accurate ray. Without it, metric measurement from images is impossible; with it, a camera becomes an angle-measuring instrument [1,10].

Focal length and principal point

The focal length in pixels sets the field of view and the scale of the image (Exercise 1.1). The principal point is where the optical axis hits the sensor. It is usually near the image centre but not at it, because lens and sensor are never perfectly aligned; errors of tens of pixels are common in inexpensive cameras. The principal point and focal length are correlated during estimation, which is one reason calibration needs views from varied angles [2,10].

Lens distortion

Real lenses bend rays away from the pinhole ideal. The dominant effect is radial distortion: points move toward or away from the image centre by an amount that grows with radius. Wide-angle lenses typically show barrel distortion (straight lines bow outward), telephoto lenses pincushion (lines pinch inward). Smaller tangential (decentring) distortion arises when lens elements are not exactly centred on the optical axis. Conrady described decentring distortion in 1919 and Brown gave the polynomial model in 1966; it is still the standard and is often called the Brown–Conrady model [25,26]. In normalised coordinates with r² = x² + y², OpenCV applies it as [4]:

xd = x(1 + k₁r² + k₂r⁴ + k₃r⁶) + 2p₁xy + p₂(r² + 2x²)
yd = y(1 + k₁r² + k₂r⁴ + k₃r⁶) + p₁(r² + 2y²) + 2p₂xy

and then multiplies by K. The ki are radial and the pi tangential coefficients. Applying distortion is a closed-form forward map; removing it is not, so undistortion is done by iteration or by a precomputed lookup table (cv2.initUndistortRectifyMap). Fitzgibbon's single-parameter division model, xu = xd / (1 + λrd²), has the opposite trade-off and can be estimated linearly together with two-view geometry [27].

import numpy as np, cv2

K = np.array([[800., 0, 320], [0, 800., 240], [0, 0, 1]])
dist = np.array([-0.28, 0.07, 1e-3, -5e-4, 0.0])    # k1, k2, p1, p2, k3 (OpenCV order)

def distort(xn, d):
    k1, k2, p1, p2, k3 = d
    x, y = xn[:, 0], xn[:, 1]; r2 = x * x + y * y
    radial = 1 + k1 * r2 + k2 * r2**2 + k3 * r2**3
    xd = x * radial + 2 * p1 * x * y + p2 * (r2 + 2 * x * x)
    yd = y * radial + p1 * (r2 + 2 * y * y) + 2 * p2 * x * y
    return np.c_[xd, yd]

uv_cv, _ = cv2.projectPoints(X, np.zeros(3), np.zeros(3), K, dist)
print("max |ours - cv2.projectPoints| = %.2e px" % np.abs(uv - uv_cv[:, 0]).max())

def undistort(xd, d, iters=20):

Zhang's method

The most widely used calibration procedure, due to Zhang, needs only a flat pattern of known geometry (a checkerboard, a grid of circles, or a ChArUco board) photographed in several orientations [10]. It works in three stages:

  1. One homography per view. Points on the board have Z = 0 in the board frame, so the projection reduces to H = K [r₁ r₂ t] (the first two columns of R and the translation), which is estimated by the DLT of Section 04.
  2. Closed-form intrinsics. Because r₁ and r₂ are orthonormal, each H gives two linear constraints on the symmetric matrix B = K−TK−1, h₁TBh₂ = 0 and h₁TBh₁ = h₂TBh₂. B has five unknowns up to scale (six if skew is free), so three views in general position suffice, or two if skew is fixed at zero. K follows from a Cholesky factorisation of B, and each view's R and t from K−1H.
  3. Non-linear refinement. Starting from that solution, Levenberg–Marquardt minimises the total reprojection error over K, the distortion coefficients and every view's pose. This maximum-likelihood step is what gives the final accuracy.

Earlier methods used precise 3D targets: Tsai's two-stage technique, for example, combined a closed-form solution with radial distortion and was influential in machine-vision metrology [28]. Zhang's planar target made calibration routine for anyone with a printer, and it is the method implemented in cv2.calibrateCamera [4,10].

06Epipolar geometry

Two cameras looking at the same scene are linked by a constraint that depends only on their relative pose and intrinsics, not on the scene. Given a point in one image, its match in the other must lie on a known line. This is epipolar geometry, and it turns correspondence search from 2D into 1D [1,29].

The epipolar constraint

Let the cameras have centres C₁ and C₂, and let a 3D point X project to x₁ and x₂. The three points C₁, C₂ and X span a plane, the epipolar plane. Its intersection with each image is an epipolar line, and every epipolar line in an image passes through the epipole, the image of the other camera's centre. The line C₁C₂ is the baseline [1].

The essential matrix

Suppose the cameras are calibrated, so we can work in normalised coordinates x̂ = K−1ũ, and the second camera's frame is related to the first by X₂ = R X₁ + t. The vectors t, R x̂₁ and x̂₂ are coplanar (they all lie in the epipolar plane, seen from C₂), so their triple product vanishes: x̂₂ · (t × R x̂₁) = 0. Writing the cross product as a matrix gives the epipolar constraint of Longuet-Higgins [30]:

x̂₂T E x̂₁ = 0,    E = [t]× R

E is the essential matrix. It has five degrees of freedom: three for rotation and two for the direction of t, since the overall scale of t cannot be seen from images alone. A 3×3 matrix is an essential matrix exactly when it has two equal non-zero singular values and one zero singular value [1,3]. Five correspondences are the minimum needed to estimate it; Nistér's solver finds the up to ten solutions of the minimal problem and is used inside RANSAC by most structure-from-motion and visual-odometry systems [31]. Decomposing E with the SVD gives four (R, t) candidates; only one puts the triangulated points in front of both cameras (the cheirality test), which cv2.recoverPose applies [1,4].

The fundamental matrix

Substituting x̂ = K−1ũ gives the same constraint in pixel coordinates, ũ₂T F ũ₁ = 0, with the fundamental matrix

F = K₂−T E K₁−1 = K₂−T [t]× R K₁−1

F works without any calibration. It has rank 2 and seven degrees of freedom. The epipolar line in image 2 of a point ũ₁ is l₂ = F ũ₁, the line in image 1 of ũ₂ is l₁ = FTũ₂, and the epipoles are the null vectors: F e₁ = 0 and FTe₂ = 0 [1,29]. Zhang's review compares the many estimation methods and their uncertainty [32].

The eight-point algorithm

Each correspondence gives one linear equation in the nine entries of F. With eight or more, stack them into A f = 0 and take the singular vector with the smallest singular value; then enforce rank 2 by zeroing F's smallest singular value. Longuet-Higgins introduced the method for E [30]. It was considered numerically poor for F until Hartley showed that the Hartley normalisation of Section 04 makes it competitive with iterative methods [23]. Listing 6.1 confirms this: on the same noisy data the normalised version roughly halves the Sampson (first-order geometric) error. Real matches contain outliers, so practical estimation wraps a minimal solver in RANSAC: fit to random minimal samples, count the matches that agree within a threshold, keep the best, and refit to its inliers [24]. Variants such as MLESAC and MAGSAC score hypotheses by likelihood or marginalise over the threshold [33,34].

import numpy as np, cv2
from common import K, two_views

X, R, t, x1, x2, rng = two_views()
x1n, x2n = x1 + rng.normal(0, 0.5, x1.shape), x2 + rng.normal(0, 0.5, x2.shape)

def hartley_normalise(p):
    c = p.mean(0); s = np.sqrt(2) / np.linalg.norm(p - c, axis=1).mean()
    T = np.array([[s, 0, -s * c[0]], [0, s, -s * c[1]], [0, 0, 1]])
    return (T @ np.c_[p, np.ones(len(p))].T).T, T

def eight_point(p1, p2, normalise=True):
    if normalise:
        a, T1 = hartley_normalise(p1); b, T2 = hartley_normalise(p2)
    else:
        a, b, T1, T2 = np.c_[p1, np.ones(len(p1))], np.c_[p2, np.ones(len(p2))], np.eye(3), np.eye(3)
    A = np.array([np.kron(bi, ai) for ai, bi in zip(a, b)])   # row = x2^T F x1 coefficients
    F = np.linalg.svd(A)[2][-1].reshape(3, 3)
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(F); F = U @ np.diag([S[0], S[1], 0]) @ Vt  # enforce rank 2
    F = T2.T @ F @ T1
    return F / np.linalg.norm(F)

def sampson(F, p1, p2):
    a, b = np.c_[p1, np.ones(len(p1))], np.c_[p2, np.ones(len(p2))]
    Fa, Ftb = a @ F.T, b @ F
    num = np.sum(b * Fa, 1) ** 2
    return num / (Fa[:, 0]**2 + Fa[:, 1]**2 + Ftb[:, 0]**2 + Ftb[:, 1]**2)

def tx(v): return np.array([[0, -v[2], v[1]], [v[2], 0, -v[0]], [-v[1], v[0], 0]])

Exercise 6.3 — Pure rotation

What happens to E and F if the camera rotates about its centre without translating? What relates the two images instead?

Show solution

With t = 0, E = [0]×R = 0 and F = 0: the epipolar constraint is satisfied by every pair of points and carries no information, and estimating F from such data gives an arbitrary answer. Exercise 6.4 — Why eight and not seven?

F has seven degrees of freedom. Why does the linear algorithm need eight correspondences, and what does the seven-point algorithm do differently?

Show solution

The linear system has nine unknowns up to scale, so eight equations are needed for a unique null vector; the rank-2 condition det F = 0 is cubic and cannot be included in a linear solve, so it is imposed afterwards. With seven points the null space is two-dimensional, F = αF₁ + (1 − α)F₂; imposing det F = 0 gives a cubic in α with one or three real roots, each a candidate F. Fewer points per sample means fewer RANSAC iterations, which matters at high outlier rates [1,23].

07Stereo vision

A binocular (stereo) camera is two cameras on a rigid mount, calibrated once so that both intrinsics and the relative pose (R, t) between them are known. With that knowledge, depth follows from a single synchronised pair of images. Scharstein and Szeliski's taxonomy describes dense stereo as four steps: matching cost, cost aggregation, disparity optimisation and refinement [35]. This section covers the geometry; the companion article Seeing in Depth covers matching algorithms and learned stereo.

Rectification

Matching is easiest when epipolar lines are horizontal and corresponding lines share the same row, the configuration of Exercise 6.1. Rectification applies a homography to each image so that this holds: it virtually rotates both cameras to be parallel to the baseline and gives them the same intrinsics. Fusiello, Trucco and Verri gave a compact algorithm for calibrated cameras; Hartley, and Loop and Zhang, gave methods that need only F [36,37,38]. In OpenCV, cv2.stereoRectify computes the rectifying rotations and new projection matrices, and cv2.initUndistortRectifyMap builds lookup tables that remove distortion and rectify in one remap [4].

Disparity and depth

After rectification, a point at depth Z appears at column uL in the left image and uR in the right, on the same row. The disparity d = uL − uR is inversely proportional to depth. By similar triangles (Figure 8), with focal length f in pixels and baseline B:

Z = f B / d,    X = (uL − cx) Z / f,    Y = (v − cy) Z / f

OpenCV packages this as the 4×4 reprojection matrix Q returned by stereoRectify, and cv2.reprojectImageTo3D applies it to a whole disparity map [4].

Depth resolution

Differentiating Z = fB/d gives the first-order depth error for a disparity error δd:

δZ ≈ (Z² / f B) · δd

Depth error grows with the square of distance, and falls with longer baselines and longer focal lengths. This is the central design trade-off of a stereo rig: a wider baseline improves far-range accuracy but increases occlusion and the disparity search range, and makes matching harder because the two views differ more. Okutomi and Kanade's multiple-baseline stereo and Gallup and colleagues' variable baseline and resolution stereo are two responses [39,40].

Listing 7.1 tabulates this relation and runs OpenCV's semi-global block matcher, based on Hirschmüller's semi-global matching, on a synthetic random-texture pair with a known disparity map [4,41]. Note that StereoSGBM returns fixed-point disparities with four fractional bits, so the output must be divided by 16 before using Z = fB/d.

08Multi-view geometry: triangulation

Triangulation recovers a 3D point from its images in two or more cameras with known projection matrices. In theory the back-projected rays intersect at X. With noisy measurements they are skew lines that never meet, so triangulation is an estimation problem whose answer depends on what error is minimised [1,42].

Linear (DLT) triangulation

Each view gives ũi ≃ Pi X̃. The cross product ũi × (Pi X̃) = 0 eliminates the unknown scale and gives two independent linear equations per view, u p3TX̃ − p1TX̃ = 0 and v p3TX̃ − p2TX̃ = 0, where pkT are the rows of Pi. Stacking two or more views gives A X̃ = 0, solved by the SVD. This is the method of cv2.triangulatePoints, and it extends directly to n views [1,4].

Midpoint and optimal triangulation

The midpoint method finds the closest points on the two rays and returns the midpoint of the segment joining them. It is geometrically intuitive but minimises a 3D distance that has no statistical meaning, and it is not invariant to projective transformations. Hartley and Sturm showed that when pixel noise is Gaussian, the maximum-likelihood estimate minimises the reprojection error — the sum of squared image distances between the observed points and the projections of X — and that for two views it can be found exactly by solving a degree-6 polynomial [42]. For many views, linear triangulation followed by a few Gauss–Newton steps on reprojection error is standard, and this per-point refinement is a special case of bundle adjustment [18]. Lee and Civera compared these methods and argued that cheap closed-form methods with suitable corrections are often good enough [43].

def triangulate_dlt(P1, P2, u1, u2):
    A = np.array([u1[0] * P1[2] - P1[0], u1[1] * P1[2] - P1[1],
                  u2[0] * P2[2] - P2[0], u2[1] * P2[2] - P2[1]])
    Xh = np.linalg.svd(A)[2][-1]
    return Xh[:3] / Xh[3]

    c1, d1 = np.zeros(3), Kinv @ np.r_[u1, 1]               # ray 1 in camera-1 frame
    c2, d2 = -R.T @ t, R.T @ Kinv @ np.r_[u2, 1]           # ray 2 moved into camera-1 frame
    # least squares for depths s1, s2 minimising |c1 + s1 d1 - (c2 + s2 d2)|
    s = np.linalg.lstsq(np.c_[d1, -d2], c2 - c1, rcond=None)[0]
    return 0.5 * ((c1 + s[0] * d1) + (c2 + s[1] * d2))

Exercise 8.3 — Points on the baseline

Why can a point lying on the line through both camera centres not be triangulated?

Show solution

Such a point projects to the epipole in both images, and both rays coincide with the baseline, so every point on that line explains the measurements equally well: A in the DLT becomes rank-deficient by one more and the depth is undetermined. Near the baseline the rays are nearly parallel and the error blows up, which is the same effect as the near-zero triangulation angle above [1,42].

Exercise 8.4 — Which error to minimise?

Give one reason to prefer minimising reprojection error over minimising the 3D distance between rays.

Show solution

Measurement noise lives in the images. If it is independent and Gaussian with equal variance in pixels, minimising the sum of squared image residuals is maximum-likelihood. The 3D ray distance weights errors by depth instead: a 1 px error for a far point displaces its ray much more than for a near point, so the midpoint method over-trusts distant observations. Reprojection error is also invariant to the choice of world frame and projective coordinates, while the midpoint method is only invariant to rigid transforms and scale [1,42].

09Pose estimation

A pose is a position and orientation, six degrees of freedom, an element of SE(3). Pose estimation asks for the rigid transform between a camera and something it observes. The same mathematics answers two questions that differ only in which frame is held fixed [1,2]:

  • Camera pose (camera localisation, resectioning): where is the camera in a known world? The answer is Twc. Examples: an AR headset localising against a pre-built map, a robot localising against fiducial markers on the walls, or a new image being registered to a structure-from-motion model.
  • Object pose (6-DoF object pose): where is a known object relative to the camera? The answer is Tco. Examples: a robot picking a part whose CAD model is known, or tracking a marker or a tool in surgery.

Since Toc = Tco−1, one is the inverse of the other; which you report depends on which frame is the reference. Either way the input is a set of 2D-to-3D correspondences: pixels ui matched to known 3D points Xi expressed in the reference frame. How the correspondences are obtained varies: corners of fiducial markers such as ArUco and AprilTag are detected and identified directly [45,46]; features matched to a 3D map carry the map's coordinates; and learned methods predict 2D keypoints of a known object, as in PVNet, or predict the pose directly [47]. The BOP benchmark standardises the evaluation of 6-DoF object pose estimation across datasets [48].

Uncalibrated resectioning: the DLT for P

If K is unknown, the whole 3×4 matrix P can be estimated linearly. Each correspondence ũi ≃ P X̃i gives two equations in the twelve entries of P, which has eleven degrees of freedom, so at least six points are needed, and they must not all be coplanar [1,22]. P is then split into K and R by an RQ decomposition of its left 3×3 block, and the camera centre is the right null vector of P. Listing 10.1 shows the method's sharpest edge: the SVD returns P only up to sign, and with the wrong sign the decomposition puts the camera facing backwards, with negative focal lengths and a 180° rotation error. Fixing the sign so that det(P3×3) > 0 (equivalently, so the points have positive depth) solves it [1].

10Perspective-n-Point (PnP)

When K is known, pose estimation becomes the Perspective-n-Point problem: find R and t such that ũi ≃ K(R Xi + t) for n known points. There are six unknowns and each point gives two equations, so three points are the minimum, the P3P problem. PnP is the workhorse of visual localisation and object pose estimation, and many solvers exist with different trade-offs [1,49].

P3P: the minimal case

Three points X₁, X₂, X₃ and their rays give a triangle of known side lengths (from the 3D model) and known angles between the rays (from the calibrated image). The law of cosines gives three quadratic equations in the three unknown distances from the camera to the points. The problem was first solved by Grunert in 1841; Haralick and colleagues reviewed the classical solutions [50]. It reduces to a quartic, so it has up to four real solutions; Gao and colleagues classified exactly when each number occurs [51]. Kneip, Scaramuzza and Siegwart gave a direct parameterisation that computes the pose without first solving for the distances, which is faster and numerically stable [52]. A fourth point resolves the ambiguity, as Listing 10.1 shows: of the two P3P candidates for its data, only the correct one reprojects the fourth point exactly. P3P is mainly used as the minimal solver inside RANSAC.

Planar targets and the pose ambiguity

When all points lie on a plane, as with a fiducial marker or a checkerboard, PnP can have two solutions that both fit the data well: the plane can appear tilted toward or away from the camera. The ambiguity is worst when the target is small in the image or viewed nearly head-on, where perspective cues are weak. Schweighofer and Pinz analysed this case and showed how to find both local minima; Collins and Bartoli's IPPE returns both solutions in closed form [55,57]. Listing 10.2 estimates a 10 cm marker's pose with IPPE_SQUARE: the correct solution reprojects with 0.11 px RMS and the alternative with 3.6 px, so here the ambiguity is easily resolved. For a marker a few tens of pixels across the two errors can be nearly equal, and the sign of the tilt then flips from frame to frame.

import numpy as np, cv2
from common import K, project

    M = np.eye(4); M[:3, :3] = R; M[:3, 3] = np.ravel(t); return M

Exercise 9.1 — Camera pose or object pose?

A robot's wrist camera sees a marker and solvePnP returns rvec, tvec. The robot knows Twc (the camera's pose in its base frame) from forward kinematics. Write the expression for the marker's position in the base frame, and for the camera's position relative to the marker.

Show solution

solvePnP returns the transform that takes object (marker) coordinates to camera coordinates, Tcm = [R(rvec) | tvec]. Then Twm = TwcTcm, and the marker's origin in the base frame is the translation column of Twm. The camera relative to the marker is Tmc = Tcm−1, whose translation is −RTtvec. Listing 10.2 computes both; a classic bug is to use tvec itself as "the camera position", which it is not unless R = I [4,13].

Exercise 9.2 — When does the DLT fail?

How many points does the DLT for P need, and why can it not use points that all lie on one plane?

Show solution

P has 11 degrees of freedom and each point gives two equations, so six points are needed (five and a half, strictly). If all points lie on a plane, say Z = 0, then the third column of P multiplies zero for every point and is unconstrained, so the system has a multi-dimensional null space. The planar case is instead handled by estimating a homography (as in Zhang's calibration) or, with known K, by planar PnP such as IPPE [1,10,55].

Exercise 9.3 — The sign of P

Without the sign check, the DLT in Listing 10.1 reports fx = −799 and a 179.9° rotation error. Explain why, and why checking det(P3×3) > 0 fixes it.

Show solution

The SVD null vector is defined only up to a non-zero scalar, including −1, and −P projects every point to the same pixel as P. But the decomposition of −P = −K[R | t] into an upper-triangular matrix with positive diagonal and a rotation is not the same as for P: the depths p₃TX̃ become negative, i.e. the points appear behind the camera. With R a rotation (det +1) and K having positive diagonal, det(KR) = det K > 0; flipping the sign of a 3×3 matrix flips its determinant, so requiring det(P3×3) > 0 picks the physically valid sign [1].

Exercise 10.1 — Why three points, and why four solutions?

Explain why P3P is minimal and why it can have up to four solutions. Why does Listing 10.1 find only two?

Show solution

The pose has six degrees of freedom and each point gives two constraints, so three points give six. Geometrically, three rays from the camera centre must each pass through one vertex of a known triangle; the law of cosines gives three quadratics in the three distances, which reduce to a polynomial of degree four, so up to four real solutions. The number of real roots depends on the configuration; for the points in Listing 10.1 two of the four roots are complex (or give points behind the camera) and are discarded [50,51].

Exercise 10.2 — How many RANSAC iterations?

RANSAC needs N = log(1 − p) / log(1 − ws) iterations to draw at least one all-inlier sample of size s with probability p, when a fraction w of the data are inliers [24]. For p = 0.99, compute N for (a) P3P (s = 3, using 3 points per sample) with 75% inliers, (b) the DLT (s = 6) with 75% inliers, and (c) P3P with 50% inliers.

Show solution

(a) w³ = 0.422, N = log 0.01 / log 0.578 ≈ 8.4, so 9. (b) w⁶ = 0.178, N = log 0.01 / log 0.822 ≈ 23.5, so 24. (c) w³ = 0.125, N = log 0.01 / log 0.875 ≈ 34.5, so 35. Minimal solvers matter because N grows exponentially with s; this is the same reason five-point solvers are preferred over eight-point ones for essential matrices in RANSAC [24,31]. (OpenCV's P3P samples a fourth point for disambiguation, which raises s to 4 in practice.)

Exercise 10.3 — Refine a pose with Gauss–Newton

Write a function that refines a pose (rvec, tvec) by Gauss–Newton on reprojection error, using the Jacobian that cv2.projectPoints returns. Start from a rough guess about 15° and 1 m off on the data of Listing 10.1.

Show solution

The residual vector is r = π(X; rvec, tvec) − u, of length 2n. projectPoints returns the 2n×(6 + …) Jacobian whose first six columns are with respect to rvec and tvec. Each Gauss–Newton step solves (JTJ)δ = −JTr. Levenberg–Marquardt adds a damping term λI to JTJ for robustness far from the solution [4,18].

Solution — Gauss–Newton pose refinement (run)NumPy, OpenCV
import numpy as np, cv2
from common import K, project

def refine_pose_gn(X, uv, K, rvec, tvec, iters=10):
    p = np.r_[rvec.ravel(), tvec.ravel()].astype(float)
    for i in range(iters):
        proj, J = cv2.projectPoints(X, p[:3], p[3:], K, None)
        r = (proj.reshape(-1, 2) - uv).ravel()             # residuals, 2N
        J = J[:, :6]                                       # d(proj)/d(rvec, tvec)
        dp = np.linalg.solve(J.T @ J, -J.T @ r)            # Gauss-Newton normal equations
        p += dp
        if np.linalg.norm(dp) < 1e-10:
            break
    rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(r**2) * 2)
    return p[:3], p[3:], rms, i + 1

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
X = np.c_[rng.uniform(-1, 1, 40), rng.uniform(-1, 1, 40), rng.uniform(-.5, .5, 40)]
R = cv2.Rodrigues(np.array([0.3, -0.4, 0.2]))[0]; t = np.array([0.1, -0.2, 5.0])
uv = project(K, R, t, X) + rng.normal(0, 1.0, (40, 2))
rv0 = np.array([0.25, -0.3, 0.1]); tv0 = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 4.0])     # rough initial guess
rv, tv, rms, n = refine_pose_gn(X, uv, K, rv0, tv0)
print("converged in %d iterations, RMS reprojection %.3f px" % (n, rms))
print("t =", tv.round(3))
Output (Python 3.13, NumPy 2.5.3, OpenCV 5.0.0)
converged in 6 iterations, RMS reprojection 1.213 px
t = [ 0.099 -0.2    5.001]

From this poor start it converges in six iterations to an RMS reprojection error of 1.2 px, consistent with the 1 px per-coordinate noise. Updating rvec additively is acceptable for small steps; a more principled update composes a small rotation, R ← exp([δω]×)R, which avoids the singularity of the rotation vector near 180° [14].

common.py — intrinsics, projection and a synthetic two-view sceneNumPy, OpenCV
import numpy as np, cv2

K = np.array([[800., 0, 320], [0, 800., 240], [0, 0, 1]])

def project(K, R, t, X):
    x = (K @ (R @ X.T + t.reshape(3, 1))).T
    return x[:, :2] / x[:, 2:]

def two_views(n=100, seed=0):
    """Random scene seen by two cameras: cam1 at the origin, cam2 rotated and moved right."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    X = np.c_[rng.uniform(-2, 2, n), rng.uniform(-1.5, 1.5, n), rng.uniform(4, 8, n)]
    R2 = cv2.Rodrigues(np.array([0.02, -0.15, 0.01]))[0]
    t2 = np.array([-1.0, 0.05, 0.1])                  # X_c2 = R2 X_c1 + t2
    x1 = project(K, np.eye(3), np.zeros(3), X)
    x2 = project(K, R2, t2, X)
    return X, R2, t2, x1, x2, rng
