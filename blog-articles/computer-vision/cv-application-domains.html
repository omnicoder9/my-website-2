Why domains matter

General-purpose computer vision has a shared toolkit: image formation, feature extraction, learned representations, and a handful of task formulations such as classification, detection, segmentation and geometric estimation [1–3]. What changes from one application domain to the next is everything around that toolkit. The sensor may be a stereo rig, a CT scanner, a 13-band satellite instrument or a fluorescence microscope. The cost of an error may be a mis-shelved can of soup or a missed tumour. Labels may be plentiful and cheap, or each one may cost a radiologist's hour.

This article walks through twelve specialized domains. For each, it names the major problems, explains the concepts that make the domain distinctive, shows working Python code for a representative technique, and ends with exercises and worked solutions. Every factual claim is tied to a numbered reference in the Vancouver style, listed at the end in order of first citation.

Shared foundations

Three ideas recur in nearly every domain below: the camera model that links pixels to the world, the task formulations that turn a business question into a learnable target, and the metrics that decide whether a system is good enough.

The pinhole camera

A calibrated camera maps a 3D point X in world coordinates to a pixel x through a rotation R, a translation t and an intrinsic matrix K that holds the focal lengths and principal point [2]:

λ x = K [ R | t ] X,   K = [[fx, 0, cx], [0, fy, cy], [0, 0, 1]] (1)

The intrinsics and lens distortion are usually estimated by imaging a planar checkerboard in several orientations, the method introduced by Zhang [4] and implemented in OpenCV [5]. Robotics, driving, AR/VR and industrial metrology all depend on equation (1); medical and remote sensing replace it with scanner geometry or a georeferenced sensor model, but the idea of an explicit forward model remains.

pinhole.py
import numpy as np

def project(points_w, K, R, t):
    """Project Nx3 world points to Nx2 pixel coordinates with a pinhole camera."""
    X_c = R @ points_w.T + t.reshape(3, 1)     # world frame -> camera frame
    in_front = X_c[2] > 0                       # points behind the camera are invalid
    x = K @ X_c                                 # camera frame -> homogeneous pixels
    uv = (x[:2] / x[2]).T
    return uv, in_front

K = np.array([[800.0, 0.0, 320.0],
              [0.0, 800.0, 240.0],
              [0.0,   0.0,   1.0]])
R, t = np.eye(3), np.zeros(3)
pts = np.array([[0.0, 0.0, 4.0],
                [0.5, -0.25, 2.0]])
uv, ok = project(pts, K, R, t)
print(uv)   # [[320. 240.]
            #  [520. 140.]]

Task formulations

Most domain problems reduce to a few standard outputs. Classification assigns a label to a whole image. Detection outputs boxes with class scores. Semantic segmentation labels every pixel, and instance segmentation also separates individual objects [6]. Keypoint estimation localizes landmarks such as body joints [7]. Geometric estimation recovers depth, pose or a transform between images [2]. Tracking links detections over time. Anomaly detection learns what normal looks like and flags departures from it. Deep convolutional networks, with residual networks as a widely used backbone, are the default learned component for all of these [3,8], typically pretrained on ImageNet [9] and fine-tuned for the domain. Promptable foundation models such as Segment Anything now provide zero-shot masks that many domains adapt rather than train from scratch [10].

Measuring success

Detection and segmentation are scored by overlap. Intersection over union (IoU, the Jaccard index) divides the overlap of a prediction and a ground-truth region by their union. A detection usually counts as a true positive when its IoU with an unmatched ground-truth object is at least 0.5, the PASCAL VOC criterion [11]; COCO averages over IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 [7]. Ranking detections by confidence gives a precision–recall curve, and average precision (AP) is the area under its monotone envelope [11]. Medical segmentation more often reports the Dice coefficient [12], which is a monotone function of IoU (see the medical imaging exercises).

metrics.py
import numpy as np

def iou(a, b):
    """Axis-aligned boxes given as (x1, y1, x2, y2)."""
    ix1, iy1 = max(a[0], b[0]), max(a[1], b[1])
    ix2, iy2 = min(a[2], b[2]), min(a[3], b[3])
    inter = max(0.0, ix2 - ix1) * max(0.0, iy2 - iy1)
    area = lambda r: (r[2] - r[0]) * (r[3] - r[1])
    return inter / (area(a) + area(b) - inter + 1e-12)

def average_precision(scores, is_tp, n_gt):
    """All-point interpolated AP, as used by PASCAL VOC from 2010 onward."""
    order = np.argsort(-np.asarray(scores, dtype=float))
    tp = np.asarray(is_tp, dtype=float)[order]
    tp_cum, fp_cum = np.cumsum(tp), np.cumsum(1.0 - tp)
    recall = tp_cum / max(n_gt, 1)
    precision = tp_cum / np.maximum(tp_cum + fp_cum, 1e-12)
    mrec = np.concatenate(([0.0], recall, [1.0]))
    mpre = np.concatenate(([0.0], precision, [0.0]))
    for i in range(len(mpre) - 2, -1, -1):        # monotone (non-increasing) envelope
        mpre[i] = max(mpre[i], mpre[i + 1])
    idx = np.where(mrec[1:] != mrec[:-1])[0]      # points where recall changes
    return float(np.sum((mrec[idx + 1] - mrec[idx]) * mpre[idx + 1]))

Exercises Foundations

  1. Compute the IoU of boxes (0, 0, 4, 4) and (2, 2, 6, 6). Would this pair count as a true positive under the VOC rule?
    Show solution

    The intersection is the square from (2, 2) to (4, 4), with area 4. Each box has area 16, so the union is 16 + 16 − 4 = 28 and IoU = 4/28 ≈ 0.143. That is well below 0.5, so it is a false positive under the VOC criterion [11].

  2. A detector produces four detections with scores 0.9, 0.8, 0.7 and 0.6. Only the first and third are true positives, and there are three ground-truth objects. Compute the all-point AP.
    Show solution

    Cumulative recall is 1/3, 1/3, 2/3, 2/3 and precision is 1, 0.5, 0.667, 0.5. The monotone envelope is 1 up to recall 1/3, then 0.667 up to recall 2/3, then 0 (recall 1 is never reached). AP = (1/3)(1) + (1/3)(0.667) + (1/3)(0) ≈ 0.556. Running average_precision([0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.6], [1, 0, 1, 0], 3) returns 0.5556.

  3. Using equation (1) with the K in pinhole.py, where does the point (1, 0, 8) project? What happens to its pixel coordinate as Z doubles?
    Show solution

    u = 800 · 1/8 + 320 = 420 and v = 240. Doubling Z to 16 gives u = 370: the offset from the principal point halves, because projection divides by depth [2]. This is why a single image cannot tell a small near object from a large far one.

A robot uses vision to answer three questions in a closed loop: where am I, what is around me, and how should I move to act on it. Unlike offline image analysis, every estimate feeds a controller, so latency, consistency over time and calibrated uncertainty matter as much as single-frame accuracy [13].

Localization and mapping

Localization estimates the robot's pose in a known map; mapping builds that map from known poses. Doing both at once is simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM). Modern SLAM systems split into a front end, which extracts features, associates them across frames and detects revisited places, and a back end, which solves a nonlinear least-squares problem over a factor graph of poses and landmarks [14]. ORB-SLAM is a widely used feature-based example: it tracks binary ORB features [15], maintains a covisibility graph of keyframes, and corrects accumulated drift with loop closure [16].

Between two calibrated views, relative motion follows from the essential matrix, which can be estimated from five point correspondences [17] inside a RANSAC loop that rejects outlier matches [18]. With a single camera the translation is recovered only up to scale [2]; stereo, an IMU or wheel odometry supply the metric scale. Learned alternatives regress camera pose directly from an image, as PoseNet did for relocalization [19], although geometric pipelines remain more accurate in most settings [14].

two_view_vo.py
import cv2
import numpy as np

def relative_pose(img1, img2, K):
    """Relative rotation and unit-length translation between two grayscale frames."""
    orb = cv2.ORB_create(nfeatures=2000)
    kp1, des1 = orb.detectAndCompute(img1, None)
    kp2, des2 = orb.detectAndCompute(img2, None)
    matcher = cv2.BFMatcher(cv2.NORM_HAMMING, crossCheck=True)
    matches = sorted(matcher.match(des1, des2), key=lambda m: m.distance)
    p1 = np.float32([kp1[m.queryIdx].pt for m in matches])
    p2 = np.float32([kp2[m.trainIdx].pt for m in matches])
    E, inliers = cv2.findEssentialMat(p1, p2, K, method=cv2.RANSAC,
                                      prob=0.999, threshold=1.0)
    _, R, t, mask = cv2.recoverPose(E, p1, p2, K, mask=inliers)
    # t has unit norm: metric scale is unobservable from one camera
    return R, t, int(np.count_nonzero(mask))

Occupancy grids

For navigation, a robot often keeps an occupancy grid: a lattice of cells, each holding the probability that it is occupied. Elfes introduced the representation [20]. Storing each cell as log-odds turns the Bayesian update into an addition: cells a range ray passes through receive a negative increment, and the cell where it ends receives a positive one [13].

occupancy_grid.py
import numpy as np

L_OCC, L_FREE, L_MIN, L_MAX = 0.85, -0.4, -4.0, 4.0   # log-odds increments and clamps

def bresenham(x0, y0, x1, y1):
    """Integer grid cells on the segment from (x0, y0) to (x1, y1)."""
    cells, dx, dy = [], abs(x1 - x0), -abs(y1 - y0)
    sx, sy = (1 if x0 < x1 else -1), (1 if y0 < y1 else -1)
    err = dx + dy
    while True:
        cells.append((x0, y0))
        if x0 == x1 and y0 == y1:
            return cells
        e2 = 2 * err
        if e2 >= dy:
            err += dy
            x0 += sx
        if e2 <= dx:
            err += dx
            y0 += sy

def integrate_ray(logodds, origin, hit):
    ray = bresenham(*origin, *hit)
    for x, y in ray[:-1]:                            # traversed cells: more likely free
        logodds[y, x] = np.clip(logodds[y, x] + L_FREE, L_MIN, L_MAX)
    x, y = ray[-1]                                   # end point: more likely occupied
    logodds[y, x] = np.clip(logodds[y, x] + L_OCC, L_MIN, L_MAX)

grid = np.zeros((100, 100))                          # log-odds 0 means p = 0.5 (unknown)
integrate_ray(grid, (50, 50), (80, 60))
prob = 1.0 - 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(grid))              # back to probabilities

Obstacle detection

Obstacles are found either geometrically, from depth (stereo, structured light or LiDAR) projected into an occupancy grid or height map, or semantically, by detecting object classes such as people or pallets. Geometric methods need no training data and respond to any solid object, while semantic methods know that a person may move and a wall will not. Practical systems fuse both [13]. The depth-from-stereo relation used for obstacles is derived in the autonomous driving section.

Grasping

Grasp planning chooses where to place a gripper so that an object can be lifted without slipping. Analytic methods evaluate force closure on a known 3D model; data-driven methods predict grasp success directly from sensor data. Dex-Net 2.0 trained a grasp-quality convolutional network on 6.7 million synthetic point clouds labelled with analytic robustness metrics, and transferred it to a physical robot picking novel objects [21]. The pattern of training on simulated data with physics-based labels recurs whenever real labels are expensive.

Visual servoing

Visual servoing closes the control loop directly on image measurements. In image-based visual servoing (IBVS), the controller drives a feature vector s (for example, the image coordinates of four points) toward its desired value s*. The interaction matrix L relates camera velocity to feature velocity, and the classical exponential-decay control law is [22]:

vc = −λ L+ (s − s*) (2)

where L+ is the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse. For a normalized image point (x, y) at depth Z, each point contributes two rows to L. Position-based servoing (PBVS) instead estimates the 3D pose of the target and controls in Cartesian space [22].

ibvs.py
import numpy as np

def interaction_matrix(x, y, Z):
    """Image Jacobian of a normalized image point (x, y) at depth Z (Chaumette & Hutchinson)."""
    return np.array([
        [-1.0 / Z, 0.0,      x / Z, x * y,       -(1.0 + x * x),  y],
        [0.0,      -1.0 / Z, y / Z, 1.0 + y * y, -x * y,         -x],
    ])

def ibvs_velocity(s, s_star, Z, lam=0.5):
    """s, s_star: (N, 2) normalized coordinates; Z: N depths.
    Returns the camera twist (vx, vy, vz, wx, wy, wz)."""
    L = np.vstack([interaction_matrix(x, y, z) for (x, y), z in zip(s, Z)])
    e = (np.asarray(s) - np.asarray(s_star)).reshape(-1)
    return -lam * np.linalg.pinv(L) @ e

Exercises Robotics

  1. Why does relative_pose return a translation of unit length, and name two ways to recover metric scale on a mobile robot.
    Show solution

    The essential matrix satisfies x₂ᵀ E x₁ = 0, which is unchanged if E is multiplied by any non-zero scalar, so translation is defined only up to scale [2,17]. Scale can be recovered from a calibrated stereo baseline, from an IMU's accelerometer, from wheel odometry, or from an object of known size in the scene [14].

  2. A cell starts at log-odds 0. Over time it is traversed by three rays (free) and is the end point of two rays (occupied). Using the constants in occupancy_grid.py, what is its occupancy probability?
    Show solution

    l = 3(−0.4) + 2(0.85) = 0.5, so p = 1 − 1/(1 + e^0.5) ≈ 0.622. The additive form follows from assuming independent measurements in log-odds [13].

  3. With three point features, L in equation (2) is 6 × 6. Why do practitioners usually servo on at least four points?
    Show solution

    Chaumette and Hutchinson show that with three points there are configurations where L is singular, and that four distinct camera poses produce the same three-point image, so the controller can converge to the wrong pose. Using more points gives a redundant, better-conditioned system [22].

  4. Dex-Net 2.0 learned from synthetic data. Name one risk of this choice and one mitigation.
    Show solution

    The risk is a domain gap: real depth sensors produce noise, holes and reflections that simulation may not model. Dex-Net mitigated this by training on point clouds rendered with a sensor noise model and validated on a physical robot with novel objects [21].

Autonomous driving

  • Lane detection
  • 3D detection
  • Tracking
  • Depth
  • Occupancy prediction

Driving perception must turn a ring of cameras, LiDAR and radar into a metric, time-consistent model of everything near the vehicle. SAE J3016 defines the levels of driving automation, from driver assistance to full automation, that set how much of this responsibility moves from the human to the system [23]. Public benchmarks such as KITTI [24] and nuScenes, which provides six cameras, five radars and one LiDAR with 360° coverage [25], define most of the standard tasks.

Lane detection

The classical pipeline detects edges with the Canny operator [26], restricts them to a region of interest in front of the vehicle, and fits line segments with the Hough transform [27]. This works on clear, straight highway markings and fails under occlusion, worn paint and curves. Learned methods treat lanes as thin, long structures: SCNN passes messages slice by slice across the feature map so that information propagates along a lane, and introduced the CULane dataset [28].

lanes_classical.py
import cv2
import numpy as np

def detect_lanes(bgr):
    """Return left and right lane segments (x1, y1, x2, y2) from one frame."""
    gray = cv2.cvtColor(bgr, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
    edges = cv2.Canny(cv2.GaussianBlur(gray, (5, 5), 0), 50, 150)
    h, w = edges.shape
    roi = np.zeros_like(edges)
    trapezoid = np.array([[(0, h), (w // 2 - 40, int(0.6 * h)),
                           (w // 2 + 40, int(0.6 * h)), (w, h)]], dtype=np.int32)
    cv2.fillPoly(roi, trapezoid, 255)
    edges = cv2.bitwise_and(edges, roi)
    lines = cv2.HoughLinesP(edges, rho=2, theta=np.pi / 180, threshold=50,
                            minLineLength=40, maxLineGap=100)
    left, right = [], []
    for x1, y1, x2, y2 in ([] if lines is None else lines.reshape(-1, 4)):
        if x2 == x1:
            continue
        slope = (y2 - y1) / (x2 - x1)          # image y grows downward
        if abs(slope) < 0.5:                   # reject near-horizontal clutter
            continue
        (left if slope < 0 else right).append((x1, y1, x2, y2))
    return left, right

3D object detection

Planning needs objects as oriented 3D boxes (position, size, heading, velocity), not image rectangles. LiDAR detectors discretize the point cloud: PointPillars groups points into vertical columns, encodes each pillar with a small PointNet, scatters the features into a bird's-eye-view (BEV) pseudo-image and runs a 2D detection head, which made it fast enough for real-time use [29]. Camera-only methods lift image features into BEV with predicted depth. The nuScenes detection score combines mean AP with errors in translation, scale, orientation, velocity and attributes [25].

Multi-object tracking

Tracking-by-detection runs a detector every frame and links detections into tracks. SORT predicts each track forward with a constant-velocity Kalman filter [30], builds a cost matrix between predictions and new detections, and solves the assignment with the Hungarian method [31,32]. DeepSORT adds an appearance embedding so that tracks survive longer occlusions [33]. Tracking is scored with the CLEAR MOT metrics, of which MOTA combines misses, false positives and identity switches [34].

sort_lite.py
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment

class Track:
    """Constant-velocity Kalman filter on a box centre (a simplified SORT state)."""
    def __init__(self, z, tid, dt=1.0):
        self.x = np.array([z[0], z[1], 0.0, 0.0])          # cx, cy, vx, vy
        self.P = np.diag([10.0, 10.0, 100.0, 100.0])       # velocity is unknown at birth
        self.F = np.array([[1, 0, dt, 0], [0, 1, 0, dt], [0, 0, 1, 0], [0, 0, 0, 1]], float)
        self.H = np.array([[1, 0, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0, 0]], float)
        self.Q = np.eye(4) * 0.01
        self.R = np.eye(2) * 1.0
        self.id, self.misses = tid, 0

    def predict(self):
        self.x = self.F @ self.x
        self.P = self.F @ self.P @ self.F.T + self.Q
        return self.x[:2]

    def update(self, z):
        y = z - self.H @ self.x
        S = self.H @ self.P @ self.H.T + self.R
        K = self.P @ self.H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)
        self.x = self.x + K @ y
        self.P = (np.eye(4) - K @ self.H) @ self.P
        self.misses = 0

def associate(preds, dets, gate=50.0):
    if len(preds) == 0 or len(dets) == 0:
        return [], list(range(len(preds))), list(range(len(dets)))
    cost = np.linalg.norm(preds[:, None, :] - dets[None, :, :], axis=2)
    rows, cols = linear_sum_assignment(cost)
    matches = [(r, c) for r, c in zip(rows, cols) if cost[r, c] < gate]
    used_r, used_c = {r for r, _ in matches}, {c for _, c in matches}
    return (matches,
            [r for r in range(len(preds)) if r not in used_r],
            [c for c in range(len(dets)) if c not in used_c])

def step(tracks, dets, next_id, max_misses=3):
    dets = np.asarray(dets, dtype=float).reshape(-1, 2)
    preds = np.array([t.predict() for t in tracks]).reshape(-1, 2)
    matches, lost, born = associate(preds, dets)
    for r, c in matches:
        tracks[r].update(dets[c])
    for r in lost:
        tracks[r].misses += 1
    for c in born:
        tracks.append(Track(dets[c], next_id))
        next_id += 1
    return [t for t in tracks if t.misses <= max_misses], next_id

Depth estimation

For a rectified stereo pair with focal length f (pixels) and baseline B (metres), a point with disparity d lies at depth [2]:

Z = f B / d,    δZ ≈ (Z² / f B) δd (3)

The second expression, obtained by differentiating the first, shows that depth error grows with the square of distance, which is why long-range perception leans on LiDAR or radar. Monocular networks learn depth from data; Eigen et al. introduced a scale-invariant loss for this ill-posed problem [35], and MiDaS showed that mixing many datasets improves zero-shot generalization of relative depth [36].

Occupancy prediction

Boxes cannot describe a fallen branch, a construction barrier or an overturned trailer. Occupancy prediction instead labels every voxel around the vehicle as free or occupied, often with a semantic class, so that planning can avoid objects of any shape. Occ3D built benchmarks for this task on top of Waymo and nuScenes and distinguishes voxels that were observed from those that were never visible to the sensors [37]. The first step in building such labels is voxelizing accumulated LiDAR.

voxelize.py
import numpy as np

def voxelize(points, origin=(-40.0, -40.0, -1.0), size=0.4, shape=(200, 200, 16)):
    """Boolean occupancy grid from an (N, 3+) LiDAR array; covers 80 m x 80 m x 6.4 m."""
    idx = np.floor((points[:, :3] - np.asarray(origin)) / size).astype(int)
    keep = np.all((idx >= 0) & (idx < np.asarray(shape)), axis=1)
    grid = np.zeros(shape, dtype=bool)
    grid[tuple(idx[keep].T)] = True
    return grid

Exercises Autonomous driving

  1. A stereo rig has f = 720 px and B = 0.54 m. A car shows a disparity of 20 px. How far away is it, and what is the depth uncertainty for a ±0.25 px disparity error?
    Show solution

    Z = 720 × 0.54 / 20 = 19.44 m. δZ ≈ Z² δd / (fB) = 19.44² × 0.25 / 388.8 ≈ 0.24 m. At 40 m the same disparity error gives about 1.03 m, roughly four times larger, as equation (3) predicts [2].

  2. In sort_lite.py the initial covariance on velocity is 100 while the covariance on position is 10. Why?
    Show solution

    A new track has one position measurement and no velocity information. A large velocity variance lets the first few updates set the velocity quickly; a small one would make the filter trust a zero-velocity prior and lag behind a moving object [30,32].

  3. Give two scene elements that occupancy prediction handles and a box detector does not.
    Show solution

    Objects outside the detector's class list, such as debris or an animal of an unseen species, and objects whose shape is poorly described by a box, such as a crane arm or an articulated bus. Occupancy represents both as occupied voxels without needing a class or box [37].

  4. The lane detector rejects segments with |slope| < 0.5. What failure does this cause on a sharp curve, and how do learned methods avoid it?
    Show solution

    On a sharp curve the far part of the lane becomes nearly horizontal in the image and is discarded, so the fitted lane is truncated or wrong. SCNN-style methods predict per-pixel lane masks and propagate context along the lane, so they are not tied to a straight-line model [28].

Medical imaging

  • Classification
  • Segmentation
  • Registration

Medical images are measurements, not photographs. CT voxels carry calibrated attenuation in Hounsfield units, MRI intensities depend on the pulse sequence, and histopathology slides can be gigapixels in size. Litjens et al. reviewed more than 300 deep learning papers in the field and found segmentation to be the most common task, with convolutional networks dominant across modalities [38].

Reading the data correctly

DICOM stores CT pixel values that must be rescaled to Hounsfield units with the RescaleSlope and RescaleIntercept attributes [39]. Radiologists then view a window, a range of HU mapped to the display, which a model's preprocessing should mimic so that soft-tissue contrast is not compressed into a few grey levels.

ct_window.py
import numpy as np
import pydicom

def load_hu(path):
    ds = pydicom.dcmread(path)
    return ds.pixel_array.astype(np.float32) * float(ds.RescaleSlope) + float(ds.RescaleIntercept)

def window(hu, center=40.0, width=400.0):
    """Map a HU window to [0, 1]; center 40 / width 400 is a common soft-tissue setting."""
    lo, hi = center - width / 2, center + width / 2
    return np.clip((hu - lo) / (hi - lo), 0.0, 1.0)

Classification

Image-level classification asks whether a finding is present. Esteva et al. fine-tuned an ImageNet-pretrained network on 129,450 clinical images of skin lesions and matched the performance of 21 board-certified dermatologists on two binary tasks [40]. Such results depend on the test distribution: performance must be confirmed on data from other sites, scanners and patient populations before clinical use [38].

Segmentation

U-Net is the reference architecture: a contracting path that captures context, an expanding path that restores resolution, and skip connections that copy high-resolution features across [41]. nnU-Net showed that a self-configuring U-Net pipeline, which sets preprocessing, patch size, network topology and post-processing from dataset properties, outperformed most specialized solutions across 23 public datasets [42]. Segmentation quality is reported with the Dice coefficient [12], and the V-Net paper introduced a differentiable Dice loss to counter the extreme imbalance between small structures and background [43].

tiny_unet.py
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

def block(cin, cout):
    return nn.Sequential(
        nn.Conv2d(cin, cout, 3, padding=1), nn.BatchNorm2d(cout), nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
        nn.Conv2d(cout, cout, 3, padding=1), nn.BatchNorm2d(cout), nn.ReLU(inplace=True))

class TinyUNet(nn.Module):
    """Input height and width must be divisible by 4."""
    def __init__(self, cin=1, cout=1, w=32):
        super().__init__()
        self.e1, self.e2, self.e3 = block(cin, w), block(w, 2 * w), block(2 * w, 4 * w)
        self.pool = nn.MaxPool2d(2)
        self.up2, self.d2 = nn.ConvTranspose2d(4 * w, 2 * w, 2, stride=2), block(4 * w, 2 * w)
        self.up1, self.d1 = nn.ConvTranspose2d(2 * w, w, 2, stride=2), block(2 * w, w)
        self.head = nn.Conv2d(w, cout, 1)

    def forward(self, x):
        s1 = self.e1(x)
        s2 = self.e2(self.pool(s1))
        b = self.e3(self.pool(s2))
        x = self.d2(torch.cat([self.up2(b), s2], dim=1))
        x = self.d1(torch.cat([self.up1(x), s1], dim=1))
        return self.head(x)                      # logits

def soft_dice_loss(logits, target, eps=1e-6):
    """logits, target: (B, 1, H, W); target is a {0, 1} mask."""
    p = torch.sigmoid(logits)
    dims = tuple(range(1, p.ndim))
    inter = (p * target).sum(dims)
    denom = p.sum(dims) + target.sum(dims)
    return 1 - ((2 * inter + eps) / (denom + eps)).mean()

def dice(pred_mask, true_mask, eps=1e-6):
    inter = (pred_mask & true_mask).sum()
    return (2 * inter + eps) / (pred_mask.sum() + true_mask.sum() + eps)

Registration

Registration finds the spatial transform that aligns one image to another: a follow-up scan to a baseline, an MRI to a CT, or a patient to an atlas. Transforms range from rigid (six parameters in 3D) through affine to deformable fields. When the two images come from different modalities, intensities are not directly comparable, so the similarity measure is mutual information, which rewards a consistent statistical relationship between intensities rather than equality [44]. VoxelMorph trains a network to output a deformation field in a single forward pass, replacing per-pair iterative optimization [45]. SimpleITK exposes the classical approach [46]:

    sitk.CenteredTransformInitializerFilter.GEOMETRY)

reg.SetMetricSamplingStrategy(reg.RANDOM)
reg.SetMetricSamplingPercentage(0.05)
reg.SetInterpolator(sitk.sitkLinear)
reg.SetOptimizerAsGradientDescent(learningRate=1.0, numberOfIterations=200)
reg.SetOptimizerScalesFromPhysicalShift()
reg.SetShrinkFactorsPerLevel([4, 2, 1])          # coarse-to-fine pyramid
reg.SetSmoothingSigmasPerLevel([2, 1, 0])

Anomaly detection

Many pathologies are rare or heterogeneous, so collecting labelled examples of each is impractical. Unsupervised anomaly detection trains only on healthy images and flags regions the model cannot explain. f-AnoGAN trained a generative adversarial network on normal retinal OCT scans and scored anomalies by a combination of image reconstruction error and discriminator feature residual [47]. The same idea, with a different model of normality, drives industrial defect detection.

Validation pitfallSplit data by patient, not by image. Slices from one patient are highly correlated, and placing them on both sides of a split inflates every metric. Report results on an external cohort where possible [38].

Exercises Medical imaging

  1. Show that Dice D and IoU J satisfy D = 2J/(1 + J). A model reaches IoU 0.6 on a tumour; what is its Dice?
    Show solution

    Let I = |A∩B| and S = |A| + |B|. Then J = I/(S − I) and D = 2I/S. From the first, I = J S/(1 + J); substituting gives D = 2J/(1 + J). For J = 0.6, D = 1.2/1.6 = 0.75 [12].

  2. A voxel has a value of 100 HU. What display value does window() assign with center 40 and width 400? With a bone window (center 400, width 1800)?
    Show solution

    Soft tissue: lo = −160, so (100 + 160)/400 = 0.65. Bone: lo = −500, so (100 + 500)/1800 ≈ 0.333. The same voxel occupies very different parts of the display range, which is why preprocessing should follow the clinical window for the task.

  3. A liver lesion occupies 0.2% of the voxels in a scan. Explain why plain per-voxel cross-entropy can produce a model that predicts no lesion, and how the Dice loss helps.
    Show solution

    Predicting background everywhere already achieves 99.8% voxel accuracy, so the cross-entropy gradient is dominated by background. The Dice loss is normalized by the size of the predicted and true foreground, so missing the whole lesion yields a loss near 1 regardless of how small it is [43].

  4. Why does registration of MRI to CT use mutual information instead of the sum of squared differences?
    Show solution

    Bone is bright in CT and dark in most MRI sequences, so aligned images can have very different intensities and SSD would be minimized at the wrong alignment. Mutual information is maximal when one image's intensities best predict the other's, whatever the mapping between them [44].

Industrial inspection

  • Metrology

Factory vision is the most controlled setting in this article: fixed cameras, engineered lighting, parts that arrive in known positions. That control is deliberate. Steger, Ulrich and Wiedemann treat illumination, lens and camera selection as the first and most important design step, because a defect that is not visible in the image cannot be found by any algorithm [48].

Defect detection as anomaly detection

Good parts are plentiful and defects are rare and varied, so supervised classifiers lack training data for the defects that matter. The MVTec AD benchmark formalized the alternative: train on defect-free images only, then detect and localize anomalies, across 15 object and texture categories with over 70 defect types [49]. PatchCore extracts mid-level features from an ImageNet-pretrained network, stores a subsampled memory bank of patch features from good images, and scores each test patch by its distance to the nearest stored patch [50]. It reported image-level AUROC of up to 99.6% on MVTec AD [50].

patchcore_lite.py
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torchvision.models import wide_resnet50_2, Wide_ResNet50_2_Weights
from torchvision.models.feature_extraction import create_feature_extractor

weights = Wide_ResNet50_2_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V1
backbone = create_feature_extractor(
    wide_resnet50_2(weights=weights).eval(),
    return_nodes={"layer2": "l2", "layer3": "l3"})
preprocess = weights.transforms()                 # resize, crop, normalize

@torch.no_grad()
def patch_features(x):
    """x: (B, 3, H, W) normalized batch -> (B, h*w, 1536) patch features."""
    f = backbone(x)
    l2 = F.avg_pool2d(f["l2"], 3, 1, 1)          # aggregate a 3x3 neighbourhood
    l3 = F.interpolate(F.avg_pool2d(f["l3"], 3, 1, 1), size=l2.shape[-2:],
                       mode="bilinear", align_corners=False)
    feats = torch.cat([l2, l3], dim=1)           # 512 + 1024 channels
    b, c, h, w = feats.shape
    return feats.permute(0, 2, 3, 1).reshape(b, h * w, c), (h, w)

def build_memory_bank(good_batches, keep=0.1):
    feats = torch.cat([patch_features(x)[0].reshape(-1, 1536) for x in good_batches])
    idx = torch.randperm(len(feats))[: int(keep * len(feats))]
    return feats[idx]          # PatchCore uses greedy coreset selection instead of random

@torch.no_grad()
def anomaly_map(x, bank):
    """x: (1, 3, H, W). Returns an (h, w) distance map and the image-level score."""
    f, (h, w) = patch_features(x)
    d = torch.cdist(f[0], bank).min(dim=1).values
    return d.reshape(h, w), d.max().item()

Metrology

Metrology measures dimensions, not just presence. It needs a calibrated camera [4], and often a telecentric lens whose magnification does not change with object distance [48]. Edges can be located to a fraction of a pixel by fitting a model to the gradient profile across the edge [48]. Every reported dimension should carry a stated measurement uncertainty, evaluated according to the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM) [51].

subpixel_edge.py
import numpy as np

def subpixel_edge(profile):
    """Edge position (in pixels) along a 1-D intensity profile, via a parabola
    fitted to the three gradient samples around the strongest gradient."""
    g = np.abs(np.diff(profile.astype(float)))
    i = int(np.argmax(g[1:-1])) + 1
    a, b, c = g[i - 1], g[i], g[i + 1]
    den = a - 2 * b + c
    offset = 0.0 if den == 0 else 0.5 * (a - c) / den
    return i + 0.5 + offset          # +0.5 because diff() sits between samples

profile = np.array([10, 10, 11, 30, 80, 98, 100, 100])
print(round(subpixel_edge(profile), 3))     # 3.492 pixels
mm_per_px = 0.0204                          # from calibration
print(round(subpixel_edge(profile) * mm_per_px, 4), "mm from the profile start")   # 0.0712

Quality assurance

An inspection system feeds statistical process control. Two quantities connect vision measurements to production decisions: the process capability index Cpk, which compares the spread of a measured dimension with its tolerance, and the two error rates of the inspection itself, false rejects (good parts scrapped) and escapes (bad parts shipped) [52].

Cpk = min(USL − μ, μ − LSL) / 3σ (4)
capability.py
import numpy as np

def cpk(samples, lsl, usl):
    mu, sigma = np.mean(samples), np.std(samples, ddof=1)
    return min(usl - mu, mu - lsl) / (3 * sigma)

Security vision runs continuously on many cameras and is judged by its false-alarm rate as much as by its detection rate, because operators stop trusting a system that cries wolf. Surveillance: motion, detection and tracking

With a static camera, the cheapest cue is change against a learned background. Stauffer and Grimson modelled each pixel as an adaptive mixture of Gaussians, so that swaying trees and flickering screens become part of the background [54]; Zivkovic's refinement adapts the number of components per pixel and is the basis of OpenCV's MOG2 subtractor [5,55]. Modern systems run a person or vehicle detector such as YOLO [56] and a multi-object tracker (see tracking), then apply rules or learned models to the tracks.

        cv2.rectangle(frame, (x, y), (x + w, y + h), (0, 160, 255), 2)

Access control: face verification

Face recognition systems map a face crop to an embedding vector so that images of the same person lie close together. FaceNet trained embeddings directly with a triplet loss [57]; ArcFace added an additive angular margin to the softmax, which became a standard training objective [58]. Verification (is this the claimed person?) compares one pair of embeddings against a threshold. Identification (who is this?) searches a gallery, and its false-match rate grows with gallery size. The threshold sets the trade-off between the false accept rate (FAR) and the false reject rate (FRR).

face_verify.py
import numpy as np

def cosine(a, b):
    return float(a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)))

def far_frr(genuine_scores, impostor_scores, tau):
    far = np.mean(np.asarray(impostor_scores) >= tau)
    frr = np.mean(np.asarray(genuine_scores) < tau)
    return far, frr

def threshold_for_far(impostor_scores, target_far=1e-4):
    """Smallest threshold whose empirical FAR does not exceed the target."""
    return float(np.quantile(impostor_scores, 1.0 - target_far, method="higher"))

NIST's evaluation of 189 algorithms found demographic differentials in false positive rates for most of them, with rates varying by factors of 10 to over 100 between groups in some algorithms [59]. A deployed threshold must be checked per subgroup, not only overall. Access control also has to resist presentation attacks (printed photos, screens, masks); ISO/IEC 30107-3 specifies how presentation attack detection is tested and reported [60].

Behavior and event detection

Events are defined on tracks: a person crosses a virtual line, enters a restricted zone, loiters beyond a time limit, or leaves an object behind. These rules are transparent and auditable. Learned detectors address events that are hard to write as rules; Sultani et al. trained an anomaly detector on 1,900 long surveillance videos using only video-level labels, through multiple-instance ranking [61].

import numpy as np

def side(p, a, b):
    """Sign of the cross product: which side of the line a->b the point p is on."""
    return np.sign((b[0] - a[0]) * (p[1] - a[1]) - (b[1] - a[1]) * (p[0] - a[0]))

def crossed(prev, curr, a, b):
    """True when a track centre moved from one side of the (infinite) line a-b to the other.
    A real counter also checks that the crossing point lies between a and b."""
    s0, s1 = side(prev, a, b), side(curr, a, b)
    return s0 != 0 and s1 != 0 and s0 != s1

Crop monitoring with spectral indices

Healthy leaves absorb strongly in red light, which chlorophyll uses for photosynthesis, and reflect strongly in the near infrared, because of leaf cell structure. The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) exploits this contrast [63,64]:

NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red) (5)

NDVI ranges from −1 to 1. Dense green vegetation gives high values, bare soil gives low positive values, and water is usually negative [64]. Tracked across a season, NDVI reveals emergence, stress and senescence. On Sentinel-2, red is band 4 and near infrared is band 8, both at 10 m resolution [65].

Earth observation images are georeferenced, multispectral and repeated. Each pixel has a location on the ground, a dozen or more spectral bands, and a revisit history. Zhu et al. reviewed how deep learning has been adapted to these properties and to the shortage of labelled data in the field [69].

Satellite imagery

Sentinel-2 carries a multispectral instrument with 13 bands from visible to shortwave infrared at 10, 20 and 60 m resolution and a 290 km swath [65]. Synthetic aperture radar complements optical sensors because it images through cloud and at night. Working with this data means handling coordinate reference systems, tiling scenes too large for GPU memory, masking clouds, and preserving bands that RGB-pretrained networks were never designed to accept.

Land-cover classification

EuroSAT is a benchmark of 27,000 labelled Sentinel-2 patches in 10 land-use and land-cover classes, on which the authors reported 98.57% overall accuracy with a fine-tuned CNN [70]. Pixel-level land-cover mapping is semantic segmentation. Map accuracy is assessed from a confusion matrix built against reference samples [71]. By the convention used here, rows are the mapped class and columns the reference class. Overall accuracy is the diagonal sum divided by the total. User's accuracy, one minus commission error, is computed per row. Producer's accuracy, one minus omission error, is computed per column. Cohen's kappa [72] has long been reported too, but Pontius and Millones argued it is misleading for map comparison and recommended reporting quantity and allocation disagreement instead [73], and Olofsson et al. recommend estimating class areas and their confidence intervals from a probability sample [74].

map_accuracy.py
import numpy as np

def accuracy_report(cm):
    """cm[i, j]: samples mapped as class i whose reference class is j."""
    cm = np.asarray(cm, dtype=float)
    n = cm.sum()
    overall = np.trace(cm) / n
    users = np.diag(cm) / cm.sum(axis=1)          # 1 - commission error
    producers = np.diag(cm) / cm.sum(axis=0)      # 1 - omission error
    pe = (cm.sum(axis=0) * cm.sum(axis=1)).sum() / n ** 2
    kappa = (overall - pe) / (1 - pe)
    return overall, users, producers, kappa

print(accuracy_report([[50, 5], [10, 35]]))

Change detection

Change detection compares co-registered images of the same place at two dates. Classical methods include image differencing, band ratios and change vector analysis, which thresholds the magnitude of the spectral difference vector [75]. Otsu's method [76] gives an automatic threshold when the difference histogram is bimodal. Learned methods feed both dates through a shared-weight (Siamese) encoder and decode a change mask; Daudt et al. proposed fully convolutional Siamese variants trained end to end [77]. Before any of this, the two images must be radiometrically comparable and precisely registered, or misalignment shows up as false change along every edge.

change_vector.py
import numpy as np
from skimage.filters import threshold_otsu

def change_map(before, after):
    """before, after: co-registered (bands, H, W) reflectance arrays."""
    magnitude = np.linalg.norm(after - before, axis=0)      # change vector magnitude
    t = threshold_otsu(magnitude[np.isfinite(magnitude)])
    return magnitude > t, magnitude

A store shelf is one of the hardest detection scenes in everyday life: hundreds of small, nearly identical objects packed edge to edge, a catalogue of tens of thousands of products, and packaging that changes every season. The task is less "what is this?" than "which of 40,000 products is this, and is it where the planogram says it should be?"

Product recognition

Two features make retail recognition unusual. First, the catalogue is large and changes constantly, so a fixed-class classifier must be retrained for every new product. Most systems instead detect generic "product" boxes and then identify each crop by nearest-neighbour search against a gallery of reference embeddings, so adding a product only means adding gallery images. Second, reference images are usually clean studio shots while queries are shelf photos. Merler, Galleguillos and Belongie named this the in vitro versus in situ gap in their GroZi-120 grocery dataset [78]. For packaging with rich texture, classical local features remain useful. SIFT keypoints [79] matched with a ratio test and verified with a RANSAC homography [18] locate a known package even under perspective and partial occlusion.

import cv2
import numpy as np

def find_product(shelf_gray, template_gray, min_inliers=15, ratio=0.75):
    """Locate a packshot in a shelf image; returns its 4 corners or None."""
    sift = cv2.SIFT_create()
    kt, dt = sift.detectAndCompute(template_gray, None)
    ks, ds = sift.detectAndCompute(shelf_gray, None)
    if dt is None or ds is None:
        return None
    pairs = cv2.BFMatcher(cv2.NORM_L2).knnMatch(dt, ds, k=2)
    good = [p[0] for p in pairs if len(p) == 2 and p[0].distance < ratio * p[1].distance]
    if len(good) < min_inliers:
        return None
    src = np.float32([kt[m.queryIdx].pt for m in good]).reshape(-1, 1, 2)
    dst = np.float32([ks[m.trainIdx].pt for m in good]).reshape(-1, 1, 2)
    H, mask = cv2.findHomography(src, dst, cv2.RANSAC, 5.0)
    if H is None or int(mask.sum()) < min_inliers:
        return None
    h, w = template_gray.shape
    corners = np.float32([[0, 0], [w, 0], [w, h], [0, h]]).reshape(-1, 1, 2)
    return cv2.perspectiveTransform(corners, H)

def identify(crop_embedding, gallery, skus, min_similarity=0.6):
    """Open-set lookup: return the nearest SKU, or 'unknown' if nothing is close."""
    g = gallery / np.linalg.norm(gallery, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    q = crop_embedding / np.linalg.norm(crop_embedding)
    sims = g @ q
    best = int(np.argmax(sims))
    return skus[best] if sims[best] >= min_similarity else "unknown"

Shelf analysis and inventory tracking

Densely packed shelves break standard detectors, whose non-maximum suppression merges adjacent identical boxes. SKU-110K, a dataset of shelf images with an average of about 147 objects per image, was built to study this, and its authors added a learned soft-IoU score and an expectation–maximization merging step to resolve overlapping detections [80]. Automated checkout is the related problem of recognizing every item on a counter; the RPC dataset provides 83,739 images of 200 product categories for it [81]. Once products are detected and identified, shelf analytics compares the observed layout with the planogram, the planned arrangement of products: gaps indicate out-of-stock items, and wrong neighbours indicate misplaced ones.

planogram.py
from difflib import SequenceMatcher

def shelf_rows(detections, row_tol=40):
    """Group (x_center, y_center, sku) detections into rows by vertical position."""
    rows = []
    for det in sorted(detections, key=lambda d: d[1]):
        if rows and abs(rows[-1][-1][1] - det[1]) < row_tol:
            rows[-1].append(det)
        else:
            rows.append([det])
    return [[sku for _, _, sku in sorted(r)] for r in rows]

def compliance(detections, planogram_rows):
    observed = shelf_rows(detections)
    return [SequenceMatcher(None, obs, plan).ratio()
            for obs, plan in zip(observed, planogram_rows)]

dets = [(30, 100, "A"), (90, 102, "A"), (150, 99, "B"), (270, 101, "D"), (330, 100, "D")]
print(compliance(dets, [["A", "A", "B", "B", "C", "D"]]))   # [0.727...]

Document AI

Document AI turns scans and photos of pages into structured data: text, its reading order, the tables and forms it forms, and the meaning of each field. Unlike natural images, documents are mostly binary, highly structured and full of text whose correct reading depends on two-dimensional layout.

Preprocessing and OCR

Binarization separates ink from paper. Otsu's global threshold [76] works on clean scans, while Sauvola's local threshold adapts to uneven illumination and stains [83]. Small rotations are corrected by deskewing. Tesseract, originally developed at HP and later open-sourced, finds text lines and recognizes words [84]. A widely used neural design, CRNN, stacks convolutional features, a bidirectional recurrent network and a connectionist temporal classification (CTC) output [85]. CTC lets a network trained on unsegmented line images emit a label or a "blank" at every time step, and the final text is read by collapsing repeats and removing blanks [86]. OCR quality is measured with the character error rate, the edit distance [87] between output and reference divided by the reference length.

ocr_pipeline.py
import cv2
import numpy as np
import pytesseract

def deskew(bw, max_angle=5.0, step=0.1):
    """Projection-profile deskew: choose the rotation that makes row sums peakiest."""
    ink = (bw < 128).astype(np.uint8)
    h, w = ink.shape
    best, best_score = 0.0, -1.0
    for a in np.arange(-max_angle, max_angle + step, step):
        M = cv2.getRotationMatrix2D((w / 2, h / 2), a, 1.0)
        rot = cv2.warpAffine(ink, M, (w, h), flags=cv2.INTER_NEAREST)
        score = float(np.var(rot.sum(axis=1)))       # aligned text lines = sharp profile
        if score > best_score:
            best, best_score = a, score
    M = cv2.getRotationMatrix2D((w / 2, h / 2), best, 1.0)
    return cv2.warpAffine(bw, M, (w, h), flags=cv2.INTER_LINEAR, borderValue=255), best

data = pytesseract.image_to_data(straight, config="--oem 1 --psm 3",

import numpy as np

def ctc_greedy_decode(logits, alphabet, blank=0):
    """logits: (T, C) scores per frame; class c > 0 maps to alphabet[c - 1]."""
    best = np.asarray(logits).argmax(axis=1)
    out, prev = [], blank
    for k in best:
        if k != blank and k != prev:        # collapse repeats, then drop blanks
            out.append(alphabet[k - 1])
        prev = k
    return "".join(out)

def levenshtein(a, b):
    prev = list(range(len(b) + 1))
    for i, ca in enumerate(a, 1):
        cur = [i]
        for j, cb in enumerate(b, 1):
            cur.append(min(prev[j] + 1, cur[j - 1] + 1, prev[j - 1] + (ca != cb)))
        prev = cur
    return prev[-1]

def cer(hypothesis, reference):
    return levenshtein(hypothesis, reference) / max(len(reference), 1)

Handwriting

Handwritten text recognition (HTR) uses the same CTC-based line recognizers, trained on data with far more variability between writers. The IAM database, a standard English benchmark, contains forms of handwritten text from 657 writers [88]. Results are reported as character and word error rates on writer-independent splits, so that the test writers never appear in training.

Layout understanding and table extraction

Layout analysis segments a page into regions such as title, paragraph, list, table and figure. PubLayNet created over 360,000 annotated page images automatically by matching PubMed Central XML to PDF renderings, which made detector-based layout analysis practical [89]. LayoutLM pre-trained a Transformer on text tokens together with their 2D positions on the page, improving form understanding and receipt parsing over text-only models [90]. Table extraction has three sub-tasks: detecting the table, recognizing its structure (rows, columns, spanning cells) and reading cell contents. PubTables-1M provides nearly one million tables for training detection and structure models [91]. For ruled tables, morphology alone recovers the grid:

ruled_table_cells.py
import cv2

def ruled_table_cells(bw, min_line=40, min_area=200):
    """Cells of a ruled table from a binary page (ink = 0, paper = 255)."""
    ink = 255 - bw
    horiz = cv2.morphologyEx(ink, cv2.MORPH_OPEN,
                             cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_RECT, (min_line, 1)))
    vert = cv2.morphologyEx(ink, cv2.MORPH_OPEN,
                            cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_RECT, (1, min_line)))
    grid = cv2.bitwise_or(horiz, vert)
    n, labels, stats, _ = cv2.connectedComponentsWithStats(255 - grid, connectivity=4)
    H, W = grid.shape
    cells = []
    for i in range(1, n):
        x, y, w, h, area = stats[i]
        touches_border = x == 0 or y == 0 or x + w == W or y + h == H
        if area >= min_area and not touches_border:
            cells.append((x, y, w, h))
    return sorted(cells, key=lambda c: (c[1], c[0]))      # row-major order

AR/VR

  • Tracking
  • Scene understanding
  • Depth
  • Spatial anchors

Augmented and virtual reality need the pose of the user's head or device continuously, with low latency, so that rendered content stays fixed in the world. Errors that would be harmless in a robot's map are immediately visible as jitter or drift of virtual objects.

Tracking

Early AR used fiducial markers. ARToolKit's square markers gave camera pose from a single detected square [92], and ArUco markers add error-correcting binary codes that keep detection reliable under occlusion [93]. Markerless tracking came from SLAM. PTAM split tracking and mapping into parallel threads so that a handheld camera could be tracked at frame rate while bundle adjustment refined the map in the background [94]. Current headsets and phones fuse cameras with inertial sensors: the IMU covers fast motion between frames, and vision corrects the IMU's drift. The multi-state constraint Kalman filter is a classic filter-based visual-inertial design [95]. Pose from known 3D–2D correspondences, the Perspective-n-Point problem, has efficient solutions such as EPnP [96].

aruco_pose.py
import cv2
import numpy as np

# OpenCV 4.7+ object-based ArUco API
detector = cv2.aruco.ArucoDetector(
    cv2.aruco.getPredefinedDictionary(cv2.aruco.DICT_4X4_50),
    cv2.aruco.DetectorParameters())

def marker_poses(gray, K, dist, side=0.05):
    """Pose (rvec, tvec) of each visible marker of side length `side` metres."""
    corners, ids, _ = detector.detectMarkers(gray)
    s = side / 2
    # corner order expected by SOLVEPNP_IPPE_SQUARE: TL, TR, BR, BL
    obj = np.array([[-s, s, 0], [s, s, 0], [s, -s, 0], [-s, -s, 0]], dtype=np.float32)
    poses = {}
    if ids is None:
        return poses
    for c, marker_id in zip(corners, ids.ravel()):
        ok, rvec, tvec = cv2.solvePnP(obj, c.reshape(4, 2), K, dist,
                                      flags=cv2.SOLVEPNP_IPPE_SQUARE)
        if ok:
            poses[int(marker_id)] = (rvec, tvec)
    return poses

Depth and scene understanding

Occlusion, physics and placement all need geometry. KinectFusion fused a stream of depth images into a truncated signed distance volume in real time while tracking the sensor against it [97]. Devices without active depth sensors can estimate relative depth from a single image with networks such as MiDaS [36]. Scene understanding adds semantics to the reconstruction: floors, walls, tables, chairs. ScanNet supplied 1,513 RGB-D scans of indoor scenes, annotated with 3D camera poses, surface reconstructions and instance-level semantic segmentations, for training such models [98]. Neural radiance fields represent a scene as a continuous function learned from posed images and render photorealistic novel views, which has made them attractive for capturing real places for VR [99].

backproject.py
import numpy as np

def backproject(depth, K):
    """Depth image (metres) -> (N, 3) point cloud in the camera frame (inverse of eq. 1)."""
    h, w = depth.shape
    u, v = np.meshgrid(np.arange(w), np.arange(h))
    z = depth
    x = (u - K[0, 2]) * z / K[0, 0]
    y = (v - K[1, 2]) * z / K[1, 1]
    return np.stack([x, y, z], axis=-1)[z > 0]

Spatial anchors

A spatial anchor is a pose in the tracked map to which content is attached. Storing content relative to an anchor, rather than in global world coordinates, keeps it stable when the system corrects its map: the anchor moves with the local map, and content moves with the anchor. Persistence and sharing across devices require relocalizing the stored map in a later session and recovering the transform between the old and new map frames. All of this is composition of rigid transforms [2].

anchors.py
import numpy as np

def make_T(R, t):
    T = np.eye(4)
    T[:3, :3], T[:3, 3] = R, t
    return T

def anchor_in_camera(T_world_camera, T_world_anchor):
    """Pose of the anchor in the current camera frame, used to render attached content."""
    return np.linalg.inv(T_world_camera) @ T_world_anchor

def after_relocalization(T_world_anchor_old, T_new_old):
    """Re-express a stored anchor after the session finds the old map in a new frame."""
    return T_new_old @ T_world_anchor_old

  1. The camera is at world position (0, 0, 0) with identity rotation, and an anchor sits at world position (0, 0, 2) with identity rotation. A user steps 0.5 m along +x. Where is the anchor in the camera frame?
    Show solution

    TWC is now a translation by (0.5, 0, 0), so its inverse translates by (−0.5, 0, 0). The anchor in the camera frame is at (−0.5, 0, 2). It appears to move left as the user moves right, as it should for world-fixed content.

  2. Why do headsets fuse an IMU with cameras instead of using vision alone?
    Show solution

    The IMU samples at a much higher rate than the camera and is unaffected by motion blur or textureless views, so it carries the pose estimate through fast head motion. Vision in turn corrects the drift that accumulates from integrating IMU measurements. Filter-based designs such as the MSCKF formalize this fusion [95].

  3. Why is SOLVEPNP_IPPE_SQUARE preferable to a generic PnP solver for a single square marker?
    Show solution

    Four coplanar corners are the minimal, nearly degenerate case for generic solvers. IPPE is designed for planar targets and handles the square configuration directly [100]; a generic solver like EPnP is better suited to many non-coplanar points [96].

Human-computer interaction

  • Gesture recognition
  • Gaze tracking

In HCI, vision is an input device. It has to be fast enough to feel direct, robust across users of every hand size, skin tone and body shape, and forgiving of the fact that people do not hold still. The output is usually a small set of signals, such as a pointer position, a gesture label or a gaze point, rather than a full scene description.

Pose estimation

Human pose estimation localizes body keypoints. OpenPose detects all body parts in an image and assembles them into people with part affinity fields, 2D vector fields that encode limb direction, which keeps runtime almost constant as the number of people grows [101]. COCO defines 17 body keypoints and scores predictions with object keypoint similarity (OKS), which plays the role of IoU for keypoints [7]:

OKS = Σi exp(−di² / 2s²ki²) δ(vi > 0) / Σi δ(vi > 0) (6)

where di is the distance between predicted and true keypoint i, s is the object scale, ki is a per-keypoint constant reflecting how precisely humans annotate that joint, and vi is its visibility flag.

Gesture recognition

Gestures are either static (a hand shape such as a pinch or open palm) or dynamic (a motion such as a swipe) [102]. Hand-landmark models make both easier: MediaPipe Hands detects a palm, then regresses 21 three-dimensional hand landmarks in real time on mobile hardware [103]. Static gestures become geometric rules or small classifiers on the landmarks. Dynamic gestures compare a trajectory with stored templates, for example with dynamic time warping, which aligns sequences performed at different speeds [104].

pinch_and_dtw.py
import cv2
import numpy as np
import mediapipe as mp

hands = mp.solutions.hands.Hands(max_num_hands=1, min_detection_confidence=0.6)

def pinch_ratio(landmarks, w, h):
    p = np.array([[lm.x * w, lm.y * h] for lm in landmarks])   # normalized -> pixels
    return np.linalg.norm(p[4] - p[8]) / np.linalg.norm(p[0] - p[9])

cap = cv2.VideoCapture(0)
while cap.isOpened():
    ok, frame = cap.read()
    if not ok:
        break
    h, w = frame.shape[:2]
    result = hands.process(cv2.cvtColor(frame, cv2.COLOR_BGR2RGB))
    if result.multi_hand_landmarks:
        r = pinch_ratio(result.multi_hand_landmarks[0].landmark, w, h)
        if r < 0.25:
            print("pinch", round(r, 2))

def dtw(a, b):
    """Dynamic time warping distance between two (T, D) trajectories."""
    n, m = len(a), len(b)
    D = np.full((n + 1, m + 1), np.inf)
    D[0, 0] = 0.0
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        for j in range(1, m + 1):
            cost = np.linalg.norm(a[i - 1] - b[j - 1])
            D[i, j] = cost + min(D[i - 1, j], D[i, j - 1], D[i - 1, j - 1])
    return D[n, m]

Gaze tracking

Gaze estimation methods divide into model-based approaches, which fit a geometric eye model to features such as the pupil centre and corneal reflections from infrared illumination, and appearance-based approaches, which regress gaze directly from eye images [105]. Appearance-based methods work with ordinary webcams. Zhang et al. collected the MPIIGaze dataset of 213,659 images from 15 participants during everyday laptop use and showed a CNN outperforming earlier appearance-based methods in this unconstrained setting [106]. Accuracy is reported as the angle between predicted and true 3D gaze directions.

  1. A webcam gaze tracker has 5° mean angular error. The user sits 60 cm from the screen. Roughly how large is the error on the screen surface?
    Show solution

  2. Why normalize pinch distance by ‖p₀ − p₉‖ rather than use a pixel threshold?
    Show solution

    A pixel distance shrinks as the hand moves away from the camera and differs between small and large hands. The ratio of two distances on the same hand is approximately invariant to both, so a single threshold works for many users.

  3. In equation (6), a predicted keypoint has d = s·k. What is its contribution to OKS?

  4. Two users perform the same swipe, one twice as fast. Why would Euclidean distance between their trajectories mislead a classifier, and how does DTW help?

    Point-by-point comparison pairs the fast user's end with the slow user's middle, giving a large distance for the same gesture. DTW finds the monotone alignment that minimizes total cost, so it compares corresponding phases of the motion [104].

In science, the image is evidence and the analysis must be reproducible. Measurements need units, uncertainties and documented processing, and a method that changes the data, such as denoising, must be validated so that it does not invent structure. Microscopy

The most common microscopy task is segmenting cells or nuclei to count them and measure their shape and intensity. The classical pipeline smooths the image, thresholds it with Otsu's method [76], then splits touching objects with a watershed on the distance transform [110]. Learned methods are now the default for difficult data. StarDist predicts a star-convex polygon for each nucleus [111]. Cellpose predicts a spatial gradient flow field that pixels follow to their cell's centre, and trained on a diverse dataset it generalizes across many cell types without retraining [112]. Content-aware restoration (CARE) trains networks on pairs of low- and high-quality acquisitions to denoise and restore fluorescence images, which lets biologists image with less light and less photodamage [113].

count_nuclei.py
import numpy as np
from scipy import ndimage as ndi
from skimage import feature, filters, measure, morphology, segmentation

def count_nuclei(img, min_size=30, min_distance=5):
    smooth = filters.gaussian(img, sigma=2)
    mask = smooth > filters.threshold_otsu(smooth)
    mask = morphology.remove_small_objects(mask, min_size)
    dist = ndi.distance_transform_edt(mask)
    peaks = feature.peak_local_max(dist, min_distance=min_distance,
                                   labels=measure.label(mask))
    markers = np.zeros(dist.shape, dtype=int)
    markers[tuple(peaks.T)] = np.arange(1, len(peaks) + 1)
    labels = segmentation.watershed(-dist, markers, mask=mask)
    table = measure.regionprops_table(labels, intensity_image=img,
                                      properties=("label", "area", "intensity_mean"))
    return labels, table

Astronomy

Astronomical images are mostly empty sky with faint sources on a noisy, varying background. SExtractor established the standard source-extraction pipeline: estimate and subtract the background, detect connected pixels above a threshold expressed in multiples of the background noise, deblend overlapping sources, and measure positions, fluxes and shapes [114]. Morphological classification at survey scale started with citizen science: Galaxy Zoo collected visual classifications of about 900,000 SDSS galaxies [115], and those labels later trained CNNs that reproduce the volunteers' answers, as in Dieleman et al.'s rotation-invariant network [116]. At the other extreme, the Event Horizon Telescope reconstructed an image of the shadow of the M87 black hole from sparse interferometric measurements, an inverse problem where imaging algorithms were validated on synthetic data by independent teams [117].

detect_sources.py
import numpy as np
from astropy.stats import sigma_clipped_stats
from scipy import ndimage as ndi

def detect_sources(img, nsigma=3.0, min_pixels=5):
    """Threshold above a sigma-clipped background; return centroids and fluxes."""
    _, median, std = sigma_clipped_stats(img, sigma=3.0)
    mask = img > median + nsigma * std
    labels, n = ndi.label(mask)
    sizes = ndi.sum(mask, labels, index=np.arange(1, n + 1))
    keep = np.flatnonzero(sizes >= min_pixels) + 1
    residual = img - median
    centroids = ndi.center_of_mass(residual, labels, keep)
    fluxes = ndi.sum(residual, labels, keep)
    return centroids, fluxes

Material analysis

Materials science uses optical and electron micrographs to relate microstructure to properties. DeCost and Holm represented microstructure images with bag-of-visual-words features and classified microstructure types with a support vector machine, an early demonstration of general computer vision features for this task [118]. A traditional and still standardized measurement is grain size: ASTM E112 defines the grain size number G and gives planimetric and intercept procedures for estimating it from micrographs [119]. Segmenting grain boundaries automatically turns these manual procedures into image analysis.

G = 3.321928 log10 NA − 2.954 (7)

Here NA is the number of grains per mm² at 1× magnification [119].

Exercises Scientific imaging

  1. Why does count_nuclei run the watershed on -dist rather than on dist?
    Show solution

    The watershed floods from minima. Nucleus centres are maxima of the distance transform, so negating it turns them into basins; boundaries form where floods from neighbouring centres meet, at the narrow neck between touching nuclei [110].

  2. The nucleus count rises sharply when min_distance is reduced from 5 to 2. What is happening, and what are two remedies?
    Show solution

    Over-segmentation: small bumps on an irregular nucleus become separate peaks, so one nucleus is split into several. Remedies are a larger min_distance or more smoothing, or a learned method such as StarDist or Cellpose that models object shape directly [111,112].

  3. A segmented micrograph at 1× equivalent magnification shows 256 grains per mm². Compute the ASTM grain size number.
    Show solution

    log₁₀ 256 ≈ 2.408, so G ≈ 3.3219 × 2.408 − 2.954 ≈ 5.05, which reports as grain size number 5 [119].

  4. Why must a denoising network such as CARE be validated before its outputs are measured?
    Show solution

    A network trained to produce clean-looking images can hallucinate plausible structure that is not in the sample, particularly on data unlike its training set. The CARE authors therefore assessed restoration accuracy against ground truth and estimated per-pixel uncertainty [113].

Three practical rules follow. First, measure on data drawn from the deployment setting, split so that near-duplicates cannot leak between training and test; the drop Mohanty et al. observed on field images is typical, not exceptional [67]. Second, choose metrics that reflect the cost of errors in the domain, such as Dice for small medical structures [43], false-reject rate for a production line, or per-group false-match rates for biometrics [59]. Third, keep the geometry and physics that a domain already understands, such as camera models, spectral indices and control laws, and let learning fill in what those models cannot express.

Capstone exercise

  1. A vineyard asks for a system that counts grape clusters from a tractor-mounted camera to forecast yield. Using at least four domains in this article, sketch the pipeline and name the main risk at each stage.
    Show solution

    One reasonable design follows. Calibration and localization (robotics): calibrate the camera [4] and fuse GNSS with visual odometry so that each image is tied to a vine position; the risk is drift in long rows. Detection (retail, agriculture): a dense detector for small, clustered objects [68,80]; the risk is occlusion by leaves. Tracking (driving): associate detections across frames so that each cluster is counted once [32]; the risk is identity switches that double-count. Measurement and validation (industrial, science): convert counts to mass with hand-harvested calibration vines and report uncertainty [51]; the risk is calibration that does not transfer between varieties or seasons, so test on held-out blocks [67].
