01

The word names a portfolio, not an architecture

On 20 October 2025 a race condition in the automation that manages DNS records for a single database service in a single AWS region emptied a regional endpoint record, and the consequences ran for roughly fifteen hours across a large fraction of the services that people use without thinking about where they run.1,2,3 Nine days later an inadvertent configuration change propagated across the Azure Front Door fleet, edge nodes failed to load it, traffic concentrated on the survivors, and Microsoft rolled back to a last-known-good state over a window that ran from the afternoon of 29 October into the early hours of the 30th.4,5 Both providers published careful accounts. Both accounts described a control plane failing in a way the data plane could not route around.6

In the weeks afterwards a great many organisations discovered something uncomfortable about their own estates. They had two clouds. They had had two clouds for years. Procurement knew it, finance knew it, the architecture diagram on the wiki showed both logos. And when one provider had a bad day, they went down anyway.

This is not a paradox and it is not bad luck. It is the direct, predictable consequence of a category error that the word multicloud encourages: the assumption that having two providers is an architecture. It is not. It is an accounting fact. Two logos on an invoice is compatible with at least eight distinct arrangements of software, and those eight arrangements have almost nothing in common. Two of them make the estate measurably less available than a single provider would. Three leave availability exactly where it was. Only two improve it, and one of those two is so expensive that most organisations that claim to have it do not.

The thesis of this article is that the useful unit of analysis is not the provider count but the seam: the boundary between the two providers, and specifically what crosses it, how often, and what happens to the crossing when one side is unavailable. Every one of the eight patterns named in the literature — workload separation, disaster recovery, geographic distribution, best-of-breed, regulatory separation, migration, acquisition, active-active — is fully characterised by the answer to those three questions. Get the seam right and the arithmetic follows. Get it wrong and you have paid for a second cloud and bought a second way to fail.

A note on what follows. The eight patterns below are treated as engineering objects with measurable properties, not as strategy slides. Where a claim is quantitative it comes from a small model included in full at the end, which anyone can run and disagree with; where it comes from a standard, a regulator or a provider's own documentation, it is cited. The model's premises are stated and are certainly wrong in detail for your estate. The point of a model like this is not to predict your numbers. It is to show which quantities dominate, and in this subject the quantities that dominate are consistently not the ones the strategy slides emphasise.

02

What the standards actually say

The foundational vocabulary is older than the argument. NIST's definition of cloud computing gives five essential characteristics, three service models and four deployment models, and it is worth noticing what is absent: there is no deployment model for two providers. The document's deployment models are private, community, public and hybrid, and hybrid means a composition of two or more distinct cloud infrastructures that remain unique entities but are bound together by technology enabling data and application portability.7 That is a statement about composition, not about vendor count.

ISO closed the gap. ISO/IEC 22123-1 defines multi-cloud as a deployment model in which a customer uses public cloud services from two or more providers.8,9 ISO/IEC 22123-2 goes further and distinguishes the ways multiple providers can be arranged, separating customer-mediated multi-cloud — where the customer is the only thing joining the providers together — from provider-connected multi-cloud, and separating both from federated cloud, inter-cloud and hybrid cloud.10 ISO/IEC 5140 is devoted entirely to concepts for multi-cloud and the use of multiple cloud services, and ISO/IEC TR 10822-1 catalogues the management problems that follow.11,12 The reference architecture in 22123-3 supplies the role vocabulary — cloud service customer, cloud service provider, cloud service partner — that regulatory texts increasingly borrow.13 Public-sector lexicons increasingly adapt these definitions directly, which matters because it means procurement, audit and engineering can at least be made to use the same words.14

Two distinctions from that body of work do real work in practice, and both are routinely collapsed in conversation.

Hybrid is not multi. Hybrid names a composition across deployment models — typically some private or on-premises estate bound to a public one. Multi-cloud names a plurality of providers. An organisation can be hybrid and single-cloud, multi-cloud and entirely public, or both at once, and the three cases have different failure modes and different regulators. Flexera's 2026 survey puts hybrid at 73% of respondents and finds a further tranche using multiple public clouds without any private estate at all, which is a useful reminder that these are orthogonal axes and not points on one scale.15,16

Customer-mediated is not provider-connected. If the only thing joining two providers is your Terraform, your CI system and your on-call rota, then you are the integration layer, and every failure of the integration is yours. If instead the providers have built the join — a private interconnect product, a database service physically installed inside the other provider's data centre — then some of the integration risk has moved to a party with an SLA.10 The commercial landscape in 2026 contains a great deal more of the second kind than it did five years ago, and this changes the economics of several patterns below.

One more piece of vocabulary, because the rest of the article leans on it. The literature on recovery gives us two numbers that discipline every conversation about failure: the recovery time objective, the wall-clock duration you are prepared to be unavailable, and the recovery point objective, the quantity of recent work you are prepared to lose.17 These are not architectural properties. They are commitments, and like all commitments they are worth exactly as much as the last time somebody tested them. That observation turns out to be the single most important quantitative result in this article.

03

The seam: five channels and what crosses them

Put two provider estates side by side and draw a line between them. Five distinct things can cross that line, and each has a different failure signature.

Control. Does software on one side call the other side's management API? A pipeline in provider A that provisions resources in provider B has a control-plane crossing. Control crossings are the least obvious and among the most dangerous, because control planes are regional, are frequently the first thing to degrade in an incident, and are exactly the component that the October 2025 incidents took down while data planes kept serving.1,5 An estate whose deployment pipeline lives in one provider cannot deploy a fix to the other provider during that provider's incident, which is the worst possible moment to discover it.

Identity. Does a principal minted on one side authorise an action on the other? The modern answer is workload identity federation: a workload presents a short-lived OIDC token issued by its own cloud, the other cloud validates the signature against a configured trust relationship, and issues its own short-lived credential in exchange.18,19,20 This is a large improvement on the alternative of long-lived access keys copied into the other cloud's secret store, and it also creates a hard runtime dependency on the issuer's token endpoint that most architecture diagrams do not draw.

Data. Do bytes have to exist on both sides for the system to be correct? This is the channel with the steepest cost curve, because it is the only one where the physics bites: replication consumes egress, adds latency, and forces a choice about consistency that no amount of engineering effort removes.21,22 It is also the channel that regulators ask about first, because it is the one that determines where personal data actually is.

Network. Is there a private path between the estates, or does traffic traverse the public internet? In 2026 there are three answers: the internet with encryption, a dedicated cross-cloud interconnect bought from one of the providers, or a colocation cross-connect through a carrier. Google's Cross-Cloud Interconnect offers dedicated circuits at 10 or 100 Gbps to other providers with a 99.99% availability commitment; a partner variant offers finer bandwidth increments and same-day provisioning against the multi-week lead time of a physical circuit.23,24,25 AWS reached general availability with its own multicloud interconnect product in April 2026, with Google Cloud as the launch partner and others named for later in the year.26 A decade ago this channel was a do-it-yourself problem; it is now a purchasable one, which is a genuine change.

Money. Is one provider's bill a function of the other provider's traffic? This sounds like a finance concern and is actually an architecture concern, because it is the channel that quietly vetoes designs. Egress pricing is the mechanism: at published US and European list rates, data leaving AWS to the internet costs $0.09 per GB for the first 10 TB in a month after a 100 GB monthly allowance, tiering down to $0.085, $0.07 and $0.05 per GB at higher volumes, while inter-region transfer runs roughly $0.01–$0.02 per GB and cross-zone traffic is charged at $0.01 per GB in each direction.27,28,29 A chatty cross-cloud design is not merely expensive; it is a design whose cost scales with its success.

Reading the gauge

Every pattern below carries a five-cell gauge showing which channels cross its seam: C control, I identity, D data, N network, $ money. A lit cell is a dependency you own. The gauges are collected in the seam ledger at the end, and the pattern of lit cells is more diagnostic than any of the names.

The reason this framing earns its place is that it makes an otherwise slippery question concrete. “Are we multicloud?” has no useful answer. “Which of these five channels cross a provider boundary on the request path, and what is the fallback when the far side does not answer?” has an answer, and the answer is usually written down somewhere in the codebase rather than in the strategy document.

04

How portfolios arrive

It matters, for reasons that become concrete in the patterns below, that most multicloud estates were not designed. They accumulated. The sequence is so consistent that it is worth setting out as a natural history.

The first era runs from the launch of commercial infrastructure services through the early 2010s, and its characteristic mechanism is the individual decision. A team picks a provider because someone on it already knows one; a second team picks differently for the same reason; a data science group needs a warehouse that only one vendor sells well. Nothing is centrally chosen and nothing is centrally forbidden. By the time anybody notices, the estate is plural.

The second era is the abstraction era, roughly from the arrival of container tooling and declarative infrastructure through the end of the decade. Its characteristic claim is that a sufficiently good abstraction makes the provider irrelevant. It delivered something real — Kubernetes is now in production use at 82% of container-using organisations, and cloud-native technique adoption is close to universal30 — but what it delivered was uniform packaging and scheduling, not uniform infrastructure. The identity model, the network model, the storage semantics and the managed services around the cluster remained resolutely provider-specific, and those are where the work is. Developer-level survey data tells the same story from the other end: hybrid and multi-provider working are now ordinary rather than exceptional, with no corresponding convergence in what the providers actually offer.31

The third era is economic, and it opens with an argument. In 2020 Corey Quinn published a widely read piece defining multicloud strictly as running the same workload across providers in a provider-agnostic way, and arguing that so defined it is a practice to be avoided by default: the agnostic estate is limited to the intersection of what all providers offer, the operational cost of two of everything is systematically underestimated, and splitting spend weakens rather than strengthens negotiating leverage.32,33 The rejoinder, made by many people, is that the definition is too narrow to describe reality — most organisations are plural inadvertently, and the interesting question is not whether to become multicloud but what to do about the estate you already have.34,35 Both positions are correct about different objects, which is precisely the category error this article is about.

The fourth era, which began in earnest in 2025, is the era of obligation, and it is genuinely new. Its mechanism is that third parties — regulators, auditors, insurers — started asking to see the reasoning. That story is told in §19.

Two commercial changes deserve their own mention because they altered the cost of leaving. In early 2024, following the switching provisions of the EU Data Act, several providers introduced free egress programmes for customers moving off their platform; the UK competition authority's own survey of the market records these programmes and their conditions.36 And Article 29 of the Data Act sets a hard schedule: from 11 January 2024 switching charges may only recover costs directly linked to the switch, and from 12 January 2027 they must be zero.37,38,39 The exit toll that made leaving expensive enough that nobody priced the option is being legislated away. Whether that changes behaviour is a separate question — the toll was never the largest component of switching cost — but it removes the excuse.

Meanwhile the market itself has not deconcentrated. Synergy's tracker put AWS at 28% of worldwide cloud infrastructure spending in the first quarter of 2026, Azure at 21% and Google Cloud at 14%, with total spending of $129 billion in the quarter, growing 35% year on year; the three providers' combined share has been broadly stable for two years while the market underneath them has roughly quadrupled.40 A stable share of a rapidly growing market is the precise condition under which concentration risk becomes a systemic question rather than a procurement one.

05

Reading the pattern off the seam

With the seam in hand, the eight patterns sort themselves onto two axes. The first asks whether the two sides run the same workload or different ones. The second asks whether the seam sits on the request path — whether a user-visible operation depends, right now, on both sides answering.

That geometry already yields the article's central arithmetic result, which is worth stating before any of the individual patterns, because it is what most of them are quietly violating.

If a user-visible operation requires both providers to be working, the two availabilities multiply. Two components in series are less available than either alone. If instead the operation requires only one of the two providers, and can be served by whichever is up, the unavailabilities multiply and the composition is more available than either. There is no third case. Every architectural decision in this subject is a decision about which of these two arrangements you are building, and a startling number of estates have built the first while budgeting for the second.

On the model's premises — a per-provider availability of 99.95% for a regional service, two impairments a year, and a modest correlation between providers — a single-provider estate expects 4.38 hours of downtime a year. An estate where the business needs two independent workloads, one on each provider, expects 8.76. That is not a rounding difference; it is the same estate being twice as broken because it was described as diversified.

The rest of this article walks the eight patterns in the order they are usually listed, which is roughly the order of increasing seam intimacy. Each carries a plate recording what crosses, how tightly the sides are coupled, what a whole-provider loss does to it, what it costs relative to a single-provider baseline, and the diagnostic question that distinguishes it from the pattern next to it. The plates are collected at the end.

06

Workload separation

Provider A runs one application. Provider B runs another. Neither calls the other. This is by a wide margin the most common thing that gets called multicloud, and it is the pattern with the weakest claim to the name.

The engineering appeal is real and should not be dismissed. Each application gets the provider its team knows, its licensing suits, or its data already sits in. There is no seam on the request path, so there is no cross-cloud latency, no cross-cloud egress on the hot path, no distributed transaction, no consistency argument. Each estate can be reasoned about in isolation, which is exactly what a team wants when it is trying to ship.

The trouble is the arithmetic in §05 and the fact that the pattern is almost always sold using an argument that belongs to a different pattern. Splitting applications across providers does not diversify risk in any sense a business would recognise, because businesses do not consume applications independently. If order management is down, it does not console anyone that analytics is fine. The relevant availability is the availability of the set, and the set is a series composition: 4.38 hours a year becomes 8.76.

The second cost is subtler and larger: everything above the infrastructure has to exist twice. Two identity models with different policy languages. Two network models with different primitives. Two observability pipelines, or one with two collectors. Two patching regimes, two compliance evidence trails, two incident vocabularies, two sets of quota limits nobody knows about until they are hit. This is the operational tax, and it is charged whether or not the seam is ever used. The management challenges are catalogued formally in ISO/IEC TR 10822-1, which is a useful document to put in front of anyone who believes the tax is small.12

None of this makes the pattern wrong. It makes it a pattern whose justification has to be stated honestly. Workload separation is justified by fit — this workload is better served here — or by organisation, and never by resilience. The tell is simple: ask what a whole-provider impairment does. If the honest answer is “half the company stops, and the other half cannot help”, the pattern is separation and the resilience slide should be deleted.

07

Disaster recovery

The workload runs on provider A. Provider B holds enough of it that, on a bad day, it can be made to serve. This is the first pattern that is actually about surviving a provider, and it is where the discipline of the subject lives.

The recovery ladder is well established and does not change when you cross a provider boundary: backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby, multi-site active/active, in increasing order of standing cost and decreasing order of recovery time and recovery point.17,41 Backup and restore keeps data in the second estate and nothing else, and recovers in hours to a day. Pilot light keeps the core — usually the data and the minimum control surface — warm and everything else cold, recovering in minutes to hours. Warm standby keeps a scaled-down but fully functional environment running continuously, recovering in minutes.42 The published guidance is explicit that if your definition of a disaster extends beyond one facility to an entire region, or if regulation demands it, the lower rungs stop being sufficient.17

What changes across the seam is everything the ladder quietly assumes. Within one provider, a recovery region speaks the same API, honours the same identity policies, offers the same managed services, and can be described by the same infrastructure code with a region parameter changed. Across providers, none of that holds. Recovery requires a second implementation of the workload, maintained in lockstep with the first, exercised on infrastructure that behaves differently under load. The recovery plan is not a plan; it is a second product.

The result that should change how this pattern is budgeted

A recovery path is not a property of an architecture. It is a claim about the last time somebody proved it, and the claim decays. Model it: assume a small number of changes each week touch the recovery path, assume each unexercised change has a small independent probability of breaking it, and assume a ceiling below one for things no exercise can rehearse. Then the probability that a cross-cloud failover works is a decaying exponential in the time since the last end-to-end exercise.

On the model's premises, weekly exercise sustains a 91% success rate, monthly 75%, quarterly 44%, and annually 4%. Now add the fact that a failed failover is not neutral: it consumes attention during an incident, it can leave the two estates disagreeing about which is authoritative, and it adds remediation time. With a modest half-hour penalty for a botched attempt, there is a break-even success rate of 24.7%. Below that, the recovery path is a net contributor to downtime. On these premises a half-yearly exercise cadence sits below the line: expected downtime rises from 4.38 hours to 4.57. An annually-exercised cross-cloud failover adds 0.83 hours a year relative to not having one at all.

That result is not an argument against cross-cloud DR. It is an argument that the exercise cadence, not the topology, is the deliverable. An organisation that cannot commit to monthly end-to-end failover exercises — with production traffic, with the primary genuinely unavailable, with the people who would actually be on call — is buying a diagram. The most useful challenge in this area remains the one Quinn posed: before running the same workload across two providers, run it active-active across two regions of one provider, where the APIs are identical, and see how simple it turns out to be.32

What a DR seam actually carries

Data crosses continuously, which means egress and a replication lag that sets the recovery point. Control crosses at the worst possible moment — failover is a control-plane operation, executed while a control plane is impaired. If the pipeline that promotes the standby runs inside the primary provider, the pattern has a dependency loop that only appears on the day it matters.
08

Geographic distribution

Provider A serves one region's users, provider B serves another's. The same application, different populations, no shared request.

This pattern is usually driven by one of three forces, and it is worth separating them because they have different implications. The first is latency: users are served from infrastructure near them, and the provider chosen per region is whichever has the better footprint there. The second is lawfulness: a jurisdiction requires data about its residents to stay inside it, and one provider has a compliant offering there while the other does not. The third is market access, which is the most absolute of the three.

The clearest illustration of the third is mainland China, where the constraint is not a preference but a structural fact. AWS operates there through two regions run by separate licensed Chinese operators, Sinnet in Beijing and NWCD in Ningxia; customers contract with those operators rather than with Amazon, require a distinct set of account credentials that do not work in global regions, and face a different service catalogue.43,44 Publishing public web content additionally requires an ICP filing.45 An organisation with a Chinese user base is running a genuinely separate estate whether or not it thinks of itself as multicloud, and the seam between that estate and the rest of the world is closer to an air gap than to a network link.

The critical property of this pattern, and the one most often misread, is that it does not improve availability for any individual user. Each user is served by exactly one estate. If that estate's provider has an impairment, that user is down for its full duration, and the fact that users on another continent are fine is a fact about the blast radius, not about the availability. Geographic distribution reduces the fraction of the population affected by any single event. It does not reduce anyone's exposure.

That distinction matters when writing an SLA. “99.95% availability” measured as a global average across three estates is a much weaker promise than the same number measured per-user, and the two diverge exactly during the events people care about. If you are going to distribute geographically, measure per-region and report the worst region, because that is the number a customer experiences.

What crosses this seam is usually thin and usually control: a shared deployment pipeline, a shared configuration repository, a shared observability backend, sometimes a shared identity directory for staff. Each of those is a correlated dependency, and each is a candidate for the failure mode in §21. The estates are independent in the data plane and joined at the top, which is the opposite of the shape most diagrams show.

09

Best-of-breed

Use each provider where it has a real advantage. This is the pattern with the most persuasive story and the widest variance in outcomes, because the name covers two architectures that behave nothing alike.

The story is straightforward: providers are not interchangeable, their strengths differ, and an estate that refuses to use the best available tool for a job is paying for uniformity it did not need. That is true. What the story omits is the question that determines everything: is the second provider's capability consumed at build time or on the request path?

Build-time best-of-breed uses the second provider to produce an artefact: trained model weights, a compiled feature set, an aggregated report, a rendered index. The artefact is versioned, cached in the serving estate, and served from there. When the second provider is unavailable, the artefact goes stale and the system keeps working with a slightly older answer. The composition is parallel-ish: the seam carries data on a schedule, and its failure mode is degradation rather than outage.

Runtime best-of-breed calls the second provider synchronously inside a user-visible operation. When the second provider is unavailable, the operation fails. The composition is strictly serial — 8.76 hours a year rather than 4.38 — and the seam now carries data, identity and network on every request, with egress charged per request and latency added per request.

The commercial landscape has recently made runtime crossings dramatically more practical, which cuts both ways. Oracle installs its database hardware physically inside the other providers' data centres and reports interconnect latencies in the region of two milliseconds, with unified identity and joint support across the boundary;46 the private-connectivity products from Google and AWS make dedicated cross-cloud circuits a purchasable item.24,26,47 Two milliseconds is small enough that the latency objection evaporates. The availability objection does not: a synchronous dependency on a second provider is a synchronous dependency on a second provider regardless of how short the wire is.

The engineering move that converts the second architecture into the first is not exotic. Put a cache, a queue or a last-known-good store on the near side of the seam, define what the system does when the far side is silent, and make that behaviour a tested requirement rather than an accident. If the answer to “what do we serve when provider B does not answer?” is “an error”, then the capability is not best-of-breed; it is a single point of failure that happens to be excellent.

10

Regulatory separation

A defined class of data and the processing attached to it lives in a specified provider, partition or jurisdiction, and does not leave. Every other pattern in this article is an availability or economics decision. This one is a confinement decision, and confinement is a different kind of constraint: it is satisfied or violated, never optimised.

The engineering question is unusually crisp, and unusually easy to get wrong by omission. It is not “where is the database?” It is: where are the backups, the derived aggregates, the search indices, the message queues that briefly hold payloads, the logs that contain field values, the metrics with high-cardinality labels, the encryption keys and the systems that can use them, the directory that holds the staff identities entitled to read any of it, the support tooling those staff use, and the billing records that describe the shape of the workload? A confined estate is confined only if every one of those answers is inside the boundary.

The market has responded with sovereign offerings that go considerably further than a region in the right country. AWS made its European Sovereign Cloud generally available on 15 January 2026, with a first region in Brandenburg, Germany, physically and logically separate from other AWS regions, operated by EU-resident staff, under a dedicated European governance structure with locally incorporated entities and an advisory board, and with customer data, metadata, identity and billing information held inside the EU; expansion is planned through sovereign local zones in Belgium, the Netherlands and Portugal.48,49,50 Independent commentary has been appropriately sceptical about what this does and does not resolve — the operating entity remains a subsidiary of a US parent, and a single launch region complicates geo-redundancy expectations that some national supervisors set in kilometres.51

Which brings out the essential feature of this pattern: regulatory separation and availability are in tension, and the tension is structural. Confinement says data must not leave a boundary. Cross-provider survival says data must exist on both sides of a seam. These are compatible only if both providers are inside the boundary. If a jurisdiction has one credible provider, then a requirement to both confine data and survive the loss of a provider is unsatisfiable as written — not difficult, unsatisfiable — and the correct engineering response is to escalate the requirement rather than to architect around it. The model in §22 finds exactly this: of six requirement vectors tested, one is satisfied by none of the eight patterns, and it is the one that demands confinement and provider-loss survival together.

Note also that this pattern often produces multicloud without any of the usual motivations. An organisation may run its main estate on one provider and its confined estate on a sovereign partition or a national provider purely because the first cannot lawfully hold the data. That is two providers on the invoice and zero of the resilience benefits, and it should be described as what it is.

11

Cloud migration

Applications are moving from one provider to another. The portfolio is plural because the move is not finished. This is the only pattern with a defined end state, which is exactly why it is the one most likely to become permanent.

The mechanics are the familiar strangler sequence with a provider boundary in the middle. A read-only mirror is established and verified without serving traffic. Writes are duplicated to both sides while one remains authoritative. Reads shift to the new estate while writes still land in the old one. Truth moves, and the old estate becomes the fallback. Finally the old estate is decommissioned and the seam closes.

Every phase in the middle has the seam maximally lit. Data crosses continuously and in both directions during dual-write. Control crosses because the pipeline has to deploy to both. Identity crosses because a service in one estate calls a service in the other. Network crosses on the request path. Money crosses in both directions. For the duration of the migration the estate has the cost profile of active-active and the availability profile of series composition, which is the worst combination available.

Three failure modes are specific to this pattern and worth naming.

The permanent migration. The last fifteen per cent of a migration is the part with no business sponsor: the batch job nobody owns, the integration with a vendor whose contract names an IP range, the reporting database that finance built. A stalled migration does not look like a failed project on any dashboard. It looks like a multicloud strategy, and it gets retrospectively described as one. The tell is that nobody can name the date the double-cost window closes.

Dual-write divergence. Writing to two stores from application code is not replication; it is two independent writes with no atomicity, and they will disagree. The disagreement is usually invisible for months because it affects the tail of the data. Every serious migration needs a continuous reconciliation job whose output is a divergence count, and that count needs to be a release gate, not a dashboard nobody reads.

Cutover without a rehearsed reverse. The moment truth moves is a one-way door unless somebody has built and tested the path back. Building the reverse path costs perhaps a tenth of building the forward one and is the difference between a bad night and a bad quarter.

The commercial environment now helps a little. Free-egress-on-exit programmes and the Data Act's schedule mean the raw transfer cost of leaving is falling toward zero.36,37,52,53 That removes one line item. It does not touch the two that dominate: rewriting what has no equivalent, and running two estates while you do it.

12

Acquisition and enterprise consolidation

Different business units run on different providers because they were different companies, or because they were governed separately for long enough to make different choices. Nobody chose this portfolio; it was inherited.

Flexera's 2026 findings are blunt about the mechanism: multi-cloud adoption continues to rise, but often unintentionally, with mergers, siloed application teams and inherited architectures cited ahead of deliberate strategy.16,54 That framing deserves to be taken seriously rather than treated as an embarrassment, because the resulting estate has properties that a designed one does not.

The distinguishing property is that the seam is administrative rather than technical. There is usually no data crossing, no control crossing, no shared request path. What crosses is identity — staff eventually need single sign-on across both estates — and money, in the form of a consolidated bill and a committed-spend negotiation that now has to cover two vendors. Everything else is duplication: two landing zones, two guardrail frameworks, two sets of baseline controls, two evidence trails for the same audit, two answers to every question a security questionnaire asks.

The recurring strategic error is to treat consolidation as obviously correct. It usually is not, and the reason is that consolidation is a migration — pattern P06 — with all of its costs and none of its urgency. A rational consolidation programme starts by asking which of the duplicated layers actually needs to be single. In practice the answers cluster: identity and access almost always should be unified, because the alternative is two joiner-mover-leaver processes and a predictable audit finding; security telemetry usually should, because correlation across estates is the whole point of having it; cost management should, because the negotiating position depends on it. Compute platforms, deployment tooling and application architectures frequently should not, because unifying them means rewriting working software to no external benefit.

There is also a real, unfashionable argument in favour of leaving the estates apart. Two independently operated estates with different tooling and different failure modes are genuinely less likely to fail together than one estate operated by one team with one pipeline. Nobody plans this way, but when an inherited portfolio is examined honestly, its accidental diversity is occasionally the most valuable property it has — and the consolidation programme is proposing to spend money to remove it.

13

Active-active

The same application serves production traffic from both providers simultaneously. This is the only pattern that survives the loss of a whole provider without a recovery procedure, and it is the most expensive object in this article by a wide margin.

Within a single provider the multi-site active/active strategy sits at the top of the recovery ladder, and the guidance is careful to note that it is the costliest rung and demands correspondingly strong reasons.17,55 Across providers it inherits all of that and adds the fact that the two sites do not share an API, an identity model, a network model or a storage semantics.

The consistency ceiling

Everything hard about this pattern reduces to one question: what happens to a write when the two sides cannot talk? The formal result is old and unmoved. In an asynchronous system with partitions, you cannot have both linearizable consistency and availability;21 and the extended formulation makes the trade explicit in the partition-free case too, where the choice becomes one between latency and consistency.22 Consensus itself is impossible in the fully asynchronous case with even one faulty process, which is why every practical system substitutes timeouts for the ability to distinguish a slow peer from a dead one.56 Majority-quorum protocols — the Paxos family and its more teachable descendants — are the standard resolution, and globally distributed systems that offer external consistency do so by paying for it in bounded-uncertainty clocks and commit latency.57,58,59

Which leads to the sharpest structural result in this article, and one that quietly invalidates a great many two-cloud designs.

A majority quorum of n replicas needs ⌊n/2⌋ + 1 of them to commit. If the replicas are spread across two failure domains, one domain necessarily holds at least half of them, and losing that domain leaves the survivors short of a majority. This is true for three replicas, for five, for six, for any count and any split. Two clouds is not a quorum topology. An active-active design that survives the loss of an arbitrary provider needs a third independent failure domain — a third provider, an on-premises site, or at minimum a witness that participates in elections without holding data. The model enumerates the placements: every two-domain arrangement scores either 0% or 50% on arbitrary-domain tolerance, while a three-domain arrangement scores 100%, at the cost of a commit that must cross the seam.

The alternative is to abandon single-copy semantics deliberately: partition the keyspace so that each provider owns a disjoint set of writes and the other serves reads, or adopt data types whose merges are commutative and accept that some invariants cannot be expressed. Both are legitimate and both are architectural decisions with product consequences, which is the point — cross-cloud active-active is not an infrastructure choice that the product can be insulated from.

The bill

The model prices a concrete case: two terabytes a day of change, replicated in both directions, plus the compute needed to size each plane for the whole load rather than for its share. Replication over the public internet at list egress rates runs about $112,942 a year; over private interconnect ports, about $65,491. The compute above a warm standby adds about $420,000. Against a monthly-exercised cross-cloud standby, the pattern avoids 1.70 hours of downtime a year.

Divide one by the other and the break-even is between $285,262 and $313,143 per hour of downtime. For a payment network, an exchange, or a national-scale identity service, that is a low bar. For most organisations it is not close: Uptime Institute's survey finds that 57% of respondents' most recent major outage cost more than $100,000 in total, and about one in five exceeded $1 million in total — not per hour.60,61 The honest conclusion is that cross-cloud active-active is correct for a small number of systems and is being aspired to by a much larger number.

Identity across the seam

Of the five channels, identity is the one most likely to be implemented badly and least likely to appear on the architecture diagram.

The naive implementation is an access key from provider B, created once, stored in provider A's secret manager, and used forever. It works immediately, which is its entire appeal. It also creates a long-lived credential with standing privilege in a second blast radius, rotated by a process nobody owns, discovered during an incident by someone who did not know it existed.

The correct implementation is workload identity federation. Each provider can act as an OIDC issuer for its own workloads, and each can be configured to trust another provider's issuer. A workload obtains a short-lived, signed token asserting its own identity in its home cloud, presents it to the far cloud, and receives a short-lived credential scoped to a role there. No shared secret exists at any point. Google's implementation exchanges tokens through workload identity pools and providers, and documents specific paths for AWS and Azure principals;18,19 Microsoft Entra ID accepts federated credentials from external OIDC issuers including AWS, which lets an AWS-hosted workload call an Azure API without holding an Azure secret.20 A survey of the mechanisms across providers, together with the platform-neutral SPIFFE/SPIRE alternative for heterogeneous estates, is a useful orientation for anyone designing this once rather than five times.62

Three design consequences follow, and they are not obvious.

Federation is a runtime dependency on the issuer. If provider A's token endpoint is impaired, workloads in A cannot obtain credentials for B, even though B is entirely healthy. This is a control-plane crossing wearing an identity costume, and it is precisely the dependency that bites in the incidents described in §01. Any workload whose job is to keep working when its home provider is unwell needs a credential path that does not run through its home provider's control plane — which usually means a short pre-issued credential with a lifetime long enough to cover the incident and short enough to be acceptable, and an explicit decision about that trade rather than an accident.

Human identity and workload identity are different problems. Staff single sign-on across two providers is a directory question and is largely solved. Machine-to-machine identity across two providers is a trust-configuration question, and the two are frequently owned by different teams who have never spoken.

Policy semantics do not federate. Even with a clean token exchange, the permission expressed on each side is written in a different language with different evaluation order, different deny semantics and different notions of resource hierarchy. There is no cross-cloud least privilege; there are two least privileges, maintained separately, drifting apart. This is one of the divergent capabilities counted in §20.

Network across the seam

Three transports exist and the choice among them is mostly a choice about who owns the failure.

The public internet with encryption is free to start, has no lead time, and has the availability and jitter characteristics of the public internet, which is to say adequate for asynchronous replication and unacceptable for synchronous quorum traffic. It also attracts the full egress list rate on every byte.

Provider-operated cross-cloud connectivity is the significant development of the last three years. Google's Cross-Cloud Interconnect provisions dedicated 10 or 100 Gbps circuits between Google Cloud and other named providers with a 99.99% availability commitment;25,23 its partner variant removes the physical provisioning step, offers bandwidths from 1 Gbps upward, can be ordered from either side, and provisions in a day rather than the one-to-four weeks a dedicated circuit takes.24 AWS's own multicloud interconnect reached general availability in April 2026 with a single-fee structure based on bandwidth and geographic scope, and Google Cloud as the first launch partner.26 Oracle's arrangement is more radical still: its database hardware is installed inside the other providers' facilities, which is why it can quote single-digit-millisecond latencies and joint support across the boundary.46,47

Carrier or colocation cross-connects remain the answer where neither provider offers a direct product for the pair you need, and where a single vendor relationship across many on-ramps is worth more than any individual circuit.

Two numbers should govern the choice. The first is the latency budget: within a metropolitan area, cross-provider round trips of a few milliseconds are achievable, which is enough for synchronous quorum participation; across regions the round trip is tens of milliseconds and synchronous participation is off the table for anything interactive. The second is the volume at which the private path pays for itself. At list rates, a private path at roughly $0.02 per GB against $0.09 per GB to the internet pays back its port charges at a few tens of terabytes a month — which, for anything doing continuous replication, arrives fast.27,63

The bandwidth that is not there

Cross-cloud circuits are provisioned in fixed increments and are subject to per-flow limits: a single traffic flow cannot exceed the documented ceiling regardless of the aggregate size of the connection.64 A replication design that puts all of its traffic in one TCP flow will find its ceiling well below the circuit's rated capacity, which is the kind of thing discovered during the first full resynchronisation rather than during design.

Data gravity and the consistency ceiling

Data is the channel that decides whether a pattern is affordable, because data is the only thing in the estate that has mass.

Three properties give it that mass. It is large, so moving it costs egress and time. It is referenced, so everything near it must be near it. And it is consistent under some model, and that model is a promise made to application code that cannot be changed without changing the application.

The practical taxonomy for cross-cloud state has four rungs, and it is worth being explicit about which rung a design is on, because teams routinely believe they are on a higher one than they are.

Periodic copy. Snapshots or exports move on a schedule. The recovery point is the schedule interval. Cheap, simple, and honest, and sufficient for a great many workloads whose tolerance for lost work is measured in hours.

Asynchronous replication. A log ships continuously; the far side lags by whatever the pipe and the batching allow. The recovery point is the lag, which is a distribution rather than a number, and the tail of that distribution is what you actually lose. Monitoring replication lag as a percentile rather than a mean is the difference between a real recovery point objective and an aspirational one.

Synchronous quorum. A write is not acknowledged until a majority of replicas has it. The recovery point is zero and the write latency is the round trip to the slowest member of the quorum. Across a metropolitan seam this is viable; across regions it is not, for anything a human waits on. And as §13 establishes, a quorum spread across two failure domains does not survive the loss of one of them.

Partitioned ownership. Each side owns a disjoint set of keys and is authoritative for them; the other side serves reads and forwards writes. This sidesteps the consistency problem rather than solving it, at the cost of an availability model that is per-key rather than global, and a routing layer that has to know which side owns what.

Underneath all four sits the same unmovable result: with a partition you choose consistency or availability, and without one you still choose latency or consistency.21,22 No product, interconnect or replication technology alters this. What the recent generation of cross-cloud products alters is the constant factor — a two-millisecond seam makes rung three viable in cases where a fifty-millisecond seam did not46 — and constant factors matter enormously in practice. They just do not repeal the theorem.

One control plane or two

Every multicloud estate has to answer a question that sounds like tooling and is actually strategy: is there one control plane spanning both providers, or two control planes that happen to be operated by the same people?

The single-control-plane answer uses one declarative tool, one policy engine and one pipeline to manage both estates. The appeal is obvious: one place to look, one review process, one set of guardrails, one audit trail. The cost is that the tool becomes a shared singleton in exactly the sense §21 describes, and that its abstraction over the two providers is either thin — in which case it is two configurations in one repository, which is fine and honest — or thick, in which case it must model the intersection of two evolving APIs and will lag both.

Kubernetes is the most consequential instance of the thick abstraction, and its record deserves a fair reading. It is genuinely ubiquitous: production use among container users reached 82% in 2025, and cloud-native technique adoption is now essentially universal.30 It genuinely standardises packaging, scheduling, service discovery and rollout across providers, and a workload that expresses its needs entirely in those terms really does move. What it does not standardise is everything the cluster talks to: the identity model that grants the pod access to storage, the load balancer in front of it, the managed database behind it, the object store it writes to, the secret manager it reads from, the network policy semantics, and the specific behaviours of each provider's managed control plane. The honest summary is that Kubernetes moves the seam down the stack rather than removing it, and moving the seam down is valuable precisely to the degree that you were prepared to give up the managed services above it.

The two-control-plane answer accepts duplication and invests instead in equivalence testing: the same conformance suite runs against both estates and fails the build when they diverge. This is less elegant and considerably more robust, because it makes drift visible instead of assuming an abstraction has prevented it. For patterns where the seam is thin — separation, geography, acquisition — it is almost always the right answer. For patterns where the seam is thick — active-active, migration — the two estates have to be interchangeable, and interchangeability is a property you test for, not a property a tool confers.

The general rule is worth stating plainly: an abstraction layer is a bet that the providers will converge; a conformance suite is a bet that they will not. The second bet has been the winning one for fifteen years.

Observability, cost, and the operational tax

The costs that kill multicloud programmes are almost never the ones in the business case. The business case contains compute, storage and egress. The costs that actually bite are these.

Correlated observability. Two estates producing telemetry in two formats with two identifier schemes cannot be correlated during an incident unless somebody made them correlatable in advance: a shared trace context, a shared service naming convention, a shared clock discipline, a shared severity vocabulary. Retrofitting this during an incident is not possible, and the failure mode is not missing data but two dashboards that disagree while everyone argues about which is right.

Two cost models that cannot be added. Providers do not price the same units, do not bill on the same cycle, do not attribute shared resources the same way, and do not define “committed spend” identically. Adding two bills produces a number; comparing two bills requires a normalisation layer that somebody has to own. The formal management activities involved are catalogued in the multi-cloud management technical report, which is a more useful reference than most vendor material on the subject.12 It is no accident that governance structures have grown alongside portfolio plurality, with cloud centres of excellence at 71% and dedicated cost-management teams at 63% of Flexera's 2026 respondents.16

Skill dilution. An engineer who is expert in one provider and competent in another is worth more than two engineers who are competent in both, for the specific task of diagnosing a subtle problem at three in the morning. Plurality dilutes depth, and depth is what incident response consumes. This cost never appears in a plan and is paid every time something unusual happens.

The negotiating position that did not materialise. The standard commercial argument for a second provider is leverage. In practice, discount tiers are a function of committed spend, and splitting a commitment across two vendors moves both commitments into lower tiers. The credible version of the leverage argument is not “we spend with both” but “we have demonstrated we can move this workload, and here is the exercise record” — which is a capability, not a spend split, and which almost nobody actually has.32,35

Concentration risk and the regulator's question

Until recently, the argument about multicloud was internal. It is not any more, and the change is worth understanding precisely, because it alters which patterns are defensible.

The EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act has applied to financial entities since 17 January 2025.65 Its third-party chapter requires, among other things, a documented strategy for ICT third-party risk, a maintained register of contractual arrangements, a pre-contractual assessment of concentration risk, mandated contractual provisions, and — the clause that matters most here — exit strategies for arrangements supporting critical or important functions. On 18 November 2025 the European Supervisory Authorities published the first list of designated critical ICT third-party providers, bringing named providers under direct EU-level oversight with a lead overseer assigned to each; the designation followed a prescribed methodology using the registers of information that financial entities had already filed.66,67 The list comprised nineteen providers spanning hyperscale cloud, data centres, network infrastructure and financial-sector-specific technology.68,69

The UK arrived by a different route at a similar place. The Bank of England, the Prudential Regulation Authority and the Financial Conduct Authority published final rules for critical third parties in November 2024, effective 1 January 2025, with designation reserved to HM Treasury and oversight expectations set out in a joint supervisory statement; the regime places obligations on the designated providers themselves without reducing the responsibilities of the firms that use them.70,71,72,73

Three consequences follow for architecture, and they are not the ones usually assumed.

The regulator is not asking for two clouds. Nothing in either regime mandates multicloud. What both require is that dependency is understood, documented, assessed for concentration, and exitable. An organisation running a critical function entirely on one provider, with a tested and credible exit plan and a documented concentration assessment, is in a defensible position. An organisation running on two providers with an untested failover and no exit plan is not, and its second provider will not help it.

“Exitable” is a testable claim, and this is where the model in §22 earns its place. An exit plan whose recovery path has never been exercised end to end has a success probability that decays measurably with time since the last exercise. Presenting an untested plan as a control is not a documentation problem; it is a misstatement of the firm's resilience posture. The supervisory question that separates the two is not “do you have a plan?” but “when did you last run it, and what broke?”

Confinement obligations and survivability obligations can conflict. This was established in §10 and it becomes acute under supervision, because both obligations may be written by the same authority for the same function. The sovereign-cloud programmes are the market's attempt to resolve it by putting a second credible operator inside the boundary.48,51 Until that resolution is complete in a given jurisdiction, some requirement vectors have no satisfying architecture, and the correct professional response is to say so in writing.

The commercial side of the same movement is the Data Act's switching regime, which by 12 January 2027 removes switching charges entirely, having already limited them to direct cost recovery since January 2024.37,39,38 Regulators are, in effect, legislating the preconditions for exit while separately demanding that exit be credible. That is a coherent policy even though it is often reported as two unrelated stories.

What the second cloud actually costs

The intersection argument is the oldest objection to provider-agnostic design and the most frequently misstated. It is not that agnostic estates are impossible. It is that portability is bought one capability at a time, and the price per capability is wildly non-uniform.

Model a reference workload with twenty-four capabilities and sort them into three classes. Eleven are parity capabilities: virtual machines, block and object storage, virtual networking, layer-seven load balancing, authoritative DNS, managed Kubernetes, a managed relational database, a container registry, metrics collection, backup. These exist in both providers in recognisably the same shape, and making a workload use either costs a day or two of adapter work each. Seven are divergent: object lifecycle and event semantics, message queues, the policy language, event streaming, functions and their event sources, edge cache behaviours, autoscaling semantics. The idea exists in both; the semantics differ enough that behaviour changes, and each costs roughly a working fortnight to abstract plus continuing maintenance. Six are absent: capabilities with no equivalent, which must be self-operated or forfeited, at roughly five weeks of build each and a day a month to run.

The totals are 220 build-days and 100 run-days a year, and the distribution is the point: the hardest 25% of capabilities consume 68% of the build effort. Refusing to pay for them leaves 75% of capabilities portable but only 62% of what the workload is actually for — because the capabilities with no equivalent are, unsurprisingly, the ones that were worth choosing a provider for.

This is the precise content of the lowest-common-denominator objection, and it explains why the argument is so persistent: both sides are right about different regions of the curve. Restricting a workload to the flat left-hand portion is cheap and often sensible. Insisting on the steep right-hand portion is where estates go to die.32,33,35

To the capability tax add the recurring costs already named: duplicated governance, duplicated observability, duplicated compliance evidence, diluted expertise, and — for any pattern with a live seam — replication egress that scales with success. And subtract the one cost that is genuinely falling: the toll for leaving, which EU law reduces to zero from January 2027.37,36

Four ways a portfolio fails

1. The correlated singleton

This is the failure that makes the second cloud disappointing, and it is almost universal. Duplicating the compute planes does nothing about the components that sit above both of them on the request path: the public DNS zone, the global traffic steering layer that decides which plane a user reaches, the identity provider that issues the tokens, the secrets and key custody service, and whichever external partner API the transaction cannot complete without.

Model them at plausible availabilities and the shared layer alone accounts for 21.97 hours a year. A single-cloud estate with that layer expects 26.34 hours; an active-active cross-cloud estate with the same layer expects 22.62. The second cloud, with all its cost, removes 3.71 hours — while making just two of those shared components dual removes 7.87, more than twice as much, for a small fraction of the money. On these premises 83% of observed downtime lives above both clouds.

The diagnostic is a request-path inventory: list every component a user request touches, mark each as duplicated or singleton, and confirm that no singleton is in the path. Almost every estate that performs this exercise finds at least three. This is also why the October 2025 incidents were so instructive — both were failures of a component that sits above the compute plane and that the compute plane cannot route around.1,5,6

2. The untested path

Covered quantitatively in §07 and worth restating as a failure mode: a recovery path that is not exercised is not a control. The decay is fast, the break-even is 25%, and below it the path is a liability. The organisational tell is that the exercise is scheduled, then deferred because of a release freeze, then deferred because of an incident, then deferred because of the deferral backlog.

3. The silent asymmetry

The two estates are declared equivalent and are not. The standby runs an older image; a feature flag defaults differently; a quota was raised in one estate after an incident and not the other; a certificate was renewed by hand on one side; the second estate's instance family has a different memory-to-CPU ratio and the workload's tuning does not transfer. None of these is visible until failover, and each is individually trivial, which is why nobody prioritises finding them. The only reliable answer is a conformance suite that runs continuously against both estates and fails loudly on divergence — the equivalence-testing approach of §17.

4. The migration that became a strategy

Covered in §11. A stalled migration presents as a portfolio, gets described as one in board material, and thereby loses the only force that was going to close it. The tell is the absence of a date.

A short diagnostic

Five questions that separate a designed portfolio from an accumulated one. 1. Which of the five channels cross a provider boundary on the request path? 2. What is served when the far side does not answer, and is that behaviour tested? 3. When was the recovery path last exercised end to end with production traffic? 4. Which components on the request path exist exactly once? 5. If this is a migration, what date does the second estate switch off? An estate that cannot answer these has a portfolio, not an architecture.

Choosing

Pattern selection is usually approached as a menu. It is better approached as an elimination, because most requirement vectors do not discriminate at all and the ones that do, discriminate hard.

The model tests six requirement vectors against the eight patterns and the results are instructive in their spread. A public web estate with a twenty-four-hour recovery objective and no confinement rule admits all eight patterns — which is to say the requirement does not constrain the architecture at all, and any choice made in its name was made on other grounds. A payments authorisation function requiring a fifteen-minute recovery time and no data loss admits exactly one. A confined EU workload admits four, none of which survive a provider loss. And a function that must both stay inside a jurisdiction and survive the loss of a provider admits none — the vector is unsatisfiable unless two credible providers exist inside the boundary.

The practical procedure is three questions, asked in order and answered honestly.

Must this function keep serving through the loss of a whole provider? For most functions the answer is no, and saying so out loud is the single highest-value act available in this subject. A no here means the portfolio, if it exists, exists for fit, organisation, jurisdiction or history — all legitimate — and the resilience argument should be struck from the document.

Must a data class stay inside one jurisdiction or one operator? A no admits active-active, or cross-cloud standby with a real exercise cadence, and the choice between them is the economics of §13. A yes moves to the third question.

Are there two independent credible providers operating inside that boundary? A yes means active-active across them, with a third failure domain to hold the quorum. A no means the requirement as written cannot be met, and the professional obligation is to escalate that rather than to build something that appears to meet it.

Two supplementary rules earn their keep. If a pattern is chosen for resilience, the exercise cadence is part of the design and is budgeted at design time; a pattern whose exercise cadence is “annually, subject to release freezes” should be downgraded on the ladder until it is one the organisation will actually run. And before committing to a cross-provider active-active design, build one across two regions of a single provider first — same APIs, same identity model, same tooling — and treat the difficulty of that exercise as a lower bound on the difficulty of the real thing.32

What multicloud does not buy

It does not buy availability by default. Whether a second provider raises or lowers expected availability depends entirely on whether user-visible operations require both sides or either side, and the majority of real estates are built the first way while being described the second.

It does not buy the ability to leave. Portability is bought per capability, and the capabilities that are hard to port are the ones that made the provider worth choosing. The falling cost of egress on exit is real and welcome and is not the binding constraint.36,37

It does not buy negotiating leverage on its own. Split commitments earn worse rates. The leverage that exists comes from a demonstrated, exercised ability to move a workload, which is a different asset and a rarer one.

It does not buy regulatory comfort. Supervisors are asking for understood dependency, assessed concentration and credible exit, and an untested second provider satisfies none of those.65,70

What a second provider can buy, when the pattern is chosen deliberately and the seam is engineered rather than inherited, is real and worth having: lawful access to markets that would otherwise be closed; capabilities that genuinely do not exist elsewhere; a survival path for the small number of functions where an hour of downtime costs more than a second estate costs in a year; and, for organisations that inherited plurality, an accidental diversity that is occasionally their most valuable operational property.

The discipline that separates those outcomes from the disappointing ones is not a technology choice. It is the willingness to answer five questions about the seam, in writing, and to accept what the answers imply about the diagram on the wall. Two logos is a fact about procurement. What crosses the line between them is the architecture, and it is the only thing that will matter on the day one of them has a bad morning.
