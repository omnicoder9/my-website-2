The compute ladder: how AWS runs your code

Amazon EC2, ECS, EKS, Fargate, Lambda and the services around them are not competing products. They are successive answers to a single question — how much of the machine do you still want to be responsible for? This is a working guide to each rung, what it costs you, and where the boundary actually falls.

01The ladder, not the catalogue

AWS sells something like a dozen ways to run a process — its own overview of the platform lists EC2, Auto Scaling, Lightsail, App Runner, Batch, Elastic Beanstalk, Fargate, Lambda and Outposts in a single "compute" chapter.[1] Presented as a catalogue they look like a marketing accident — overlapping products competing for the same workload. Presented as a ladder they make sense. Each service is a decision about where to draw one line: the point above which you are responsible for what runs, and below which somebody else is.

That line is the only thing that really distinguishes Amazon EC2 from AWS Fargate, or Fargate from AWS Lambda. All of them ultimately schedule your code onto a physical server in an Amazon data centre. What changes is how much of the stack between the silicon and your business logic you have agreed to operate, patch, monitor and pay for when it sits idle. Moving up the ladder buys you less work and less control, in roughly equal measure. Moving down buys back control at the cost of an operations burden that never goes away.

Read the ladder from the bottom and the history of AWS compute — now genuinely two decades long, across a platform that has grown past 240 services[2] — reads as a single sustained move: take one more layer away from the customer, and charge for a smaller, more precisely metered unit. Virtual machines by the hour became containers by the task became functions by the millisecond. The engineering that made each step possible — hardware-offloaded virtualization, an open-source microVM monitor, custom silicon — is mostly invisible from the API, which is the point.

This article walks the ladder from the bottom rung upward: the substrate, then EC2 and its supporting cast, then containers, then functions, then the specialised services that sit off to one side. Throughout, the questions worth keeping in mind are the ones that actually decide architectures — who patches this, what happens when demand triples in ninety seconds, what does an idle system cost, and what does the failure of a single rack take down with it.

02What is underneath all of it

Every compute service on the list runs on the same fleet. Understanding the three pieces of engineering that fleet is built from explains a surprising number of the constraints that show up higher in the stack.

Virtualization, and then the offload of virtualization

EC2 launched in limited beta in August 2006 with a single instance type — the m1.small, roughly a 1.7 GHz Xeon-equivalent with 1.75 GB of memory and local disk that vanished when the instance did — running in a single Region on the Xen hypervisor.[3,​4] The beta opened to all developers in late 2007 with the arrival of larger 64-bit instance types,[5] and the service reached general availability with a 99.95% availability SLA in October 2008, by which time Elastic Block Store had given instances storage that survived a reboot.[6]

A classical hypervisor spends host CPU cycles doing work on the guest's behalf: virtualizing the network interface, virtualizing storage, enforcing isolation, running management agents. Those cycles are cycles the customer paid for and cannot use. The AWS Nitro System is the long-running project to move that work off the host processor entirely, onto dedicated cards, and to shrink the remaining hypervisor to a thin layer that does little more than allocate memory and CPU.[7] The consequences are practical rather than romantic: bare-metal instance types become possible, instance storage and EBS can be presented as NVMe devices, and the security boundary becomes small enough to reason about.

Firecracker and the microVM

Serverless and container platforms need something the classical hypervisor is bad at: thousands of tiny, short-lived, mutually distrusting workloads per host, each starting in milliseconds. Containers start fast but share a kernel; virtual machines isolate properly but boot slowly and carry hundreds of megabytes of emulated legacy hardware. Firecracker, the virtual machine monitor AWS open-sourced in 2018 and described at NSDI '20, refuses the trade-off: roughly 50,000 lines of Rust built on Linux KVM, with no BIOS, no PCI bus and no device emulation beyond a handful of virtio interfaces.[8] A single server can create around 150 microVMs per second and run thousands concurrently.[9]

Firecracker is the reason Lambda and Fargate can offer per-request isolation at a hardware boundary rather than a kernel one, and — because a microVM's memory can be snapshotted and restored — it is also the reason Lambda SnapStart exists.[10] It is the clearest example in the portfolio of a low-level engineering decision that propagates all the way up to a pricing model.

Silicon

The third piece is the processor itself. AWS announced its Arm-based Graviton family and the A1 instances at re:Invent 2018; Graviton2 followed in 2019 with claims of up to 40% better price-performance than comparable x86 instances of the day,[11,​12] and by the Graviton4 generation AWS reported more than 150 Graviton-powered instance types in service.[13] For architects the significance is not the benchmark numbers but the portability question they force: anything that runs on Linux and compiles cleanly for arm64 — which is most modern service code, and a decreasing amount of legacy code — has a cheaper place to run. Fargate and Lambda both expose Arm as a simple configuration flag, which makes the migration a question of container image architecture rather than infrastructure.

03Amazon EC2

EC2 is the bottom rung that most people ever touch: a virtual machine, on demand, with an operating system you own. Everything else in this article is either built on it, priced against it, or defined by what it makes you do that you would rather not.

The mental model is a small number of composable primitives. An instance is a running virtual machine of a particular instance type, booted from an Amazon Machine Image, attached to EBS volumes and network interfaces, living in a subnet of a VPC inside one Availability Zone, its lifecycle optionally managed by an Auto Scaling group and its physical placement optionally constrained by a placement group. Each of those has a section below.

3.1 Instance types

The instance type is the single most consequential choice, and its name is a specification rather than a SKU. A family letter states the resource ratio the type is optimised for, a generation number states the hardware vintage, optional letters state the processor vendor and any special capability, and the size suffix states how much of the underlying host you are renting.[14]

The families that matter in practice are narrower than the catalogue suggests. General-purpose (m) types sit at roughly 4 GB of memory per vCPU and are the right default when you do not yet know your bottleneck. Compute-optimised (c) types halve that ratio for CPU-bound work: encoding, simulation, busy application servers. Memory-optimised (r, x, z) types double or quadruple it for caches, in-memory analytics and large relational databases. Storage-optimised (i, d) types attach large local NVMe devices for workloads whose performance depends on local disk. Accelerated types (p, g, inf, trn) attach GPUs or purpose-built AI silicon. Burstable (t) types deliver a fraction of a core continuously and accumulate credits that permit short bursts above it, which makes them excellent for development environments and dangerous for anything with a sustained floor of load.

The recurring failure. Teams choose an instance type once, at the start of a project, from whatever appeared in a tutorial, and never revisit it. Because instance families are re-benchmarked every generation and Graviton variants frequently cost less per unit of work, a right-sizing pass across an established fleet is usually the single highest-return infrastructure exercise available — and it requires no application changes at all when the workload is already architecture-portable.

3.2 Amazon Machine Images

An AMI is the template an instance boots from. It carries a snapshot of the root volume, a block device mapping describing which additional volumes to attach, launch permissions controlling which accounts may use it, and metadata fixing the architecture and virtualization type.[15,​16] You cannot launch an instance without specifying one.[17]

Two properties of AMIs shape real deployment pipelines. First, AMI IDs are Region-scoped: copying an image to another Region produces a new identifier,[18] which is why any template that hard-codes an AMI ID is a multi-Region deployment waiting to break. Resolving images through AWS Systems Manager public parameters, or through a parameter your own pipeline writes, removes the problem entirely.

Second, an AMI is a build artifact and should be treated like one. The "golden image" pattern — bake the operating system, agents, runtime and application into an immutable image, then roll the fleet by replacing instances rather than patching them — is what makes Auto Scaling groups safe to use aggressively. It is also what makes container images and AMIs conceptually the same idea applied at two different layers, and it is why teams that already build good container images find EC2-based deployment less painful than teams that do not.

3.3 EBS volumes

Elastic Block Store provides durable, network-attached block devices that an instance sees as ordinary disks, and that survive the instance being stopped and started.[19] Because they are network-attached and Availability-Zone-scoped, they can be snapshotted to Amazon S3, restored elsewhere, and re-attached to a replacement instance — the property that turns an instance failure into an inconvenience rather than a data loss event.

The volume types divide cleanly into SSD-backed types optimised for random I/O and HDD-backed types optimised for large sequential streams.[20] Within the SSD group, gp3 is the current general-purpose default and io2 Block Express serves the demanding transactional tier, with an average latency under 500 microseconds for 16 KiB operations.[21] Within the HDD group, st1 handles throughput-intensive work such as log processing and Kafka log segments, and sc1 is the low-cost option for data that is large and rarely read.[22]

The architecturally interesting change was gp3. Its predecessor gp2 tied performance to capacity at 3 IOPS per GiB, which meant that a workload needing IOPS had to buy capacity it did not need. gp3 delivers a baseline of 3,000 IOPS and 125 MB/s at any volume size and lets you provision more of either independently.[23] That decoupling quietly invalidated a decade of sizing folklore, and it is why "we striped eight volumes together to get IOPS" is now a legacy pattern rather than a best practice.

3.4 Auto Scaling

Auto Scaling arrived in 2009 alongside CloudWatch and Elastic Load Balancing — the launch that arguably made EC2 elastic in more than name.[24] An Auto Scaling group maintains a desired number of instances launched from a launch template, distributes them across Availability Zones, replaces instances that fail health checks, and adjusts the count according to policies you attach.

Three policy styles cover almost everything. Target tracking names a metric and a target value — keep average CPU at 55%, or keep requests-per-target at 900 — and lets the service compute the arithmetic; it is the right default and the one most teams should start with. Step scaling attaches explicit capacity changes to CloudWatch alarm thresholds, which is useful when the relationship between your metric and the capacity you need is not proportional. Scheduled scaling simply sets capacity by the clock.

All three are reactive: they observe a metric that has already moved and then launch instances that need time to boot, join the load balancer, warm their caches and pass health checks. For applications with slow initialisation, that lag is the entire problem. Predictive scaling, introduced in 2018 and available as a native EC2 Auto Scaling policy type since May 2021, addresses it by forecasting from historical patterns and adding capacity ahead of the anticipated load.[25,​26] It needs at least twenty-four hours of history, suits cyclical and recurring patterns rather than genuinely unpredictable spikes, and is designed to complement dynamic scaling rather than replace it.[26] A forecast-only mode, plus a backfill that generates fourteen days of retrospective forecasts as soon as a policy is created, lets you check the model against reality before it is allowed to spend money.[27] The same predictive machinery has since been extended to ECS service auto scaling.[28]

3.5 Placement groups

By default EC2 decides where in an Availability Zone your instances physically land, and it spreads them for its own reasons. A placement group lets you override that with an explicit statement about the correlation you want between your instances and the failure domains beneath them.[29] There are three strategies.

A cluster group packs instances close together inside one Availability Zone to obtain the low latency and high packet rate that tightly coupled high-performance computing requires.[29] Cluster groups are the natural companion to Elastic Fabric Adapter networking, and AWS recommends pairing the two for HPC compute node groups.[30] The trade-off is explicit: you have asked for correlated placement, so you have asked for correlated failure.

A partition group divides the group into logical partitions, each with its own racks, network and power, and guarantees that instances in different partitions do not share underlying hardware.[29] This is the strategy for large distributed and replicated systems — HDFS, Cassandra, Kafka — and the usual advice is to mirror the application's replication factor in the partition count, so that a rack-level fault destroys at most one replica.[31]

A spread group strictly places a small number of instances on distinct underlying hardware, at either rack or host level.[29,​32] It is the right choice for a handful of instances that must not fail together — a quorum of controllers, a pair of licence servers — and it is deliberately limited in size, because the guarantee is only meaningful when the count is small.

Placement groups are free to create and declarable in CloudFormation with a strategy and, for partition groups, a partition count.[33] Their real cost is capacity: a constraint on placement is a constraint on which hosts can satisfy a launch, and large cluster launches into a busy Availability Zone can and do fail. Launching all the instances in a cluster group in a single request, rather than incrementally, materially improves the odds.

3.6 Paying for it

EC2's purchasing options are best read as a two-dimensional trade. You can reduce price by committing — Savings Plans commit to a dollar-per-hour spend for one or three years, Reserved Instances commit to a specific configuration — or by accepting interruption, which is what Spot Instances sell: spare capacity at a steep discount, reclaimable with two minutes' notice. Dedicated Hosts and Dedicated Instances address licensing and tenancy requirements rather than cost, and On-Demand Capacity Reservations buy the right to launch in a specific Availability Zone without buying a discount.[34]

The mature pattern layers them: a Savings Plan sized to the steady-state floor, On-Demand for the predictable daytime ramp, and Spot for anything stateless and interruption-tolerant — batch jobs, CI runners, stateless web tiers behind a load balancer, and a fraction of container capacity. Mixed-instance Auto Scaling groups make this concrete by drawing from several instance types and both purchase models in one group, which both improves Spot availability and reduces exposure to a single capacity pool.

04Containers

4.1 Docker, Kubernetes and why AWS needed both

The container era has a precise starting gun: a five-minute lightning talk at PyCon in March 2013 in which Solomon Hykes demonstrated Docker.[35] Linux containers were not new — the kernel primitives had existed for years — but Docker made them usable, and the image format it introduced turned "it works on my machine" into a portable artifact. The image, not the runtime, is the durable contribution; it was subsequently standardised through the Open Container Initiative, founded in June 2015 by Docker, CoreOS and the Linux Foundation.[36]

Portable images immediately created a scheduling problem. Google had solved a version of it internally with Borg, and in June 2014 announced Kubernetes at DockerCon; version 1.0 shipped in July 2015, at which point the project was donated to the newly formed Cloud Native Computing Foundation.[35] What followed was several years of orchestrator competition that Kubernetes decisively won, not on technical merit alone but because a neutral foundation made it safe for competitors to standardise on it.

AWS responded twice. Amazon ECS, announced at re:Invent in November 2014 and generally available in April 2015, was AWS's own orchestrator: simpler, deeply integrated, and expressed entirely through AWS APIs.[37] Amazon EKS, previewed at re:Invent 2017 and generally available on 5 June 2018, was the concession that customers wanted upstream Kubernetes and would run it on AWS with or without help.[38,​39] Both survive because they answer different questions, and neither is deprecating the other.

4.2 Amazon ECS

ECS is a container orchestrator whose entire interface is the AWS API. The unit of deployment is a task definition: a JSON document naming one or more container images, their CPU and memory reservations, environment, logging configuration, networking mode and — importantly — an IAM task role. A task is a running instance of that definition. A service keeps a desired number of tasks running, registers them with a load balancer target group, and performs rolling or blue/green deployments. A cluster is a logical grouping with a capacity provider behind it.

The taskRoleArn line is the argument for ECS in a sentence. Because a task is an AWS resource, it can assume an IAM role natively; there is no identity translation layer, no webhook, no token projection. Every AWS SDK call the container makes is signed with credentials scoped to that task. Similarly, awsvpc network mode gives each task its own elastic network interface and therefore its own security group, so network policy is expressed with the same primitive that governs everything else in the VPC.

ECS has continued to absorb capabilities that on Kubernetes would be add-ons: Service Connect provides service-to-service discovery, traffic telemetry and resilience settings across clusters and VPCs without deploying a mesh,[40] and ECS Anywhere, generally available in May 2021, extends the same APIs, task definitions and scheduling to customer-managed hardware on premises.[41] Service auto scaling now supports target tracking, scheduled and predictive policies at the task level, independently of how the underlying capacity scales.[28]

4.3 Amazon EKS

EKS runs an upstream-conformant Kubernetes control plane across multiple Availability Zones, replaces unhealthy control plane nodes, and patches and upgrades the management infrastructure on request.[38,​39] What it does not do is change Kubernetes. You still write manifests, still run kubectl, still reason about Deployments, Services, Ingresses and CRDs, and still own the upgrade cadence of your clusters.

That is precisely the point, and it is why the ECS-versus-EKS decision is an operating model decision rather than a workload one. Choosing EKS buys access to an enormous ecosystem — Argo CD, Istio or Linkerd, Prometheus and the operator pattern, Karpenter for node provisioning, Helm charts for essentially every piece of infrastructure software — and a deployment description that is, in principle, portable to another Kubernetes anywhere. It costs you a control plane version to track, a set of cluster add-ons to keep compatible, an RBAC model that must be reconciled with IAM through IRSA or EKS Pod Identity, and a body of knowledge your on-call engineers now need.

AWS has spent recent years narrowing the operational gap. EKS Anywhere, launched in 2021, brings cluster lifecycle management to on-premises infrastructure, and has been extended to ruggedised Snow devices for disconnected edge deployments.[42] More recently, EKS Capabilities packages commonly self-hosted platform components — Argo CD, AWS Controllers for Kubernetes, Kube Resource Orchestrator — as managed, integrated add-ons, which is a direct attempt to remove the platform-engineering tax that made Kubernetes expensive to adopt.[43]

A useful heuristic. If your team already has Kubernetes expertise, or your deployment artifacts must run in more than one environment, or you depend on a specific CNCF project, choose EKS. If neither is true, ECS will do the same job with materially less to learn and less to keep current — and the migration path from ECS to EKS later is far less painful than the reverse.

4.4 AWS Fargate

Fargate is the most commonly misunderstood service in the portfolio, because it is routinely described as a third orchestrator. It is not. Fargate is a capacity provider: it replaces the EC2 fleet underneath ECS or EKS, so that the unit you provision is a task or a pod rather than an instance.[44] Announced at re:Invent 2017 for ECS,[45] it gained EKS support at re:Invent 2019 by way of a mutating webhook and a custom scheduler rather than a fork of Kubernetes.[44]

Each Fargate task runs in its own Firecracker microVM, which is what allows AWS to place tasks from different customers on the same physical host without relying on kernel isolation.[8,​7] The practical consequences follow from that architecture. There is no instance to patch and no AMI to bake, because there is no instance you can see. There is also no privileged access to the host, no DaemonSet that reads the node's kernel, and no way to attach a GPU, which rules out a specific and important class of workloads. Billing is per vCPU-second and GB-second of the task's requested size, so idle headroom you would have paid for on EC2 simply does not exist — and equally, bin-packing gains you would have engineered on EC2 are not available either.

The cost comparison is therefore not a like-for-like rate comparison. Fargate's per-unit price is higher than the equivalent EC2 instance, but the relevant question is what your average EC2 utilisation actually is once you account for scaling headroom, uneven task sizes and the instances that sit idle during a deployment. For clusters running below roughly half utilisation, Fargate frequently wins on total cost as well as on operational effort. For large, dense, steadily-loaded clusters — especially ones that can absorb Spot capacity — EC2 usually remains cheaper. Fargate Spot exists and narrows the gap for interruption-tolerant work.

4.5 Putting the container choices together

The container decision decomposes into two independent questions, and conflating them is the usual source of confusion. First: which control plane — ECS or EKS? Second: which capacity — EC2 or Fargate? All four combinations are supported and all four are sensible for some workload. ECS on Fargate is the lowest-effort path to running a containerised service in production. EKS on EC2 with Karpenter is the highest-control path and the one large platform teams converge on. ECS on EC2 suits teams that need host access but not Kubernetes. EKS on Fargate suits teams that want the Kubernetes API without a node fleet, accepting Fargate's restrictions in exchange.

05Serverless: AWS Lambda

Lambda, announced at re:Invent in November 2014 and generally available in April 2015, removed the last visible unit of infrastructure.[46] You upload a function; AWS runs it when something happens; you pay for the milliseconds it executes. There is no instance, no cluster, no scaling policy and no idle cost.

5.1 The execution model, and its edges

A Lambda function runs inside an execution environment — in practice a Firecracker microVM — that Lambda creates, reuses for subsequent invocations, and eventually discards.[8] Concurrency is the number of environments running simultaneously; Lambda creates more as demand rises and there is no capacity to pre-provision.

The quotas define the shape of the workloads that fit. A single invocation may run for at most fifteen minutes, and a function may be allocated between 128 MB and 10,240 MB of memory in 1 MB increments, with CPU allocated proportionally.[47] Ephemeral storage in /tmp starts at 512 MB and can be raised to 10 GB for workloads such as media transcoding or document generation that need real scratch space.[48] Synchronous payloads are capped at 6 MB. Since December 2020, billing has been per millisecond rather than per 100 ms, and functions can be packaged as container images rather than zip archives.[49]

Those numbers are not arbitrary irritations; they are the definition of the service. Anything that needs to run for an hour, hold a socket open, keep state between requests or use a GPU is not a Lambda workload, and attempting to make it one produces the characteristic anti-pattern of a function that orchestrates twelve other functions to evade the timeout. Step Functions, ECS tasks and AWS Batch exist for exactly those cases.

5.2 Layers

Lambda Layers, launched in November 2018 alongside custom runtimes, are a zip archive of supplementary code or data — libraries, a custom runtime, configuration — that can be referenced by many functions at once.[50,​51] Each layer version is immutable and identified by an ARN ending in a version number, and functions must reference an exact version.[51]

Layers solve three problems. They keep deployment packages small, which keeps the console editor usable and shortens uploads. They separate dependency updates from application updates, so a shared cryptography library can be patched without redeploying every function that uses it. And they provide a distribution channel for vendor-supplied extensions — observability agents, secrets caching, parameter fetching — which is now how most third-party tooling reaches Lambda. The mechanics are runtime-specific: Python layers place packages under a python/ directory, Java layers under java/lib/, and the contents are mounted at /opt in the execution environment.[52,​53]

The cost is real if unglamorous: each layer must be downloaded and mounted during initialisation, so three thin layers can add more startup latency than one consolidated one, and immutable versioning means a shared layer update requires updating every consuming function's configuration.

5.3 Event sources

Almost nothing calls a Lambda function directly. The interesting question is which of three invocation models is in play, because the model determines who retries, where failures accumulate and how concurrency behaves.

Synchronous invocation — API Gateway, Application Load Balancer, function URLs, a direct Invoke call — makes the caller wait and hands errors straight back. Retries are the caller's problem, and the caller's timeout, not Lambda's, is the binding constraint.

Asynchronous invocation — S3 event notifications, SNS, EventBridge — hands the event to an internal queue, returns immediately, and lets Lambda retry on failure before routing the event to a dead-letter queue or an on-failure destination. The subtlety is that the producer has no idea whether processing succeeded, which means the destination configuration is not optional in production.

Event source mappings are a distinct resource that Lambda itself operates: a poller that reads from SQS, Kinesis, DynamoDB Streams, Amazon MQ, Amazon DocumentDB change streams, or self-managed and managed Kafka, batches records, and invokes the function with them.[54] Lambda autoscales the number of pollers with message volume, and a provisioned mode lets you pin minimum and maximum polling resources for latency-sensitive workloads.[55] Event source mappings also support filtering, so events that do not match your criteria never invoke the function at all — which is both a cost optimisation and a way to keep filtering logic out of application code. For ordered stream sources, a failing batch pauses the affected shard until the error is resolved, which is a correctness feature and an availability hazard in equal measure.

5.4 Cold starts

A cold start is what happens when no warm execution environment is available: Lambda must create one, download the function's code, start the language runtime, and run the initialisation code outside your handler before the handler itself is called.[56] The largest contributor is almost always that last step — your own initialisation — rather than anything AWS does.[57]

Four mitigations exist, in increasing order of commitment. Write less initialisation code: keep heavy imports out of the module scope, defer client construction, avoid frameworks that scan the classpath at startup. Provisioned concurrency keeps a specified number of environments initialised and waiting, which eliminates cold starts on that portion of traffic and reintroduces an idle cost. SnapStart takes an encrypted Firecracker snapshot of the memory and disk state of an initialised environment when you publish a function version, caches it, and restores from it on invocation instead of initialising afresh — delivering up to roughly ten times faster startup at no additional charge, initially for Java and later for Python and .NET.[57,​58]

SnapStart is not free of consequences, and they are instructive. Anything that must be unique per environment cannot survive a snapshot: pseudo-random number generator seeds must be reseeded, and network connections established during initialisation are dead on restore and must be re-established, which claws back some of the benefit.[59] Getting the most from it requires priming — deliberately exercising the code paths you want warm during the initialisation phase so that their work lands inside the snapshot.[60] The fourth mitigation is simply to accept cold starts: on asynchronous and stream-driven paths, where nobody is waiting, they rarely matter, and engineering effort spent eliminating them there is effort wasted.

06The rest of the portfolio

Five further services exist because the ladder has side rails: cases where the constraint is not how much infrastructure you want to operate but what kind of workload, what kind of team, or what kind of building the compute has to sit in.

6.1 AWS Elastic Beanstalk

Announced in January 2011, Beanstalk was AWS's entry into platform-as-a-service: upload an application and the service handles capacity provisioning, load balancing, auto scaling and health monitoring, while leaving you full access to the underlying resources.[61] The initial release supported only Java on Tomcat; support for .NET, Python, Ruby, PHP, Node.js, Go and Docker followed.[62]

Structurally, Beanstalk is an orchestration layer over EC2, Auto Scaling groups, Elastic Load Balancing, S3 and CloudWatch rather than a distinct runtime, and there is no charge for the service itself.[61] That transparency is its defining trait: you can drop into the generated resources and modify them, which is both its virtue and the reason it drifts. It remains a reasonable answer for a conventional web application maintained by a small team, and a poor answer for anything that wants to be a fleet of microservices.

6.2 AWS Batch

Batch, previewed at re:Invent in December 2016 and generally available in January 2017, is a scheduler for finite jobs rather than a platform for long-running services.[63,​64] You define compute environments, job queues with priorities, and job definitions; Batch dynamically provisions the quantity and type of compute — including Spot Instances — that the submitted work requires, handles dependencies and retries, and tears the capacity down afterwards. There is no charge for Batch itself; you pay for the underlying resources.[63] Since 2020 it can run jobs on Fargate as well as EC2, which removes the instance fleet from the picture entirely for jobs that fit Fargate's constraints.[65]

Batch matters most in genomics, financial risk, media rendering, simulation and any domain where the work arrives as a directed graph of thousands of independent tasks. Its natural comparison is not to ECS but to on-premises HPC schedulers such as Slurm or LSF.

6.3 Amazon Lightsail

Launched in November 2016, Lightsail bundles a virtual machine, SSD storage, a data transfer allowance, DNS management and a static IP into a flat monthly price.[66] It exists because the EC2 console asks a beginner to make a dozen decisions — VPC, subnet, security group, IAM role, volume type, elastic IP — before a WordPress site can exist, and because DigitalOcean and Linode had built a business on not asking any of them.

Lightsail runs on the same infrastructure and can be connected to other AWS services as requirements grow, which makes it a reasonable on-ramp rather than a dead end.[66] It is the wrong choice the moment you need fine-grained IAM, private networking topology or infrastructure-as-code across environments.

6.4 AWS Outposts

Outposts is the inversion of the whole model: rather than moving the workload to AWS, AWS ships racks of its own hardware into your data centre or colocation space.[67] Announced at re:Invent 2018 and generally available in December 2019, an Outpost runs EC2 instances and EBS volumes locally, supports container services such as ECS and EKS and databases such as RDS on premises, and connects back to a parent Region for control plane operations and the services that remain there.[68,​69] AWS delivers, installs, monitors, patches and updates the hardware.[69] A second-generation rack, generally available in 2025, adds current x86 instance families and accelerated networking aimed at ultra-low latency work such as trading systems and telecom 5G core.[70]

The justification is always one of three things: a latency requirement to on-premises systems that no Region can meet, a data residency or regulatory constraint that forbids the data leaving a building, or local data processing at a volume that makes egress impractical. It is not a cost optimisation, and treating it as one ends badly.

6.5 AWS App Runner

App Runner, generally available on 18 May 2021, takes a container image or a source repository and produces a running, load-balanced, TLS-terminated, auto-scaling web service with no orchestrator to configure.[71,​72] It builds and deploys the application, balances traffic, scales on demand and monitors health, and it is explicitly aimed at customers with no prior container or infrastructure experience.[71] AWS Copilot gained App Runner support the day after launch, giving a single-command path from a Dockerfile to a running service.[73]

App Runner is best understood as Beanstalk redesigned around containers and request-driven scaling: more managed, less configurable, and appropriate for a request-response web service that does not need to reach into its own infrastructure. Its narrower Regional availability and smaller feature surface relative to ECS remain the main reasons teams graduate away from it.

07How compute composes with everything else

Compute services are rarely interesting alone. What makes an architecture is the set of services on either side of them, and the integration patterns are remarkably consistent across the ladder.

Identity is the spine. Every rung has an IAM principal: an instance profile on EC2, a task role on ECS, a service account mapped to an IAM role on EKS, an execution role on Lambda. Once that is in place, no application in any tier should hold a long-lived access key. This is the single most valuable property AWS compute services share, and it is why identity design should precede compute selection rather than follow it.

Networking is uniform. EC2 instances, awsvpc-mode ECS tasks, EKS pods with the VPC CNI, Fargate tasks and VPC-attached Lambda functions all receive elastic network interfaces in your subnets and are governed by the same security groups and route tables. A network diagram therefore does not need to know which compute service is in each box.

Queues and events are how tiers decouple. SQS in front of a consumer absorbs bursts that would otherwise force the compute tier to scale instantly; EventBridge routes events between bounded contexts without either side knowing the other exists; Step Functions coordinates long-running workflows across Lambda functions, ECS tasks and Batch jobs, which is how systems escape the fifteen-minute limit without heroics. Lambda's event source mappings, described above, are the managed expression of this pattern.[54]

State goes elsewhere. The higher you climb the ladder, the more strictly the compute tier must be stateless, because the platform reserves the right to destroy it. RDS, Aurora, DynamoDB, ElastiCache, S3 and EFS are the places state lives; EFS in particular is what lets Lambda functions and Fargate tasks share a filesystem when they genuinely must.

Observability is not automatic. CloudWatch collects metrics and logs from all of these, but the useful signals differ sharply by rung: instance CPU and disk for EC2, task count and service deployment state for ECS, pod scheduling and node pressure for EKS, and concurrency, throttles, initialisation duration and iterator age for Lambda. Distributed tracing through X-Ray or an OpenTelemetry collector is what makes a multi-service request comprehensible, and it needs to be designed in rather than bolted on.

08Choosing

The honest version of the selection advice is short: start as high on the ladder as the workload permits, and descend only when a specific requirement forces you down. Descending is always possible; ascending later usually means a rewrite.

The requirements that force a descent are worth naming explicitly, because they are fewer than teams assume. A GPU or a specialised accelerator forces EC2 or EC2-backed container capacity. A software licence tied to physical cores or sockets forces Dedicated Hosts. A kernel module, a custom kernel, or a workload that needs privileged host access forces EC2. Sustained execution beyond fifteen minutes forces containers or Batch rather than Lambda. Sub-millisecond locality to on-premises equipment forces Outposts, Local Zones or Wavelength. Everything else is a preference, and preferences should be settled in favour of whichever option asks the team to operate less.

8.1 The cost question, framed properly

Comparing list prices across rungs is close to meaningless, because the services meter different things. EC2 bills for capacity you hold, whether or not you use it. Fargate bills for capacity you requested per task, whether or not the task uses it. Lambda bills for memory multiplied by execution time, so an idle function costs nothing and an inefficient one costs linearly more. The right comparison is cost per unit of delivered work at your actual utilisation, including the engineering time to operate each option.

Three rules of thumb hold up in practice. Steady, high-utilisation, predictable load favours EC2 with a Savings Plan; the discount is large and the operational overhead is amortised across a big fleet. Spiky or low-utilisation load favours Fargate or Lambda, because you stop paying for the trough. And any workload whose traffic is genuinely intermittent — internal tools, scheduled jobs, webhooks, low-volume APIs — is almost always cheapest on Lambda by an order of magnitude, because the alternative is paying for a server to wait.

09What the ladder implies

Two things are worth taking away from the shape of this portfolio rather than from any individual service.

The first is that the abstractions are converging on capacity as a commodity. Fargate, Lambda and App Runner are all expressions of the same idea — that the customer should describe the workload and not the machine — arrived at from different starting points. Meanwhile EKS Capabilities, ECS Service Connect and predictive scaling at the task level are all attempts to take operational work that used to be the customer's and absorb it into the service.[43,​40,​28] The direction of travel has not changed since 2006.

The second is that the choice you make is mostly a choice about who is on call. The brass line in Figure 1 is, in the end, a line drawn through an on-call rotation. Every rung you climb removes a category of incident from your team — kernel CVEs, disk exhaustion, cluster upgrades, capacity shortfalls — and replaces it with a category of constraint you must design around. Teams that pick a rung by counting the constraints usually pick too low. Teams that pick by counting the incidents usually pick correctly.
