Findings in brief

  1. Privacy has moved, not died. It now lives in what it takes to turn a record into a person. This article codes thirty mechanisms by that cost on a six-rung seal ladder, from S0 (open) to S5 (sealed).
  2. Model C's cell is empty. Twelve mechanisms reveal a specific target. The seven that rely on policy or permission include six in use at scale; the five that put targeted identification behind a technical seal include zero in use at scale.
  3. Names are not seals. In synthetic data, postal district, full birth date and sex make 94.29% of residents unique; four location points identify 99.25% of people.
  4. The detector, not the cipher, sets population privacy. At 99.9% specificity and a 1-in-10,000 base rate, 9.01% of flags are true; every false flag is an unsealing request about an innocent person.
  5. Independence is the whole product. Thresholds cut abuse by orders of magnitude, but one authority able to compel every keyholder sets a floor of 1% that no threshold removes.
  6. Verdict. Model C is feasible today as narrow, predicate-specific systems. As a general architecture it is blocked by institutions, not cryptography: who chooses the predicate, who holds the keys, and how long the sealed archive may exist.

§1 The wrong question

"Is privacy dead?" is the wrong question, because it treats privacy as a quantity of hidden information that is being used up. On that view the answer is obviously yes: phones register their position with the network all day, Moscow had fitted live facial recognition to more than 220,000 cameras by 20221, and data brokers sell location and browsing data on nearly everyone to any agency with a procurement budget2. But a world in which a great deal is recorded is not the same as a world in which anyone can look at it. The Stasi needed nothing more advanced than paper files and index cards to build a society without privacy3, while an end-to-end encrypted messaging service relays messages it has no means of reading4. What separates those two worlds is not how much is collected. It is what it takes to look.

This article asks that question directly. What would it take to look? For any system that holds information about people, who can turn a record into a named person, what must they do first, how much does one look reveal, and who would ever know that it happened? Those four questions decide whether a surveillance system is a tool, a threat, or both, and they can be answered in engineering terms, which "is privacy dead?" cannot.

The question matters most for a proposal that the material behind this article calls privacy by default, targeted identification when authorised: systems that monitor narrowly defined events without identifying anyone, and that reveal an identity only when an independent authority agrees and the disclosure is recorded where others can check it. Call it Model C, to distinguish it from surveillance by default (Model A) and privacy by default with no exceptional access at all (Model B). Model C is attractive because it promises to dissolve a trade-off that has defined the politics of surveillance since the Church Committee5: the claim that a society can have either the ability to investigate wrongdoing or the freedom of ordinary people from routine identification, but not both.

Whether Model C is feasible is partly a question about cryptography and partly a question about institutions, and the honest answer depends on which part one asks about. To keep the two apart, the article does four things. It sets out the history, concepts and schools of thought that frame the debate (§2–§3). It shows why the cheapest form of privacy protection, removing names, fails (§4), and surveys the privacy-enhancing technologies that can do better (§5). It then defines a six-rung seal ladder that classifies any mechanism by what stands between a would-be looker and an identity, and codes thirty real mechanisms on it, from the Stasi card index to zero-knowledge age proofs (§6–§8). Finally, it runs nine deterministic experiments on the arithmetic of looking (§9) and uses all of it to assess Model C requirement by requirement (§10–§11).

The headline finding is structural. Of the thirty mechanisms coded here, twelve reveal the records of a specific target when they are used. Seven of those twelve rely on policy or legal permission (rungs S1–S2), and six of the seven have operated at scale. The other five place targeted identification behind a technical seal (rungs S3–S5), which is exactly what Model C requires, and none of those five has ever operated at scale: two were abandoned, three remain research. Where a technical seal is in daily use, it reveals only aggregates or nothing at all. Model C's defining cell, targeted identification under a technical seal, is empty in practice.

That finding is not a proof that Model C is impossible; the research prototypes work, and the experiments in §9 show some of them working. It is evidence about where the difficulty actually lies. The lock is the easy part. The hard parts are deciding what the lock should open for, and who holds the key.

§2 A short history of watching

2.1 From the inspection-house to the right to be let alone

Jeremy Bentham's Panopticon of 1791 proposed a circular prison in which a single inspector could observe every cell while the inmates could never tell whether they were being watched6. Its insight was that the possibility of observation, not observation itself, disciplines behaviour, and that the architecture of a building could enforce that possibility. Michel Foucault later made the panopticon a metaphor for modern power in general: a society organised so that people behave as if seen7.

The legal idea of privacy arrived a century after Bentham and was provoked, as much of the field still is, by a new recording technology. In 1890 Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis argued in the Harvard Law Review that instantaneous photography and the newspaper trade had made it necessary for the common law to recognise a right "to be let alone"8. Brandeis carried the idea onto the Supreme Court, and his dissent in Olmstead v. United States in 1928, where the majority held that a wiretap placed outside a home was no search at all, is still the most quoted defence of privacy against government surveillance in American law9. The majority's reasoning, that the Fourth Amendment protected places and things, survived until Katz v. United States in 1967 held that it protects people, and that a warrant is needed to listen to a public telephone booth10.

2.2 Databanks, files and the first principles

The postwar decades produced two very different responses to the growth of state records. In East Germany, the Ministry for State Security built a filing system that eventually filled some 111 kilometres of shelving and indexed millions of people3; its archive is now the most complete record of what surveillance by default looks like when the watcher answers to no one. In the democracies, the response was a set of principles. Alan Westin's Privacy and Freedom defined privacy as the claim of individuals to decide for themselves when, how and to what extent information about them is communicated to others11. The US Department of Health, Education, and Welfare turned that idea into the Fair Information Practices in 197312, and the OECD Guidelines of 1980 carried them internationally: collection limitation, purpose specification, use limitation, security, openness, individual participation and accountability13.

Those principles were written for databanks that organisations held about people. They said little about intelligence agencies, and in 1975–76 the Church Committee showed why that mattered: decades of domestic surveillance, mail opening and political targeting by agencies that had operated without meaningful external authorisation5. The legislative answer, the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978, created a secret court to authorise national-security surveillance14. It is worth noticing what kind of answer that was. It did not make surveillance technically harder. It inserted a permission step, a judge, between the agency and the target, and it relied on the agency to respect it. In the vocabulary of §6, it moved intelligence surveillance from rung S1 to rung S2.

At the same time the Supreme Court was opening a gap that would matter more than any statute. United States v. Miller (1976) and Smith v. Maryland (1979) held that people have no reasonable expectation of privacy in records they have handed to a bank or a telephone company15,16. This third-party doctrine meant that the more of life passed through intermediaries, the less of it the Fourth Amendment covered.

2.3 The cryptographic counter-tradition

While lawyers were building permission structures, a small group of cryptographers began asking whether privacy could be guaranteed by mathematics instead of by rules. In 1981 David Chaum described mix networks that hide who is communicating with whom17, and in 1985 he published a paper whose title states the programme plainly: security without identification, transaction systems to make Big Brother obsolete18. Chaum's argument was that individuals could carry unlinkable credentials and pseudonyms, so that organisations could verify what they needed to verify without ever assembling the dossier. The theoretical tools followed: Shamir's secret sharing (1979)19, the idea of computing on encrypted data (1978)20, zero-knowledge proofs (1985, published 1989)21, and secure multiparty computation (1982–87)22,23. Chaum and van Heyst's group signatures (1991) even built in a form of Model C: members sign anonymously, but a designated manager can open a signature when necessary24.

2.4 The crypto wars and the lesson of escrow

The first attempt to engineer targeted access into mass-market technology came in 1993, when the US government proposed the Clipper chip: a telephone encryption device whose unit keys would be split between two government escrow agents and released only to agencies holding a wiretap order4. Clipper was, in effect, a Model C proposal with a two-of-two threshold, and it failed for two separate reasons that recur throughout this article. Technically, Matt Blaze showed that the law-enforcement access field could be forged with a brute-force search of only 216 possibilities, letting users defeat the escrow while appearing to comply25. Institutionally, both keyholders were agencies of the same executive branch, so the threshold provided little protection against the actor most likely to abuse it. A 1997 report by eleven cryptographers generalised the objection: key-recovery systems add complexity, new targets and new insiders, and their risks scale with deployment26. Meanwhile, the Communications Assistance for Law Enforcement Act of 1994 had already required US carriers to build lawful-intercept capability into their switches27. That interface, rather than any escrowed key, became the working mechanism for targeted interception, and its later history is the strongest empirical evidence in the debate (plate D01).

2.5 After 2001: collection, then revelation

The years after the September 2001 attacks moved the centre of gravity from targeted to programmatic surveillance. Under Section 215 of the PATRIOT Act the NSA collected domestic telephone call records in bulk from US carriers, querying them when an analyst judged that a phone number met a standard of reasonable articulable suspicion28. Section 702 of FISA, added in 2008, authorised the collection of foreigners' communications from US providers under annual programmatic certifications, without individual orders; Americans' communications swept in along the way could later be queried29.

Most of this was secret until June 2013, when documents provided by Edward Snowden revealed the bulk telephony programme30. The Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board reported in January 2014 that it could identify no instance in which the programme had made a concrete difference to the outcome of a counter-terrorism investigation28. The Second Circuit held in 2015 that the statute did not authorise bulk collection31, and Congress replaced it the same year with a system in which carriers kept the records and the government queried them32. That successor programme ended its own life in 2018, when the NSA deleted more than 685 million call records after discovering "technical irregularities" in what carriers had supplied33.

European courts moved faster and further. In 2014 the Court of Justice struck down the EU Data Retention Directive for requiring general and indiscriminate retention of communications data34; in 2015 and 2020 it invalidated two successive EU–US data-transfer frameworks because US surveillance law did not offer Europeans equivalent protection35,36. The third framework survived its first challenge in the General Court in September 2025 and is now on appeal37. The General Data Protection Regulation, applicable from 2018, made "data protection by design and by default" a legal duty38, and the European Court of Human Rights held in 2021 that bulk interception is not inherently unlawful but must be subject to "end-to-end safeguards", including independent authorisation and oversight of selectors39. India's Supreme Court recognised privacy as a fundamental right in 201740.

2.6 The 2020s: location, faces, and the interface that was used

In the United States, the decisive change was a series of cases recognising that digital records are different in kind. United States v. Jones (2012) held that attaching a GPS tracker to a car was a search41; Riley v. California (2014) required a warrant to search a phone seized on arrest42; and Carpenter v. United States (2018) held that obtaining historical cell-site location records from a carrier is a search, refusing to extend the third-party doctrine to them43. On 29 June 2026 the Court applied Carpenter to geofence warrants in Chatrie v. United States: obtaining a person's Google Location History is a search, even for a short period and even from a third party, and the case was remanded to decide whether the warrant was adequately particular44. The opinion notes that Google received its first geofence warrant in 2016 and more than 11,000 a few years later, and that in 2025 Google moved Location History onto users' devices and says it can no longer answer such warrants44.

The same period produced the first serious test of privacy by default at population scale. In 2020, decentralised exposure-notification systems let phones warn contacts of infected people without any server learning who met whom45; the NHS app alone was estimated to have averted roughly 284,000 cases in its first three months46. In 2021 Apple proposed detecting known child-abuse images using threshold private set intersection, a design in which the provider could see nothing about an account until thirty matches accumulated47,48; it withdrew the plan in December 2022 after sustained criticism from security researchers49,50.

And in 2024 the strongest argument against exceptional access stopped being hypothetical. US officials confirmed that a Chinese state-sponsored group known as Salt Typhoon had compromised networks at multiple telecommunications companies, including the systems used to process court-ordered wiretap requests51; the FBI later said the campaign reached more than 80 countries and that it had notified at least 600 organisations52. The interface built in 1994 to make targeted surveillance easy had made it easy for the wrong party too, as a rootkit in Vodafone Greece's switches had already shown in 2004–0553.

2.7 Where things stand in November 2026

The present settlement is unstable in almost every jurisdiction. Section 702, last reauthorised in April 2024 with a two-year sunset54, lapsed on 12 June 2026 for the first time since its enactment, after short extensions and a failure to agree on reforms, yet collection continues under certifications the FISA Court approved in March 2026 that run into early 202729,55. The UK has twice ordered Apple to provide access to encrypted iCloud data; Apple withdrew Advanced Data Protection for UK users in 2025 and is challenging the latest, UK-only notice before the Investigatory Powers Tribunal56,57. The EU's interim permission for voluntary scanning of private messages expired on 4 April 2026, was revived from 31 July 202658, and the permanent Child Sexual Abuse Regulation was still in trilogue at the end of September59. The AI Act has prohibited most real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement since February 2025, with exceptions requiring prior judicial or independent authorisation60. And EU member states are obliged to offer citizens a digital identity wallet built on selective disclosure by the end of 2026, though most will miss the deadline61,62.

§3 Concepts and schools of thought

3.1 A working vocabulary

The debate is muddled by words that are used interchangeably but mean different things. This article uses them as follows.

  • Confidentiality is a property of data: unauthorised parties cannot read it. Encryption provides confidentiality.
  • Privacy is a property of a person's situation: control over what is collected, inferred, used, shared and retained about them. Confidential data can still be used against someone by the party authorised to read it.
  • Anonymity means an action cannot be linked to an identity; pseudonymity means it is linked to a stable identifier that can sometimes be linked back. European law treats pseudonymised data as personal data in the hands of anyone who can reasonably re-identify it, though the Court of Justice held in 2025 that this is assessed from the position of the party holding it38,63.
  • Unlinkability means two actions by the same person cannot be connected. It is often the property that matters, because a pseudonym used everywhere is a name.
  • Surveillance is the systematic observation of people's activities, communications or movements. Privacy-preserving surveillance is detection of specified events without routine exposure of the identities of the people observed.

3.2 Privacy as being let alone, and as control

The oldest account treats privacy as a zone of seclusion, the right to be let alone that Warren and Brandeis named8. It explains intuitions about homes and bodies well and intuitions about data poorly, because most data is created in public or in transactions with others. Westin's account, privacy as control over information about oneself11, fitted the databank era and underwrites the Fair Information Practices and their descendants, including consent and access rights in the GDPR12,13,38. Its weakness is that individual control does not scale: nobody can meaningfully consent to every flow of data in a modern economy, and much of what matters is inferred rather than disclosed.

3.3 Contextual integrity

Helen Nissenbaum's theory of contextual integrity replaces the public/private dichotomy with a question about flows: information is shared under the norms of a context (medicine, friendship, commerce, policing), and a privacy violation is a flow that breaks those norms64. A diagnosis passing from patient to doctor is appropriate; the same diagnosis passing to an employer is not. The theory explains why aggregation of individually public facts can be a harm, and it fits engineering well, because a flow has a sender, a recipient, a type of information and a transmission principle that a system can enforce. The seal ladder in §6 is, in effect, a classification of transmission principles by how they are enforced.

3.4 A taxonomy of harms

Daniel Solove argued that privacy is not one thing but a family of related problems, and organised them into information collection (surveillance, interrogation), information processing (aggregation, identification, insecurity, secondary use, exclusion), information dissemination (breach of confidentiality, disclosure, exposure) and invasion65. The taxonomy matters for surveillance design because it separates harms that occur at collection from harms that occur at use. A Model C system can eliminate some (identification, disclosure) while leaving others (collection, aggregation) fully in place. Solove's companion essay dismantles the "nothing to hide" argument by showing that it assumes privacy is only about concealing wrongdoing, whereas most of the harms in the taxonomy fall on people who have done nothing wrong66.

3.5 Privacy as a structural condition

A third school treats privacy less as an individual interest than as a condition for a free society. Julie Cohen argues that privacy shelters the processes by which people develop as selves and form views not yet ready for scrutiny67; Neil Richards that surveillance chills intellectual activity and shifts the balance of power between watcher and watched, so that its danger does not depend on any particular misuse68. On this view the harm of mass surveillance is not mainly the exposure of secrets but the existence of a capability that changes how people behave, which is Bentham's insight inverted into a warning.

3.6 Surveillance studies

Sociologists have described surveillance as a feature of modern organisation rather than a deviation from it. David Lyon's surveillance society is one in which everyday life is routinely monitored by institutions for administration, commerce and security69. Haggerty and Ericson's "surveillant assemblage" describes how data from many separate systems is abstracted into data doubles and reassembled wherever it is useful, so that no single panoptic tower is needed70. Shoshana Zuboff's account of surveillance capitalism argues that the dominant business model of the commercial internet extracts behavioural data to predict and modify behaviour71. For this article the important point is the assemblage: the threat to privacy is less often one system that sees everything than many systems whose outputs can be joined. Plate D06, governments buying commercially available data, is the assemblage in action2.

3.7 Privacy by design and the engineering tradition

Ann Cavoukian's seven principles of privacy by design, among them proactive not reactive, privacy as the default setting, and full functionality as positive-sum not zero-sum72, became the slogan of the engineering school and, through Article 25 of the GDPR, a legal duty38. The cryptographic tradition from Chaum onwards is the stronger version of the same idea: it asks not for good defaults but for systems in which the undesired flow is impossible18. Phillip Rogaway's 2015 essay argues that this is a moral choice for cryptographers, because their work either increases or decreases the power of those who watch73.

3.8 Two framings of the trade-off

Public debate tends to use one of two framings. The balancing framing treats privacy and security as quantities on a seesaw and asks where to set the fulcrum; it underlies proportionality review in European human-rights law39 and most legislative compromises. The architectural framing denies that the seesaw is fixed and asks whether systems can be built that give investigators what they legitimately need while denying them what they do not. Model C belongs to the second framing. The rest of this article tests how far the architecture can go before it runs back into the balancing question.

§4 Why removing names is not a seal

Every surveillance design that promises not to identify people must decide what it means by identity. The cheapest answer is to delete names, addresses and account numbers and keep everything else. Thirty years of research say that this answer fails.

4.1 The classic results

Latanya Sweeney showed in 2000 that 87% of the US population was likely to be uniquely identified by five-digit ZIP code, date of birth and sex74; Philippe Golle's re-analysis with the 2000 census put the figure at 63%, still a majority75. Sweeney's proposed defence, k-anonymity, requires every combination of quasi-identifiers to be shared by at least k records76. Narayanan and Shmatikov then showed that sparse, high-dimensional data such as film ratings could be re-identified with very little auxiliary knowledge, de-anonymising subscribers in the Netflix Prize dataset77. Paul Ohm drew the legal conclusion: anonymisation had failed as a regulatory category, because data can be either useful or perfectly anonymous but rarely both78.

Behavioural traces are worse. De Montjoye and colleagues found that four spatio-temporal points were enough to uniquely identify 95% of individuals in a mobility dataset of 1.5 million people, and that coarsening the data's resolution reduced uniqueness only slowly79; the same group showed comparable results for credit-card metadata80. Rocher, Hendrickx and de Montjoye estimated in 2019 that 99.98% of Americans would be correctly re-identified in any dataset using 15 demographic attributes, even a heavily sampled one81. And metadata reveals content: Mayer, Mutchler and Mitchell showed that telephone metadata alone allowed sensitive inferences about health, religion and relationships for volunteer participants82.

4.2 Two experiments

Experiments E1 and E2 in the laboratory (Appendix A) reproduce the shape of these results on synthetic data, so that the mechanism is visible rather than taken on trust.

E1 builds a city of 100,000 residents spread unevenly across 40 postal districts, with realistic birth-year weights. ZIP code, birth year and sex together leave only 0.05% of residents unique. Replace birth year with full date of birth and 94.29% become unique. The defence that people instinctively reach for, coarsening the location, barely helps: collapsing the forty districts into four leaves 62.87% unique, because the date of birth carries most of the information. Coarsening the date is what works, and it works because it destroys the attribute that does the identifying.

E2 builds weekly mobility traces for 20,000 people over 400 antenna cells and 168 hourly slots, with homes, workplaces and a few favourite places each. One observed point identifies almost no one. Two points identify 76.60%; four points identify 99.25%. Coarsening both space and time by a factor of four, which would make the data much less useful, still leaves 84.30% unique at four points and 93.50% at five.

4.3 What this means for Model C

The consequence for privacy-preserving surveillance is direct. A system that stores pseudonymised records of where people went, replacing names with stable tokens, has not stopped anyone from identifying them; it has made identification require one join with another dataset. Such a store sits at rung S0 or S1 on the ladder defined in §6, however careful its access policy, because whoever can read it can re-identify most of it. The European courts' relative approach to pseudonymisation, which asks whether a particular recipient has means reasonably likely to re-identify63, is coherent law, but it is not an engineering guarantee: the answer changes whenever the recipient buys another dataset2.

The design rule that follows: in a Model C system, the sealed thing must be the link between an event and a person, protected cryptographically, not the person's name in a record that still contains their trajectory. Pseudonymisation protects against casual browsing. It does not seal.

§5 The privacy-enhancing toolkit

Privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs) are mechanisms that let information be processed, verified or shared while limiting exposure of the underlying personal data. The OECD, the UK Information Commissioner and NIST all maintain overviews83–85. The table below sets out the main families by what each protects and, just as important, what each leaves exposed. The guarantees are different in kind: some protect inputs, some protect outputs, some protect against the operator and some only against outsiders, and they compose rather than substitute.

5.1 Three families of guarantee

It helps to sort these tools by where the guarantee comes from, because that determines what can break it.

Guarantees from computational hardness. Encryption, zero-knowledge proofs, secure multiparty computation, homomorphic encryption, private set intersection and private information retrieval all rest on the assumption that certain mathematical problems are infeasible to solve21,22,86–88. They fail if the assumption fails, if the implementation is wrong, or if keys leak. For long-lived sealed records, the relevant threat is harvest now, decrypt later: a sealed identity encrypted today with classical public-key cryptography may be opened by a future quantum computer, which is why NIST standardised the first post-quantum key-encapsulation mechanism in 2024100.

Guarantees from statistics. Differential privacy bounds how much any one person's data can change a published result: for a mechanism with parameter ε, the probability of any output changes by at most a factor of eε when one person is added or removed89,101. The bound holds against an attacker with unlimited side information, which is what distinguishes it from de-identification. It fails only through bad parameter choices or implementation errors, and NIST's 2025 guidelines exist largely to help organisations evaluate those choices90.

Guarantees from hardware. Trusted execution environments isolate computation inside processor enclaves whose memory the operator cannot read, and remote attestation lets a client verify which software is running. These guarantees rest on the hardware vendor and on physical security. They have been broken repeatedly, from the Foreshadow transient-execution attack on SGX in 2018102 to TEE.fail in 2025, which extracted attestation keys from Intel TDX and AMD SEV-SNP machines using a DDR5 bus interposer that cost under $1,000 to build94.

5.2 The metadata problem

Encryption protects content. It does not, by itself, hide who communicated with whom, when, from where and how often, and that metadata is often as revealing as content82. Hiding it requires a different class of tool: mix networks17, onion routing98, or designs that avoid central servers altogether, as decentralised exposure notification did45. Any Model C system that protects the content of observations but sends identifiable metadata to a central point has not protected the observed population.

5.3 Tools that already encode Model C

Several primitives already contain the shape of targeted identification on demand. Group signatures have an opening authority24. Identity escrow lets a user prove membership anonymously while depositing an encrypted identity that a designated agency can open103. Camenisch and Lysyanskaya's anonymous credentials offer "optional anonymity revocation" by a separate revocation manager95. Intersection warrants use private set intersection so that investigators learn only the phones present at every one of several crime scenes104,105. And accountable-process designs use commitments, zero-knowledge proofs and multiparty computation to let the public verify that secret surveillance orders were followed, without revealing what they were106. Each of these appears as a plate in §7, coded by who actually holds the power to open.

§6 Three models and a ladder

6.1 Three models

The material behind this article distinguishes three models of a monitoring system, and the distinction is useful enough to keep.

Model A: surveillance by default. Collect extensively, identify individuals, store centrally, let authorised operators search. Privacy depends on access policies and on the restraint of whoever holds database access.

Model B: privacy by default. Minimise collection, process locally, prefer aggregates, avoid identification, delete when no longer needed. There is no exceptional access because there is nothing to access.

Model C: privacy by default, targeted identification when authorised. Monitor narrowly defined events; keep ordinary people anonymous; preserve evidence for legitimate investigations; reveal an identity only with independently verifiable authorisation; audit every exceptional disclosure.

The models differ in where identity lives. In A it is in the database next to the activity. In B it never leaves the person's device. In C it exists but is sealed away from the activity record, and the system's whole value lies in the rules and mechanisms that govern unsealing. That is why Model C cannot be assessed by asking whether it encrypts data. It must be assessed by asking what it takes to unseal.

6.2 The seal ladder

To make "what it takes to look" comparable across very different systems, this article codes every mechanism on a six-rung ladder. Each rung is defined by the strongest thing that stands between the party who wants to look and an identity: not what the system's designers intend, but what actually prevents the look.

Two further fields complete the code. Granularity records what one authorised look reveals, from nothing (N: a proof, a bit, or nothing) through an aggregate (A), one target's records (T), a target plus bystanders swept in with them (B), to anyone in the collection (P). Trace records who would know a look happened: outsiders can verify it (V), only insiders or cleared overseers can see a record (L), or no one outside the looking party would know (X).

The ladder deliberately ignores intentions, legal names and marketing. A system that encrypts data but lets its operator decrypt at will is S1, not S5, because the only thing stopping a look is the operator's own policy. A warrant requirement is S2 however strict, because the carrier's switch will tap a line without one if instructed (plate D01). A threshold scheme is S4 only if the keyholders are independent of the looking party, and the meaning of "independent" turns out to be the hardest question in the article (§9.3).

6.3 How to read a plate

Each of the thirty plates in §7 describes one mechanism under five fixed headings: who can look, what looking requires, what one look reveals, who would know, and where it fails. Each carries a gauge, the unsealing strip, that encodes the three fields at a glance: the rung (six cells, the occupied one filled in its rung colour), the granularity (a row of twelve figures, filled according to how many people one look exposes, with aggregates shown as a single hatched block and "nothing" as an empty row with a one-bit tick), and the trace (an eye that is solid for verifiable, dashed for logged and struck through for unseen).

§7 Thirty mechanisms, coded

The thirty plates below are grouped into four families according to where each mechanism's guarantee lives: in a rule (A), a key (B), a published statistic (C) or the physical location of the data (D). Plates are coded as each mechanism is actually specified or operated, not as its advocates describe it. Where a mechanism has changed over time, the plate codes the version named in its title. The ledger after §8 collects every code in one table.

§8 What the census shows

Coded together, the thirty plates occupy 14 of the 30 cells in the grid formed by the six rungs and five granularities. The occupied cells are not scattered. They form a diagonal band running from the top left (open systems that expose everyone) to the bottom right (sealed systems that expose nothing), with a Spearman rank correlation of −0.765 between rung and exposure.

Four observations follow, each computed by experiment E0 from the plate codes.

Every open system exposes everyone. All four plates at S0 (the Stasi archive, de-identified releases, city-wide face recognition and purchased commercial data) reveal the whole population to whoever looks. There is no such thing as an open system with narrow exposure, because nothing defines the edge of the exposure.

Sealed systems almost never identify anyone. Of the eight plates at S5, four reveal nothing and three reveal only aggregates. The single exception is plate B06, Apple's threshold matching against a hash list, and it was withdrawn before launch.

Targeted identification lives on permission. Twelve plates reveal a specific target's records. Seven of them sit at S1 or S2, where the looking party could look without the safeguard and is restrained by its own rules or by an outside authority's permission: the particularised warrant, third-party subpoenas, Section 702 queries, the UK's double lock, the AI Act's authorised biometric search, the lawful-intercept interface and the group-signature opener. Six of the seven (A02, A03, A06, A07, A08, D01) have operated at scale.

Model C's cell is empty in practice. The other five target-revealing plates put identification behind a witnessed, shared or sealed mechanism: Clipper escrow, revocable anonymous credentials, intersection warrants, threshold hash matching and the accountable ledger of secret orders (B02, B04, B05, B06, B07). The number of those that have ever operated at scale is zero. Two were abandoned, three remain research.

The same pattern appears in the trace column. Only four plates make looks verifiable by outsiders, and the two that do so at scale (C02, D05) are the 2020 Census, which reveals aggregates, and Apple's attested confidential cloud, which retains nothing. No deployed system makes the identification of a target publicly verifiable.

The census therefore relocates the feasibility question. Nobody doubts that sealed systems can be built; there are eight of them here, most in daily use. Nobody doubts that targeted identification can be authorised; there are seven ways of doing it here, most in daily use. What has never been done at scale is to combine them: to make the targeted look itself depend on a technical seal held by parties independent of the looker, and visible to the public afterwards.

That gap could have two explanations. Either the combination is technically harder than either half, or it is institutionally unwelcome to the parties who would have to adopt it. Section 9 tests the technical half.

§9 The arithmetic of looking

The laboratory in Appendix A contains nine experiments, written in standard-library Python with a fixed seed and executed at build time. Its output is reproduced verbatim in the appendix and is byte-identical under different hash seeds. Experiments E1 and E2 appeared in §4. This section reports the rest, in the order a Model C system would meet the problems: detecting an event, publishing about the population, holding the key, narrowing the target, bounding the reach of a query, and recording the disclosure.

9.1 Detection: why a good detector mostly flags the innocent

Model C begins with automated detection of "potential incidents", and every detector has a false-positive rate. Experiment E3 takes a monitored population of 10,000,000 containing 1,000 genuine targets, one in 10,000, and a detector that catches 99% of them. At 99.9% specificity the detector raises 9,999 false flags and 990 true ones: only 9.01% of the people it flags are targets. Specificity must reach 99.99% before half of the flags are correct, and 99.999% before nine in ten are.

This is the arithmetic behind the National Research Council's 2008 conclusion that automatically identifying terrorists by mining data was not a feasible objective and should not be a goal of technology development115, and behind the wrongful arrests that face recognition has produced where a match was treated as an identification116,117. In a Model C system it has a sharper meaning: every false positive is an unsealing request about an innocent person. If the system unseals on detection, the privacy of the population is set by the detector's specificity, not by the cryptography.

There is one powerful remedy, and E3's second half measures it. If a disclosure requires many independent matches rather than one, the false-positive rate per account falls as a power of the threshold. With a per-item false-match rate of one in a million and ten thousand items per account, a threshold of one would flag about 9.95 million innocent accounts in a billion; a threshold of three, about 165; a threshold of five, fewer than one in a thousand billion-account populations. That is why Apple's 2021 design required thirty matches48. But the calculation assumes that errors are independent. An adversary who can craft collisions breaks that assumption: if one account in a million is seeded with forty crafted matches, a thirty-match threshold flags 1,000 innocent accounts per billion regardless of how high the threshold is set. Critics of client-side scanning made exactly this argument49.

9.2 Publication: what ε promises, and what noise prevents

Model C promises population-level statistics without individual exposure, which is the job of differential privacy. Experiment E4 makes the parameter concrete. Under ε-differential privacy, an attacker's odds that a given person is in the data can rise by at most a factor of eε after seeing any output101. Starting from a 1% prior, ε = 1 caps the posterior at 2.67%; ε = 4.5 at 47.62%; ε = 12.2, the global budget of the Census Bureau's April 2021 demonstration data, at 99.95%. At the production value of 17.14 for the 2020 persons file111, the worst-case odds ratio is about 28 million to one, and the formal bound no longer constrains a single inference. That is not evidence the Census leaked anything, because the bound is a worst case; the National Academies note that the protection actually realised at such values is hard to know112. It is evidence that ε is a policy dial, and that the dial should be published.

The second half of E4 shows why the dial cannot simply be turned to zero noise. Dinur and Nissim proved in 2003 that answering too many queries too accurately allows an attacker to reconstruct the underlying data118, the reconstruction threat the Census Bureau cites in explaining its move away from older disclosure-avoidance methods119. E4 hides 48 private bits behind 384 random subset-sum queries and attacks them with least squares. With answers perturbed by up to ±2, every bit is recovered. At ±4 the attacker still recovers 98.02%; noise has to grow past the square root of the database size (6.93 here) before reconstruction degrades, to 83.65% at ±8 and 57.29% at ±32, where guessing would give 50%. Aggregates are not automatically safe. A Model C system that publishes many accurate statistics about a small population has, in effect, published the population.

9.3 Custody: independence is the whole product

Model C's central promise is that no single party can unseal an identity. Threshold secret sharing delivers that promise mathematically: split a key into n shares so that any k reconstruct it and any k − 1 reveal nothing19. E5 implements Shamir's scheme over the prime 2127 − 1 and confirms both halves: any three of five shares reconstruct the sealed record's key, and two shares produce an unrelated value.

The interesting question is what the threshold buys. Suppose each keyholder is independently corruptible, coercible or careless with probability 5%, and unavailable when needed with probability 2%. A single keyholder gives a 5% chance of abuse. Two-of-two cuts it to 0.25%; three-of-five to 0.12%; five-of-nine to 0.0033%; seven-of-nine to about 2.6 in 100 million, at the cost of blocking a lawful disclosure 0.0614% of the time. Thresholds are extraordinarily effective, provided the keyholders fail independently.

Now add a single common-mode failure: some authority, with probability 1%, can compel every keyholder at once, because they are all agencies of one government, or all bound by one secret statute, or all within reach of one court order. The probability of abuse then never falls below 1%, whatever the threshold. Seven-of-nine with a 1% common authority is worse than two-of-two without one. Clipper's two escrow agents both answered to the US executive4, and Section 702's certification, collection and querying all sit within one executive branch under one court29. The mathematics of the threshold is not where Model C succeeds or fails. The independence of the keyholders is, and it is a constitutional property, not a cryptographic one.

9.4 Narrowing: the intersection warrant

The clearest demonstration that targeted identification under a seal can work is the intersection warrant. Investigators in a series of robberies want the phones present at every scene; the traditional method is to obtain full tower dumps from each scene and intersect them in plaintext, which hands every bystander's identity to the police. Segal, Ford and Feigenbaum proposed doing the intersection under encryption, so that only common elements are ever revealed104,105.

E6 simulates three tower dumps of about 5,000 devices each, drawn from a population of 200,000, with two bandits present at all three. The plaintext method discloses 14,629 identities. A private set intersection using commutative encryption, which costs 45,018 modular exponentiations, discloses four. That is a reduction of more than three orders of magnitude, and it is exactly the kind of result that makes Model C attractive.

But look at the four. Two are the bandits and two are innocent people who happened to pass all three sites, against an analytic expectation of 3.13 at these dump sizes. The cryptography revealed only the intersection, and the intersection contained bystanders. Relax the predicate to two of three sites, which an investigator might reasonably want if one bandit's phone was off, and the bystander count rises to 369. The seal is exact; the predicate is not. What a Model C system reveals is determined by the rule it evaluates, and the rule is chosen by people who want results.

9.5 Reach: how far a targeted query goes

Some "targeted" operations are expansive by construction. Contact chaining, the method of the Section 215 programme, starts from a seed number and retrieves numbers in contact with it, then numbers in contact with those28. E8 builds a 100,000-subscriber network with the heavy-tailed contact distribution typical of communication graphs (mean 16.0 contacts, maximum 1,334) and chains from sixty random seeds. The median seed reaches 542 people within two hops and 19,593 within three: 19.59% of the entire network, almost five times the 4,095 that naive multiplication of the mean degree predicts, because chains run through hubs. Refusing to expand through numbers with more than 200 contacts, the kind of rule an analyst might adopt to skip switchboards, call centres and voicemail systems, cuts three-hop reach to 7,208 (7.21%), still thousands of people per seed.

Place-and-time warrants scale in a different way. E9 takes the funnel reported in Chatrie: 19 anonymised devices in a 150-metre, one-hour geofence, nine expanded, three identified, one charged44. Under a simple model in which the number of devices grows with the area and the time window, the same procedure in a dense urban centre at 300 metres would return about 1,140 devices, and at 500 metres over two hours about 5,542. Granularity B is not a fixed property of the geofence; it is a function of radius, window and density that the warrant's author controls. Segal and colleagues showed that privacy-preserving contact chaining is practical105; what the arithmetic shows is that a privacy-preserving three-hop chain still reaches a fifth of the network.

9.6 Record: making the look visible

The last requirement of Model C is that every exceptional disclosure be audited. The best existing pattern is the append-only Merkle log, standardised for certificate transparency in RFC 696299 and used by Apple to publish every production build of its confidential cloud93. E7 builds such a log over 1,000 disclosure records, each naming an order, an authority and a scope. An inclusion proof for any record is ten hashes; a log of a billion disclosures needs at most 30. When record 400's scope is silently widened from a target to a population, the root changes and the old proof fails.

This is cheap, mature and directly applicable, and the census in §8 found no deployed surveillance system that uses it for disclosures of identity. Frankle and colleagues showed in 2018 that commitments and zero-knowledge proofs of this kind can operate at the scale of the US federal courts106. A log proves that a record exists and has not been altered. It cannot prove that a look was not made outside the log, which is why it must be paired with a seal: a witnessed system (S3) is only as strong as the guarantee that the operator cannot read without writing.

§10 Is Model C feasible?

10.1 Requirement by requirement

Model C, as specified in §6.1, decomposes into eight requirements. The table assesses each against the evidence in §4–§9, on a four-level scale: mature (deployed at scale, with known failure modes), demonstrated (deployed somewhere or shown practical at realistic scale), prototype (works in research systems) and open (no known solution).

Five of the eight requirements are mature or demonstrated. The two that are open, choosing the predicate and making keyholders independent, are not engineering problems in the usual sense, and the one at prototype stage, verifiable disclosure, is held back less by its technology than by the absence of anyone obliged to adopt it.

10.2 Where Model C works now

Model C is feasible today, with existing technology, when four conditions hold together. The predicate is narrow, fixed and checkable (over eighteen, present at all three scenes, in possession of a listed credential). The disclosure is a bit or a short list, not a trajectory. The keyholders are institutionally independent of the party that wants the answer, and preferably of one another. And the population of plausible matches is small enough that the base rate does not swamp the result.

Several real systems meet all four. Zero-knowledge attribute proofs meet them trivially, because the disclosure is one bit and there is no unsealing at all61,107. Decentralised exposure notification met them by never creating a central record to unseal, and still delivered measurable public-health benefit45,46,120. Secure multiparty computation across institutions meets them whenever the agreed function is an aggregate109,110. And the intersection warrant meets them by construction, as E6 shows, with the caveat that its predicate must be set strictly enough that coincidence does not supply the answer. These are Model B with a narrow, pre-agreed window, and they are the realistic near-term form of Model C.

10.3 Where Model C is not yet feasible, and why

Model C fails as a general architecture for monitoring a population for unspecified future threats, for three reasons the evidence makes concrete.

The predicate problem. A technical seal is only as accountable as the rule that opens it. In Apple's design the seal was mathematically excellent and the predicate, a list of hashes the user could not inspect, was the point of attack49. In the intersection warrant, the predicate determines whether the answer contains two people or three hundred and seventy. Detection-triggered unsealing inherits the base-rate arithmetic of §9.1. No cryptographic technique can make the choice of predicate legitimate; at most it can make the predicate public and its use countable.

The keyholder problem. Threshold schemes turn institutional independence into mathematical protection, but they cannot create the independence. E5 shows that a single common authority sets a floor under abuse that no threshold removes. Most legal systems give one sovereign the power to compel every domestic keyholder, and most surveillance statutes are written by the government that does the surveilling. Spreading keys across jurisdictions, or across branches with separately guaranteed tenure, is the only known way to lower the floor, and it is a constitutional design problem.

The retention problem. Investigators value surveillance most for questions they did not know to ask in advance: who was near this place last month, who called this number last year. Answering such questions requires retaining sealed records about everyone, and a sealed archive of everyone is a panopticon whose lock is political. The Court of Justice struck down general and indiscriminate retention in 2014 precisely because of what such an archive makes possible34. Model C can make that archive harder to open. It cannot make it not exist, and over the decades such records must stay sealed, harvest-now-decrypt-later attacks and changes of government are both live risks100.

10.4 The bypasses

Even a well-built Model C system governs only the data it holds. Three bypasses remain outside it. The first is purchase: intelligence agencies have acquired commercially available location and browsing data that they treated as publicly available, so the warrant requirements of Carpenter and Chatrie never applied2,43,44. The second is metadata: a system that seals content but routes identifiable traffic through a central point has protected less than it appears to82. The third is the endpoint: a sealed record is plaintext on the device that created it, and client-side scanning, Abelson and colleagues argue, is bulk interception in a distributed form49. A society that wants Model C's guarantees must close all three, which is a matter of procurement law, network architecture and device security rather than of the surveillance system itself.

10.5 What a credible Model C deployment would need

Taking the technical and institutional findings together, a Model C system would be credible only if it satisfied at least the following. These are design conclusions drawn from the evidence above, not a description of any existing system.

  • Statutory exclusivity. The sealed system must be the only lawful route to the data it covers, with purchases and side channels prohibited, or it merely adds a polite door to a building with open windows.
  • Independent, plural keyholders. At least one keyholder outside the executive, ideally one outside the jurisdiction, and a threshold set so that a lawful disclosure is rarely blocked (E5).
  • A public predicate register. Every class of predicate that can trigger unsealing published in advance, with its expected false-positive rate at realistic base rates (E3, E6).
  • A verifiable disclosure log. Every unsealing committed to an append-only log with public counts, and delayed publication of details when investigations close (E7)99,106.
  • Bounded reach. Hop limits, radius and window limits written into the protocol rather than the policy (E8, E9).
  • Hard retention limits. Cryptographic erasure of sealed records on a fixed schedule, so that retrospective search has a horizon.
  • Notification and remedy. Notice to people whose identities were unsealed in error, with a forum that can award relief, the element Strasbourg's end-to-end safeguards treat as indispensable39.

10.6 Verdict

Model C is feasible as a family of narrow, predicate-specific systems, several of which already exist. It is not yet feasible as a general architecture for population monitoring, and the obstacles are not where the debate usually places them. The cryptography is ready: thresholds, private set intersection, zero-knowledge proofs and transparency logs all work at realistic scale, as the laboratory shows. What is not ready is the institutional structure that would make those tools mean what Model C promises: a legitimate way to choose predicates, keyholders who are genuinely independent of the government that wants the answer, and retention limits that survive a change of government. Building Model C is therefore less a matter of inventing new cryptography than of being willing to deploy existing cryptography in a way that binds the deployer.

§11 Objections from both directions

11.1 From the security side

"Narrow predicates cannot catch unknown threats." Investigators argue that the most valuable intelligence comes from patterns no one anticipated, which a predicate register would forbid. This is a serious objection, and the census supports half of it: none of the sealed systems coded here supports open-ended retrospective search. The counter-argument is the base-rate arithmetic of §9.1 and the PCLOB's finding that bulk telephony collection made no concrete difference to any counter-terrorism investigation it reviewed28; open-ended search of a population yields mostly innocent hits.

"Going dark is real." Law-enforcement agencies argue that end-to-end encryption puts evidence beyond reach even with a valid warrant. That is accurate as a description of plate B01. The response of the security research community has been that every proposal for exceptional access so far adds systemic risk that outweighs its investigative value4,26, and Salt Typhoon's compromise of lawful-intercept systems has made that argument empirical rather than hypothetical51,52.

"Courts already provide independent authorisation." In European human-rights law, bulk interception with end-to-end safeguards is lawful39, and many judges and officials regard warrant processes as adequate. The census does not dispute that S2 can work; it records that S2 is enforced after the fact, and that the systems behind it can be reached by insiders and intruders who never see a judge.

11.2 From the civil-liberties side

"Model C legitimises mass collection." A sealed archive of everyone is still an archive of everyone, and critics argue that offering a cryptographic lock makes collection politically easier to approve, after which the lock can be changed by statute. This is the retention problem of §10.3, and the history of function creep supports it: systems built for one purpose acquire others1.

"Any exceptional access is a backdoor." The strongest form of this view holds that the only safe number of people who can unseal is zero, and that Model B, not Model C, is the right goal. The census is consistent with it: the systems that have preserved privacy at scale are almost all Model B.

"Cryptography cannot fix power." Rogaway's argument that cryptography is political work73 cuts both ways. Tools that constrain the watcher can be built, but the watcher decides whether to adopt them, and the empty cell in §8 is consistent with that reading.

11.3 Where the disagreement actually lies

Stripped down, the two sides disagree less about technology than about the probability of the common-mode failure in E5: how likely is it that the authorities who hold the keys will, at some point over the life of the archive, be compelled or inclined to open more than they should? Security officials tend to treat that probability as small and manageable by oversight; civil-liberties advocates tend to treat it as near-certain over long horizons. The mathematics shows that this one number dominates every other design choice, which is a reason to argue about it explicitly.

§12 Conclusion

Privacy is not dead, but it has moved. It no longer lives mainly in what is not recorded, because almost everything is. It lives in what it takes to turn a record into a person: in the rung of the ladder between the record and whoever wants to read it, in how much one look reveals, and in whether anyone else would know.

Measured that way, the past half-century shows real progress at both ends of the ladder. The open archive has become legally indefensible in most democracies, and sealed systems that reveal nothing or only aggregates are now in daily use at population scale: end-to-end encryption, differentially private censuses, decentralised exposure notification, zero-knowledge proofs of age. What has not moved is the middle. Targeted identification, the thing investigators need most, still depends almost entirely on permission and policy, rungs S1 and S2, enforced after the fact and exposed to every insider and intruder who can reach the interface.

Model C proposes to close that gap, and its technical components are ready. Its institutional components are not. A society that wants privacy by default with targeted identification when authorised must decide in public what may trigger an unsealing, give the keys to parties who can actually refuse, and accept limits on how long the sealed archive may exist. Those are decisions about the distribution of power, and cryptography can only enforce them once they have been made.

So the useful question for the next decade is not whether privacy is dead. It is whether those who hold surveillance power will accept systems in which the act of looking costs something, requires someone else's key, and leaves a mark that the public can see.

def poisson_tail(lam, t):

def solve(A, b):
    n = len(A)
    M = [row[:] + [b[i]] for i, row in enumerate(A)]
    for c in range(n):
        piv = max(range(c, n), key=lambda r: abs(M[r][c]))
        M[c], M[piv] = M[piv], M[c]
        pv = M[c][c]
        if abs(pv) < 1e-12:
            continue
        for r in range(n):
            if r != c:
                f = M[r][c] / pv
                if f:
                    Mr, Mc = M[r], M[c]
                    for k in range(c, n + 1):
                        Mr[k] -= f * Mc[k]
    return [M[i][n] / M[i][i] if abs(M[i][i]) > 1e-12 else 0.5 for i in range(n)]

def shamir_split(secret, k, n, rng):
    coeffs = [secret] + [rng.randrange(P127) for _ in range(k - 1)]
    return [(x, sum(c * pow(x, e, P127) for e, c in enumerate(coeffs)) % P127) for x in range(1, n + 1)]

def shamir_join(shares):
    s = 0
    for i, (xi, yi) in enumerate(shares):
        num, den = 1, 1
        for j, (xj, _) in enumerate(shares):
            if i != j:
                num = num * (-xj) % P127; den = den * (xi - xj) % P127
        s = (s + yi * num * pow(den, P127 - 2, P127)) % P127
    return s

def leaf_hash(b): return hashlib.sha256(b"\x00" + b).digest()
def node_hash(a, b): return hashlib.sha256(b"\x01" + a + b).digest()

def mth(leaves):
    n = len(leaves)
    if n == 1:
        return leaf_hash(leaves[0])
    k = 1
    while k * 2 < n:
        k *= 2
    return node_hash(mth(leaves[:k]), mth(leaves[k:]))

def audit_path(m, leaves):
    n = len(leaves)
    if n == 1:
        return []
    k = 1
    while k * 2 < n:
        k *= 2
    if m < k:
        return audit_path(m, leaves[:k]) + [("R", mth(leaves[k:]))]
    return audit_path(m - k, leaves[k:]) + [("L", mth(leaves[:k]))]

def verify(leaf, path, root):
    h = leaf_hash(leaf)
    for side, sib in path:
        h = node_hash(h, sib) if side == "R" else node_hash(sib, h)
    return h == root
