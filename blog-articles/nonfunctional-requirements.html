01 The half of the specification that decides the outcome

Ask an engineer what a system is required to do and you will get a list of behaviours. Ask what it is required to be and the answers arrive as adjectives: fast, secure, maintainable, scalable, reliable. The first list gets acceptance tests. The second gets a paragraph in a document nobody opens again.

The imbalance is not a matter of taste. It is visible in the international standard that most organisations nominally follow. ISO/IEC 25010:2023 defines a product quality model composed of nine characteristics, each decomposed into subcharacteristics, intended as a reference vocabulary for specifying, measuring and evaluating a product.1 Exactly one of those nine — functional suitability — covers what most teams mean when they say “the requirements.” The other eight describe everything else that determines whether the software is worth owning.

The 2023 revision is worth dwelling on, because the changes read like a summary of what the last decade taught the industry. Safety was promoted to a top-level characteristic, with subcharacteristics for operational constraint, risk identification, fail-safe behaviour, hazard warning and safe integration. Usability was replaced by interaction capability and portability by flexibility. Inclusivity and self-descriptiveness were added under interaction capability, resistance under security, and scalability under flexibility. Quality-in-use was moved out into ISO/IEC 25019 and the overview of the model family into ISO/IEC 25002.1 Software grew up in public, and the standard followed.

The name “nonfunctional requirement” is itself a problem, and has been criticised as such for a long time: the term is used inconsistently, defined by negation, and applied to at least four incompatible kinds of thing — attributes, constraints, performance targets and design restrictions.2 The software architecture literature generally prefers quality attribute, which has the advantage of naming what is actually being specified.3 The label matters less than the consequence of the label: calling something “nonfunctional” invites the reading that it is optional, and a requirement that sounds optional will be traded away at the first schedule pressure.

Requirements engineering standards are unambiguous about the remedy. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 holds that a requirement must be verifiable — that it must be possible to demonstrate, by inspection, analysis, demonstration or test, that the delivered system satisfies it.4 For a functional requirement, verifiability is usually free: the behaviour either happens or it does not. For a quality attribute, verifiability has a precondition. There must be a number.

A functional defect announces itself. A quality-attribute defect accumulates silently, then arrives all at once, in public, at the worst possible moment.

There is an asymmetry in how the two kinds of failure surface, and it explains most of the organisational neglect. A missing feature is discovered by the first user who looks for it, in week one, and the argument about it is cheap. A missing quality attribute is discovered when a threshold is crossed — a load level, a codebase size, an attacker, a team handover — and by then the cost of the remedy has compounded for years. The three sections that follow put numbers on the compounding, then look at what it cost three organisations that did not.

02 The bill arrives later, with interest

The economic case for quality attributes rests on a claim that sounds like an excuse when made qualitatively and like a budget line when made quantitatively: neglected quality does not save work, it defers work at an unfavourable rate.

Ward Cunningham introduced the metaphor in 1992, describing shipping code that is not quite right as going into debt — acceptable if repaid promptly, ruinous if it is not.5 Three decades later the metaphor has an aggregate price tag. The Consortium for Information & Software Quality estimated the cost of poor software quality in the United States at approximately $2.41 trillion for 2022, with accumulated technical debt at roughly $1.52 trillion — a figure the report notes is close to the total spent on the entire US IT labour base that year.6 Estimates at that scale involve modelling assumptions worth arguing about; the order of magnitude is the part that survives the argument.

Manny Lehman explained why the debt accumulates by default. His laws of software evolution distinguish E-type programs — those embedded in a real-world environment — and hold that such a program must be continually adapted or it becomes progressively less satisfactory; that its complexity increases as it evolves unless work is done to reduce it; and that its quality will appear to decline unless it is rigorously maintained against a changing operational context.7 Decay is not a failure of discipline. It is the resting state. Quality attributes are the counterforce, and counterforces cost money.

The most useful recent measurement of what that money buys comes from a study of 39 proprietary production codebases covering 30,737 files, which classified code health independently of the delivery data and then joined it to issue trackers. Low-quality code contained 15 times more defects than high-quality code. Resolving an issue in low-quality code took, on average, 124% more time in development. And the maximum time-in-development — the worst case a planner has to survive — was 9 times longer.8

That third finding deserves more attention than the first two. Doubling the mean cost of a change is a budgeting problem. Multiplying the worst case by nine is a predictability problem, and predictability is what organisations actually buy when they buy engineering. A team whose estimates are wrong by a factor of nine at the tail cannot make commitments, which means it cannot be trusted with anything strategic, which means the work drifts elsewhere. Related survey work has put developer time lost to technical debt at up to 42%.9,8

None of this is an argument for gold-plating. It is an argument for pricing. The correct amount of investment in maintainability is not “as much as possible”; it is the amount at which the marginal hour spent on structure returns more than the marginal hour spent on features. You cannot find that point without measuring both sides of it.

03 Three failures that were nonfunctional all the way down

In each of the following, the software did what its functional specification said. The specification was simply silent about the property that mattered.

3.1 Knight Capital: forty-five minutes, $460 million

The Securities and Exchange Commission's order sets out the sequence plainly. In 2005 Knight moved a section of code within an automated equity router, which rendered a function of the router defective; the function was not meant to be used, and the code was left in place.10 In late July 2012, preparing for the New York Stock Exchange's Retail Liquidity Program, a technician deployed new code to seven of the firm's eight servers. No second technician reviewed the deployment, and no written procedure required that anyone do so.11

On the morning of 1 August 2012, orders routed to the eighth server triggered the dormant function. While processing 212 small retail customer orders, the system sent millions of orders into the market over a 45-minute period, obtaining over 4 million executions in 154 stocks for more than 397 million shares. An attempt to remediate by uninstalling the new code from the seven correctly configured servers made things worse, activating the dormant code there too. Knight lost over $460 million and paid a $12 million penalty for failing to maintain adequate risk-management controls; pre-market e-mail messages that described the fault were not designed to function as system alerts and were not reviewed before the market opened.11,10

Every functional requirement of that router was met. What was absent was a set of quality attributes nobody had written down with a number attached: a deployment process whose success is verifiable across all targets, a policy on the lifetime of unreachable code, an alerting requirement distinguishing logs from alarms, and an operability requirement binding order entry to a capital threshold. All four are trivially quantifiable. None was quantified.

3.2 Equifax: the inventory question nobody could answer

The Government Accountability Office's account of the 2017 breach reads as a chain of small maintainability failures that added up to a catastrophic security failure. A critical Apache Struts vulnerability was publicly disclosed with a patch available; a US-CERT notice reached the company, and the internal notice circulated on a recipient list that was out of date, so it did not reach the people responsible for the affected system. Scans intended to identify vulnerable systems did not identify this one. A device capable of inspecting network traffic was not functioning because a digital certificate it required had expired roughly ten months earlier, so encrypted traffic went uninspected throughout that period. Attackers queried 51 databases over a span of about 76 days, extracting records containing the personal information of at least 145.5 million US consumers, deliberately in small increments to avoid detection.12

The proximate cause is a security failure. The structural cause is that the organisation could not answer, quickly and reliably, the question where does this component run? — and could not detect that one of its own controls had silently stopped working. Both are maintainability and operability properties, and both have obvious response measures: time to produce a complete component-to-host inventory; percentage of security controls with an automated liveness check; certificate-expiry lead time. A software bill of materials requirement is now a matter of US federal procurement policy for precisely this reason.13,14

3.3 Ariane 501: the quantified requirement always wins

On 4 June 1996 the maiden flight of Ariane 5 ended about forty seconds after lift-off at an altitude of roughly 3,700 metres: both inertial reference systems failed almost simultaneously, the nozzles of the solid boosters and then the main engine swivelled to their extreme positions, the launcher veered, broke up, and the self-destruct sequence triggered correctly.15

The inquiry board traced it to an operand error during a conversion from a 64-bit floating-point value to a 16-bit signed integer — the horizontal bias variable, computed by an alignment function inherited from Ariane 4 that served no purpose at all after lift-off. Ariane 5's trajectory produced horizontal velocity values outside the range Ariane 4 had ever produced, and the conversion was not protected.16

Then comes the sentence that ought to be printed above every architecture review. The board recorded that not all conversions were protected because a maximum workload target of 80% had been set for the inertial reference computer; an analysis had identified seven variables at risk of an operand error, and protection was added to four of them.16

One requirement had a number — 80% CPU workload. Its competitor, robustness to out-of-range input, had only an adjective. The number won, three times out of seven.

This is the general case, not an aerospace curiosity. When a quantified requirement meets an unquantified one in a design meeting, the quantified one wins, every time, regardless of which actually matters more. The remedy is not to abolish performance budgets. It is to make sure the properties competing with them carry numbers of equal standing.

04 Three moves that turn an adjective into a requirement

The failure mode is grammatical before it is technical. Quality requirements arrive as adjectives, and an adjective cannot fail a test.

Move one: attach a scale and a meter. Tom Gilb's Planguage notation, developed for exactly this problem, requires that every quality requirement carry a Scale (the unit of measure), a Meter (the procedure that produces a value on that scale), and then a set of levels: Must (the failure threshold), Plan (the target), Wish (the ideal), and — the field most often skipped and most often decisive — Past, the measured current value.17 A team that cannot fill in Past does not yet have a meter, and a requirement without a meter is a preference.

Move two: write a six-part scenario. The Software Engineering Institute's formulation decomposes a quality attribute requirement into source, stimulus, artefact, environment, response and response measure.3,18 The response measure is the requirement; the other five parts exist to stop it being ambiguous. “p99 latency under 400 ms” means nothing until someone says at what request rate, on which component, with how much of the infrastructure already degraded.

Move three: derive the metric from the goal, not from the tool. The Goal– Question–Metric approach insists the chain runs downward: state a goal, derive the questions whose answers would tell you whether the goal is met, and only then choose metrics that answer those questions.19 Most industrial measurement programmes run the chain backwards — they start from whatever the tooling emits and search for a narrative. That is how organisations end up tracking line coverage, which nobody has a decision attached to, while not tracking time-to-restore, which everybody does.

There is a single practical test for whether the three moves have been made. Can a build fail on it? If the answer is no, what you have written is a value statement. Value statements are not worthless — they set direction — but they will lose every argument they enter, and they should not be mistaken for requirements.

05 Attribute by attribute: what to measure, and with what

What follows is a working reference. For each characteristic: the metrics that survive contact with practice, the ones that do not, and a specimen requirement written the way it should be written.

5.1 Maintainability

Maintainability is the attribute most often measured badly, because the structural properties of code are easy to count and the thing anyone actually cares about — the cost of the next change — is not.

The structural proxies are venerable. McCabe's cyclomatic complexity counts linearly independent paths through a control-flow graph and remains a reasonable local signal.20 Halstead volume, afferent and efferent coupling, depth of inheritance and duplication ratio all measure something real. The trouble begins when they are combined. The Maintainability Index — a polynomial in Halstead volume, cyclomatic complexity and lines of code — was fitted by regression against engineers' subjective ratings of a handful of Hewlett-Packard systems written in C and Pascal in the late 1980s, ranging from roughly 1,000 to 10,000 lines.21 It is still shipped in mainstream IDEs. Its problems are well documented: the coefficients were never recalibrated for modern languages or scales, averaging metrics per module destroys exactly the signal you want, and the composite is heavily confounded by size, so that a developer looking at lines of code alone would usually be better informed.22

The second family expresses maintainability as time, which at least has the right unit. The SQALE method converts static-analysis rule violations into estimated remediation effort and aggregates it into a debt figure.23 SonarQube's widely used implementation defines the technical debt ratio as remediation cost divided by the estimated cost of developing the code, where the latter defaults to 30 minutes per line; the maintainability rating grid then maps that ratio to a letter, with A at or below 5% and E above 50%.24 This is genuinely useful for communicating with people who think in budgets. It is also a model, not a measurement: the per-rule remediation estimates are conventions, and a debt figure computed from conventions should never be reported to three significant figures.

The third family measures outcomes, and is the one to anchor on. Time-in-development per file — the elapsed working time between a developer starting on an issue and the change landing — is a direct observation of what maintainability costs, and it is what the 39-codebase study above used.8 At the delivery level, the four DORA measures — deployment frequency, change lead time, change failure rate and time to restore service — have a decade of research behind them and describe throughput and stability together, which is the pairing that matters.25 26 Later revisions of that programme have moved failed-deployment recovery time into the throughput grouping and added a rework rate alongside change failure rate; the specific taxonomy matters less than the discipline of measuring speed and stability in the same breath.

Ranking by change frequency does the complementary job: the parts of a system that never change do not need to be maintainable, whatever their complexity score says.

5.2 Security

Security quantification has three distinct layers that are constantly confused with one another: how bad a flaw would be, how likely it is to be used, and how quickly you can make it go away.

The first layer is severity. CVSS version 4.0, published by FIRST in November 2023, organises scoring into four metric groups — Base, Threat, Environmental and Supplemental — and introduced explicit nomenclature (CVSS-B, CVSS-BT, CVSS-BE, CVSS-BTE) precisely to stop people quoting a base score as if it were a risk assessment.27,28 It is a severity scale. It was never a probability.

The second layer is exploitation likelihood, and this is where the numbers become operationally decisive. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System estimates the probability that a given vulnerability will be exploited in the wild in the next thirty days. Evaluated on real exploitation data, the contrast with severity-based triage is stark. Holding effort constant at roughly 15% of published CVEs, a strategy of remediating everything with a CVSS base score of 9.1 or above achieves 33.5% coverage of actually-exploited vulnerabilities at 6.1% efficiency; an EPSS v3-based strategy at the same effort achieves 90.4% coverage at 24.1% efficiency.29 Same budget. Roughly three times the coverage and four times the precision.

The vocabulary here is worth adopting wholesale, because it makes security tractable as an engineering trade-off rather than a moral one. Effort is what you spend. Coverage is the fraction of things that will hurt you which you addressed. Efficiency is the fraction of your spend that was not wasted. Any two can be fixed and the third measured.

The third layer is process, and it is the one an engineering organisation can actually control. Mean time to remediate, by severity band and by exploitation probability. Patch latency: the distribution of days between an upstream fix and its presence in production. Coverage of the software bill of materials, and the time required to answer “which of our deployed artefacts contain component X, at what version?” — the question Equifax could not answer. NIST's Secure Software Development Framework organises the practices into four groups (Prepare the Organization, Protect the Software, Produce Well-Secured Software, Respond to Vulnerabilities) and maps them to the clauses of Executive Order 14028, giving a ready-made conformance checklist that federal suppliers must now self-attest against.14,13 The OWASP Application Security Verification Standard supplies a comparable levelled requirement set for applications.30

There is a fourth kind of security metric, rarer and more powerful: eliminating a whole vulnerability class and measuring the decline. Android's move to memory-safe languages for new code, begun around 2019, is the best-documented instance. The proportion of Android vulnerabilities attributable to memory safety fell from 76% in 2019 to roughly 24% in 2024, with the absolute count dropping from 223 to fewer than 50 — achieved without rewriting the existing unsafe code, on the reasoning that vulnerabilities concentrate in new and recently modified code and that old code gets safer exponentially as it ages. The same programme reported that the rollback rate for Rust changes was less than half that for C++, which is a maintainability result arriving as a side effect of a security decision.31

5.3 Reliability and availability

Reliability is the attribute with the most mature measurement practice, largely because one organisation published its playbook. The Google site reliability engineering material sets out a chain of three ideas that has since become industry vocabulary: a service level indicator is a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of service; a service level objective is a target value for that indicator; and an error budget is what is left over.32

The arithmetic is deliberately trivial. An error budget is one minus the objective: a service with a 99.99% availability target has a 0.01% budget for unavailability.32 Expressed against a 99.9% objective over a four-week window and a million requests, the budget is a thousand failed requests.33 The purpose of the arithmetic is not precision. It is to convert an argument about values into an argument about a shared, depleting resource, so that the release-versus-stability negotiation has an agreed unit.

Two consequences follow that teams routinely miss. The first is that 100% is the wrong target for essentially everything, because no user can distinguish a system that is 100% available from one that is 99.999% available — their laptop, home network and ISP are collectively far less reliable than either.32 The second is that where you measure decides what you measure: moving Gmail's error and latency measurement from the server to the client substantially reduced Google's own assessment of its availability, and prompted changes on both sides of the wire.32 An SLI measured at the wrong boundary is a comfortable fiction.

The budget only bites if a policy is attached to it. The canonical form: while budget remains, the development team ships freely; when it is exhausted, releases stop except for fixes addressing the cause, until the budget recovers or the window resets.32,33 That is the entire mechanism. It works because it removes the need for anyone to have an opinion.

error_budget.pythe whole of availability arithmetic
from math import prod

MINUTES = {"day": 1_440, "week": 10_080, "month": 43_200, "year": 525_600}


def error_budget(slo: float, window: str = "month") -> float:
    """Minutes of unavailability an objective permits over a window."""
    return (1.0 - slo) * MINUTES[window]


def series(*parts: float) -> float:
    """Components that must ALL work. Availability multiplies -- and only ever falls."""
    return prod(parts)


def parallel(part: float, n: int) -> float:
    """n independent replicas, any one of which suffices."""
    return 1.0 - (1.0 - part) ** n


def burn_rate(bad_fraction: float, slo: float) -> float:
    """1.0 means the budget lasts exactly to the end of the window."""
    return bad_fraction / (1.0 - slo)


# A single dependency at "three and a half nines" looks harmless.
error_budget(0.9995)                       # -> 21.6 minutes / month

# Three of them in the same request path are not harmless.
chain = series(0.9995, 0.9999, 0.999)      # -> 0.998401 (barely 99.84%)
error_budget(chain)                        # -> 69.1 minutes / month

# Redundancy is the only lever that moves the number the other way.
parallel(0.995, 3)                         # -> 0.99999987

# Two per cent of a 99.9% budget spent in one hour = 14.4x burn. Page someone.
burn_rate(bad_fraction=0.0144, slo=0.999)  # -> 14.4

Every serial dependency you add subtracts from an objective you have already promised. This is the arithmetic that makes an architecture diagram a reliability argument.

5.4 Performance efficiency

The governing rule of performance measurement is that the average is not merely uninformative, it is actively misleading, because the distribution of response times in any real system has a long right tail and it is the tail that users experience.

Dean and Barroso's account of tail latency at scale gives the reason in one calculation. Consider a server whose 99th-percentile latency is one second. If a user request must collect responses from 100 such servers in parallel, 63% of user requests will exceed one second. Even at one-in-ten-thousand — a server where only 0.01% of requests are slow — a service fanning out to 2,000 such servers sees almost one in five user requests take more than a second.34 The measurements from a real Google service make the same point empirically: the 99th-percentile latency for a single random leaf request measured at the root was 10 ms, but the 99th percentile for all requests to finish was 140 ms, with waiting for the slowest 5% accounting for half of that total.34

This is why a performance requirement must name a percentile, a load level and a measurement point, and why architectural decisions that increase fan-out have a latency cost that does not appear in any single-service benchmark. It is also why the classical queueing relations remain worth teaching: Little's law — that the average number of items in a stable system equals arrival rate times average time in system — lets you convert between concurrency, throughput and latency without measuring all three.35

Performance is also the attribute with the cleanest published link to money, which makes it the easiest one to get funded. A study of mobile site data from 37 retail, travel, luxury and lead-generation brands across Europe and the US found that a 0.1-second improvement in site speed corresponded to an 8.4% increase in retail conversion and a 9.2% increase in average order value; travel conversions rose 10.1%; on luxury sites, progression from product detail to add-to-basket rose 40.1%.36 A team that cannot get a latency budget approved is usually a team that has not translated milliseconds into the currency its executives use.

5.5 Interaction capability

The characteristic ISO/IEC 25010:2023 renamed from usability inherits a measurement tradition that is older and more rigorous than most engineers assume. ISO 9241-11 frames usability as effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use — three separable quantities, not one impression.37 Effectiveness is task completion rate and error rate; efficiency is time on task and interaction cost; satisfaction is where the questionnaires live.

The System Usability Scale is the workhorse: ten alternately-phrased items on a five-point response scale, yielding a single score from 0 to 100.38 Its value comes from the normative data behind it. Analysis of more than 5,000 individual responses across some 500 studies puts the population mean at 68 with a standard deviation of 12.5, which converts a raw score into a percentile rank — and makes clear that a score of 68 is exactly average rather than a passing grade of 68%.39 That normalisation is the whole point: a usability number without a reference population is a number with no meaning.

Two subcharacteristics added in the 2023 revision deserve explicit requirements of their own. Inclusivity has an established measurement apparatus in accessibility conformance testing, which yields countable defects against published success criteria. Self-descriptiveness is measurable as the proportion of tasks a first-time user completes without recourse to external documentation.1

5.6 Safety, where the number is the law

In safety-critical domains the quantification problem was solved decades ago by regulators, and the results are instructive for everyone else because they show what a fully specified quality requirement looks like when there is no option to leave it vague.

IEC 61508 assigns each safety function a safety integrity level, expressed for high-demand or continuous operation as a tolerable frequency of dangerous failure per hour: broadly, SIL 1 in the 10⁻⁶–10⁻⁵ band, down to SIL 4 in the 10⁻⁹–10⁻⁸ band.40,41 Aviation, rail and general programmable electronics converge on the same order of magnitude at the top: the strictest levels — SIL 4 in rail and electronics, design assurance level A in civil aviation — correspond to failure rates around 10⁻⁹ per hour, the level reserved for failures that could cause multiple fatalities.42,43

The important lesson is not the numbers but their consequence. Once a target failure rate is written down, the verification method is no longer a matter of engineering preference — it is derived. You cannot test your way to a demonstrated 10⁻⁹ failures per hour, because you cannot run 10⁹ hours of trials. Beyond a certain assurance level the only available arguments are structural: diverse redundancy, formal specification, proof. That is why the highest integrity levels are where formal methods live, and why the cost curve in the next section bends the way it does.

5.7 Flexibility, compatibility, and the rest

The remaining characteristics are quantified the same way, and the exercise is worth doing even when the answer is “we do not care about this one,” because writing that down is itself a decision. Flexibility — which in the 2023 model absorbs adaptability, installability, replaceability and the newly added scalability — is measured in the cost of a specific anticipated change: engineer-days to add a new payment provider, or to run in an additional region. Compatibility is measured in the number of concurrently supported interface versions and the notice period for a breaking change. Portability, in the era of containers, has largely collapsed into installability, measured as time-to-first-successful-deploy in a clean environment.

06 What it actually costs to hit a number

Quantification cuts both ways. Once a quality attribute has a number, the cost of reaching that number also becomes measurable — and the published figures are sobering enough to be useful.

seL4 is the reference point for the top of the scale. The microkernel comprises roughly 8,700 lines of C and 600 of assembler; the machine-checked proof that its implementation conforms to an abstract specification cost approximately 20 person-years in total, including around 9 person-years invested in the underlying proof tooling and libraries. The team estimated the development of the kernel itself at about 4 person-years, comparable to conventional microkernel efforts.44,45 Later work put the non-recurring verification engineering at roughly 12 person-years for 8,500 source lines, an estimated $350 per line — and noted the scaling property that governs everything: verification effort grows with the square of specification size.46 That single fact is the strongest available argument for small, simple trusted computing bases.

SQLite occupies the tier below, and is more instructive for ordinary projects because it reaches extraordinary reliability by conventional means applied without compromise. Against roughly 155.8 thousand source lines of C, the project maintains about 590 times as much test code and test script — some 92 million lines. Four independently developed harnesses; 100% branch coverage and 100% modified condition/decision coverage of the core in an as-deployed configuration; a proprietary fuzzer running continuously on sixteen cores at around 500 million cases per day; 6,754 assertion statements; anomaly tests that simulate out-of-memory, I/O failure and power loss at every point in an operation; and a release checklist of roughly 200 manually verified items.47

The project's own assessment of that regime is the most honest sentence in the software quality literature: maintaining 100% MC/DC is laborious and time-consuming, and the level of effort is probably not cost-effective for a typical application — but it is justified for a very widely deployed infrastructure library.47 That is a quality attribute requirement being derived from context of use, exactly as it should be.

SQLite also documents the tension: code tested to 100% MC/DC tends to be more vulnerable to fuzzing, because MC/DC discourages the defensive branches that fuzzers otherwise stumble into. Two reliability techniques, pulling in opposite directions, both correct.

Amazon Web Services shows the other end of the cost-effectiveness curve, and is the example to reach for when arguing for a budget. Engineers applied TLA+ and PlusCal specifications to some of the company's more complex systems: a 939-line specification of DynamoDB's replication and group-membership system found three bugs, one of which required a trace of up to 35 steps to trigger; a 645-line specification of background data redistribution in S3 found one bug and then another in the proposed fix; a 102-line specification of EBS volume management found three bugs.48 The engineer who wrote the DynamoDB specification reported that the effort was more reliable and less time-consuming than the informal proofs he had written instead. The practice has since broadened at AWS into a portfolio of formal and semi-formal methods applied across services.49

Set beside these, continuous fuzzing is close to free. Google's OSS-Fuzz service reported having helped identify and fix over 13,000 vulnerabilities and 50,000 bugs across a thousand projects as of May 2025.50 The lesson for a requirements author is that assurance techniques differ in cost by orders of magnitude, and the cheap ones are cheap enough that declining them is a decision that should have to be justified in writing.

07 Making the numbers bite

A threshold that lives in a document degrades at the same rate as the system. A threshold that lives in a pipeline does not.

The organising concept here is the architectural fitness function: an objective integrity assessment of some architectural characteristic, implemented by whatever mechanism suits — a test, a metric, a monitor, a log analysis, occasionally a manual review.51,52 The formulation matters because it detaches the governance of a quality attribute from the ceremony that traditionally enforced it. Architectural rules used to be enforced by inspection at review boards, which is to say by memory, availability and seniority. A fitness function enforces them by failing a build.

Fitness functions are usefully classified along several axes: atomic (checking one characteristic) versus holistic (checking characteristics in combination, which is where real conflicts surface); triggered (run on a commit) versus continual (running in production); automated versus manual; static versus dynamic.51 The classification is not academic. It tells you where a given check belongs in the pipeline, and the cheap-early, expensive-late gradient falls out of it directly.

A practical inventory, roughly in order of how much value each returns per hour of setup:

  • Dependency and layering rules. A test that fails when the presentation layer imports the persistence layer costs an afternoon to write and permanently retires an entire category of review comment. Tools such as ArchUnit make these ordinary unit tests.
  • New-code quality gates. Coverage, complexity and duplication thresholds applied to the diff. The legacy mass is exempt by construction, so the gate is always passable and the trend is always correct.24
  • Composition analysis gated on exploitability. Fail on EPSS percentile and known-exploited status rather than base severity, or the gate will be disabled within a month.29
  • Performance budgets in continuous integration. A synthetic run on a fixed device or instance profile, compared against a committed budget file, with the diff shown in the pull request.
  • Mutation score floors. The honest answer to coverage gaming: a mutation score measures whether the tests would actually detect a change, which line coverage does not.
  • Continuous fuzzing. For anything parsing untrusted input, this is the highest defect-yield-per-currency-unit technique available.50,47
  • Burn-rate alerting on the error budget, in place of threshold alerting on raw error counts.33
  • Chaos experiments. A continual, holistic fitness function for fault tolerance: the requirement is not “the system is resilient” but “the system meets its SLO while a named dependency is unavailable,” and the experiment is the test.
  • Provenance and bill-of-materials generation at build time, which makes the inventory question answerable by machine.14

One caution. Every gate you add is a tax on every change, and a gate whose failures are routinely waived is worse than no gate, because it trains the organisation that thresholds are advisory. Add gates in the order of the risks that would actually end the product, and retire any gate that has not caught a real problem in a year.

08 Architecture is where the trade-offs get priced

Quality attributes conflict. That is not a defect in the requirements; it is the reason architecture exists as a discipline.

The Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method formalised the practice of surfacing those conflicts before they are decided by accident. Its central artefacts are a utility tree, which decomposes the abstract goal of “utility” into quality attributes and then into concrete, prioritised scenarios; sensitivity points, where a single architectural decision strongly affects one attribute; and trade-off points, where a decision affects two or more in opposite directions. Every sensitivity and trade-off point is a candidate risk until it is explicitly classified as a risk or a non-risk.18,3

The value of the utility tree is that it forces the customer and the evaluation team to state “-ility” requirements precisely enough to be analysed. The value of naming trade-off points is that it converts a class of future disagreement into a documented decision with a date on it.

Every row in that table appeared earlier in this article as a real event. The Ariane 501 board's finding that a CPU workload budget of 80% led to three of seven at-risk conversions being left unprotected is a trade-off point that was resolved without being named.16 SQLite's observation that maximal MC/DC coverage and maximal fuzz resistance work against each other is a trade-off point that was named, in public, by the people who own it.47 The difference between those two outcomes is roughly the whole discipline.

Two further notes on trade-offs that the literature tends to underplay. First, some trade-offs are illusory and dissolve under measurement: the DORA research programme's most durable finding is that throughput and stability move together rather than trading off, which contradicted a widely held prior belief.26,25 Second, some apparent trade-offs are just staging problems. Android's memory-safety programme delivered security improvements and a lower rollback rate, because the cost being avoided (vulnerability response) and the cost being paid (a new language toolchain) landed in different budgets over different horizons.31 Ask whether a trade-off is real or merely badly accounted before you accept it.

09 How quantification goes wrong

Measuring quality attributes badly is worse than not measuring them, because a bad number is harder to argue with than no number.

  1. The measure becomes the target. Strathern's compact statement of Goodhart's law — when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure — describes the failure mode exactly.53 Line coverage mandates produce assertion-free tests. Change-failure-rate targets produce incidents reclassified as planned maintenance. Any metric attached to an individual's performance review stops being a measurement and becomes a negotiation. This is the specific reason delivery metrics are defined at the team and system level.25
  2. Composite scores hide their inputs. The Maintainability Index is the standing example: three defensible metrics, averaged per module and combined by coefficients fitted to a small sample of 1980s code, producing a single figure that is dominated by size and cannot be acted on.21,22 If a score cannot be decomposed into the specific change that would improve it, it is a mood, not a measurement.
  3. Averages destroy the information you needed. Every argument in the tail-latency section applies to any distribution with a long right tail, which is most of them.34 Report percentiles, and be specific about which; a p50 improvement bought by a p99 regression is a net loss for users even when the mean improves.
  4. Proxies drift from what they proxy. CVSS was designed to communicate severity and is widely used as a proxy for risk, a job it does poorly enough to be measurably wasteful.27,29 Every proxy should be periodically re-validated against the outcome it stands in for, and retired when it stops predicting.
  5. Thresholds set where they cannot be met. A 100% availability target, a 100% coverage mandate applied retroactively to a legacy codebase, a zero-known-vulnerabilities policy: each guarantees permanent violation, and permanent violation teaches everyone that violation is normal.32
  6. Measuring what is easy instead of what is decisive. The Goal–Question– Metric discipline exists precisely to prevent this, and it is bypassed whenever a dashboard is built before a decision is identified.19 The test is simple: name the action that a change in this number would trigger. If there is none, stop collecting it.

Underlying all six is a single principle. A quality attribute metric earns its place by changing a decision. Anything else is telemetry theatre, and it has a real cost: it consumes the organisational attention that the two or three metrics that matter were supposed to receive.

10 A protocol you can start on Monday

  1. Name the two or three attributes that could end the product. Not the nine characteristics of the standard — the two or three whose failure is existential for this system. Use the model as a checklist to make sure you have not missed a category, then discard the rest explicitly and in writing.1
  2. Measure the present before you set a target. Fill in Gilb's Past field first. A team that discovers its 90th-percentile lead time is 46 days will set a different target than a team that guessed it was a week.17
  3. Write one six-part scenario per attribute. Source, stimulus, artefact, environment, response, response measure. If you cannot fill in the environment, you do not yet understand the requirement.3,18
  4. Convert each response measure into a fitness function. Where in the pipeline does this fail a build? If the answer is “nowhere,” the requirement is not yet real.51
  5. Gate the delta, not the mass. Apply thresholds to changed code and new deployments so the gate is always passable today and the trend is monotonically correct.24
  6. Attach a policy to every budget. An error budget without a documented consequence is a report. Write down what stops when it is exhausted, and who has the authority to override.33
  7. Publish the trade-off points. Record, at the time of the decision, which attribute was preferred over which and on what evidence. Ariane's board could reconstruct the workload-versus-protection decision only after the launcher was destroyed.16
  8. Review the metrics on the same cadence as the code. Retire any that have not changed a decision in a year. Re-validate any proxy against the outcome it stands in for.

11 Where to go next

Standards worth owning

  • ISO/IEC 25010:2023 — the product quality model; use it as a completeness checklist for attribute selection.1
  • ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018 — requirements engineering processes and the verifiability criterion.4
  • ISO 9241-11:2018 — usability as effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context.37
  • NIST SP 800-218 (SSDF v1.1) — secure development practices grouped as PO / PS / PW / RV, mapped to EO 14028.14
  • IEC 61508 — functional safety and the SIL failure-rate bands; the clearest example of a fully quantified quality requirement.40
  • OWASP ASVS — levelled, testable application security requirements.30

Books

  • Software Architecture in Practice, Bass, Clements & Kazman — the canonical treatment of quality attribute scenarios and tactics.3
  • Competitive Engineering, Gilb — Planguage, and the most uncompromising argument in print that every quality requirement must be quantified.17
  • Building Evolutionary Architectures, Ford, Parsons, Kua & Sadalage — fitness functions and automated architectural governance.51
  • Site Reliability Engineering and The Site Reliability Workbook — SLIs, SLOs, error budgets and the policies that make them binding.32,33
  • Accelerate, Forsgren, Humble & Kim — the research design behind the delivery metrics.26
  • Quantifying the User Experience, Sauro & Lewis — the statistics behind usability measurement and the SUS normative data.39

Papers to read in full

  • Dean & Barroso, The Tail at Scale — eight pages that change how you specify latency forever.34
  • Tornhill & Borg, Code Red — the business case for code quality, with data.8
  • Jacobs et al., on EPSS — effort, coverage and efficiency as the currency of remediation.29
  • Newcombe et al., How Amazon Web Services Uses Formal Methods — what lightweight formal specification costs and returns.48
  • Klein et al., on seL4 — the price of the highest assurance level anyone has paid.44
  • Lehman, Programs, Life Cycles, and Laws of Software Evolution — why decay is the default.7
  • Glinz, On Non-Functional Requirements — why the term is a mess, and a workable replacement.2

Projects to study as exemplars

  • SQLite's testing documentation — the most detailed public account of an extreme reliability regime, including its costs and internal tensions.47
  • seL4 — functional correctness proved down to C, with the effort published.45
  • OSS-Fuzz — continuous fuzzing as infrastructure; the aggregate defect counts make the cost-benefit argument for you.50
  • Android's memory-safety programme — class elimination, measured over five years.31
  • The AWS formal-methods practice — specification sizes and bugs found, service by service.48,49

The recurring theme across all of these is not sophistication. It is that a handful of organisations decided which properties of their systems were non-negotiable, wrote those properties down with units and thresholds attached, built machinery that fails loudly when a threshold is crossed, and published what it cost them. Everything else in this article is commentary on that sequence.
