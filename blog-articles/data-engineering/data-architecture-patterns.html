Data Architecture Patterns: Medallion, Lambda, Kappa and the Mesh

Four patterns that answer different questions — how data is refined, how it is computed over time, and who owns it

Medallion, lambda, kappa and data mesh are routinely presented as four options on one menu. They are not. Two of them describe how a computation is arranged in time, one describes how a dataset is refined in space, and one describes who is accountable for the result. This is an engineering history of where each came from, what each actually commits you to, and how they compose on a modern lakehouse.

Ask three data engineers to draw the reference architecture and you will get three drawings that disagree about what the boxes even represent. One draws storage tiers. One draws a batch path beside a streaming path. One draws an org chart. All three are correct, and that is precisely the problem: the four patterns that dominate the current vocabulary are not competing answers to one question. They are answers to four different questions that happen to be drawn on the same whiteboard.

The confusion is not the reader's fault. Terms like architecture, framework, platform and ecosystem are used interchangeably across the practitioner and vendor literature, and a recent survey of the field opens by noting exactly this ambiguity before attempting to impose a common vocabulary.[1] Earlier attempts to map the terrain reached the same conclusion: the popular architectures are interdependent rather than mutually exclusive, and comparing them head-to-head requires first agreeing on what dimension you are comparing.[2]

So it is worth being blunt about what each pattern actually governs before going near an implementation:

Medallion

How is data refined? A staged pipeline of increasing quality: raw, then conformed, then consumer-shaped.

Lambda

How is a result computed over time? Two paths — a slow complete one and a fast approximate one — reconciled at read time.

Kappa

Same question, different answer. One path over a replayable log; history is reprocessed by replaying it.

Data mesh

Who is accountable? Domain teams own their data as a product; governance is federated and executed as code.

You can run kappa inside a medallion layer inside a mesh domain. That sentence is not a joke; it is a fairly ordinary description of a mature platform, and it is only confusing if you believed the four were alternatives. The rest of this article traces where each came from, states what each genuinely costs, and shows where they meet.

01Fifty-six years of moving the same argument around

Almost every debate in modern data architecture is a re-run of an argument that predates the tooling by decades. It helps to know which one you are having.

The separation that started everything

Codd's 1970 paper is usually remembered for the relational algebra, but its opening argument is about independence: users of large data banks should be protected from knowing how data is physically organised, and applications should survive changes to that internal representation.[3] Every layered architecture since is a variation on that promise — put a contract between the consumer and the storage so the storage can change.

The second foundational move was organisational rather than mathematical. In 1988, Devlin and Murphy described the architecture IBM's European division had adopted, drawing a hard line between the transaction-processing environment where operational databases live and the informational environment where people ask questions of the business. Their proposal — an integrated warehouse of company data in a relational environment, reached through a consistent set of end-user tools and described by a business data directory — introduced the term "business data warehouse" to the literature.[4] The operational/informational split they formalised is still the seam along which most of today's arguments run.

The warehouse wars

Inmon's 1992 treatment defined the warehouse as a subject-oriented, integrated, time-variant and non-volatile collection of data supporting management decisions, built top-down as a normalised enterprise store from which departmental marts are derived.[5] Kimball's 1996 answer inverted the build order: model the business processes directly as dimensional star schemas, deliver a usable mart quickly, and achieve enterprise integration through conformed dimensions shared across marts — the data warehouse bus.[6,7]

This is the original centralised-versus-federated argument. Inmon integrates once, centrally, and pays for it in time-to-first-value. Kimball integrates through shared contracts on the dimensions and pays for it in governance discipline. Data mesh, thirty years later, makes the same trade at the level of teams rather than tables.

The scale-out detour

MapReduce made it economical to run batch computation across large clusters of commodity machines by reducing the programmer's job to a map and a reduce function while the framework handled partitioning, scheduling and failure.[8] The practical consequence for architecture was that storing everything first and deciding on structure later became affordable.

James Dixon named the result in 2010. Contrasting a data mart with a store of bottled water — cleansed, packaged, structured for easy consumption — he described a data lake as a body of water in a more natural state, fed by a source, which users can sample or dive into.[9] Four years later Gartner published a caution that has aged into a checklist of every lake failure since: lakes lack semantic consistency and governed metadata, they tend to begin as ungoverned stores, and they assume that everyone across an enterprise is skilled at data manipulation and understands the contextual bias of how each source was captured.[10]

The log, and the two ways to use it

Meanwhile LinkedIn had a different problem: activity and operational event data at a volume that existing messaging systems handled badly, particularly when consumers were offline batch jobs that let messages accumulate rather than consuming immediately. Kafka's design answer was a partitioned, distributed, durable commit log with cheap sequential reads and consumers that track their own offsets.[11] Once a durable ordered log exists, history stops being something you archive and becomes something you can re-run.

Two architectures came out of that realisation within three years of each other, and the rest of this article is largely about their difference. Nathan Marz proposed running the same input through a batch layer and a speed layer in parallel, arguing that immutable data plus recomputation isolates you from the consistency problems that the CAP result formalises and, more importantly, from your own bugs.[12,13] Jay Kreps replied that a mixture of systems does not beat CAP, and that maintaining logic that must produce identical results in two complex distributed systems is exactly as painful as it sounds; the alternative is one implementation over a replayable log.[14]

The cloud unbundling and the re-integration

Snowflake's 2016 paper made the architectural consequence of cloud economics explicit: separate storage from compute so that isolated compute clusters can be started, resized and stopped over one shared body of data, with time travel and semi-structured support as first-class features.[15] The lake got the same treatment from the other direction. Uber built Hudi in 2016 to make near-real-time ingestion with updates, deletes and incremental processing work on a Hadoop-scale lake;[16] Netflix built Iceberg to get correctness and atomic transactions that Hive's table conventions could not guarantee, open-sourcing it to the Apache Software Foundation in November 2018;[17] Databricks published Delta Lake, an ACID table storage layer over cloud object stores.[18]

By 2021 the argument had a name and a manifesto. The lakehouse paper argued that the prevailing two-tier design — a lake for cheap storage plus a warehouse for management features — is structurally flawed: it is hard to keep the two consistent, every extra ETL hop adds failure modes, warehouse data is stale relative to the lake, and machine learning workloads are poorly served by reading through ODBC/JDBC.[19] In parallel, Zhamak Dehghani argued that the bottleneck was never the technology at all but the centralised team sitting between domains and their own data.[20] Gartner recast data fabric around active metadata in the same period.[21] Academic synthesis arrived later, and is still catching up.[22,1]

02The force space: which question are you answering?

Three technical forces pull against each other in any analytical pipeline, and a fourth force is not technical at all.

Freshness
How old is the newest fact a consumer can see? Bounded by ingestion cadence, transformation latency and serving refresh.
Correctness
Not just "no bugs" but recoverability from bugs: can you recompute the published answer from retained inputs after fixing the logic? Marz's central claim is that immutability plus recomputation buys human fault tolerance, which matters more than machine fault tolerance.[12]
Frugality
Cost, in compute and in the scarcer currency: the number of distinct systems your team must reason about at 3am.
Ownership
Who is accountable when a number is wrong — the team that produced the data, or a central team that inherited it?[20]

Lambda and kappa are competing answers to the freshness/correctness/ frugality problem. Medallion is a discipline for arranging the transformation itself so that correctness is auditable at each hop. Data mesh answers the ownership question and is silent on the other three. Treating them as one menu guarantees you will argue about the wrong axis: an organisation whose real problem is that a central team of six cannot keep up with forty domains does not fix anything by switching from micro-batch to streaming.

A useful test: if the proposal changes who gets paged, it is an ownership decision. If it changes how long the answer takes, it is a computation decision. If it changes what a table is allowed to contain, it is a refinement decision. Most failed migrations are one of these dressed as another.

03Medallion: refinement as a contract

Medallion architecture — also called multi-hop — is a data design pattern for logically organising data in a lakehouse, whose stated goal is to incrementally and progressively improve the structure and quality of data as it flows from bronze to silver to gold.[23,24] The names denote quality, not technology: bronze is raw, silver is validated, gold is enriched.[25]

The canonical worked example in the vendor documentation is worth stating precisely because it is more restrained than most implementations: bronze ingests raw data from cloud storage, Kafka and a SaaS CRM with no cleanup or validation performed at all; silver cleans and validates — dropping nulls, quarantining invalid records — and joins customers with transactions into a dataset that data scientists can use for predictive analytics; gold aggregates for business consumption.[23] In a warehouse-shaped deployment the enterprise model is expressed in silver and gold holds the specialised marts.[26]

The layer contracts

The pattern only pays for itself if each boundary is a real contract. Written as obligations rather than adjectives:

What each layer promises and what it is forbidden from doing
LayerPromisesForbiddenRebuilt from
Bronze Every byte received is retained, with ingest timestamp, source and batch identifier. Append-only. Business logic, deduplication, type coercion, "small fixes". The source system, if it still has the data. Often nothing.
Silver Declared schema holds; keys are conformed; entities are joined; failures are quarantined with a reason. Consumer-specific aggregation; metric definitions; anything a single dashboard asked for. Bronze, deterministically.
Gold Named business metrics with owners, documented definitions and a freshness SLA. Being the only place a cleaning rule exists. Silver, cheaply and often.

A minimal implementation

The shape below is deliberately dull. Bronze does nothing but stamp and land; silver validates and splits; gold aggregates. Every write targets a transactional table format so that a failed job leaves the previous version intact rather than a half-written directory.[18]

# bronze: land exactly what arrived, plus provenance
raw = (spark.readStream.format("cloudFiles")
       .option("cloudFiles.format", "json")
       .load(SOURCE_PATH))

(raw.withColumn("_ingested_at", F.current_timestamp())
    .withColumn("_source", F.lit("orders-api"))
    .withColumn("_batch_id", F.lit(BATCH_ID))
    .writeStream.format("delta").outputMode("append")
    .option("checkpointLocation", CKPT + "/bronze")
    .toTable("prod.bronze.orders_raw"))

# silver: validate, conform, split — never drop silently
bronze = spark.readStream.table("prod.bronze.orders_raw")

typed = (bronze
         .withColumn("order_id",   F.col("payload.id").cast("string"))
         .withColumn("customer_id", F.col("payload.cust").cast("string"))
         .withColumn("amount",     F.col("payload.amt").cast("decimal(18,2)"))
         .withColumn("ordered_at", F.to_timestamp("payload.ts")))

rules = {
    "order_id_present": F.col("order_id").isNotNull(),
    "amount_non_negative": F.col("amount") >= 0,
    "event_time_parsed": F.col("ordered_at").isNotNull(),
}
failed = F.array_except(
    F.array(*[F.when(~cond, F.lit(name)) for name, cond in rules.items()]),
    F.array(F.lit(None)))

checked = typed.withColumn("_violations", failed)

(checked.filter(F.size("_violations") == 0).drop("_violations")
        .dropDuplicates(["order_id"])
        .writeStream.format("delta")
        .option("checkpointLocation", CKPT + "/silver")
        .toTable("prod.silver.orders"))

(checked.filter(F.size("_violations") > 0)          # the part teams skip
        .writeStream.format("delta")
        .option("checkpointLocation", CKPT + "/quarantine")
        .toTable("prod.silver.orders_quarantine"))

-- gold: one owned definition of a business metric, rebuildable at will
CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE prod.gold.daily_revenue AS
SELECT
  date_trunc('day', o.ordered_at)          AS order_date,
  c.region                                  AS region,
  count(DISTINCT o.order_id)                AS orders,
  sum(o.amount)                             AS gross_revenue
FROM prod.silver.orders           o
JOIN prod.silver.customers        c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
WHERE o.ordered_at >= current_date - INTERVAL 90 DAYS
GROUP BY 1, 2;

Where medallion goes wrong

  • Layer inflation. Three layers is a convention, not a law; the documentation describes a series of layers and permits more or fewer per deployment.[23] If a hop adds no guarantee, it adds only latency and cost.
  • Logic in bronze. The moment bronze is cleaned, it stops being a replay tape, and silver can no longer be rebuilt after a logic fix.
  • Silver as a landfill. Without enforced expectations and a quarantine path, silver becomes bronze with better naming.
  • Gold-only cleaning. Each mart re-implements the same cleaning, which is exactly the duplicated-integration failure the Inmon critique of independent marts identified.[5]
  • Confusing the pattern with a product. Medallion is a logical organisation; it can be implemented on any engine that supports transactional tables.[23]

04Lambda: two paths, reconciled at read time

Marz's formulation starts from a definition rather than a topology: a query is a function of the complete dataset, and the reason data systems are hard is the interaction of mutable state and incremental updates with the constraints the CAP result imposes.[12,13] His answer is to keep the master dataset immutable and append-only, recompute views from it in batch, and accept a separate approximate path for the recent window.

The three layers each carry a distinct obligation:[12,27]

  1. Batch layer. Holds the master dataset and precomputes views over all of it. Slow, complete, and self-correcting: fix the code, run it again, and the new views replace the old wholesale.
  2. Speed layer. Computes incrementally over recent data only. Allowed to be approximate, because its output is discarded once the batch view catches up.
  3. Serving layer. Merges batch and realtime views so a query sees a complete answer.

The criticism that stuck

Kreps' response is worth reading in the original because it is narrower and more damning than the summary version. He accepts that latency and availability trade-offs exist in stream processing but points out that asynchronous computation is not kept consistent with incoming data, so CAP is untouched by the arrangement. The real problem is operational: you implement the transformation twice, in two different distributed systems with different frameworks and different semantics, and keeping them in agreement is permanently expensive.[14]

In practice the divergence shows up as small semantic gaps that nobody notices until an executive does: late-arriving records handled by event time in one path and arrival time in the other, a deduplication key that differs by one field, a currency conversion pinned to a different rate table. The batch layer eventually overwrites the discrepancy, which is exactly why it survives so long undetected.

When lambda is still the right answer

Lambda is not obsolete; it is conditional. It remains correct when the streaming source cannot serve as the system of record for full history — when retention is bounded by cost or regulation, when the only complete history lives in an archive or an operational database, or when the batch computation is qualitatively different from the streaming one (a full graph recomputation, a model retrain, a regulator-mandated restatement over seven years of records). If you cannot replay everything, you have a batch path whether or not you call it a batch layer.

05Kappa: one path, replayed

Kappa removes the batch layer by treating batch as a special case of streaming. Kreps' proposal is concrete: keep the log with enough retention to cover the reprocessing window, run one stream job, and when the logic changes, start a second job from the beginning of the log writing to a new output table, let it catch up, then switch reads over and delete the old.[14] Batch is not eliminated; it is expressed as a replay of the same code.

The log is doing more work here than it appears. It is not a queue; it is the integration point of the whole system, a role Kafka's design consciously supports through durable retention and consumer-managed offsets rather than broker-managed acknowledgement.[11] Kleppmann and Kreps make the analogy explicit: the log plays the part Unix pipes play, composing small single-purpose processors into pipelines whose interfaces are uniform and whose intermediate state is inspectable.[28] The same idea, seen from the database side, is that a stream and a table are two views of the same history, and derived data is just a materialised view maintained by a stream processor.[29]

What kappa demands that batch let you ignore

Streaming forces you to be explicit about time. The Dataflow model decomposes a pipeline into four questions — what is computed, where in event time (windowing), when in processing time results are materialised (triggering), and how later refinements relate to results already emitted (accumulation) — and argues that with unbounded, out-of-order input you must give up on completeness as a precondition and design for revision instead.[30] Engines such as Flink implement batch and stream over a single runtime precisely so that the replay case is not a different program.[31]

# the same logic, expressed once; replay is a parameter, not a rewrite
def build_pipeline(env, source_offset, sink_table):
    events = (env.from_source(kafka_source(start=source_offset))
                 .assign_timestamps_and_watermarks(
                     WatermarkStrategy
                     .for_bounded_out_of_orderness(Duration.of_minutes(10))
                     .with_timestamp_assigner(lambda e, _: e.ordered_at)))

    return (events
            .key_by(lambda e: (e.region, e.currency))
            .window(TumblingEventTimeWindows.of(Time.hours(1)))
            .allowed_lateness(Time.hours(6))          # revise, do not lie
            .side_output_late_data(LATE)              # and keep the evidence
            .aggregate(RevenueAggregate())
            .sink_to(upsert_sink(sink_table)))        # idempotent by window key

# steady state
build_pipeline(env, source_offset="latest",   sink_table="gold.revenue_v1")
# logic change: replay the whole log beside it, compare, then swap the view
build_pipeline(env, source_offset="earliest", sink_table="gold.revenue_v2")

The preconditions nobody lists in the diagram

  • Retention long enough to matter. If the log holds seven days and your restatement window is two years, kappa is a slogan.
  • Deterministic transformations. A replay that enriches events by looking up a dimension table as it is now will not reproduce history. Either the enrichment must be a stream-to-stream join against a versioned dimension, or the enriched value must be carried in the event.
  • Idempotent sinks. Replay writes the same keys again; the sink must absorb that without double-counting, which in practice means upserts into a transactional table.[18]
  • Schema evolution as a first-class concern. Old segments of the log were written by old producers. Replaying them means your current code must still read them.
  • Somewhere to put late data. Dropping it silently converts a correctness problem into an unreported one.[30]

06Data mesh: moving the accountability, not the bytes

Dehghani's original argument identifies the failure mode of the monolithic lake as organisational: a centralised, hyper-specialised data team sits between operational domains that produce data and consumers that need it, owning pipelines for domains whose semantics they do not know and cannot keep up with.[20] The follow-up formalises the response as four principles: domain-oriented decentralised data ownership, data as a product, self-serve data infrastructure as a platform, and federated computational governance.[32,33]

The four principles, stated as obligations

Domain ownership
The team that produces the data owns its analytical representation. Scaling means adding autonomous nodes, not enlarging the central team.[32]
Data as a product
A published dataset carries the obligations of a product: discoverable, addressable, documented, versioned, with an SLA and a named owner. The unit of architecture is the product — code, data, metadata and policy together — not a table.[32,33]
Self-serve platform
Domains cannot each build storage, streaming, cataloguing and lineage. The platform's success metric is the time it takes a domain team to publish a compliant product.[32]
Federated computational governance
Global rules are agreed by domain representatives and then executed automatically in every product. The word "computational" is the load-bearing one: a policy that requires a human review board has reintroduced the bottleneck the mesh was meant to remove.[32] This is also where the discipline connects to conventional governance practice, which has long distinguished policy from its enforcement.[34]

A data product contract, concretely

# orders-domain/products/order_facts/contract.yaml
product: order_facts
owner:   team-orders            # a rota, not a person
domain:  orders
version: 2.3.0                  # breaking changes bump major
interfaces:
  - type: iceberg_table
    location: catalog.orders.order_facts
  - type: kafka_topic           # same product, streaming port
    topic: orders.facts.v2
schema:
  order_id:    {type: string, unique: true, nullable: false}
  customer_id: {type: string, nullable: false, references: customers.customer_id}
  ordered_at:  {type: timestamp, semantics: event_time}
  gross_amount: {type: decimal(18,2), currency_field: currency}
guarantees:
  freshness_p95: 15m
  completeness:  "99.9% of upstream events within 1h"
  retention:     36 months
policy:                          # executed by the platform, not reviewed
  classification: internal
  pii_fields: [customer_id]
  masking: tokenised_for_role(analyst)
  lineage: emitted_to_catalog

What the evidence says

Mesh is the least empirically settled of the four. A systematic review of the gray literature synthesised 114 practitioner articles precisely because academic treatment was thin, characterised the four principles as practitioners actually describe them, and mapped the findings onto service-oriented architecture reference models to make them analysable.[22] The same body of work surfaces the pattern's central tension: a philosophy of decentralisation that in practice depends on centralised catalogues for discovery, which risks reintroducing the bottleneck at the metadata layer.[22]

The honest summary is that mesh is a sociotechnical bet. It is worth taking when the central team is genuinely the constraint on domain knowledge and the platform is mature enough that publishing a compliant product is a day's work. It is a bad bet when domains have no engineering capacity, when the platform does not exist yet, or when the actual problem is that nobody agrees what "active customer" means — in which case federating ownership will produce four incompatible definitions faster than before.

07The substrate that made the layered patterns credible

Medallion presupposes something that data lakes did not originally provide: that a write either happens completely or not at all. The lakehouse argument begins there. The two-tier design of lake plus warehouse suffers from reliability problems keeping the two consistent, staleness in the warehouse relative to the lake, poor support for advanced analytics that must read large datasets with non-SQL code, and duplicated cost.[19]

The obstacle was the storage layer itself. Cloud object stores are key-value systems: listing objects is expensive and consistency guarantees are limited, which makes ACID transactions and fast metadata operations hard to achieve directly over a directory of files.[18] The fix, common to all the modern formats, is to stop defining a table as a directory listing and define it as a transactionally maintained record of which files constitute it.[16]

The three open table formats, by the problem each was built to solve
FormatOriginDesign centre
Apache Hudi Uber, 2016[16] Near-real-time ingestion with updates and deletes; incremental consumption of a table as a stream of changes, with table services such as compaction and clustering shipped alongside the format.[16]
Apache Iceberg Netflix; donated to the ASF in November 2018[17] An engine-neutral specification: correctness and atomic transactions that Hive's conventions could not guarantee, with maintenance left to the engines.[17,16]
Delta Lake Databricks[18] An ACID table storage layer over cloud object stores, using a write-ahead log of commits to deliver transactions, versioning and time travel at scale.[18]

The other half of the unbundling came from the warehouse side. Separating storage from compute lets independent clusters run over one shared body of data without repartitioning, which is what makes it economical to have a streaming job, a nightly rebuild and an ad-hoc analyst query all reading the same silver table.[15] Between the two moves, the substrate now supplies atomic multi-file commits, snapshot isolation, time travel, schema evolution and row-level deletes — which is to say, it supplies exactly the properties that make "rebuild gold from silver" a routine operation rather than an outage.[18,19]

08The patterns that share the whiteboard

Data Vault
A modelling method rather than a platform architecture: hubs for business keys, links for relationships, satellites for descriptive and historical attributes, designed so that new sources are additive and the full audit history is preserved.[35] It frequently occupies the silver layer of a medallion deployment in organisations with heavy auditability requirements.
Event sourcing and CQRS
Operational patterns often confused with analytical ones. Event sourcing stores all changes to application state as a sequence of events, so current state is a fold over that sequence.[36] CQRS separates the model used to update information from the model used to read it, which pairs naturally with events but does not require them.[37,38] The resemblance to kappa is real — both make an ordered log the source of truth — but the scope differs: event sourcing is a property of an application's write model, kappa is a property of a derived-data pipeline.
Change data capture
The bridge between an operational database that was not built as an event log and a streaming architecture that needs one. Reading the database's own replication log turns state changes into an event stream without asking the application to publish them.[29] It is how most kappa implementations in enterprises actually get their events, and Hudi's upsert-first design was shaped by exactly this workload.[16]
Data fabric
Gartner's design concept for using metadata — specifically the transition from passive metadata to active metadata that is continuously analysed — to automate data management and integration tasks. Gartner is explicit that fabric and mesh are independent concepts that can complement each other: fabric is about automating data management, mesh is an architectural approach to building domain-owned data products.[21] Independent mapping work reaches a similar conclusion about the interdependence of the logical data warehouse, fabric and mesh.[2]

10Choosing, in the order the questions actually arrive

Two refinements to the flow above are worth stating explicitly.

Latency requirements are usually inherited, not measured. Before committing to a streaming path, find out what happens if a number arrives four hours late. Frequently the answer is nothing, because the consuming process is a weekly meeting. The Dataflow framing helps here: freshness is a spectrum of triggering decisions, not a binary between batch and stream.[30]

Mesh is an answer to an organisational diagnosis. The proposal is explicitly a response to a bottleneck created by centralised ownership.[20] If the bottleneck is somewhere else — a platform that takes six weeks to provision a table, or an absence of agreed definitions — federating ownership will not move it, and the review of practitioner experience suggests the coordination overhead is real.[22]

12What to take from fifty-six years

The pattern names change roughly every five years. The forces do not. Codd argued for insulating consumers from physical representation;[3] Devlin and Murphy argued for separating the informational environment from the operational one;[4] Inmon and Kimball argued about whether integration should happen once centrally or through shared contracts on dimensions;[5,6] Marz and Kreps argued about whether correctness is best purchased with a second code path or a longer log;[12,14] Dehghani argued that the binding constraint was never the topology but the org chart.[20] Every one of those arguments is live in a design review this week.

The practical consequence is a discipline rather than a diagram. Decide what freshness a consumer can actually justify. Decide whether your history is replayable, and be honest about the retention that answer requires. Give every hop between raw and published a contract that can be tested, and a place to put the records that fail it. Then decide who gets paged when the number is wrong — and only then, if the answer is "a central team that does not understand the domain", consider whether federating ownership is worth what it costs.

A pattern is not a destination. It is a name for a trade someone already made, written down so you can decide whether you would make the same one.
