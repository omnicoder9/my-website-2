A guide for writers

Research, Sources, and Evidence

1. Introduction: why evidence matters

Every piece of non-fiction writing asks readers to believe something. A report asks them to accept findings, an essay asks them to accept an interpretation, and a proposal asks them to accept that a course of action is wise. Evidence is what makes that request reasonable. Research is the disciplined process of finding evidence, and citation is the public record that lets readers check it. Booth and colleagues describe research writing as a conversation in which the writer makes a claim, supports it with reasons, grounds those reasons in evidence, and anticipates the questions a careful reader would ask[3]. The Association of College and Research Libraries makes the same point in its framework for information literacy, one of whose six frames is Scholarship as Conversation[4].

This article treats research, sources, and evidence as one connected practice. It moves from the question that starts an inquiry to the reference list that ends it, and it closes with the ethical obligations that run through every stage, including the new problems raised by generative artificial intelligence (AI).

2. A short history of evidence in writing

Classical rhetoric. Aristotle separated “non-artistic” proofs, such as witnesses, contracts, and laws, which exist before the speaker uses them, from “artistic” proofs that the speaker invents through character (ethos), emotion (pathos), and reasoning (logos)[5]. The distinction survives today as the difference between evidence a writer finds and the argument a writer builds from it.

The footnote and the archive. Grafton traces the scholarly footnote through early modern antiquarians and Enlightenment historians to the nineteenth-century German historian Leopold von Ranke, whose insistence on archival documents helped make source criticism the core of historical method. Grafton’s point is that the footnote is both a tool of proof and a rhetorical device that displays the writer’s labour and invites checking[6].

Journals and peer review. The learned journal, beginning with the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society in 1665, made the published article the main unit of scientific evidence. Editors in the eighteenth century already consulted others before publishing[7], but Csiszar shows that routine, systematic external refereeing became standard only in the twentieth century[8]. Peer review is therefore a relatively recent and imperfect safeguard rather than an ancient guarantee.

Citation as data. In 1955 Eugene Garfield proposed a citation index for science, arguing that following which papers cite which would let researchers trace ideas forward through the literature[9]. That idea underlies today’s citation databases and the technique of “citation chaining” described in Section 4.

Standardized styles. Formal citation styles appeared in the twentieth century. The University of Chicago Press issued its first Manual of Style in 1906[10]. A seven-page set of manuscript instructions published in Psychological Bulletin in 1929 became the seed of APA style[11]. The MLA Style Sheet appeared in PMLA in 1951[12]. The “Harvard” author-date system has been traced to an 1881 paper by the Harvard zoologist Edward Laurens Mark[13]. In 1978 a small group of medical journal editors met in Vancouver and agreed on uniform manuscript requirements, which became the ICMJE Recommendations and gave the numbered Vancouver style its name[14].

Evidence-based practice and the replication crisis. In the 1990s, evidence-based medicine was defined as “the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence” in decisions about individual patients[15], and formal systems for grading the certainty of evidence followed[16]. In 2005 Ioannidis argued that, under common conditions of small studies, small effects, and flexible analysis, most published research findings are likely to be false[17]. A decade later, a large collaboration repeated 100 psychology studies and obtained statistically significant results in about 36% of them, compared with 97% of the originals[18]. These episodes taught writers that a single published study is a piece of evidence, not a settled fact.

The digital and AI era. The web made information abundant and authority harder to read from surface cues, which is why current information-literacy guidance stresses that authority is “constructed and contextual”[4] and why researchers now teach fact-checking habits such as lateral reading[19]. Generative AI adds a further challenge, since it can produce fluent text and plausible-looking citations that are wrong or invented[20,21].

3. Defining a research question

Research begins with a question, not a topic. A topic (“social media and teenagers”) names an area; a question (“Does limiting smartphone use during school hours change pupils’ reported sleep?”) names a problem that evidence could answer. Booth and colleagues suggest a three-step test that moves from topic to question to significance: I am studying X, because I want to find out Y, in order to help my reader understand Z[3]. If the third step cannot be completed, the question probably lacks a reason for anyone to read the answer.

3.1 Criteria for a good question

Clinical researchers use the FINER criteria: a good question is Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, and Relevant[22]. Although developed for clinical studies, the criteria apply to any research writing. A question that cannot be answered with available time, data, or access fails the first test no matter how interesting it is.

For questions about interventions and outcomes, the PICO format (Patient or Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) turns a vague concern into a searchable question[23]. Each element also becomes a search concept, which links question design directly to source finding.

3.2 Questions, hypotheses, and scope

A research question asks; a hypothesis proposes a testable answer. Popper argued that a scientific hypothesis must be falsifiable, meaning that some possible observation could show it to be false[24]. Not all writing tests hypotheses. Historical, literary, and policy writing usually advances a thesis, an arguable interpretation supported by evidence. Whatever the form, the writer should decide early what kind of evidence would count as support and what would count against the claim. Deciding this in advance is one of the best protections against confirmation bias, the tendency to seek and favour evidence that fits what one already believes[25].

Questions usually change during research. That is normal, and the ACRL framework describes research as iterative inquiry in which answers produce new questions[4]. The discipline lies in recording how and why the question changed, so the final piece does not claim to answer a question it never asked.

4. Finding credible primary and secondary sources

4.1 Primary, secondary, and tertiary sources

Sources are classified by their distance from the thing studied, and the classification depends on the question being asked[3].

Table 2. Types of sources
TypeDefinitionExamples
PrimaryOriginal evidence or first-hand reports created at the time or by direct participants.Original research articles reporting new data; datasets; letters, diaries, and archival records; laws and court decisions; interviews; literary texts under study.
SecondaryAnalysis, interpretation, or synthesis of primary sources.Review articles, scholarly monographs, critical essays, histories, meta-analyses.
TertiaryCompilations that summarize and point to other sources.Encyclopaedias, textbooks, handbooks, bibliographies, indexes.

The same document can change category. A 1950s newspaper article is a secondary account of a political event but a primary source for a study of how the press covered that event. Writers should therefore classify sources in relation to their own question rather than by format.

Tertiary sources are useful for orientation. A widely reported 2005 comparison in Nature found that expert reviewers identified a broadly similar number of serious errors in science entries in Wikipedia and in Encyclopædia Britannica, although Wikipedia had somewhat more minor errors[26]. The lesson is not that either is a final authority, but that encyclopaedias are starting points whose references lead to citable primary and secondary work.

4.2 Search strategy

Effective searching is planned. The ACRL framework calls it “strategic exploration”[4]. Good practice includes:

  • Breaking the question into concepts (for example, the P, I, C, and O of a PICO question[23]) and listing synonyms for each.
  • Using Boolean operators: OR joins synonyms within a concept, AND joins different concepts, and NOT excludes terms, with care because it can drop relevant records.
  • Using controlled vocabularies such as the US National Library of Medicine’s Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), which index articles by standardized concepts regardless of the words authors used[27].
  • Citation chaining: searching backward through a key article’s reference list and forward to later works that cite it. Webster and Watson recommend both directions when building a literature review[28], an approach that rests on Garfield’s original idea of a citation index[9].
  • Recording the search: databases, dates, search strings, and numbers of results. Systematic reviews must report this in detail, and the PRISMA 2020 statement provides a checklist for doing so[29].

4.3 Kinds of review and hierarchies of evidence

Not every project needs a systematic review, but writers should know which kind of synthesis they are reading or producing. Grant and Booth distinguished 14 types of review, from narrative literature reviews to systematic reviews and meta-analyses, each with different methods and claims to completeness[30].

In health and some social sciences, evidence is often ranked by design, with well-conducted systematic reviews and randomized trials generally offering more protection against bias than case reports or expert opinion. The GRADE approach goes further, rating the certainty of a body of evidence as high, moderate, low, or very low, and allowing that rating to move up or down according to risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias[16]. The underlying idea transfers to any field: the strength of a claim should match the strength and consistency of the evidence behind it.

4.4 Where credible sources are found

Scholarly databases, publishers’ platforms, library catalogues, government and intergovernmental statistical agencies, archives, and the publications of professional bodies are the main routes to credible sources. “Grey literature” such as reports, theses, preprints, and conference papers can be valuable and current but has usually not been peer-reviewed, so it needs closer evaluation. Preprints in particular should be labelled as such when cited.

5. Evaluating authority, relevance, bias, and currency

Finding a source is not the same as trusting it. Evaluation asks four linked questions: Who is speaking and with what expertise (authority)? Does it bear on my question (relevance)? What interests or distortions might shape it (bias)? Is it still current for this purpose (currency)?

5.1 Checklists and their limits

A widely taught checklist is the CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose), developed by Sarah Blakeslee at California State University, Chico[31]. Checklists are useful prompts, but research on how experts evaluate online information shows their limits. Wineburg and McGrew compared professional fact-checkers, PhD historians, and Stanford undergraduates as they judged websites. The fact-checkers were faster and more accurate because they quickly left the site and read laterally, opening new tabs to see what other sources said about the organization behind it. The historians and students tended to read vertically, staying on the page and being swayed by professional design and official-sounding names[19]. Caulfield and Wineburg later summarized such habits as SIFT: Stop; Investigate the source; Find better coverage; Trace claims, quotes, and media to their original context[32].

5.2 Authority

Authority comes from relevant expertise, accountability, and process, not from tone or appearance. The ACRL framework stresses that authority is constructed and contextual: a community health worker may be an authority on local conditions without academic credentials, while a distinguished scientist speaking outside their field may not be[4]. Useful signs of authority include the author’s qualifications in the subject, an identifiable publisher with editorial standards, peer review where appropriate, transparent methods and data, and citations that can be checked.

Peer review is an important but limited signal[8]. So-called predatory journals use the appearance of scholarly publishing while providing little or no genuine review; an international panel defined them as entities that prioritize self-interest at the expense of scholarship, characterized by false or misleading information, deviation from best editorial and publication practices, a lack of transparency, and aggressive, indiscriminate solicitation[33]. Writers should also check whether a work has been corrected or retracted. In a study of 2,047 retracted biomedical and life-science articles, about two thirds of retractions were attributable to misconduct, including fraud or suspected fraud, duplicate publication, and plagiarism[34]. Wakefield and colleagues’ 1998 paper linking the MMR vaccine to autism, formally retracted by The Lancet in 2010[35], shows how long a discredited source can keep circulating.

5.3 Relevance

A source is relevant if it bears directly on the question, population, setting, and time period at issue. Indirect evidence, such as findings from a different population or a proxy outcome, may still be useful, but GRADE treats indirectness as a reason to lower confidence[16]. A frequent error is to cite a source that is about the general topic but does not actually support the specific sentence it is attached to.

5.4 Bias

Bias can sit in the source, in the body of literature, and in the reader.

  • Source-level bias includes conflicts of interest and advocacy. A Cochrane methodology review found that drug and device studies sponsored by manufacturers more often reported favourable efficacy results and conclusions than studies with other funding, and that this difference was not explained by standard risk-of-bias assessments[36].
  • Literature-level bias includes publication bias and selective reporting, which can make a field’s published findings more positive than the truth[17,18].
  • Reader-level bias includes confirmation bias[25] and motivated reasoning, in which people evaluate evidence more leniently when it supports conclusions they want to reach[37]. Misinformation can also continue to influence reasoning even after it has been corrected[38], which is a reason to check sources before absorbing them, not afterwards.

Bias does not automatically disqualify a source. An advocacy group’s report may contain accurate data; the writer’s task is to identify the interest, check the claims against independent sources, and disclose the source’s position to readers where it matters.

5.5 Currency

How recent a source must be depends on the field and question. In fast-moving areas, conclusions decay. Poynard and colleagues examined decades of conclusions in research on liver cirrhosis and hepatitis and estimated that the “half-life” of truth was about 45 years, with many conclusions later judged obsolete or false[39]. Older sources are not inherently weaker. In history, philosophy, or law, a foundational work may remain essential. The point is to know whether newer evidence has superseded, corrected, or retracted a source, and to say so when using older work.

6. Taking notes and synthesizing information

6.1 What note-taking is for

Kiewra’s review of note-taking research distinguished two functions: the encoding function, in which the act of taking notes helps learning, and the storage function, in which notes serve as an external record to review later. Both matter, and reviewing notes contributes substantially to their value[40]. For research writing, the storage function has a further purpose: notes are the chain of custody between a source and the final text.

A well-known 2014 study reported that students taking notes on laptops transcribed more verbatim and performed worse on conceptual questions than students writing by hand[41]. A later replication and extension found the advantage for longhand was not reliably reproduced[42]. The more robust practical lesson concerns processing, not tools: notes that put ideas into the note-taker’s own structure are more useful than transcriptions.

6.2 A note-taking system that prevents errors

Many plagiarism and misattribution problems begin in notes, when a copied sentence later looks like the writer’s own words[43,44]. A robust system separates three kinds of material at the moment of recording:

  1. Exact quotations, in quotation marks, with page or location numbers.
  2. Paraphrases and summaries, marked as the source’s ideas in the writer’s words, also with locations.
  3. The writer’s own ideas, such as comments, objections, and connections, clearly labelled (for example, with initials or a different colour).

Every note should carry full bibliographic details or a link to a reference-manager entry, so that the reference list can be built from records rather than memory.

6.3 From summary to synthesis

Summary reports what one source says. Synthesis relates several sources to each other and to the writer’s question: where they agree, where they conflict, why they might differ (methods, populations, periods, assumptions), and what remains unknown. Webster and Watson argued that a literature review should be concept-centric rather than author-centric, organized around ideas rather than marching through sources one by one, and they proposed a “concept matrix” to make this possible[28].

Graff and Birkenstein describe the rhetorical form of synthesis as moving from “they say” (what others have argued) to “I say” (the writer’s response), so that the writer’s contribution is visible against the existing conversation[45].

7. Quoting, paraphrasing, and summarizing

Writers have three ways of bringing a source’s content into their text. All three require citation[46–48].

Table 5. Quotation, paraphrase, and summary
TechniqueWhat it isWhen to use itCommon faults
QuotationThe source’s exact words, marked with quotation marks or set as a block quotation, with a precise location.When the wording itself matters: definitions, memorable or contested phrasing, primary texts being analysed, or authority that needs exact words.Over-quoting; “dropped” quotations without explanation; altering words without marking changes.
ParaphraseA specific passage restated in the writer’s own words and sentence structure, at roughly similar length.When the idea matters more than the wording, and to show understanding.Patchwriting (changing a few words while keeping the source’s structure); changing the meaning; omitting the citation.
SummaryA condensed account of the main points of a longer passage or whole work.To give context, compare positions, or report findings briefly.Distorting emphasis; summarizing only the abstract or first pages.

7.1 What writers actually do

The Citation Project analysed 1,911 citations in 174 papers written by first-year students at 16 US colleges and universities. Of the citations, 46% were quotations, 32% paraphrases, 16% patchwriting, and only 6% summaries. Nearly half of the cited material (46%) came from the first page of the source, and 69% came from the first two pages[49]. The authors concluded that many students were working at the level of individual sentences rather than engaging with whole sources. This is the most important reason to summarize: it shows that the writer has understood a source as a whole.

7.2 How to summarize well

Research on text comprehension describes summarizing as applying “macrorules” to a text: deleting trivial and redundant material, replacing lists of specifics with a superordinate term, and selecting or constructing a topic sentence[50,51]. Brown and Day found that the more sophisticated rules, especially inventing a topic sentence that the source does not state explicitly, develop late even in skilled readers[51]. A practical method is to read the whole source, close it, write the main point from memory, then reopen the source to check accuracy and add the citation.

7.3 How to paraphrase without patchwriting

Howard named “patchwriting” as copying from a source and then changing some words, rearranging grammar, or substituting synonyms, and argued that it is often a stage in learning to write from sources rather than deliberate cheating[44]. Pecorari found the same pattern in postgraduate second-language writers, many of whom did not intend to deceive[52]. Intent does not change what readers see, however, so writers should paraphrase from understanding rather than from the visible text:

  1. Read the passage until you can explain it without looking.
  2. Write your version from memory, in your own structure.
  3. Compare with the original; if distinctive phrases or the sentence pattern survive, rewrite or quote them.
  4. Cite the source, with a page or location where the style requires it.

Worked example

Original (Sackett and colleagues): evidence-based medicine is “the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients”[15].

Patchwriting (unacceptable): Evidence-based medicine is the careful, clear, and sensible use of the best current evidence when making choices about caring for individual patients.

Paraphrase (acceptable, with citation): Sackett and colleagues argue that clinicians practising evidence-based medicine deliberately and openly weigh the strongest available research when deciding how to treat a particular patient[15].

Summary of the whole article (acceptable, with citation): Sackett and colleagues define evidence-based medicine as combining individual clinical expertise with the best external research evidence, and they reject the charge that it is “cookbook” medicine[15].

7.4 Conventions for quotations

Style manuals set specific rules, but they share principles[46–48]: quote accurately, including spelling and punctuation; mark omissions with ellipses and insertions with square brackets; never alter meaning; give a page, paragraph, or other locator; and use block format for long quotations (APA, for example, uses block format for quotations of 40 words or more[46]). Accuracy needs checking. A meta-analysis of studies of quotation accuracy in medical journals estimated that about one quarter of citations (25.4%) contained a quotation error and that about one in eight (11.9%) contained a major error in which the source did not support the claim[53].

8. Integrating evidence into paragraphs

8.1 The logic of an evidenced paragraph

Toulmin’s model of argument provides a useful anatomy. A claim is supported by data (evidence), connected to the claim by a warrant (the reasoning that explains why the evidence supports the claim), which may need backing, and is limited by a qualifier and possible rebuttals[54]. Weak paragraphs usually omit the warrant: they place a quotation next to a claim and leave the reader to work out the connection.

Graff and Birkenstein describe the remedy as the “quotation sandwich”: introduce the source, present the evidence, then explain what it means and why it matters for the argument[45]. A reliable paragraph pattern is:

  1. Claim: a topic sentence in the writer’s own voice.
  2. Lead-in: a signal phrase identifying the source and, where useful, its credibility.
  3. Evidence: quotation, paraphrase, summary, or data, with citation.
  4. Analysis: the warrant, explaining how the evidence supports the claim.
  5. Qualification or counter-evidence: limits, alternative explanations, conflicting findings.
  6. Link: a sentence connecting back to the paragraph’s claim or forward to the next point.

8.2 Signal phrases and reporting verbs

Swales distinguished integral citations, in which the cited author is part of the sentence (“Ioannidis argued that…”), from non-integral citations, in which the source appears only in brackets or a number[55]. Integral citation foregrounds the source, which is useful when its identity matters; non-integral citation foregrounds the finding. Hyland showed that disciplines differ in how often and how they cite, and that the choice of reporting verb signals the writer’s stance[56]. “Shows” or “demonstrates” implies the writer accepts the claim; “argues” or “suggests” is more neutral; “claims” or “asserts” can imply doubt. Choosing these verbs deliberately is part of using evidence honestly.

Annotated paragraph

Claim Published research should be treated as provisional evidence rather than settled fact. Lead-in and evidence When the Open Science Collaboration repeated 100 studies from leading psychology journals, only about 36% of the replications produced statistically significant results, compared with 97% of the original studies[18]. Analysis Because the replications followed the original designs closely, the gap suggests that many original results overstated the strength of the evidence rather than that the replications were careless. Qualification A failed replication does not prove an original finding false, since both studies are subject to chance and to differences in samples and settings[18]. Link Writers should therefore look for converging evidence across several studies before presenting a finding as established.

8.3 Balancing voices

The writer’s voice should control the paragraph. Common problems are paragraphs that begin or end with a quotation, strings of citations with no analysis, and “source-by-source” structure in which each paragraph reports one author. Synthesis, discussed in Section 6, solves the last problem: paragraphs organized around claims draw on several sources at once[28].

9. Citation and referencing systems: APA, MLA, Chicago, IEEE

9.1 Why citation systems exist

Citations serve several purposes at once: they credit the work of others, let readers find and check sources, show how the writer’s work relates to existing scholarship, and help separate the writer’s contribution from what was borrowed[3,6]. A citation system is a set of conventions for doing this consistently. The international standard ISO 690 sets out general principles for bibliographic references across systems[57]. Disciplines prefer different systems because they value different information. Fields in which the recency of research matters tend to use author-date systems that put the year in the text; humanities fields that quote and analyse texts closely favour author-page or note systems; engineering and medicine favour compact numbered systems.

9.2 The three families of systems

  • Author-date (APA; Chicago author-date; “Harvard” variants): in-text citations give author and year, and an alphabetical reference list gives full details[13,46,48].
  • Author-page (MLA): in-text citations give author and page; a list titled “Works Cited” gives full details[47].
  • Notes (Chicago notes and bibliography): footnotes or endnotes give details, usually with a bibliography[48].
  • Numeric (IEEE; Vancouver): sources are numbered, usually in order of first citation, and the reference list is in numerical order[1,58].

9.3 The major styles

APA (American Psychological Association), 7th edition, 2020. APA is used widely in psychology, education, nursing, and the social sciences. In-text citations take the form (Author, year), with page or paragraph numbers for quotations. For works with three or more authors, the in-text citation uses the first author’s name followed by “et al.” from the first citation, and reference-list entries include up to 20 authors. DOIs are presented as URLs[46]. APA style began with the 1929 Psychological Bulletin instructions[11].

MLA (Modern Language Association), 9th edition, 2021. MLA is used mainly in literature, languages, and cultural studies. It is built around “core elements” (author, title of source, title of container, other contributors, version, number, publisher, publication date, and location) that can be assembled for any source type, and in-text citations usually give author and page, as in (Smith 42)[47]. MLA style descends from the 1951 MLA Style Sheet[12].

Chicago (The Chicago Manual of Style), 18th edition, 2024. Chicago offers two systems. Notes and bibliography, common in history and the arts, uses numbered footnotes or endnotes and a bibliography; author-date, common in the sciences and social sciences, resembles APA[48]. Chicago is also a comprehensive guide to editorial style beyond citation. Its first edition appeared in 1906[10].

IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers). IEEE style is used in electrical engineering, computer science, and related fields. Sources are numbered in square brackets in the order they are first cited, for example [1], and the reference list is in numerical order. Author names are given as initials followed by surname, and journal titles are abbreviated[58].

Vancouver (ICMJE/NLM). The style used in this article is common in biomedicine. Sources are numbered consecutively in order of first citation; numbers are reused for later citations of the same source; journal titles follow NLM abbreviations; and references omit most punctuation and formatting[1,2].

9.5 Choosing and applying a style

The choice of style is usually made by an instructor, publisher, or journal. Once a style is chosen, consistency matters more than any individual rule. Reference-management software can format citations, but it reproduces whatever metadata it is given, so automatically generated entries should be checked against the source and the manual.

10. Bibliographies and reference lists

10.1 Terms

  • A reference list contains only the works cited in the text; this is the APA and Vancouver convention[1,46].
  • Works Cited is MLA’s name for a list of cited works[47].
  • A bibliography may also include works consulted but not cited; Chicago’s notes-and-bibliography system uses this term[48].
  • An annotated bibliography adds a short descriptive or evaluative paragraph to each entry and is a common research assignment in its own right.

10.2 Principles

  1. Correspondence. Every in-text citation has a matching entry, and in reference-list styles every entry is cited in the text[1,46].
  2. Completeness. Entries give enough information to find the source: creator, title, container (journal, book, website), date, and location (volume, issue, pages, article number, DOI, or URL)[47,57].
  3. Order. Alphabetical by first author in APA, MLA, and Chicago; numerical by first citation in IEEE and Vancouver[1,46–48,58].
  4. Persistent identifiers. A Digital Object Identifier (DOI) is preferred over a URL where one exists, because it continues to resolve if a publisher’s website changes[46].
  5. Access dates for web content that may change. Vancouver style records a “cited” date for Internet sources, as in this article’s reference list[1].
  6. Secondary citation. If you read a source only as quoted in another work, cite the work you actually read and indicate the original (for example, APA’s “as cited in”)[46]. Better still, locate and read the original.

10.3 Verifying references

Reference errors are common and consequential. Beyond the quotation errors described in Section 7[53], citation databases can spread mistakes: by tracing the repetition of misprints in citations, Simkin and Roychowdhury estimated that only about one fifth of people who cite a paper have read it[59]. Every entry should be checked against the source itself, not copied from another reference list or generated without checking. This is now essential when AI tools are involved, as discussed in Section 13[21].

11. Avoiding plagiarism and accidental misattribution

11.1 Definitions and history

The word “plagiarism” derives from the Latin plagiarius, a kidnapper, a term the Roman poet Martial applied to a rival who passed off his verses as his own[60]. In US federal research policy, plagiarism is one of three forms of research misconduct, alongside fabrication and falsification, and is defined as “the appropriation of another person’s ideas, processes, results, or words without giving appropriate credit”. The same policy states that research misconduct does not include honest error or differences of opinion[61]. The definition covers ideas and results as well as words, so a properly cited idea paraphrased in new words is not plagiarism, but an uncited idea in new words is.

11.2 Forms of plagiarism and questionable practice

Table 8. Forms of plagiarism and related problems
FormDescription
Verbatim copyingCopying text without quotation marks and citation, even if the source is listed somewhere.
Patchwriting or mosaic plagiarismRetaining a source’s structure and much of its wording while substituting some words[44,52].
Idea plagiarismPresenting another’s argument, method, or finding as one’s own, even in new words[61].
Self-plagiarism and duplicate publicationReusing one’s own published text or data without disclosure, which may mislead readers or editors about novelty[43].
Citation without readingCopying citations from another work as if one had consulted the original[59].
Contract cheating or ghost-writingSubmitting work produced by another person (or, under many current policies, by an undisclosed AI tool) as one’s own[2,62].

11.3 Accidental misattribution

Not all attribution failures are plagiarism. Misattribution, meaning crediting the wrong source, misrepresenting what a source says, or relying on a chain of citations that has drifted from the original, can be just as damaging to the reliability of a text. Three documented mechanisms are:

  • Citation distortion. Greenberg analysed the citation network around a biomedical hypothesis and showed how citation bias (ignoring contradicting papers), amplification (citing papers that contained no primary data), and invention (claiming a source said more than it did) turned a weakly supported claim into apparently established fact[63].
  • Academic urban legends. Rekdal traced the widely repeated story that spinach’s iron content was exaggerated because of a misplaced decimal point and found that this debunking story was itself poorly supported, spread through citations that did not check the underlying sources[64].
  • Quotation errors. As noted above, about one in eight citations in the medical studies analysed by Jergas and Baethge contained a major quotation error[53].

11.4 Prevention

  1. Separate quotations, paraphrases, and your own ideas in your notes from the start (Section 6.2)[43].
  2. Record full source details at the time of reading.
  3. Cite while drafting, not afterwards.
  4. Paraphrase from understanding, then compare with the original (Section 7.3)[44].
  5. Read the original before citing; trace claims back to where they started (the T in SIFT)[32,59].
  6. Check that each citation supports the specific sentence it is attached to[53,63].
  7. When in doubt, cite. Common knowledge, meaning widely known facts that readers in the field would not expect to see referenced, need not be cited, but its boundaries vary by audience.
  8. Disclose reuse of your own previous work and any AI assistance according to the relevant policy[2,43,62].

12. Distinguishing facts, opinions, interpretations, and hypotheses

Evidence supports claims, but claims come in different kinds, and each needs different support. Readers often struggle to tell them apart. In a 2018 Pew Research Center survey, 26% of US adults correctly identified all five factual statements in a set of news statements, and 35% correctly identified all five opinion statements[65].

Table 9. Kinds of claims
Kind of claimDefinitionExampleAppropriate support and language
Fact (empirical statement)A statement that can be checked against evidence and shown true or false, whatever one’s values.The Open Science Collaboration repeated 100 psychology studies[18].Citation to a reliable source; precise wording; no hedging needed if well established.
Opinion (value judgement)A statement of preference, value, or recommendation that cannot be settled by evidence alone.Journals ought to require pre-registration.Reasons and values made explicit; relevant facts cited; acknowledgement of other views.
InterpretationAn explanation of what facts mean, why they occurred, or how they fit together.Low replication rates mainly reflect publication bias.Evidence plus reasoning (warrants); alternative explanations considered; hedged appropriately.
HypothesisA tentative, testable proposition put forward to be examined.Pre-registration will raise replication rates.Stated as testable; framed with conditional language; ideally specifies what would count against it.

12.1 The boundaries are porous

Philosophers of science have argued that observation is “theory-laden”, meaning that what counts as a fact depends partly on the concepts and theories through which it is observed[66], and that scientific communities interpret evidence within shared paradigms that can change[67]. A hypothesis that survives repeated attempts to refute it may come to be treated as established fact, though on Popper’s account it always remains open to future refutation[24]. None of this makes facts arbitrary. It means that writers should be clear about how a claim is known, and should label interpretations as interpretations.

12.2 Language that signals the kind of claim

Academic writers use hedges (“may”, “suggests”, “is likely”) and boosters (“clearly”, “demonstrates”) to signal how confident they are. Hyland showed that hedging in scientific articles is not vagueness but a precise way of matching the strength of a claim to the strength of the evidence, while acknowledging readers’ judgement[68]. The GRADE approach does the same thing formally, linking the wording of conclusions to the certainty of the evidence[16]. A useful rule is that the strength of the verb should match the strength of the evidence: “demonstrates” for well-replicated findings, “suggests” for single or indirect studies, and “proposes” or “hypothesizes” for untested ideas.

13. Research ethics and responsible use of AI-generated material

13.1 Foundations of research ethics

Modern research ethics developed largely in response to abuses. The Declaration of Helsinki, first adopted by the World Medical Association in 1964 and most recently revised in October 2024, sets out ethical principles for medical research involving human participants[69]. The US Belmont Report (1979) named three principles that remain the basis of research ethics review: respect for persons (including informed consent), beneficence (minimizing harm and maximizing benefit), and justice (fair distribution of the burdens and benefits of research)[70]. For writers, these principles apply whenever research involves people: interviews, surveys, observations, and the use of personal data or private documents all raise questions of consent, confidentiality, and harm.

Research integrity concerns the honesty of research itself. International statements such as the 2010 Singapore Statement on Research Integrity[71] and the European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity, which is built on reliability, honesty, respect, and accountability[72], set shared expectations. Steneck’s introduction for the US Office of Research Integrity covers responsible practice in data management, authorship, peer review, conflicts of interest, and collaboration[73]. Data should be managed so that others can find and reuse it where appropriate, following the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable)[74].

Misconduct is not rare enough to ignore. Fanelli’s meta-analysis of surveys found that about 2% of scientists admitted to having fabricated, falsified, or modified data at least once, and up to about one third admitted to other questionable research practices; reported rates for colleagues’ behaviour were higher[75]. Integrity is protected less by catching wrongdoers than by habits that make honest error visible and dishonesty difficult: transparent methods, preserved records, accurate citation, and disclosure.

13.2 What generative AI changes

Large language models generate text by predicting likely sequences of words. They can summarize, translate, rephrase, and suggest structure, but they can also produce fluent statements that are not supported by any source, a failure known as “hallucination”[20]. Fabricated citations are a specific and well-documented case. Walters and Wilder asked ChatGPT to write short literature reviews and checked 636 citations: 55% of those generated by GPT-3.5 and 18% of those generated by GPT-4 referred to works that do not exist, and many of the real citations contained errors[21]. The consequences are not only academic. In Mata v. Avianca, Inc., a US federal court sanctioned lawyers who submitted a brief citing judicial decisions that had been invented by ChatGPT[76].

13.3 Policies from publishers and professional bodies

Policies are still developing, but by 2023 a broad consensus had formed on several points:

  • AI tools cannot be authors. COPE’s position statement says AI tools cannot meet the requirements for authorship because they cannot take responsibility for submitted work, and that authors are fully responsible for the content of their manuscripts, including any parts produced by an AI tool[62]. The ICMJE Recommendations take the same position[2], as did editorials in Nature and Science in January 2023[77,78].
  • Use must be disclosed. COPE, ICMJE, and Nature require writers to describe how AI tools were used, for example in the methods or acknowledgements section[2,62,77].
  • AI output is not an authoritative source. The ICMJE Recommendations advise that AI-generated material should not be cited as a primary source, and that authors must ensure the accuracy of all references and guard against plagiarism in AI-produced text[2].
  • Humans remain accountable. European Commission guidelines for researchers stress that researchers remain responsible for scientific output, should use generative AI transparently, and should avoid substantial use in sensitive activities such as peer review and evaluation[79]. UNESCO guidance for education and research emphasizes human agency, data privacy, and validation of AI outputs[80].

Some policies are stricter than others; Science, for example, initially barred AI-generated text from its papers altogether[78]. Writers must therefore check the current policy of the specific journal, institution, course, or employer.

13.4 Citing and acknowledging AI

Style guides have issued guidance on documenting AI use. APA recommends treating the developer as author and the tool as the title, with the version, in a reference such as: OpenAI. (2023). ChatGPT (Mar 14 version) [Large language model]. https://chat.openai.com/chat, and describing the prompt in the text[81]. MLA advises against treating an AI tool as an author and suggests describing the prompt in the title element of the entry[82]. Because AI output usually cannot be retrieved by readers, writers should keep records of prompts and responses, and some instructors or journals may ask for them as an appendix.

13.5 Detection and fairness

Automated “AI detectors” are unreliable as evidence of misconduct. Liang and colleagues found that seven widely used detectors misclassified more than half of essays written by non-native English speakers for the TOEFL test as AI-generated, while classifying essays by US eighth-grade students far more accurately[83]. Such results are a reason to treat a detector score as, at most, a prompt for conversation rather than proof. The MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI has recommended that educators instead help students develop critical AI literacy, including an understanding of the tools’ limitations[84].

13.6 A protocol for responsible AI use in research writing

  1. Check the rules first. Follow the policy of your institution, course, publisher, or employer[2,62,79].
  2. Use AI as an assistant, not a source. Brainstorming, outlining, language editing, and search-term generation carry less risk than generating claims or citations.
  3. Verify every factual claim and every reference against the original source before using it[20,21]. Never cite a work you have not seen.
  4. Protect confidential material. Do not enter unpublished manuscripts, personal data, or confidential documents into tools whose data handling you do not control[79,80].
  5. Disclose what tool was used, which version, and for what purpose[2,62,77].
  6. Document and cite AI output according to your style guide when you quote or rely on it[81,82].
  7. Take responsibility for the entire text, as authorship policies require[2,62].

14. Conclusion and checklist

The practices described here form one chain. A clear question determines what evidence is relevant. Strategic searching and critical evaluation determine which sources deserve trust. Careful notes preserve the link between sources and ideas. Quotation, paraphrase, and summary bring evidence into the text, and analysis connects it to claims. Citation makes the chain visible so readers can check it. Ethics, including honest use of AI, protects the whole chain. Every link is a point where error, bias, or dishonesty can enter, and every link can be strengthened by the habits that scholarly communities have developed over centuries[3,4,6].

Before you submit

  • Can I state my research question and why it matters in one sentence?
  • Have I used primary sources where my question requires them, and have I classified sources relative to my question?
  • Have I checked the authority, relevance, bias, and currency of each key source, including searching laterally and checking for retractions?
  • Do my notes separate quotations, paraphrases, and my own ideas?
  • Have I synthesized sources by concept rather than summarizing them one at a time?
  • Does every quotation match the original exactly, with a locator?
  • Is every paraphrase in my own words and structure, and cited?
  • Does each paragraph explain how its evidence supports its claim?
  • Does every citation support the specific sentence it is attached to, and have I read the original?
  • Is my style applied consistently, with every in-text citation matched in the reference list?
  • Do my verbs and hedges match the strength of the evidence, and are facts, opinions, interpretations, and hypotheses clearly labelled?
  • Have I obtained the necessary consent or approval for research involving people?
  • Have I verified, disclosed, and, where appropriate, cited any use of AI tools?
