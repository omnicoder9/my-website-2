1Introduction: tools as extensions of judgement

Every writing technology encodes a theory of what writing is. A dictionary assumes that words have recoverable, recordable meanings; a readability formula assumes that difficulty can be estimated from the surface of a text; a version-control system assumes that a document is a sequence of states rather than a single object. Understanding those assumptions is the difference between using a tool and being used by it.

The history of writers' tools is also a history of automation moving steadily up the ladder of abstraction. The earliest aids recorded the spelling and sense of individual words. Mid-twentieth-century researchers began to quantify sentences and passages. The personal computer made text infinitely revisable and, later, shareable in real time. Statistical and neural language technologies now operate on whole discourses, proposing not just corrections but content. Vannevar Bush's 1945 vision of a desk-sized “memex” that would store and link a scholar's records anticipated much of this trajectory [1], and Douglas Engelbart's 1968 demonstration of on-screen text editing, hyperlinking, and shared-screen collaboration showed that it could be engineered [2].

This article surveys ten families of tools. For each it sketches the history, explains the underlying concepts, and assesses the evidence on strengths and limitations. A recurring theme is that tools are most valuable when they surface information for a writer to judge, and most hazardous when they substitute a mechanical verdict for that judgement [3,4].

2Dictionaries and learner dictionaries

From hard words to the whole language

The first monolingual English dictionary is usually identified as Robert Cawdrey's A Table Alphabeticall of 1604, a short list explaining “hard usual English words” borrowed from Latin, Greek, and French [5,6]. For more than a century English dictionaries remained largely glossaries of difficult vocabulary. Samuel Johnson's A Dictionary of the English Language (1755) changed the genre's ambitions: it aimed to cover the general vocabulary and, crucially, illustrated senses with quotations drawn from esteemed writers, establishing citation evidence as the foundation of lexicography [7,8]. Noah Webster's American Dictionary of the English Language (1828) did similar work for American usage and promoted distinct American spellings [6,9].

The Oxford English Dictionary carried Johnson's evidential method to its limit. Planned by the Philological Society and edited for decades under James Murray, it was published in fascicles from 1884 and completed in 1928; its “historical principles” meant that every sense was dated and documented by quotations from its earliest recorded use onward [10]. Historical dictionaries of this kind remain the authoritative record of what words have meant, but they are descriptive archives rather than guides to writing.

Descriptive and prescriptive dictionaries

Modern lexicography is predominantly descriptive: dictionaries record how a language is used, with usage labels (informal, offensive, dated, chiefly British) and usage notes signalling social and stylistic constraints [6,11]. Writers sometimes treat a dictionary entry as a licence (“it's in the dictionary, so it's correct”); a more accurate reading is that inclusion shows that a form is attested, while labels and notes show where it is appropriate.

The learner's dictionary

A distinct tradition grew from teaching English as a foreign language. Harold Palmer and A. S. Hornby, working in Japan in the 1920s and 1930s, developed controlled vocabularies and grammatical “verb patterns” for learners. Hornby's Idiomatic and Syntactic English Dictionary, published in Tokyo by Kaitakusha in 1942, was reissued by Oxford University Press in 1948 and became the Advanced Learner's Dictionary [12]. Its innovation was to give not only meanings but the syntactic information needed to produce a word correctly: countability of nouns, the complements a verb takes, and example sentences. Michael West's General Service List of about 2,000 high-frequency words, published in 1953, supplied another key idea, the restricted defining vocabulary, which later learner dictionaries adopted so that definitions use only common words [12,13].

The second revolution was the corpus. The Collins COBUILD English Language Dictionary (1987), edited by John Sinclair at the University of Birmingham, was compiled from a large computerized collection of real texts rather than from earlier dictionaries and lexicographers' intuitions [14,15]. Corpus evidence revealed which senses are actually frequent, how words cluster, and that many intuitive “core” meanings are statistically marginal. Corpus-based compilation is now standard practice across major dictionary publishers [11].

Using dictionaries well

  • Choose by task. A historical dictionary answers “what has this word meant?”; a learner's dictionary answers “how do I use this word correctly?” and is often the better tool for native speakers too when the question is grammatical behaviour [12].
  • Read the whole entry. Labels, grammar codes, and example sentences carry as much information as the definition [11].
  • Check the corpus when the dictionary is silent. Freely searchable corpora such as the Corpus of Contemporary American English let a writer see a word in thousands of authentic contexts [16].

3Thesauruses and collocation dictionaries

Roget's design

Peter Mark Roget, a physician and polymath, published his Thesaurus of English Words and Phrases in 1852 [17]. Its subtitle stated the purpose plainly: words were “classified and arranged so as to facilitate the expression of ideas.” Unlike an alphabetical synonym list, Roget organized vocabulary into a conceptual hierarchy of classes, sections, and numbered heads, inspired by earlier philosophical schemes for classifying knowledge; the alphabetical index was a finding aid into that structure [18]. The conceptual arrangement places a word among its neighbours, antonyms, and related notions, which suits the writer who knows the idea but not the word.

From synonyms to semantic networks

Computational lexicography reframed the thesaurus as a network. WordNet, developed at Princeton under George Miller, grouped English nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs into sets of cognitive synonyms (“synsets”) linked by relations such as hypernymy (a dog is a kind of canine) and meronymy (a wheel is part of a car) [19]. Resources of this type underpin many digital synonym suggesters.

The central caution about any thesaurus is that true synonymy is rare. Words listed together typically differ in connotation, register, intensity, or grammatical behaviour, so a substitute that looks equivalent can change meaning or sound unidiomatic. A thesaurus is a prompt for recall, not a source of guaranteed equivalents; a dictionary or corpus check should follow any unfamiliar choice [11,19].

Collocation: the company words keep

The British linguist J. R. Firth argued in 1957 that part of a word's meaning lies in its habitual companions, summarized in his often-quoted dictum “you shall know a word by the company it keeps” [20]. Sinclair later developed this into the “idiom principle”: speakers draw on a large stock of semi-preconstructed phrases rather than assembling every sentence freely from grammar and vocabulary [15]. Native speakers make a decision but take a photograph, voice strong criticism but not powerful criticism, and these preferences are largely arbitrary.

Collocation dictionaries codify such combinations. The BBI Combinatory Dictionary of English (1986) distinguished grammatical collocations (a word plus a preposition or construction, such as interested in) from lexical collocations (such as commit a crime) [21]; the Oxford Collocations Dictionary for Students of English (2002) presented corpus-derived combinations grouped by part of speech and meaning [22]. Research on advanced learners has found that collocational errors persist even when grammar is strong, and that verb–noun combinations are especially error-prone [23].

Corpus query tools extend the collocation dictionary indefinitely. Systems such as the Sketch Engine produce “word sketches”, automatic summaries of a word's grammatical and collocational behaviour ranked by statistical association, and are used both by lexicographers and by writers checking whether a phrase is idiomatic [24].

4Grammar and spelling checkers

The mechanics of spell checking

Automatic spelling correction is one of the oldest problems in natural language processing. In 1964 Fred Damerau reported that over 80 percent of the spelling errors in his sample were a single insertion, deletion, substitution, or transposition of adjacent letters, and proposed matching misspellings against a dictionary by allowing exactly one such operation [25]. Vladimir Levenshtein's contemporaneous work defined the minimum number of insertions, deletions, and substitutions needed to turn one string into another, now called edit distance, which remains the basis of candidate generation [26]. James Peterson's 1980 survey in Communications of the ACM described the architecture that most spell checkers still share: detect tokens absent from a word list, generate nearby candidates, and rank them [27].

Dictionary lookup has a structural blind spot. A misspelling that produces another valid word (form for from, their for there) passes unnoticed. Karen Kukich's comprehensive 1992 review classified the problem into non-word error detection, isolated-word correction, and context-dependent correction, and documented that real-word errors form a substantial share of errors in practice and require models of context to catch [28].

Grammar checking: rules, then statistics, then neural networks

Grammar checking began in the late 1970s and 1980s with rule- and pattern-based programs. Bell Laboratories' Writer's Workbench for UNIX included utilities that flagged wordy and overused phrases, doubled words, punctuation slips, potentially sexist terms, and heavy use of the passive, and it was trialled in university writing instruction [29]. Commercial checkers for personal computers followed. Microsoft's grammar checker, introduced in Word in the late 1990s, used a broad-coverage syntactic parser developed by George Heidorn's group, attempting to analyse whole sentences rather than match surface patterns [30].

The field now known as grammatical error correction (GEC) moved in the 2010s from hand-written rules to statistical machine translation, treating an erroneous sentence as a “source language” to be translated into a corrected one, and then to neural sequence-to-sequence models and large pretrained language models [31]. Bryant and colleagues' 2023 survey describes how systems are evaluated against human-annotated learner corpora using measures that reward precision more heavily than recall, because an unnecessary “correction” is usually more damaging to a writer than a missed one [31].

What the evidence says about use

Checkers help when they are right and hurt when they are wrong, and writers tend to trust them. In an experiment by Galletta and colleagues, participants proofreading with a language checker corrected more of the errors the software flagged correctly but also accepted its false alarms and missed more of the errors it failed to flag; even participants with high verbal ability performed worse than without the software on those items [3]. Earlier evaluations of grammar checkers reached similar conclusions about uneven coverage and pedagogical value [32].

Practical guidance

  • Treat every suggestion as a question (“is this an error?”) rather than an instruction [3].
  • Proofread a final draft without the checker as well as with it, because real-word errors and context-dependent errors are the hardest to detect automatically [28].
  • Add specialised terms and names to a custom dictionary so genuine errors are not lost among false alarms [27].
  • Be wary of rules that encode contested usage advice (for example, blanket bans on the passive voice or on split infinitives) [33].

5Style and readability analyzers

Readability formulas

The readability movement arose in American education and journalism in the 1920s through the 1940s, seeking to match texts to readers' abilities [34]. Its best-known products estimate difficulty from two surface features: sentence length, a proxy for syntactic complexity, and word length or familiarity, a proxy for vocabulary load.

The Flesch–Kincaid grade level was derived for the United States Navy by recalibrating existing formulas against the comprehension of enlisted personnel reading technical training material [38]. Formulas of this family are now built into mainstream word processors and are referenced by plain-language policy; the United States Plain Writing Act of 2010, for example, requires federal agencies to write public documents in “clear, concise, well-organized” language [39].

The limits of counting

Readability formulas measure correlates of difficulty, not difficulty itself. They cannot see whether the ideas are logically ordered, whether terms are defined, whether the text suits its audience, or whether a short word is obscure. Janice Redish, writing from the perspective of document design, argued that formulas are poor guides for revision: a writer who shortens sentences and words to improve a score may make a text choppier and less coherent without making it easier to understand [4]. More sophisticated tools such as Coh-Metrix compute dozens of indices of cohesion, syntactic complexity, and lexical sophistication, which better reflect the linguistic and discourse features that affect comprehension, although they remain descriptive measures rather than verdicts [40].

Style analyzers

Style checkers flag features associated with weak prose: wordy phrases, nominalisations, clichés, hedges, adverbs, and passive constructions. Writer's Workbench pioneered this genre in the late 1970s and early 1980s [29]. Much of the advice such tools encode descends from usage manuals such as Strunk and White's The Elements of Style, whose injunction to “omit needless words” remains influential [41]. Linguists have criticised parts of that tradition as inaccurate; Geoffrey Pullum, for instance, has argued that its strictures on the passive voice are confused and frequently violated in its own examples [33]. The sensible use of a style analyzer is therefore diagnostic: it highlights patterns a writer may wish to reconsider, and the writer decides whether each instance serves the reader.

6Reference managers and citation tools

Why citation styles exist

Citation systems let readers locate and evaluate the evidence behind claims. Hundreds of styles exist, but they fall into a few families: author–date systems (such as APA and Harvard), notes-and-bibliography systems (such as Chicago's humanities style), and numeric systems. The Vancouver system, used in this article, takes its name from a 1978 meeting in Vancouver of medical journal editors who agreed uniform requirements for manuscripts; the group became the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) [42]. In Vancouver style, sources are numbered consecutively in the order in which they are first cited, each later citation of the same source reuses its number, and the reference list is ordered numerically. Detailed formats are specified by the United States National Library of Medicine's Citing Medicine [43].

From card files to citation engines

Desktop reference management software emerged in the 1980s alongside word processing. In the TeX ecosystem, Oren Patashnik's BibTeX (documented in 1988) separated a database of bibliographic records from style files that format them, an architecture that later tools generalised [44]. Zotero, released in 2006 by the Center for History and New Media at George Mason University as a free, open-source tool integrated into the web browser, made capturing records directly from library catalogues and publisher pages routine [45].

Zotero's developers also helped establish the Citation Style Language (CSL), an open XML format for describing citation styles; Zotero was the first program to adopt it, and its shared repository of styles is now used by numerous reference managers and publishing tools [46]. Persistent identifiers complete the infrastructure: the Digital Object Identifier (DOI), standardised as ISO 26324, gives a work a permanent identifier that resolves to its current location and allows software to retrieve its metadata automatically [47].

Accuracy and verification

Reference managers automate formatting, not correctness. A study comparing EndNote, Mendeley, RefWorks, and Zotero across medical citation styles found that every tool generated errors, with online sources (URLs and access dates) especially problematic, and that the number of errors differed by tool [48]. Imported metadata may contain wrong capitalisation, missing issue numbers, or confused authors. The remedy is a final manual check of each reference against the source itself or an authoritative record.

Practical guidance

  • Capture sources at the moment of reading, attaching notes and the full text where licences allow [45].
  • Prefer records retrieved by DOI or from a library catalogue over manually typed ones [47].
  • Verify the final list against the style manual the journal or publisher specifies [43,48].

7Word processors and collaborative editing

The office of the future

“Word processing” was originally an office-management concept of the 1960s and 1970s, tied to automatic typewriters and dedicated text-editing machines that stored keystrokes on magnetic media so that letters could be revised and retyped mechanically [49]. Meanwhile, research laboratories explored interactive screen editing; Engelbart's NLS system, demonstrated in 1968, combined a mouse, structured documents, hyperlinks, and real-time collaboration between remote users [2].

The microcomputer brought word processing to individuals. Thomas Bergin's history identifies Electric Pencil, offered for sale in December 1976, as the first word-processing program for a microcomputer, followed by WordStar in 1979, WordPerfect, and Microsoft Word, introduced for MS-DOS in 1983 [50]. Matthew Kirschenbaum's literary history documents how novelists and poets adopted these tools from the late 1970s onward and how the ability to revise without retyping altered composing practices and the archival record of drafts [51].

Key concepts

  • WYSIWYG versus structure. Most word processors present text as it will print (“what you see is what you get”). Their more powerful features, such as paragraph styles, automatic headings, cross-references, and tables of contents, depend on the writer marking structure rather than formatting text by hand, an idea discussed further under markup below [52].
  • Revision marking. Tracked changes and comments record who altered what, making the editorial conversation visible. They also preserve deleted text inside a file, so documents should be reviewed and cleaned before external release [51].
  • Real-time co-editing. When several people edit one document simultaneously, their operations must be reconciled so that all copies converge. Ellis and Gibbs introduced operational transformation in 1989, adjusting each incoming edit to account for edits already applied [53]. A later family of algorithms, conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs), guarantees convergence mathematically without a central server deciding the order of edits [54].

Ownership and resilience

Cloud-based editors made collaboration effortless but placed documents on remote services. The “local-first” software movement argues that writers should be able to keep full, offline copies of their work in open formats while still collaborating, and identifies CRDTs as one enabling technology [55]. Whatever the platform, writers should keep exports in open formats and independent backups of important work.

8Markdown, HTML, and document formatting

Separating structure from appearance

Markup languages embed instructions in plain text. Jerome Saltzer's RUNOFF program, written for MIT's Compatible Time-Sharing System in 1964, formatted documents from text interspersed with dot commands and is an ancestor of the UNIX roff family [56]. Brian Reid's Scribe system (1980) introduced the decisive distinction between declaring what a piece of text is, such as a heading or a quotation, and leaving how it should look to a separate style definition [57]. Charles Goldfarb's work at IBM on generalized markup, which led to the Standard Generalized Markup Language (SGML), made the same argument for publishing at scale [58]. Coombs, Renear, and DeRose's 1987 analysis in Communications of the ACM set out why descriptive markup produces documents that are easier to maintain, reformat, and reuse than presentational formatting [52].

Donald Knuth's TeX (1978 onward, described in The TeXbook, 1984) brought typographic quality to mathematical and scientific text [59], and Leslie Lamport's LaTeX layered structural commands such as \section on top of it, becoming a standard in mathematics, physics, and computer science [60].

HTML and the web

Tim Berners-Lee's 1989 proposal at CERN for a linked information system led to the World Wide Web and to HTML, an SGML-inspired markup language for hypertext [61]. HTML is now maintained as a continuously updated “living standard” by the WHATWG [62]. For writers, its most important feature is semantic: elements such as headings, lists, emphasis, quotations, and citations convey structure that browsers, search engines, and assistive technologies interpret. The W3C's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, for instance, depend on correct semantic structure and text alternatives for images so that content is usable by people who rely on screen readers [63].

Markdown and lightweight markup

John Gruber released Markdown in 2004 with the stated goal of a plain-text formatting syntax that is readable as-is, with conventions borrowed from email: asterisks for emphasis, hash marks for headings, hyphens for lists [64]. Gruber's original description left many edge cases ambiguous, and implementations diverged; the CommonMark project, led by John MacFarlane and others, produced a rigorous specification with an extensive test suite [65]. MacFarlane's Pandoc converts among dozens of formats, letting a writer draft in Markdown and publish as HTML, Word, LaTeX, PDF, or EPUB, with citations processed through CSL styles [46,66].

9Version control and revision tracking

Comparing texts

Revision control rests on algorithms that compute differences between versions. Hunt and McIlroy's 1976 algorithm, implemented as the UNIX diff utility, finds a longest common subsequence of lines and reports the rest as insertions and deletions [67]. Eugene Myers's 1986 algorithm computes a minimal edit script efficiently and underlies the default comparison in many modern tools [68].

From SCCS to Git

Marc Rochkind's Source Code Control System (SCCS), developed at Bell Laboratories and described in 1975, stored a file's complete history as a series of changes and could reconstruct any earlier version [69]. Walter Tichy's Revision Control System (RCS) of the early 1980s stored reverse deltas, keeping the latest version readily available, and supported branches for parallel lines of development [70]. Distributed systems later gave every collaborator a complete copy of the history. Git, created in 2005 by Linus Torvalds for Linux kernel development, records snapshots of an entire project, makes branching and merging inexpensive, and identifies every state by a cryptographic hash [71].

Why writers use it

Version control was designed for software, but its concepts map well onto writing. A commit is a deliberate, annotated save point; a branch is an alternative draft that can be explored without disturbing the main text; a merge reconciles parallel revisions; and the log is a narrative of how the document evolved [71]. These tools work best with plain-text formats such as Markdown and LaTeX, because differences are computed line by line; binary word-processor files can be stored but not meaningfully compared [67,71]. Writers who keep each sentence or clause on its own line obtain particularly readable differences.

Within word processors, tracked changes and version history provide a simpler revision record suited to editorial exchange [51]. The two approaches are complementary: tracked changes support negotiation between author and editor on a single draft, whereas version control preserves the full lineage of a project and makes recovery from mistakes reliable.

10AI-assisted brainstorming, editing, and feedback

Background

Conversational programs have a long pedigree. Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA (1966) imitated a psychotherapist through simple pattern matching, and Weizenbaum was disturbed by how readily users attributed understanding to it [72]. Modern systems rest on the transformer architecture introduced in 2017, which models relations among all words in a passage through “attention” [73]. Scaling such models on very large text collections produced large language models (LLMs) that can perform many writing tasks from instructions and a few examples [74].

An LLM generates text by repeatedly predicting a plausible continuation. That mechanism explains both its fluency and its characteristic failure: output can be well-formed yet unsupported. Researchers call this “hallucination” and have documented it across summarisation, question answering, and dialogue [75]. Critics have also stressed that fluency is not comprehension and that training data carry social biases into outputs [76].

Uses in the writing process

  • Brainstorming. Alex Osborn's mid-century brainstorming method separated generating ideas from judging them [77]. LLMs can serve the generative phase, proposing angles, counterarguments, titles, or examples. In a controlled experiment, short stories written with access to AI-generated ideas were rated as more creative and better written, especially for less creative writers, but the AI-assisted stories were also more similar to one another, a loss of collective diversity [78].
  • Drafting and editing. In a preregistered experiment with college-educated professionals performing occupation-specific writing tasks, access to ChatGPT reduced time taken by about 40 percent and raised evaluated quality by about 18 percent, with the largest gains for weaker writers [79]. Datasets that log human–AI co-writing sessions keystroke by keystroke show how writers accept, modify, or reject suggestions, giving researchers a basis for studying the effect of AI on the writing process [80].
  • Feedback. LLMs can play the role of a critical reader, flagging unclear passages or gaps in an argument. Their feedback is most useful when the writer asks specific questions and treats responses as hypotheses to evaluate.

Risks and responsible practice

The most serious risk for writers of non-fiction is fabricated evidence. When Walters and Wilder asked ChatGPT to write short literature reviews, 55 percent of the citations produced by GPT-3.5 and 18 percent of those produced by GPT-4 referred to works that did not exist, and many real citations contained substantive errors [81]. Every fact, quotation, and reference produced with AI assistance must therefore be verified against a primary source.

Scholarly publishing has set norms for disclosure and accountability. The Committee on Publication Ethics holds that AI tools cannot be listed as authors because they cannot take responsibility for the work, and that authors must disclose how such tools were used [82]. The ICMJE recommendations likewise state that chatbots should not be listed as authors, require disclosure of AI assistance, and make human authors responsible for the accuracy of any AI-assisted content [42].

Practical guidance

  • Use AI for options, not answers: request several alternatives and choose among them deliberately [78].
  • Never accept a citation, statistic, or quotation from a model without locating the source yourself [75,81].
  • Keep a record of how AI was used so that it can be disclosed accurately where required [42,82].
  • Revise generated text into your own voice; homogenised prose is a recognised side effect [78].

11Fact-checking and plagiarism detection

The fact-checking tradition

Institutional fact-checking has two lineages. Magazines developed in-house checkers who verified every factual claim in an article before publication; from the early 2000s, independent organisations began publicly assessing the claims of politicians and other public figures. Lucas Graves's ethnography of American fact-checkers describes the methods of this second movement: selecting checkable claims, tracing them to original data and documents, consulting experts, and publishing transparent reasoning [83]. The Verification Handbook, written by journalists and humanitarian practitioners, codifies techniques for verifying user-generated content such as images and videos during breaking news, including checking provenance, location, and timing [84].

A key finding for everyday writers comes from research on how experts evaluate websites. Wineburg and McGrew found that professional fact-checkers outperformed historians and university students by “reading laterally”: rather than studying a page closely, they quickly left it to see what other sources said about its author or sponsor [85].

Automated fact-checking

Computational fact-checking divides the task into stages: identifying claims worth checking, retrieving evidence, and predicting whether the evidence supports or refutes the claim [86]. ClaimBuster, for example, scored sentences from political debates by how check-worthy they are [87], and the FEVER dataset of claims labelled against Wikipedia evidence became a standard benchmark for verification systems [88]. Guo, Schlichtkrull, and Vlachos's survey concludes that such systems are useful aids for human checkers but face unsolved problems, including the availability of trustworthy evidence and the need to justify verdicts [86].

Plagiarism detection

Text-matching software compares a submission against a database of web pages, publications, and previously submitted work. The core technique is fingerprinting: documents are broken into overlapping sequences of words or characters, which are hashed, and a selection of hashes is compared. Andrei Broder formalised measures of the “resemblance” and “containment” of documents based on such shingles [89], and Schleimer, Wilkerson, and Aiken's winnowing algorithm chooses fingerprints so that any sufficiently long shared passage is guaranteed to be detected [90].

Detection tools report similarity, not plagiarism. A match may be a properly quoted and cited passage, a common phrase, or a reference list; conversely, paraphrase and translation can evade matching. A systematic review of academic plagiarism detection emphasises that tools support, but cannot replace, expert human judgement [91], and a comparative test of fifteen text-matching systems found substantial differences in their ability to detect various forms of plagiarism and generally poor performance on paraphrased and translated text [92].

Detecting AI-generated text

The arrival of LLMs prompted tools that claim to identify machine-generated writing. Independent evaluation has been sobering. A test of fourteen detection tools concluded that they were neither accurate nor reliable and were easily defeated by light editing or paraphrasing [93]. Liang and colleagues found that several popular detectors misclassified a majority of essays written by non-native English speakers as AI-generated, while classifying essays by native-speaking students accurately, a bias with obvious consequences for fairness [94]. Theoretical and empirical work further suggests that as language models improve, reliable detection becomes increasingly difficult [95]. Detector output should never by itself be treated as proof of misconduct.

Practical guidance

  • Check claims against primary sources and read laterally to assess unfamiliar sources [83,85].
  • Run your own drafts through a similarity checker where one is available to catch accidental close paraphrase or missing quotation marks, then fix attribution rather than rewording to evade matching [91].
  • Interpret similarity and AI-detection scores as prompts for human review, never as verdicts [93,94].

12Putting it together: a principled toolkit

The tools surveyed here fall naturally into stages of the writing process. During research and planning, reference managers capture sources and AI tools can widen the space of ideas. During drafting, a word processor or plain-text editor, structured by styles or markup, keeps the document organised, while dictionaries, thesauruses, and collocation resources support precise word choice. During revision, version control or tracked changes preserve the history, and style, readability, grammar, and spelling tools surface candidates for improvement. Before publication, fact-checking, citation verification, and similarity checking protect the integrity of the work.

Across all of these, a consistent principle emerges from the evidence: tools perform best as instruments that make features of a text visible, such as an unusual collocation, a long sentence, an unsupported claim, or a reused passage, so that a writer can decide what to do. They perform worst when their outputs are mistaken for judgements about meaning, quality, truth, or honesty. Bush and Engelbart imagined machines that would augment human intellect rather than replace it [1,2]; for writers, that remains the right standard by which to choose and use their tools.
