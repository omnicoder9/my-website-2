Wide-Bandgap Semiconductors

Silicon carbide, gallium nitride and the ultra-wide-bandgap newcomers (gallium oxide, diamond and aluminium nitride): why a bigger bandgap makes smaller, cooler, faster power and RF electronics, and what it takes to build, drive and trust the devices.

1What "wide bandgap" buys you

A semiconductor is usually called wide-bandgap (WBG) when its bandgap is well above silicon's 1.12 eV, in practice about 2–4 eV; the two commercially dominant WBG materials are 4H-silicon carbide (3.26 eV) and gallium nitride (3.39 eV).1,2 Materials whose gap is substantially wider than GaN's, such as β-Ga2O3 (≈4.8 eV), diamond (5.5 eV) and AlN (≈6.0 eV), are now grouped as ultra-wide-bandgap (UWBG) semiconductors.3

The bandgap matters because the energy needed to break a bond and create an electron–hole pair sets how strong an electric field the crystal can withstand before impact ionisation runs away. To a rough approximation the critical (breakdown) field ℰc rises as a power of the bandgap, close to Eg2 for direct-gap materials and steeper still in some UWBG fits, so a 3× wider gap brings close to a 10× higher critical field.3,4 Almost every advantage follows from that one fact, plus the very low intrinsic carrier density a wide gap implies:

≈10×higher critical field of 4H-SiC and GaN than Si1,5
≈1/10drift-layer thickness for the same blocking voltage5
>300×Baliga figure of merit of 4H-SiC vs Si (this article's Table 1)
3.3×thermal conductivity of SiC vs Si (4.9 vs 1.5 W/cm·K)1
  • Thinner, more heavily doped drift regions. A power device must drop its whole blocking voltage across a depletion region. Ten times the field means a tenth of the thickness, and the allowable doping rises about a hundredfold, so the drift resistance falls by two to three orders of magnitude in the ideal limit.6,5
  • Unipolar devices at high voltage. Silicon needs conductivity modulation (IGBTs and PiN diodes) above roughly 600 V, which brings stored charge and slow turn-off. SiC and GaN can stay unipolar (MOSFETs, HEMTs, Schottky diodes) up to 1.2–3.3 kV and beyond, so switching is limited by capacitance, not by recombination.5,7
  • High-temperature operation. The intrinsic carrier density ni ∝ exp(−Eg/2kT) of 4H-SiC is about 10−9 cm−3 at room temperature against 1010 cm−3 for Si, so junction leakage stays negligible at temperatures where silicon becomes intrinsic.8,1 (Figure 2 of Semiconductor Devices plots ni(T) for all five common materials.) In practice packaging, gate oxides and solder joints, not the semiconductor, set the upper temperature limit.7
  • High saturation velocity and short-wavelength light. High ℰc and electron saturation velocity give GaN its RF power density,9 and direct WBG/UWBG gaps emit blue, ultraviolet and deep-UV light.10,11

The price is paid in materials: WBG crystals are hard to grow, defect densities are orders of magnitude higher than in silicon, p-type doping is difficult or impossible in several of them, and the devices switch so fast that they expose every parasitic in a circuit. Sections 7, 9 and 10 deal with those costs.

2.1Polytypes and crystal structure

SiC exists in more than 200 polytypes, all built from the same Si–C bilayer stacked in different sequences. The cubic 3C, and hexagonal 4H and 6H forms are the important ones; 4H-SiC (an ABCB stacking that repeats every four bilayers) won for power devices because it has the widest gap of the three and an electron mobility that is high and nearly isotropic, whereas 6H's mobility along the c-axis is several times lower.1 GaN and AlN crystallise in the hexagonal wurtzite structure, which lacks inversion symmetry; that is the origin of the spontaneous and piezoelectric polarisation exploited in section 5.17 β-Ga2O3 is monoclinic and strongly anisotropic, and diamond is cubic.12,14

3Material figures of merit

Why it matters: figures of merit let you rank materials before you have a single device, and they explain why mobility, the headline number for logic, matters less than critical field for power.

3.1Baliga's figure of merit

Consider the drift region of an ideal vertical unipolar device: a uniformly doped n-layer that must support a blocking voltage VB with a triangular field profile whose peak equals ℰc. Poisson's equation fixes the thickness and doping, and the resistance of that layer per unit area follows directly:6,5

WD = 2VBℰc,  ND = εsℰc22qVB,  Ron,sp = WDqµnND = 4VB2εsµnℰc3(3.1)

Baliga introduced the denominator, BFOM = εsµnℰc3, in 1982 as the measure of a material's suitability for low-frequency power switches, where conduction loss dominates.6 The cube on ℰc is the whole story: 4H-SiC has slightly lower mobility than Si, yet its BFOM is several hundred times higher. Worked example 3.1 · A 1200 V drift layer

Design the drift layer of an ideal 1200 V 4H-SiC unipolar device and compare it with silicon.

WD = 2 × 1200 / 2.5 × 106 V/cm = 9.6 × 10−4 cm = 9.6 µm. ND = (9.7 × 8.854 × 10−14)(2.5 × 106)2 / (2 × 1.602 × 10−19 × 1200) = 1.4 × 1016 cm−3. Ron,sp = 4(1200)2 / (8.59 × 10−13 × 1000 × 1.5625 × 1019) = 0.43 mΩ·cm2.

Silicon needs an 80 µm layer doped at 2.4 × 1014 cm−3, giving 153 mΩ·cm2, about 350× worse. A 1200 V silicon MOSFET is therefore impractical, which is why silicon uses the bipolar IGBT at this voltage. Commercial 1200 V SiC MOSFETs reach a few mΩ·cm2, several times the ideal drift value, because the MOS channel, JFET region and substrate add resistance in series.1,7

3.2Johnson's figure of merit

In 1965 E. O. Johnson of RCA argued that a transistor's transit-time-limited cut-off frequency fT and its maximum voltage cannot both be raised at will: a carrier crossing a region of length L at vsat takes L/vsat, while that region can hold at most ℰcL volts.18 Eliminating L gives the Johnson figure of merit:

fT · Vmax ≤ ℰcvsat2π ≡ JFOM(3.2)

JFOM ranks materials for high-frequency power amplifiers. With Table 1 values it is about 0.48 THz·V for Si and 13 THz·V for GaN, so a GaN transistor with fT = 100 GHz could in principle sustain about 130 V, against about 5 V for silicon. That is the physical root of GaN's dominance in high-power microwave amplifiers.9

4Silicon carbide devices

Why it matters: SiC is the WBG technology of high voltage and high power, 650 V to 3.3 kV and above, from EV traction inverters to rail and grid.

SiC has two advantages that set it apart from every other WBG material. It can be doped n-type (nitrogen) and p-type (aluminium) by ion implantation over wide ranges, and it is the only compound semiconductor that grows a native SiO2 by thermal oxidation, so the silicon MOSFET's process flow carries over with modifications.1 It also conducts heat about three times better than silicon.

4.1Schottky and JBS diodes

The first commercial SiC power devices were Schottky barrier diodes (SBDs): Infineon announced 300 V and 600 V parts in February 2001, calling itself the first manufacturer producing SiC Schottky diodes.23 A Schottky diode is a majority-carrier device and stores no minority charge, so at turn-off it only has to discharge its junction capacitance. Replacing a silicon fast-recovery PiN diode with a SiC SBD in a boost PFC stage or a half-bridge removes the reverse-recovery current spike that otherwise flows through the opposite switch at every turn-on, which cuts that switch's turn-on loss sharply.7,24

Pure Schottky diodes have high leakage at high reverse bias, because the peak field sits at the metal interface and lowers the barrier. Practical SiC diodes are therefore junction-barrier Schottky (JBS) or merged PiN-Schottky (MPS) structures: implanted p+ islands under the anode pinch off and shield the Schottky regions in reverse bias, and in MPS designs they also inject holes during surge currents, giving the diode a bipolar surge rating.1,5

4.2SiC MOSFETs

Cree launched the first commercial SiC power MOSFET, the 1200 V, 80 mΩ CMF20120D, in January 2011,25 a decade after the diodes. The delay was the gate oxide. The SiC/SiO2 interface has a far higher density of interface traps than Si/SiO2, partly from carbon left near the interface during oxidation, and those traps capture channel electrons and scatter the rest. Inversion-channel mobility in early devices was only a few cm2/V·s; post-oxidation anneals in nitric oxide (NO) raised it to roughly 20–50 cm2/V·s, still a small fraction of the bulk value.1 The channel resistance is therefore a large share of RDS(on) at 650–1200 V, which drives designers to pack more channel width per unit area.

Three practical consequences of SiC MOS physics shape circuit design (section 10):

  • Low transconductance, high gate drive. The channel only becomes fully conductive at VGS ≈ 15–20 V, so SiC MOSFETs are driven harder than silicon MOSFETs, and RDS(on) at 12 V can be much higher than at 18 V.7
  • Low, temperature-dependent threshold. VGS(th) is typically only 2–4 V and falls with temperature, and charge trapping in the oxide can shift it under gate bias stress.26
  • Usable body diode with a high forward drop. The intrinsic PiN body diode conducts at about 3 V because of the wide gap, and conducting it can trigger bipolar degradation in material with basal-plane dislocations (section 9).27

4.3Bipolar and very-high-voltage SiC

Above about 10 kV even SiC's unipolar resistance becomes large, and SiC PiN diodes, IGBTs and thyristors with conductivity-modulated drift regions are being developed for grid and pulsed-power applications. Their high built-in voltage (≈3 V) makes them worthwhile only at very high voltage, and they are directly exposed to the bipolar-degradation mechanism described in section 9.1,27

5Gallium nitride devices

Why it matters: GaN dominates RF power amplifiers and is taking over fast power conversion below about 650 V, from phone chargers to data-centre supplies.

5.1Polarisation and the two-dimensional electron gas

The wurtzite nitrides are polar: each Ga–N bond is slightly ionic and the lattice lacks a centre of symmetry, so the crystal carries a spontaneous polarisation along the c-axis even when unstrained. When a thin AlGaN barrier is grown on GaN it is under tensile strain, which adds a piezoelectric polarisation. The difference in total polarisation between the two layers leaves a fixed sheet of positive bound charge, of order 1013 q/cm2, at the interface of Ga-face material. Electrons, supplied mainly by donor-like surface states on the AlGaN, accumulate in a triangular quantum well on the GaN side to neutralise it, forming a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) with no intentional doping at all.17

Ambacher and co-workers showed in 1999 that the sheet density rises with Al fraction and barrier thickness and reaches about 1–2 × 1013 cm−2 for typical Al0.2–0.3Ga0.7–0.8N barriers.17 Because the electrons sit in undoped GaN, away from ionised impurities, their mobility is around 1500–2000 cm2/V·s at room temperature.9,2 The combination of high density and high mobility gives a sheet resistance of only a few hundred ohms per square.

Worked example 5.1 · 2DEG sheet resistance

An AlGaN/GaN 2DEG has ns = 1.0 × 1013 cm−2 and µ = 1800 cm2/V·s. Find its sheet resistance, and the resistance of a 10 µm long access region in a device 100 mm wide (total gate periphery).

Rsh = 1/(qnsµ) = 1/(1.602 × 10−19 × 1013 × 1800) = 347 Ω/□. The access region is L/W = 10 µm / 100 mm = 10−4 squares, so R = 347 × 10−4 = 35 mΩ. This is why high-current lateral GaN transistors use very wide, finely interdigitated layouts: resistance scales with drift length divided by total width.

5.2GaN HEMT structure

The high-electron-mobility transistor (HEMT), also called the HFET or MODFET, was invented in AlGaAs/GaAs by Mimura and colleagues at Fujitsu in 1980.28 Khan and co-workers reported the first AlGaN/GaN HEMT in 1993.29 A Schottky (or MIS) gate on the barrier modulates the 2DEG beneath it; source and drain ohmic contacts reach the 2DEG through the barrier. The device is lateral: current flows along the surface, and the gate-to-drain spacing sets the blocking voltage.

5.3Normally-off approaches

Because the 2DEG exists at zero gate bias, the basic HEMT is normally on (depletion mode). Power converters need normally-off switches for fail-safe start-up, and four solutions are in use:2,22

  • p-GaN gate. A Mg-doped p-GaN layer under the gate lifts the conduction band and depletes the 2DEG at zero bias, giving Vth ≈ 1–2 V. Panasonic's gate-injection transistor (GIT) of 2007 is the best-known variant; it deliberately injects holes from an ohmic p-gate.31 EPC introduced the first commercial enhancement-mode GaN-on-silicon FETs in June 2009.32
  • Cascode. A normally-on high-voltage GaN HEMT in series with a low-voltage silicon MOSFET that controls it, so the pair is driven like a silicon MOSFET with a standard gate voltage.22
  • Recessed MIS gate. Etching the barrier under the gate and adding a dielectric removes the 2DEG locally.2
  • Direct drive of a depletion-mode HEMT with a negative-voltage driver and an integrated safety switch.2

GaN HEMTs have no p–n body diode and hence no reverse-recovery charge. They still conduct in reverse: when the drain goes below the source the channel turns on with an effective drop of about Vth plus any negative gate bias, typically 2–3 V or more, which makes dead time costly.32,22

5.4GaN for RF

For microwave power amplifiers, GaN-on-SiC HEMTs combine the high JFOM of GaN with the thermal conductivity of the SiC substrate. Compared with GaAs, they can run at drain voltages of 28–50 V instead of 5–10 V, which raises the load impedance, eases matching and gives power densities of several W/mm of gate periphery, roughly ten times GaAs.9 They now dominate radar, electronic-warfare and cellular base-station amplifiers, including the massive-MIMO arrays of 5G.9,2 GaN-on-Si is used where cost matters more than heat removal.

Thermal caution

Ga2O3's thermal conductivity is about one-twentieth of SiC's. A high BFOM lets you shrink the die, but a smaller die in a poor thermal conductor runs hotter (see Exercise 10), so practical Ga2O3 devices need thinned substrates, top-side cooling or bonding to better conductors.12,3

7Substrates, epitaxy and defects

Why it matters: device cost and reliability in WBG technology are set by crystal growth far more than by device design.

7.1Silicon carbide

SiC does not melt at practical pressures; it sublimes. Lely's 1955 sublimation process produced small, high-purity platelets of random polytype, and the decisive step came in 1978 when Tairov and Tsvetkov added a seed crystal and a temperature gradient, the seeded sublimation or modified-Lely (physical vapour transport, PVT) method still used today at over 2000 °C.1,35 Homoepitaxy of device-quality 4H-SiC drift layers became possible through step-controlled epitaxy on substrates cut a few degrees off-axis, so that surface steps copy the substrate's stacking sequence and suppress 3C inclusions.36,1

The important extended defects are:1,27

  • Micropipes, hollow-core screw dislocations that kill any device they cross. Once the yield-limiting defect, they have been almost eliminated in modern wafers.
  • Basal-plane dislocations (BPDs). Under forward bipolar current, electron–hole recombination at a BPD drives a partial dislocation to glide, expanding a Shockley stacking fault that acts as a resistive quantum-well barrier. This bipolar degradation raises the forward voltage of PiN diodes and of MOSFET body diodes over time. Epitaxial processes now convert most BPDs into harmless threading edge dislocations, and buffer layers with high recombination rate stop holes from reaching remaining substrate BPDs.
  • Threading screw and edge dislocations and epitaxial surface defects (carrots, triangles), which affect leakage and oxide reliability.

7.2Gallium nitride

Bulk GaN is grown only in small volumes (by HVPE or ammonothermal methods) and is expensive, so nearly all GaN devices are heteroepitaxial: on sapphire for LEDs, on semi-insulating SiC for RF and on Si(111) for power.2 The lattice mismatch to silicon is about 17 % and the thermal-expansion mismatch puts the film in tension on cooling, so GaN-on-Si needs carefully graded AlN/AlGaN or superlattice buffers to avoid cracking and wafer bow. Even so, threading-dislocation densities of 108–109 cm−2 are normal, millions of times higher than in silicon, and the buffer traps contribute to dynamic on-resistance (section 9).2 The great economic advantage of GaN-on-Si is that it runs on large-diameter, depreciated silicon lines.2

Vertical GaN devices on native GaN substrates promise SiC-like voltage ratings with GaN's BFOM, but substrate cost and size remain the barrier.2

7.3Ga2O3, diamond and AlN

β-Ga2O3 substrates are produced by edge-defined film-fed growth (EFG) from the melt, which scales to large diameters at low cost; this is its main commercial argument.33,12 Diamond is grown by microwave-plasma CVD on small single-crystal seeds or as polycrystalline wafers.14,15 Bulk AlN is grown by PVT, as SiC is, and is currently limited in size.3

9Reliability

Why it matters: the qualification methods that work for silicon miss failure modes that are specific to WBG devices, and new standards have been written to catch them.

9.1SiC MOSFETs

  • Threshold-voltage instability (bias-temperature instability). Oxide and interface traps near the SiC/SiO2 interface charge and discharge under gate bias, so Vth drifts with time, temperature and even with the switching pattern. The measured shift depends strongly on how quickly it is measured after stress, which made early data hard to compare.26
  • Gate-oxide lifetime. SiC MOSFETs run their oxide at a higher field than silicon MOSFETs do, and the material has more extrinsic oxide defects, so manufacturers screen extensively and specify a narrow recommended VGS window.1,7
  • Body-diode bipolar degradation from BPD-induced stacking faults (section 7.1).27
  • Short-circuit ruggedness. A SiC die is several times smaller than an IGBT of the same rating and has much higher current density during a short circuit, so it heats faster; withstand times are a few microseconds, shorter than the roughly 10 µs typical of IGBTs, and protection must respond accordingly.7
  • Cosmic-ray single-event burnout, which sets a derating of DC-link voltage below the rated voltage for long-lived high-voltage systems.7

9.2GaN HEMTs

  • Current collapse and dynamic RDS(on). Electrons trapped in surface states or buffer traps during high-voltage off-state deplete the 2DEG after turn-on, so the on-resistance immediately after switching is higher than the DC datasheet value. Vetury et al. showed in 2001 that surface states form a "virtual gate"; SiN passivation and field plates largely suppress it.30 Buffer trapping still has to be characterised under realistic switching.2
  • No avalanche capability. A lateral GaN HEMT has no p–n junction to clamp an overvoltage by avalanche, so it fails destructively if it is overstressed. Manufacturers instead guarantee a transient overvoltage margin above the rated VDS.2,22
  • Gate reliability. p-GaN gates have only a few volts between threshold and maximum rating, so gate overshoot is a lifetime concern (section 10).32
  • Standards. JEDEC's JC-70 committee on wide-bandgap power semiconductors published JEP180, a guideline for switching-reliability evaluation of GaN power devices, in February 2020, recognising that DC stress alone does not reproduce field failures.39

10Gate drive and layout

Why it matters: a WBG switch that is driven or laid out like a silicon part will ring, false-trigger or fail; most "WBG doesn't work" stories are layout stories.

10.1Rules that follow from the physics

  1. Kelvin source. At di/dt of several A/ns, a few nanohenries of common source inductance subtract L·di/dt volts from the effective gate drive and slow switching. Use packages with a separate driver-source pin (TO-247-4, D2PAK-7, LGA and chip-scale GaN).32,7
  2. Minimise both loops. The power commutation loop (DC-link capacitor, high-side and low-side switch) and the gate loop must both be tiny; GaN designs aim for loop inductances around 1 nH, achieved with a vertical or optimised lateral PCB layout and capacitors immediately next to the die.32,22
  3. Survive dv/dt. Switching edges of 50–150 V/ns inject Miller current CGD·dv/dt into the off-state gate. Low-impedance turn-off paths, negative bias (SiC) or separate turn-on/turn-off resistors prevent false turn-on. Isolated drivers need high common-mode transient immunity (CMTI), and every picofarad across an isolation barrier carries C·dv/dt of common-mode current.7,22
  4. Clamp gate overshoot for GaN with tight layout, damping resistance and, where needed, a regulated driver supply; for SiC, keep the negative rail within the datasheet's limits to avoid threshold drift.32,26
  5. Fast short-circuit protection for SiC: desaturation or current-sense detection that responds within the device's withstand time of a few microseconds, with soft turn-off.7
  6. Short, adaptive dead time for GaN, because reverse conduction through the channel costs 2–3 V × load current.32
Worked example 10.1 · Miller and common-mode currents

A GaN half-bridge switches 400 V in 4 ns. The low-side device has CGD = 0.5 pF and its high-side gate driver has 2 pF of barrier capacitance. Find the dv/dt, the Miller current into the low-side gate, and the common-mode current through the isolator.

dv/dt = 400/4 ns = 100 V/ns. Miller current = 0.5 pF × 100 V/ns = 50 mA; through a 2 Ω pull-down this lifts the gate by only 0.1 V, but through a 50 Ω path it would lift it by 2.5 V, above the ≈1.5 V threshold. Common-mode current = 2 pF × 100 V/ns = 0.2 A during each edge, which must return without passing through signal grounds.

Key idea · Switching loss is now a capacitance problem

With no tail current and no reverse recovery, much of a WBG switch's turn-on loss comes from discharging its own output capacitance (EOSS) and that of the opposite device. Soft-switching topologies (LLC, ZVS totem-pole, active-clamp flyback) recycle that energy instead, which is why GaN and SiC are often paired with resonant converters running at hundreds of kilohertz to megahertz.22,21

12Exercises with solutions

Use q = 1.602 × 10−19 C, ε0 = 8.854 × 10−14 F/cm and the Table 1 parameters unless stated. Click to reveal a solution.

1. Design the ideal drift layer of a 650 V vertical GaN device: thickness, doping and specific on-resistance.
Solution

WD = 2 × 650 / 3.3 × 106 = 3.9 µm. ND = (9.0 × 8.854 × 10−14)(3.3 × 106)2/(2 × 1.602 × 10−19 × 650) = 4.2 × 1016 cm−3. Ron,sp = 4 × 6502/(7.97 × 10−13 × 1250 × 3.59 × 1019) = 0.047 mΩ·cm2.

2. Verify the BFOM ratio of 4H-SiC to Si in Table 2 by hand.
Solution

(9.7 × 1000 × 2.53)/(11.7 × 1350 × 0.33) = 151 563 / 426.5 = 355. The critical-field ratio alone contributes (2.5/0.3)3 = 579; the lower mobility and permittivity of SiC take back a factor of 1.6.

3. By what factor does the ideal Ron,sp grow when a design goes from 650 V to 1200 V, (a) with constant ℰc and (b) using the empirical VB2.5 law?
Solution

(a) (1200/650)2 = 3.4×. (b) (1200/650)2.5 = 4.6×. Either way, doubling the voltage rating costs roughly four times the die area for the same resistance, which is why the voltage class is chosen with as little margin as reliability allows.

4. Use the Johnson limit to find the maximum voltage of a transistor with fT = 30 GHz in (a) Si, (b) 4H-SiC, (c) GaN.
Solution

JFOM = ℰcvsat/2π: Si 4.8 × 1011 V/s, SiC 8.0 × 1012 V/s, GaN 1.31 × 1013 V/s. Dividing by 3 × 1010 Hz: (a) 16 V, (b) 265 V, (c) 438 V. Real devices achieve only a fraction of these limits, but the ranking holds.

5. A SiC MOSFET has Eon + Eoff = 0.15 mJ at 400 V, 20 A and RDS(on) = 56 mΩ (hot). At 50 % duty and 20 A, at what switching frequency do switching and conduction losses become equal?
Solution

Pcond = 0.5 × 202 × 0.056 = 11.2 W. f = 11.2 W / 0.15 mJ = 75 kHz. Listing 2 gives the same result, and about 15 kHz for a silicon IGBT and free-wheeling diode with 1.1 mJ.

6. In Listing 3 the Si PiN diode returned 1.8 µC of reverse-recovery charge at 400 V. Estimate the extra energy dissipated in the opposite switch per turn-on, and the resulting power at 50 kHz.
Solution

To first order the switch carries the recovery current while supporting nearly the full bus voltage: E ≈ VQrr = 400 × 1.8 × 10−6 = 0.72 mJ. At 50 kHz that is 36 W. The SiC Schottky's 54 nC of capacitive charge gives 400 × 54 nC = 22 µJ, about 1 W at 50 kHz, and part of that energy is stored in the diode capacitance rather than dissipated.

7. Find the band-edge emission wavelengths of GaN, β-Ga2O3 and AlN. Which would make a "solar-blind" detector (insensitive above 280 nm)?
Solution

λ = 1239.84/Eg: GaN 3.39 eV → 366 nm; Ga2O3 4.85 eV → 256 nm; AlN 6.0 eV → 207 nm. Ga2O3 and AlN (and high-Al AlGaN) absorb only below 280 nm and are solar-blind; GaN responds to UV-A as well.

8. An e-mode GaN HEMT has VGS(max) = 6 V. The driver supplies 5 V and the gate loop rings with 25 % overshoot. Is the design safe? Suggest two fixes.
Solution

Peak = 5 × 1.25 = 6.25 V > 6 V: not safe. Fixes: shrink the gate-loop inductance (driver next to the device, Kelvin-source return directly under the gate trace), add series gate resistance to damp the loop, or use a lower regulated drive voltage such as 4.5–5 V with a matching driver.

9. A GaN switch is rated 50 mΩ (DC, hot). Double-pulse tests show dynamic RDS(on) 30 % higher at 400 V. Find the conduction loss at 15 A rms with 50 % duty, with and without the dynamic effect.
Solution

Static: 0.5 × 152 × 0.050 = 5.6 W. Dynamic: 0.5 × 152 × 0.065 = 7.3 W. Ignoring dynamic RDS(on) would underestimate the loss by 1.7 W per device, enough to upset a thermal design.

10. A die 1 mm × 1 mm × 200 µm dissipates 10 W uniformly from its top surface, with the bottom held at a fixed temperature. Estimate the temperature drop across the die for Si, 4H-SiC, GaN, β-Ga2O3 (κ = 0.27 W/cm·K) and diamond.
Solution

ΔT = Pt/(κA) = 10 × 0.02 cm / (κ × 0.01 cm2) = 20/κ: Si 13 K, SiC 4.1 K, GaN 8.7 K, Ga2O3 74 K, diamond 0.9 K. This is the one-dimensional estimate; lateral spreading helps, but the factor-of-18 gap between Ga2O3 and SiC remains.

11. Why can't a silicon superjunction MOSFET simply be scaled to 1200 V to compete with SiC?
Solution

Superjunctions balance alternating p and n pillars so the drift region can be more heavily doped than equation (3.1) allows, breaking the silicon unipolar limit. But the pillars must be about as deep as the drift layer (≈80 µm or more at 1200 V in Si), the charge balance tolerances tighten, and the large output capacitance and its strongly nonlinear COSS with stored charge rise. Commercial Si superjunction MOSFETs therefore stop at about 900 V, and SiC remains far below the Si limit at 1200 V (Figure 2).5

13Trivia quiz

Test yourself, then reveal the answer.

1. Which person gave his name to the mineral form of silicon carbide, and where was it found?

Henri Moissan, who identified natural SiC in fragments of the Canyon Diablo meteorite in Arizona; the mineral is called moissanite.1

2. Edward Acheson was trying to make something else when he produced SiC industrially around 1891. What, and what did he call the product?

He was attempting to synthesise diamond-like abrasives and named the hard crystals carborundum, which became a major abrasive.1

3. In what material, and in what year, was electroluminescence from a solid first reported?

Silicon carbide, 1907, by H. J. Round in a short note in Electrical World.41

4. What is the exact citation of the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics?

To Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Shuji Nakamura "for the invention of efficient blue light-emitting diodes which has enabled bright and energy-saving white light sources".10

5. What was the trick that first made p-type GaN in 1989?

Amano, Akasaki and co-workers activated Mg-doped GaN by low-energy electron-beam irradiation (LEEBI); Nakamura later showed that thermal annealing in a hydrogen-free ambient works too, because hydrogen passivates the Mg acceptors.42,10

6. Who launched the first commercial SiC power devices, and what were they?

Infineon, in early 2001: SiC Schottky diodes rated 300 V and 600 V.23

7. What was the first commercial SiC power MOSFET?

Cree's CMF20120D, launched in January 2011: 1200 V, 80 mΩ at 25 °C.25

8. In what year were enhancement-mode GaN-on-silicon power transistors first sold, and by whom?

June 2009, by Efficient Power Conversion (EPC), which named them eGaN FETs.32

9. What is the shortest-wavelength LED reported in a binary semiconductor, and in what material?

A 210 nm aluminium nitride PIN LED, reported by NTT researchers Taniyasu, Kasu and Makimoto in Nature in 2006.11

10. The HEMT goes by several names. Name three, and the company where it was invented.

HEMT, MODFET (modulation-doped FET), HFET (heterostructure FET), also TEGFET (two-dimensional electron gas FET); invented at Fujitsu by Takashi Mimura and colleagues (1980).28,8

11. Why is a GaN 2DEG unusual compared with a GaAs one?

A GaAs HEMT needs a doped barrier (modulation doping) to supply electrons; an AlGaN/GaN 2DEG forms with no intentional doping, induced by spontaneous and piezoelectric polarisation.17

12. Which WBG semiconductor can be grown from a melt like silicon, and why does that matter?

β-Ga2O3, by edge-defined film-fed growth or Czochralski. Melt growth is fast and scalable, so substrates could become far cheaper than SiC (which sublimes) or GaN.33,12

"""Wide-bandgap material figures of merit and ideal drift-region design.
Representative 300 K parameters (see article Table 1 for sources)."""
import numpy as np

q, eps0 = 1.602176634e-19, 8.8541878128e-14      # C, F/cm
# name: (Eg eV, eps_r, mu_n cm2/Vs, Ec MV/cm, vsat 1e7 cm/s, k W/cm.K)
MAT = {
    "Si":      (1.12, 11.7, 1350,  0.3, 1.0, 1.5),
    "4H-SiC":  (3.26,  9.7, 1000,  2.5, 2.0, 4.9),
    "GaN":     (3.39,  9.0, 1250,  3.3, 2.5, 2.3),
    "b-Ga2O3": (4.85, 10.0,  200,  8.0, 1.1, 0.27),
    "Diamond": (5.5,   5.7, 2000, 10.0, 2.3, 22.0),
    "AlN":     (6.0,   8.5,  300, 12.0, 1.4, 3.4),
}

def bfom(eps_r, mu, Ec):          # Baliga: eps*mu*Ec^3
    return eps_r*eps0*mu*(Ec*1e6)**3

def jfom(Ec, vs):                 # Johnson: Ec*vsat/(2*pi), in GHz*V... use THz.V
    return Ec*1e6*vs*1e7/(2*np.pi)

def drift(VB, eps_r, mu, Ec):
    """Ideal non-punch-through drift layer for breakdown VB (triangular field)."""
    Ec = Ec*1e6; es = eps_r*eps0
    W  = 2*VB/Ec                  # cm
    ND = es*Ec**2/(2*q*VB)        # cm^-3
    Ron = 4*VB**2/(es*mu*Ec**3)   # ohm.cm^2
    return W, ND, Ron

si = MAT["Si"]
b_si, j_si = bfom(si[1], si[2], si[3]), jfom(si[3], si[4])
VB = 1200.0
print(f"{'Material':9s} {'BFOM/Si':>8s} {'JFOM/Si':>8s} {'W(um)':>7s} {'ND(cm-3)':>9s} {'Ron,sp(mOhm.cm2)':>17s}")
for n, (Eg, er, mu, Ec, vs, k) in MAT.items():
    W, ND, R = drift(VB, er, mu, Ec)
    print(f"{n:9s} {bfom(er,mu,Ec)/b_si:8.0f} {jfom(Ec,vs)/j_si:8.1f} "
          f"{W*1e4:7.1f} {ND:9.2e} {R*1e3:17.4f}")

14.2Listing 2 · First-order switch-loss comparison (Python)

"""First-order loss comparison for one switch of a hard-switched 400 V half-bridge.
Illustrative parameters loosely typical of 650 V-class datasheets at Tj = 125 degC;
replace them with values from the parts you are actually evaluating."""
import numpy as np

V, I, D = 400.0, 20.0, 0.5           # bus voltage, load current, duty
devices = {
    # name: (V0 [V], r_on [ohm] hot, E_on+E_off at 400 V/20 A [J] incl. diode recovery)
    "Si IGBT + Si FRD":    (0.9, 0.040, 1.10e-3),
    "SiC MOSFET + SiC SBD":(0.0, 0.056, 0.15e-3),
    "GaN HEMT (e-mode)":   (0.0, 0.063, 0.06e-3),
}
fsw = np.array([10e3, 20e3, 50e3, 100e3, 200e3, 500e3])

def loss(V0, r, Esw, f):
    p_cond = D*(V0*I + r*I**2)        # average conduction loss
    p_sw = f*Esw*(V/400.0)*(I/20.0)   # switching energy scales ~linearly with V and I
    return p_cond, p_sw

print("fsw (kHz) | " + " | ".join(f"{n:>20s}" for n in devices))
for f in fsw:
    row = []
    for V0, r, E in devices.values():
        pc, ps = loss(V0, r, E, f)
        row.append(f"{pc+ps:8.1f} W ({ps/(pc+ps)*100:3.0f}% sw)")
    print(f"{f/1e3:9.0f} | " + " | ".join(f"{s:>20s}" for s in row))

# frequency at which switching loss equals conduction loss
for n, (V0, r, E) in devices.items():
    pc, _ = loss(V0, r, E, 0)
    print(f"{n:22s} P_sw = P_cond at {pc/E/1e3:6.0f} kHz")
