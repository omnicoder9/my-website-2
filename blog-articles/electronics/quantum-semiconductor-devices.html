When a device gets smaller than the electron's wavelength, the rules change. 1Why "quantum" devices?

Every semiconductor device is quantum mechanical at heart: band structure, effective mass and the bandgap itself are consequences of electron waves in a periodic crystal.1,2 In an ordinary diode or long-channel MOSFET, though, those quantum facts are hidden inside material parameters, and the device can be described by classical drift and diffusion. A quantum semiconductor device is one where that is no longer true: its terminal behaviour depends directly on one or more of four effects that a classical model cannot reproduce.3,4

The field grew from a series of milestones: Esaki's 1958 discovery of tunnelling in heavily doped germanium junctions,5,6 Kroemer's and Alferov's heterostructure concepts,7,8 Esaki and Tsu's 1970 proposal of the man-made superlattice,9 the observation of quantised levels in GaAs quantum wells in 1974,10 modulation doping and the HEMT,11,12 the quantum Hall effect,13 the quantum-cascade laser,14 and, since 1998, proposals and demonstrations of spin qubits in semiconductor quantum dots.15,16 Four Nobel Prizes in Physics (1973, 1985, 1998 and 2000) and the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for quantum dots trace this path.6,17,18,8,19

2The quantum toolkit

2.1Length scales: when is a device "quantum"?

Quantum effects matter when a device dimension becomes comparable to one of a handful of characteristic lengths.3,24 The most important is the electron's de Broglie wavelength, λ = h/p. In a semiconductor the relevant momentum is that of an electron with effective mass m* at the thermal energy or the Fermi energy:

λth = h√(3m*kT) ,    λF = 2πkF = √(2π/ns)  (2D gas)(2.1)

Because m* in GaAs is only 0.067 m0,25 λth at 300 K is ≈ 24 nm, an order of magnitude larger than for a free electron. That is why III–V heterostructures show strong quantum effects at layer thicknesses of 5–20 nm that epitaxy can grow routinely.3,26 Two other lengths decide whether a device behaves coherently:

  • Mean free path ℓ = vFτ, the distance between momentum-randomising collisions. If the device is shorter than ℓ, transport is ballistic.24
  • Phase-coherence length ℓφ, the distance over which an electron keeps a definite phase. Interference devices and qubits need dimensions below ℓφ, which in high-mobility 2DEGs reaches micrometres only at cryogenic temperatures.24,27

Finally, the energy scales must beat temperature: a level spacing ΔE or charging energy EC is only useful if it is several times kT (25.9 meV at 300 K, 0.36 meV at 4.2 K, 1.7 µeV at 20 mK). This single inequality explains why quantum-well lasers work at room temperature (ΔE ~ 50–100 meV) while spin qubits live in dilution refrigerators.3,28

2.2Heterostructures and band offsets

The practical way to make a quantum potential is to grow layers of different semiconductors with nearly the same lattice constant, a heterostructure. At each interface the conduction and valence band edges jump by offsets ΔEC and ΔEV whose sum is the bandgap difference.7,25 Molecular-beam epitaxy (MBE) and metal-organic vapour-phase epitaxy can switch composition within a single atomic layer, so potential profiles can be designed almost freely on the nanometre scale.26 Kroemer and Alferov shared half of the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics for developing semiconductor heterostructures for high-speed electronics and optoelectronics.8

2.3Confinement and the finite square well

For an infinitely deep well of width L the levels are En = n2π2ħ2/(2m*L2), measured from the band edge.3 Real wells have finite depth V0 = ΔEC, so the wavefunction leaks into the barriers and every level is lower than the infinite-well estimate. Matching ψ = cos(kz) or sin(kz) inside to decaying exponentials outside with Eq. (2.2) gives transcendental equations for even and odd states:3,4

kmw tankL2 = κmb (even),   −kmw cotkL2 = κmb (odd),   k = √(2mwE)ħ, κ = √(2mb(V0−E))ħ(2.3)

A finite well always has at least one bound state, and the number of bound states is 1 + ⌊L√(2mwV0)/(πħ)⌋.3 Explore it below: the solver uses Eq. (2.3) directly, so the levels agree with the finite-difference code in §13.1 to within 0.5 meV.

Worked example 2.1 · Emission wavelength of a GaAs quantum well

Estimate the emission wavelength of a 10 nm GaAs/AlGaAs quantum well using infinite-well confinement for the electron (m* = 0.067 m0) and heavy hole (m* ≈ 0.35 m0 along the growth direction). Eg(GaAs, 300 K) = 1.424 eV.25

Solution. ħ2π2/(2m0L2) = 3.76 meV for L = 10 nm. Electron: 3.76/0.067 = 56.1 meV. Heavy hole: 3.76/0.35 = 10.7 meV. Photon energy ≈ 1.424 + 0.056 + 0.011 = 1.491 eV, so λ = 1239.84/1.491 = 832 nm, a blue shift of ≈ 39 nm from bulk GaAs (871 nm). The finite-well solver gives an electron level of only 30.3 meV, so the real shift is smaller (≈ 1.465 eV before exciton binding); the trend, that thinner wells emit at shorter wavelength, is the basis of wavelength engineering in QW lasers.32

A GaAs 2DEG has sheet density ns = 1.0 × 1012 cm−2 in its lowest subband. Find EF − E1 at T → 0 and the Fermi wavelength.

2.5Tunnelling and transmission

For a single rectangular barrier of height V0 and thickness d with κd ≫ 1, the transmission probability falls as T ≈ 16(E/V0)(1 − E/V0) exp(−2κd), the result derived in Physics for Electronics §3.3. For a slowly varying barrier the WKB approximation generalises this to T ≈ exp(−2∫κ(z)dz).1,3 For arbitrary piecewise-constant profiles, the standard engineering method is the transfer matrix: write ψ = Aeikz + Be−ikz in each layer, impose Eq. (2.2)'s boundary conditions at every interface, and multiply the 2 × 2 matrices.4 §13.2 implements this for a double-barrier structure, and the interactive RTD figure in §4.2 uses the same algorithm in JavaScript.

When two barriers enclose a well, waves reflected back and forth interfere. At energies matching a quasi-bound state of the well, the reflections cancel and transmission rises to 1 for symmetric barriers, even though each barrier alone transmits only a few per cent. This is resonant tunnelling, first observed in GaAs/AlGaAs double barriers by Chang, Esaki and Tsu in 1974.34,35 The resonance width Γ relates to the lifetime of an electron in the well by τ = ħ/Γ.3

2.6Ballistic conduction and the conductance quantum

When a conductor is shorter than the mean free path, Ohm's law fails. Landauer's insight was that conduction is transmission: a channel supporting M transverse modes, each with transmission Ti, has conductance36,24

G = 2e2h Σi=1…M Ti ,    G0 = 2e2h = 77.48 µS = (12.906 kΩ)−1(2.4)

A perfect single-mode conductor therefore still has a resistance of 12.9 kΩ, a contact resistance from the mismatch between many modes in the leads and few in the channel. This staircase was observed in 1988 in quantum point contacts in GaAs 2DEGs (§3.3).21,37 The same formula, with transmission computed by tools such as Kwant or NEMO5, is now standard for modelling ultra-scaled transistors, where a 10 nm channel carries only a few dozen modes.38,39

3Quantum wells, wires and dots

3.1Quantum wells, superlattices and the 2DEG

In 1974 Dingle, Wiegmann and Henry measured optical absorption of GaAs layers 7–50 nm thick sandwiched between AlGaAs barriers and saw a series of sharp steps whose positions matched particle-in-a-box levels, the first direct demonstration of size quantisation in a semiconductor.10 Repeating wells with thin barriers produces a superlattice in which the levels of neighbouring wells couple into narrow minibands, an artificial one-dimensional crystal proposed by Esaki and Tsu.9

The second breakthrough was modulation doping (Dingle, Störmer, Gossard and Wiegmann, 1978): place the donors in the AlGaAs barrier, a few nanometres away from the GaAs. Electrons fall into the GaAs and form a sheet only a few nanometres thick, the two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG), physically separated from the ionised donors that would scatter them.11 With an undoped spacer, low-temperature mobilities rose from ~104 to above 107 cm2/V·s in the best MBE material, giving mean free paths of tens to hundreds of micrometres.3,27 The 2DEG is the platform for the HEMT (§5), the quantum Hall effect, quantum point contacts and gate-defined quantum dots.

For a triangular well with interface field F, the subband energies are approximately En ≈ (ħ2/2m*)1/3[(3π/2)eF(n − ¼)]2/3, from the Airy-function solution.3 The same physics applies to the inversion layer of every modern MOSFET: at high gate fields the electron sheet is quantised, its centroid sits ~1 nm below the oxide interface, and compact models add this "quantum-mechanical" correction to the effective oxide thickness.1

3.2The quantum Hall effect: a device that defines the ohm

In 1980 von Klitzing, Dorda and Pepper measured the Hall resistance of a silicon MOSFET inversion layer at 1.5 K in an 18 T field and found plateaus at exactly h/(ie2), with i an integer, independent of sample geometry.13 The precision and universality were so high that the effect became the world's resistance standard, and von Klitzing received the 1985 Nobel Prize in Physics.17 The fractional quantum Hall effect, with plateaus at fractional i, was found in a GaAs 2DEG by Tsui, Störmer and Gossard in 1982 and won the 1998 Nobel Prize.40,18 Since the 2019 SI redefinition fixed h and e exactly, RK = h/e2 = 25 812.807 45… Ω is an exact number, and quantum Hall devices realise the ohm directly.20

3.3Quantum wires and quantum point contacts

Two metal gates on the surface of a 2DEG wafer, with a gap of a few hundred nanometres, deplete the electrons beneath them and leave a narrow constriction, a quantum point contact (QPC). Making the gate voltage more negative narrows the channel and removes transverse modes one at a time. In 1988 two groups (Delft–Philips and Cambridge) independently saw the conductance fall in steps of 2e2/h, the first direct demonstration of Eq. (2.4).21,37 Today QPCs and their cousins are used as ultra-sensitive charge detectors next to quantum dots, because on the steep side of a step a single electron nearby changes the current by a measurable fraction.41,28

Semiconductor nanowires grown by vapour–liquid–solid epitaxy (Si, Ge, InAs, InSb, GaN) and lithographically etched fins are the free-standing versions of quantum wires. Sakaki predicted in 1980 that 1D confinement would suppress scattering;42 in practice the most important industrial descendants are the gate-all-around nanosheet transistors covered in the companion FinFET and gate-all-around articles, in which confinement in a 5 nm thick sheet shifts threshold voltage and splits subbands.1

3.4Quantum dots: artificial atoms

Confining electrons in all three dimensions produces a quantum dot, an "artificial atom" with discrete levels whose spacing is set by its size.43,44 Three families matter for electronics:

  • Colloidal nanocrystals. Ekimov and Onushchenko observed size-dependent absorption in CuCl nanocrystals grown in glass in 1981;45 Brus explained the effect for colloidal CdS crystallites in 1983–84;46 and in 1993 Bawendi's group developed hot-injection synthesis giving monodisperse CdSe dots. The three shared the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.19 Colloidal dots now convert blue LED light into pure red and green in "QD" displays.47
  • Self-assembled (epitaxial) dots. When InAs is grown on GaAs, the 7 % lattice mismatch makes the strained film break up into coherent islands a few tens of nanometres across after about 1.5–1.7 monolayers, the Stranski–Krastanov mode.48 Embedded in GaAs, they act as gain media for QD lasers and as single-photon sources.44
  • Gate-defined (electrostatic) dots. Surface gates on a 2DEG, or gates on a silicon MOS or Si/SiGe structure, carve a puddle containing from hundreds down to exactly one electron, with tunnel barriers to source and drain that the gates also control. These are the single-electron transistors of §7 and the spin qubits of §8.43,49,29

4Tunnelling devices

4.1The Esaki tunnel diode

Working at Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo (now Sony) in 1957, Leo Esaki made germanium p–n junctions so heavily doped that both sides were degenerate: the Fermi level lay inside the valence band on the p side and inside the conduction band on the n side, and the depletion region was only ~10 nm wide. Under small forward bias, filled conduction-band states on the n side faced empty valence-band states on the p side across a barrier thin enough to tunnel through, and the current fell as the bias increased further.5 It was the first unambiguous observation of interband tunnelling in solids, and Esaki shared the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physics.6

A negative differential resistance (NDR) cancels the losses of a resonant circuit, so a tunnel diode biased in its NDR region oscillates or amplifies. Because tunnelling is not limited by minority-carrier storage, tunnel diodes switched and oscillated well into the microwave range in the 1960s. They lost out to transistors because of their small voltage swing and two-terminal nature, but remain in niche microwave detectors, and their physics returns in the tunnel FET and in the tunnel junctions that connect sub-cells of multi-junction solar cells.1

4.2The resonant-tunnelling diode

The resonant-tunnelling diode (RTD) is the double-barrier structure of §2.5 between two doped contacts. At zero bias the quasi-bound level E1 lies above the emitter's Fermi sea. As bias pulls E1 down into the range of occupied emitter states, current flows by resonant tunnelling; once E1 falls below the emitter conduction-band edge, no emitter electron can conserve both energy and transverse momentum, and the current drops abruptly.35,1 The result is a strong NDR whose speed is limited only by the dwell time ħ/Γ in the well and by RC charging. Sollner and co-workers detected and mixed signals up to 2.5 THz with GaAs/AlGaAs RTDs in 1983,50 and InGaAs/AlAs RTD oscillators integrated with slot antennas have since reached fundamental oscillation near 2 THz at room temperature, the highest of any electronic oscillator of this kind.51

Worked example 4.1 · Resonance width and dwell time

For 3 nm barriers and a 5 nm well, the transfer-matrix code in §13.2 finds the first resonance at 74.4 meV with a full width at half maximum of 3.86 meV. Estimate the dwell time and check the width with a semiclassical "attempt-frequency" argument.

Solution. τ = ħ/Γ = 6.58 × 10−16 eV·s / 3.86 × 10−3 eV = 0.17 ps. Semiclassically, the electron (velocity v = √(2E/m*) = 6.2 × 105 m/s) hits each barrier v/2w = 6.2 × 1013 times per second; each barrier transmits Tb ≈ 16(E/V0)(1 − E/V0)e−2κb ≈ 0.074 (κ = 6.4 × 108 m−1). The escape rate through two barriers ≈ 2 × 0.074 × 6.2 × 1013 = 9.2 × 1012 s−1, so Γ ≈ ħ × rate ≈ 6.0 meV: the right order of magnitude. A sub-picosecond dwell time is why RTDs reach terahertz frequencies.3,51

Beyond oscillators, RTDs have been explored for multi-valued logic and memory (an RTD pair in series has two stable operating points), for ultra-fast sampling circuits, and as THz sources for short-range wireless links and imaging.51,52

4.3The tunnel field-effect transistor

A MOSFET switches by lowering a barrier over which carriers are thermally emitted, so its subthreshold current can rise at most tenfold for every (kT/q)ln 10 ≈ 60 mV of gate voltage at room temperature. Supply voltage cannot therefore shrink below a few hundred millivolts without unacceptable leakage, and this "Boltzmann tyranny" is a root of the power-density problem in CMOS.53,1 The tunnel FET (TFET) replaces thermionic injection with gate-controlled band-to-band tunnelling: it is a gated p–i–n diode operated in reverse bias. When the gate pulls the channel conduction band below the source valence band, a tunnelling window opens; the source's Fermi distribution is cut off by the bandgap, which acts as an energy filter, so in principle the subthreshold swing S can be well below 60 mV/dec.53,52

Experimental TFETs in Si, Ge, III–V nanowires and 2D materials have demonstrated S below 60 mV/dec, but usually only over a narrow current range and at on-currents far below those of FinFETs, because tunnelling through a bandgap is improbable. Remedies include small, direct-gap or broken-gap heterojunctions (e.g. InAs/GaSb), very thin bodies and abrupt doping. TFETs remain a research device, valuable mainly as a demonstration of how quantum transport can circumvent a classical limit.53,52

4.4Fowler–Nordheim and direct tunnelling: the quantum effect in your pocket

The most widely manufactured quantum-tunnelling device is the flash memory cell. Its floating gate (or charge-trap layer) is charged and erased by electrons tunnelling through a thin oxide under high field, where the barrier becomes triangular and the current follows the Fowler–Nordheim form J ∝ F2exp(−B/F), first derived for field emission from metals in 1928 and confirmed for Si/SiO2 by Lenzlinger and Snow in 1969.54,55,1 Unwanted direct tunnelling through gate oxides thinner than ~2 nm is the reason the industry adopted high-κ dielectrics (see Physics for Electronics §3.3).

5HEMTs: transistors with quantum-confined channels

The high-electron-mobility transistor uses the modulation-doped 2DEG of §3.1 as its channel. Takashi Mimura at Fujitsu realised it in 1979–80 with selectively doped GaAs/n-AlGaAs heterojunctions, using a Schottky gate to deplete the 2DEG; its layer structure and AlGaN/GaN power variant are described in Semiconductor Devices §6.5.12,11 The quantum aspects of the device are:

  • The channel is a single subband. The gate changes the sheet density ns in a 2D DOS, so ns ≈ Ceff(VGS − Vth)/e, where 1/Ceff includes the barrier capacitance, the finite distance of the electron centroid from the interface, and a "quantum capacitance" e2g2D in series.3,1
  • Mobility comes from separating electrons and donors, and is highest at low temperature where phonon scattering freezes out. That is why cryogenically cooled InP HEMT amplifiers are the low-noise front ends of radio telescopes and of the readout chains of superconducting and spin qubits.1,56
  • Light mass and high velocity. InGaAs channels with high indium content on InP have electron masses near 0.04 m0,25 enabling the highest-frequency transistors: a 25 nm InP HEMT process gave the first transistor amplifier at 1 THz.57
Worked example 5.1 · Quantum capacitance in a HEMT

A GaAs HEMT has a 25 nm AlGaAs barrier (εr = 12.2). Compare the geometric barrier capacitance per unit area with the 2DEG quantum capacitance Cq = e2g2D.

Solution. Cb = ε0εr/d = 8.854 × 10−12 × 12.2/25 × 10−9 = 4.3 mF/m2 (0.43 µF/cm2). Cq = e2 × 1.75 × 1036 J−1m−2 = 45 mF/m2. In series, Ceff = 1/(1/4.3 + 1/45) = 3.9 mF/m2, a 9 % reduction, equivalent to adding ≈ 2.4 nm to the barrier. In low-mass materials (InGaAs, or graphene and other 2D channels) Cq is smaller and the effect larger: the electrons' kinetic energy literally appears as a series capacitor.3

6Quantum-confined optoelectronics

6.1Quantum-well and quantum-dot lasers

Nearly every semiconductor laser made today, from telecom DFB lasers to the red diode in a barcode scanner, has one or several quantum wells as its active region. Room-temperature QW laser operation was reported in 1978 by Dupuis, Dapkus, Holonyak and co-workers using MOCVD-grown GaAs/AlGaAs.58 The advantages follow from §2:32,59

  • The step-like 2D DOS puts more carriers at the band edge, so less injected charge is needed for population inversion; the active volume is tiny, so threshold current is low.
  • The emission wavelength is tuned by well width as well as composition (Worked example 2.1).
  • Strained wells, slightly lattice-mismatched but thinner than the critical thickness, split the heavy- and light-hole bands and reduce the in-plane hole mass, lowering threshold further and allowing wavelengths unreachable by lattice-matched alloys.

Arakawa and Sakaki predicted in 1982 that reducing dimensionality further, to wires and dots, would make the threshold current insensitive to temperature, because carriers can no longer spread thermally over a continuum of states.33 Self-assembled InAs QD lasers realised much of this promise; because dots also trap carriers before they reach defects, QD lasers tolerate the dislocations of growth directly on silicon and are a leading route to integrated lasers for silicon photonics.44,32

6.2Quantum-cascade lasers

All interband lasers emit at a photon energy close to the material bandgap. The quantum-cascade laser (QCL) breaks that link: it is a unipolar device in which electrons make transitions between confined subbands within the conduction band. The photon energy is set by layer thicknesses, not by the bandgap, so the same InGaAs/InAlAs material system can lase anywhere from ≈ 3 µm to beyond 20 µm, and GaAs-based QCLs cover the terahertz range (≈ 1–5 THz).14,60

Kazarinov and Suris proposed amplification by intersubband transitions in superlattices in 1971.61 Faist, Capasso and colleagues at Bell Labs demonstrated the first QCL in 1994, emitting at ≈ 4.2 µm at cryogenic temperature,14 and continuous-wave operation at room temperature followed in 2002.62 QCLs are now the standard source for mid-infrared gas sensing (trace CO, NO, CH4 and breath analysis), infrared countermeasures and spectroscopy, because the strong fundamental vibrational absorption lines of most molecules lie in their range.60

Worked example 6.1 · Designing the photon energy

A QCL needs to emit at λ = 4.6 µm (a strong CO absorption band). What subband spacing E3 − E2 is required, and how does it compare with the InGaAs bandgap (≈ 0.74 eV)?

Solution. E = 1239.84/4600 = 0.270 eV, about a third of the InGaAs bandgap and only possible because the transition is intersubband. The required depth for two well-separated levels plus the level-1 phonon step exceeds 0.4 eV, which is why mid-IR QCLs use the deep InGaAs/InAlAs (ΔEC ≈ 0.52 eV) or strain-compensated systems.25,14

6.3Quantum-dot emitters and quantum-well detectors

Colloidal QDs have narrow emission linewidths (typically 20–40 nm full width) set by size uniformity, and their colour is set by size alone. In current displays they are mostly used as photoluminescent down-converters: a blue LCD backlight or blue OLED pumps red and green dots, widening the colour gamut. Electrically driven QD-LEDs, with the dots in a thin-film diode, have reached external quantum efficiencies comparable to OLEDs in the laboratory.47 Epitaxial dots, by contrast, are bright single-photon sources for quantum communication.44

Intersubband absorption also works in reverse: quantum-well infrared photodetectors (QWIPs) absorb mid- or long-wave infrared photons that promote electrons from a bound well state into the continuum, and type-II InAs/GaSb superlattices form an engineered narrow gap for thermal imaging.4,1

7Single-electron devices

Put a small conducting island between two tunnel junctions. Adding one electron to it raises its electrostatic energy by the charging energy EC = e2/2CΣ, where CΣ is the island's total capacitance. If EC ≫ kT, and if the junctions are opaque enough that the electron number is a good quantum number (tunnel resistance RT ≫ h/e2 ≈ 25.8 kΩ), electrons can only pass one at a time and only when the energy books balance. This is Coulomb blockade.63,64 Fulton and Dolan observed it in 1987 in a lithographically made Al/AlOx device with a gate, the first single-electron transistor (SET).65

The SET is the most sensitive electrometer known: a charge of a small fraction of e on the gate shifts the operating point along a Coulomb peak, and charge sensitivities of order 10−5 e/√Hz have been demonstrated.64 In semiconductor dots the levels are also quantised, so the addition energy is EC plus a level spacing, and the peak pattern reveals atom-like shell filling.43 Applications include:

  • Charge sensing next to qubits, where an adjacent SET or QPC detects whether a single electron has moved (§8).41,29
  • Single-electron pumps that move exactly one electron per cycle of an RF drive, giving I = ef; since the 2019 SI revision fixed e, these are candidate primary realisations of the ampere.67
  • Logic and memory: proposed by Likharev and others, but impractical at room temperature because EC ≫ kT requires islands of a few nanometres, and because random background charges shift every device's operating point.64

8Semiconductor qubits for quantum computing

A qubit needs a two-level system that can be initialised, manipulated, coupled to its neighbours and read out, with coherence lasting far longer than a gate operation (DiVincenzo's criteria).68 In 1998 Loss and DiVincenzo proposed using the spin of a single electron in a gate-defined quantum dot: in a magnetic field B, spin-up and spin-down are split by the Zeeman energy gμBB, single-qubit gates are made by electron spin resonance, and two-qubit gates by pulsing the tunnel coupling, and hence the exchange interaction J, between neighbouring dots.15 The same year Kane proposed using the nuclear spins of 31P donors in silicon.69 Semiconductor qubits appeal to engineers because they are made with the same lithography, gate stacks and 300 mm wafers as CMOS, and each qubit occupies roughly 100 nm × 100 nm.56,29

8.2Why silicon won over GaAs

The early experiments used GaAs, but every Ga and As nucleus carries a nuclear spin, and the fluctuating hyperfine field of ~106 nuclei dephases the electron in tens of nanoseconds.28,70 Natural silicon contains only 4.7 % 29Si (the sole stable isotope with nuclear spin), and isotopically purified 28Si reduces this to below 0.01 %, making silicon a "semiconductor vacuum" for spins with coherence times orders of magnitude longer.81,29 The challenges that remain are engineering ones that electronics engineers will recognise:16,56

  • Valley splitting. Silicon's conduction band has six equivalent valleys; strain and the interface split them, but if the splitting is small and variable, the "valley" degree of freedom leaks into the qubit.
  • Charge noise from interface and oxide traps, the same 1/f noise that limits analog MOSFETs, now limits qubit coherence when micromagnet gradients couple charge to spin.74
  • Wiring and cryo-CMOS. Each qubit needs several DC gates plus RF lines; scaling to millions of qubits requires on-chip multiplexing and control electronics operating at 1–4 K, which motivates operating qubits "hot".56,75
  • Uniformity. Threshold-voltage variability that is tolerable for logic is fatal for dots, so 300 mm process control is a key advantage.80

13.1Finite-difference Schrödinger solver for a quantum well

Discretises Eq. (2.2) on a grid with the mass evaluated at half-points, which keeps the Hamiltonian symmetric and satisfies the BenDaniel–Duke conditions automatically.30,4 The levels agree with the analytic solution of Eq. (2.3) (30.3, 119.0 and 239.5 meV for 10 nm) to within 0.3 meV.

# qw_solver.py - 1D effective-mass Schrodinger solver (BenDaniel-Duke) for a quantum well
import numpy as np
hbar, m0, q = 1.054571817e-34, 9.1093837015e-31, 1.602176634e-19

def solve_well(Lw=10e-9, V0=0.243, mw=0.067, mb=0.0919, Lb=20e-9, N=1500, nstates=6):
    z = np.linspace(-Lb - Lw/2, Lb + Lw/2, N)
    dz = z[1] - z[0]
    inside = np.abs(z) <= Lw/2
    V = np.where(inside, 0.0, V0) * q              # conduction-band profile (J)
    m = np.where(inside, mw, mb) * m0               # position-dependent mass
    mh = 0.5 * (m[:-1] + m[1:])                     # mass at half-grid points
    # H psi = -hbar^2/2 d/dz (1/m d psi/dz) + V psi  (Hermitian 3-point stencil)
    t = hbar**2 / (2 * mh * dz**2)
    H = np.diag(V) + np.diag(np.r_[t, 0] + np.r_[0, t]) - np.diag(t, 1) - np.diag(t, -1)
    E, psi = np.linalg.eigh(H)
    E = E / q
    bound = E < V0
    return z, V / q, E[bound][:nstates], psi[:, bound][:, :nstates] / np.sqrt(dz)

def infinite_well(Lw, mw, n):
    return (n * np.pi * hbar / Lw)**2 / (2 * mw * m0) / q

13.2Transfer-matrix transmission of a resonant-tunnelling diode

Builds the 2 × 2 interface matrices for ψ = Aeikz + Be−ikz with BenDaniel–Duke matching, and scans the energy to find resonances and their widths.4,3

# rtd_transmission.py - transfer-matrix transmission of a double-barrier RTD
import numpy as np
hbar, m0, q = 1.054571817e-34, 9.1093837015e-31, 1.602176634e-19

def transmission(E, layers, m_eff):
    """E in eV; layers = list of (thickness_m, V_eV); leads at V=0 on both sides.
    Piecewise-constant potential, BenDaniel-Duke matching (psi and psi'/m continuous)."""
    V = [0.0] + [v for _, v in layers] + [0.0]
    m = [m_eff[0]] + [mm for mm in m_eff[1:-1]] + [m_eff[-1]]
    d = [0.0] + [t for t, _ in layers] + [0.0]
    k = [np.sqrt(2 * mi * m0 * (E - Vi) * q + 0j) / hbar for mi, Vi in zip(m, V)]
    M = np.eye(2, dtype=complex)
    x = 0.0
    for j in range(len(V) - 1):
        x += d[j]
        kj, kn = k[j], k[j + 1]
        r = (kj / m[j]) / (kn / m[j + 1])
        # interface at x: maps (A_j, B_j) -> (A_{j+1}, B_{j+1}) for psi = A e^{ikx} + B e^{-ikx}
        D = 0.5 * np.array([[(1 + r) * np.exp(1j * (kj - kn) * x), (1 - r) * np.exp(-1j * (kj + kn) * x)],
                            [(1 - r) * np.exp(1j * (kj + kn) * x), (1 + r) * np.exp(-1j * (kj - kn) * x)]])
        M = D @ M
    # incident from left: (1, rr) -> (t, 0)
    rr = -M[1, 0] / M[1, 1]
    t = M[0, 0] + M[0, 1] * rr
    return (np.abs(t)**2 * (k[-1].real / m[-1]) / (k[0].real / m[0])).real

Uses the orthodox tunnel rate Γ = (−ΔF/e2R)/(1 − exp(ΔF/kT)) for each junction and solves the steady-state master equation for the island charge probabilities.63,64

# set_orthodox.py - single-electron transistor: orthodox theory + master equation
import numpy as np
e, kB = 1.602176634e-19, 1.380649e-23

def rate(dF, R, T):
    """Orthodox tunnel rate (1/s) for a free-energy change dF (J) across resistance R."""
    x = np.clip(dF / (kB * T), -700, 700)
    small = np.abs(x) < 1e-6
    xs = np.where(small, 1.0, x)
    # Gamma = (-dF/e^2R) / (1 - exp(dF/kT)), written in an overflow-safe form
    g = (kB * T) * (-xs) / np.expm1(-xs) * np.exp(-xs)
    return np.where(small, kB * T, g) / (e**2 * R)

def set_current(Vds, Vg, C1=1e-18, C2=1e-18, Cg=2e-18, R1=1e5, R2=1e5, T=1.0, nmax=10):
    Cs = C1 + C2 + Cg
    V1, V2 = +Vds / 2, -Vds / 2                     # source and drain potentials
    n = np.arange(-nmax, nmax + 1)                  # extra electrons on the island
    E = lambda nn: (-nn * e + C1 * V1 + C2 * V2 + Cg * Vg)**2 / (2 * Cs)
    on1, on2 = rate(E(n+1) - E(n) + e*V1, R1, T), rate(E(n+1) - E(n) + e*V2, R2, T)
    off1, off2 = rate(E(n-1) - E(n) - e*V1, R1, T), rate(E(n-1) - E(n) - e*V2, R2, T)
    # birth-death chain: p(n+1) * off(n+1) = p(n) * on(n)
    logp = np.zeros(n.size)
    for i in range(1, n.size):
        logp[i] = logp[i-1] + np.log(on1[i-1] + on2[i-1] + 1e-300) - np.log(off1[i] + off2[i] + 1e-300)
    p = np.exp(logp - logp.max()); p /= p.sum()
    # conventional current from lead 1 to lead 2 = e * (electrons leaving island to lead 1, net)
    return e * np.sum(p * (off1 - on1))
