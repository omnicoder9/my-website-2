01The question

An industrial robot is one of the most precise machines on a factory floor and one of the least accurate. It will return to a stored position again and again within a few hundredths of a millimetre, and it will miss a position it has been told about in numbers by one or two whole millimetres. Those two sentences are not in tension. They describe different quantities, and the difference between them is the organising fact of the whole discipline.1

Run the test. Command a pose, approach it thirty times from the same direction, and measure where the tool actually ends up. In the lab that accompanies this article the thirty points fall inside a sphere of 0.087 mm, and the centre of that sphere sits 1.70 mm away from the point that was commanded — a factor of 21 between the two numbers. Averaged over five poses the figures are a pose repeatability of 0.083 mm against a pose accuracy of 1.75 mm. Figure 1 draws them both to the same scale, which is the only honest way to look at them.

Every technique in this article exists because of that gap. A cell designer cannot make the robot accurate, or not cheaply. What they can do is arrange the world so that only repeatability is needed: bolt the part to a fixture the robot was taught against, machine a chamfer into the hole so the peg finds it, put a camera over the belt so the stored path can be offset by a measured amount. Each of those moves takes the same position and refers it to something different — to the robot's own encoder counts, to a piece of steel, to a model identified once with a laser tracker, to a feature on the part in front of it now.

So the question this article asks of every mechanism it meets is: where does the millimetre come from? Not how big the error is, which is usually the wrong question, but what the number in the program is ultimately referred to, and therefore what has to hold still for the number to keep meaning what it meant when it was written.

The robot is a repeatability machine. Everything else in the cell is there to make repeatability sufficient.

The scope is the whole of an industrial cell as the term is normally used: the arm and its program, the fixtures and feeders that present work to it, the processes it performs — welding, painting, dispensing, assembly, handling, packaging, inspection — the machine tools it tends, the controller and network that sequence it, and the safeguards that keep people out of the way.2,3 Forty mechanisms are catalogued as numbered plates, each coded onto a six-rung reference ladder and drawn on a common logarithmic scale of length. One of the forty is not a robot at all in this sense, and it is included precisely so that the boundary can be drawn where it belongs.

Two numbers are fixed throughout and should be read as conventions, not as claims. The reference tolerance is 0.500 mm: an ordinary positional requirement for an automated mechanical assembly, tight enough to be interesting and loose enough to be common. The reference tool speed is 1 000 mm/s, used whenever a quantity of time has to be turned into a quantity of length. Both are arbitrary. Neither affects any conclusion except by sliding the whole picture along its axis.

02Two words that are not synonyms

The vocabulary is standardised, and the standard is unusually clear about the distinction. Pose accuracy is the deviation between a commanded pose and the mean of the attained poses. Pose repeatability is the closeness of agreement between the attained poses themselves. The first is a property of the model the controller holds of its own geometry; the second is a property of the mechanism's noise.1,4 A manufacturer's datasheet quotes the second because the second is small. The first is rarely quoted at all, and where it is, the figure is roughly an order of magnitude larger.5

The reason is structural. The encoder is on the motor, not on the load. Between the count the controller can see and the point in space the tool occupies lie a gearbox, a link, a bearing, a wrist and a tool, and every one of them contributes a small constant error to the transform. The controller multiplies out a chain of homogeneous transformations6,7,8 built on a convention for attaching frames to links that every textbook in the field teaches9,10, using link lengths and offsets that came from a drawing, not from this particular machine. Where the machine differs from the drawing, the arithmetic is wrong by an amount that is perfectly repeatable and completely invisible from inside.

That is also what makes it correctable. If the error is constant with respect to configuration, then measuring the machine in enough poses identifies the parameters that were wrong, and the controller can be given better ones. This is robot calibration, and it has a literature reaching back to the early nineteen-eighties.11,12,13 Published results are consistent: a small industrial arm measured with a laser tracker in a thousand arbitrary configurations had its mean position error reduced from 0.968 mm to 0.364 mm, and its maximum from 2.158 mm to 0.696 mm, using a twenty-nine parameter model that combined geometric errors with compliance in four joints.14

The lab reproduces that experiment in miniature. A generic six-revolute arm is given random geometric errors, joint compliance under a six kilogram payload, servo noise of 0.0014 degrees per joint, and one thing more: a gear transmission error of half an arc-minute, periodic in the joint angle. Uncalibrated, the mean position error over 1000 configurations is 1.79 mm with a maximum of 3.14 mm. A 26-parameter fit to 60 measured configurations removes 94.7 per cent of it, leaving 0.095 mm mean and 0.260 mm maximum.

What is left is the interesting part. It is not measurement noise and it is not repeatability; it is the transmission error, which oscillates with the joint angle and therefore cannot be absorbed by any parameter that is a constant. The lab's residual is smaller than the published one because the lab contains exactly one unmodelled effect and a real arm contains many — thermal growth, link flexibility, friction that depends on direction, backlash that depends on history.15,16 The shape of the result is the same either way, and it is worth stating plainly: calibration converts accuracy error into repeatability error, and stops. It moves a machine from one rung of the ladder to another. It does not make the machine better at going to the same place twice.

The history of the industry is the history of people arranging not to need accuracy. George Devol's 1961 patent describes a machine that replays recorded positions — not one that computes them.17 The first installations were die-casting and spot welding, both chosen because the work was held in heavy fixtures and the tolerances were generous.18 The arm that changed what robots were for, Victor Scheinman's, was built to be computed rather than replayed,19 and the two architectures that followed were designed around the shape of the task rather than around generality: the SCARA, stiff vertically and compliant horizontally because that is what inserting a part into a hole wants,20 and the delta, whose actuators all sit on the base so that almost nothing accelerates except the payload.21,22 Neither was a bid for accuracy. Both were bids for a narrower job done faster.

Today about 540 000 industrial robots are installed a year and roughly four million are at work, a figure that has doubled in a decade.23,24 The overwhelming majority of them are doing what Devol's machine did: replaying stored positions into a world that has been arranged to make that sufficient.

03The budget

Position error in a cell is not one quantity but a chain of them, and the chain is the unit of analysis. Between the datum on a drawing and the point where the tool does work there are typically six or eight contributors, each with a distribution, and the question a designer has to answer is whether their combination fits inside the tolerance. This is ordinary tolerance stacking, and it has the same two answers here as anywhere else.25,26,27 The vocabulary for writing the requirement down is itself standardised, both for size limits and fits and for the geometrical characteristics a datum system controls.28,29

Take 7 contributors of the kind an assembly cell actually has: robot repeatability, the transfer of a taught point to a part that is not the part it was taught on, the residual in the tool centre point definition, the fixture's location on the table, the part's seating in the fixture, the uncertainty in where the gripper grasped it, and thermal growth across a shift. Add the half-widths arithmetically and the worst case is 0.930 mm, which fails the 0.500 mm tolerance outright. Combine them in quadrature and three standard deviations is 0.679 mm, a factor of 1.37 smaller, and 2.16 per cent of parts — 21645 parts per million — still fall outside.

The variance shares are where the design decisions live. One contributor, the grasp uncertainty in the gripper, holds 40.6 per cent of the variance while being only 26.9 per cent of the arithmetic sum, because variance goes as the square. Remove it and three sigma falls to 0.524 mm, an improvement of 22.9 per cent from a single change. Meanwhile the robot's own repeatability — the number on the datasheet, the number the purchasing decision was made on — owns 1.6 per cent. Buying a robot twice as repeatable improves this cell by an amount too small to measure.

The specification everyone compares is the contributor that matters least.

Two cautions about the quadrature rule, both old and both still routinely ignored. It assumes the contributors are independent, which fails the moment two of them share a cause — a thermal expansion that moves the fixture also moves the robot's base. And it assumes they are centred, which fails the moment one of them is a bias rather than a spread. A worn locating pin is not a distribution; it is an offset, and offsets add.30,31 The practical rule is that statistical tolerancing is a statement about a population of parts, and a cell producing one part at a time is entitled to the benefit only if it is genuinely sampling from that population.

04The reference ladder

Coding the census needs an axis, and the useful axis is not accuracy. It is the answer to a different question: when the controller commands a position, what is that position finally referred to? Six answers cover the whole of industrial practice.

D0 — nothing positional. The mechanism has no spatial reference at all. It may still contribute a length: a deflection under cutting load, a watchdog interval that becomes dead travel. What it does not have is anything to be measured against.

D1 — the robot's own encoder counts and a point stored in the controller. This is the taught position, and it is the default state of an industrial robot. The reference is internal, which is why it is cheap and why it goes stale.

D2 — a mechanical datum fixed in the cell. A fixture, a hard stop, a chamfer, a chuck, a bowl feeder's orienting tooling. The part's position is a property of steel, established by machining and maintained by clamping.32,33

D3 — a model of the cell, identified once by measurement. A tool centre point found by a four-point routine, a work object frame touched off on three features, a mastering, a kinematic calibration. The reference is a set of numbers that were true on the day they were taken.11,34,35

D4 — a feature on this part, measured before the motion. A camera locates the part and the stored path is offset. The reference is specific to one workpiece and expires when it is released.36,37

D5 — a feature on this part, measured during the motion. Force-guided search, seam tracking, visual servoing, conveyor following with a closed loop. The reference is being re-established continuously while the work is being done.38,39,40

How each plate is coded

The rung is assigned by the weakest link that carries the position through. If a camera locates the part but the gripper then holds it by an unknown amount, the placement is not at D4: the chain is broken at the grasp, and the plate describing the grasp is coded on its own terms. A mechanism is coded by what it itself refers to, not by what a well-designed cell around it might refer to.

A second axis records how long the reference stays true without being re-established: per shot, per part, per shift, per installation, or permanently, where permanence means the reference is a property of the structure rather than a setting. The two axes are close to independent, which is what makes them worth having together.

Each plate carries a band on a common seven-decade logarithmic scale, from one micrometre to ten metres. The band is the residual uncertainty the mechanism leaves at the tool point for everything downstream to deal with: what a positioning device contributes, what survives a corrector, and, for the control and safety layers, the mechanism's timing or standoff expressed as a length. The bands are coarse, read off published performance figures and the ranges reported in the cited literature. They are orders of magnitude and they are not specifications for any product.

The gauge on each plate shows all of this at once: the six rungs with the plate's own filled, the band on the shared axis, the 0.500 mm reference tolerance as a dashed line, and a marker saying whether the band clears it. Because the axis never changes, any two plates in the article can be compared by holding a finger on the page.

05The stored position

Almost every industrial robot program in existence is a list of positions that somebody stood in a cell and recorded. The teach pendant is the oldest interface in the field and it has outlasted every proposal to replace it, for a reason that is easy to state: it guarantees that the position is reachable, collision-free and correct on the part in front of the operator, which is more than any offline method can promise.18,41

What it does not guarantee is that any of those things remain true for the next part, the next fixture, or the next tool. A taught point is a D1 reference: it means whatever the robot's encoders meant on the day it was recorded. That is why the rest of this family exists.

The tool centre point is the quiet disaster of industrial robot programming. Every taught position is expressed relative to a frame at the working end of the tool, and that frame is established by a procedure — usually touching a single fixed point from four or more orientations and solving for the offset that makes them agree. The procedure reports a residual, and the residual is routinely ignored. When the tool is bumped, reground or replaced with a spare, the frame changes and every stored position in the program moves with it, silently and together. A cell that has been running for two years on a tool frame nobody has re-verified is carrying an unknown constant in every motion it makes.42,43

Mastering is the same failure one level deeper. The encoders are incremental with respect to a zero that is established mechanically, by a notch or a dial or a pin, and the whole kinematic model hangs off those six numbers. Change a motor, lose a battery, hit something hard enough to slip a count, and the robot is confidently wrong everywhere.

Offline programming is the case where the reference is explicit and explicitly wrong. A path generated from a CAD model is geometrically correct with respect to a cell that exists only in the model. The translation to the real cell is a calibration problem, and it is usually settled not by calibration but by an operator touching up points on the first article — which converts the whole exercise back into teaching, with extra steps. Where offline programming does work at scale, it is because somebody did the calibration: identified the arm's real parameters, located the fixture in the robot's frame, and accepted the residual as a known budget item rather than a surprise.44,45,46

A stored point is a measurement of a world that has since moved on.

06Holding the part and presenting it

If the robot cannot find the part, the part must be brought to the robot. This is the oldest trade in automation and it is still, by a wide margin, the most effective. A machined fixture is a mechanical assertion about where a part is, and the assertion holds without power, without software and without a sensor.

The discipline inside a good fixture is the 3-2-1 rule: three points define a plane, two more a line in that plane, one more the position along it. Six contacts remove exactly six degrees of freedom and no more. A seventh contact does not improve the location; it overconstrains the part, and which six of the seven actually take the load then depends on the part's own form error.47,48,49 Whole literatures exist on automating the design of such fixtures and on making them reconfigurable, and both keep meeting the same wall: the reference is cheap to use and expensive to change.50,51 This is the tightest positional reference available anywhere in a cell, and the lab's census bears that out: the mechanical-datum rung leaves a geometric mean residual of 0.187 mm, against 0.430 mm for the identified-model rung above it and 0.281 mm for the camera.

The gripper is where most of that precision is given back. A parallel gripper closing on a blank does not locate it; it captures it, in a position that depends on where the part was, how it was oriented and how the surfaces happened to slide. Self-centring geometry helps and costs time; a chuck or a locating nest at the destination removes the problem entirely by re-referencing the part once it arrives.52,53 The general move is worth naming: when a reference is lost, the cheapest recovery is usually to establish a new one at the destination rather than to preserve the old one in transit.

Feeding is the same argument at higher rate. A vibratory bowl feeder is a chain of mechanical filters, each of which either passes a part in the wanted orientation or returns it to the bowl. The yields multiply: four stations passing 75, 86, 72 and 92 per cent give a per-pass yield of 42.7 per cent, so every delivered part has been round the track 2.3 times on average. From 300 parts a minute presented to the first station, 128 a minute come out oriented.54,55,56,57,58

The alternative is to orient nothing and look instead. A flexible feeder tips parts onto a backlit surface, a camera finds the ones that are graspable, and the robot picks them one at a time. Including failed picks and the cycles spent re-presenting the field, the lab's flexible feeder delivers 56 parts a minute against the bowl's 128 — the bowl is 2.3 times faster.59,60

That ratio is the comparison usually made, and it is not the comparison that decides anything, because the two technologies are paid for differently. The bowl needs tooling for each part number, weeks of lead time and a changeover of the better part of an hour. The camera needs a recipe and a few minutes. Ask when the bowl finishes a run first, and the answer is a run of 4209 parts. Ask when the bowl has paid for its own tooling, and the answer is 0.99 million parts. The two crossings are 235 times apart.

Conveyor tracking deserves its own note, because it is the first mechanism in this article whose reference is a moving frame. The position of the part is reconstructed from an encoder on the belt and a trigger that saw the part go past, which makes it a D4 reference: this part, measured before the motion. The lab's error budget for that frame is 0.210 mm at one sigma, and 68 per cent of the variance is a single term — the part slipping on the belt after the trigger fired. No amount of network determinism, encoder resolution or camera quality touches it, because nothing in the system observes it.

07The processes

Joining: two very different tolerances

Resistance spot welding was the first mass application of industrial robots and it remains the largest by installed base. It is generous: the electrodes close on sheet held in a geometry fixture, the gun equalises to find the sheet, and a nugget a few millimetres across tolerates the robot being a millimetre or two off. What matters is the fixture, the current and the electrode condition, not the arm.61,62 The robot's contribution to weld quality is close to irrelevant, which is why an industry that welds with hundred-kilogram guns on the end of enormous arms has never much cared about absolute accuracy.

Arc welding is the opposite, and for a reason that has nothing to do with the robot: the workpiece moves while you weld it. Transverse shrinkage behind the arc applies a bending moment to the assembly, so the joint drifts away from where it was tacked, and it drifts as the square of the distance welded.63,64,65,66,67 The lab models a metre of seam finishing 3.0 mm off line, which is the order of magnitude reported for unrestrained thin plate. The taught path leaves a half-millimetre window after 408 mm — 41 per cent of the seam — and a one-millimetre window after 577 mm.

This is the cleanest example in the article of a D1 reference expiring during use. The program is right when it is written and wrong thereafter, at a rate set by the heat input. Nothing in the controller can know it. The fix is to stop referring to the stored path and start referring to the joint: through-arc sensing, which weaves the torch and reads the current to find the sidewalls, or a laser profile sensor looking ahead of the pool.68,69,70 The process brings its own hazards — arc radiation, fume, spatter — which belong to the integrator rather than to the robot manufacturer and are dealt with in their own specification.71 That is the move from D1 to D5, and it is the reason seam tracking exists.

The bandwidth that move requires is lower than people expect. Over a 125 second weld the joint's lateral rate never exceeds 0.048 mm/s, so a sensor correcting in 1.0 Hz steps holds it to five hundredths of a millimetre. What actually forces a fast sensor is not distortion but fit-up: the gap, the tack, the part-to-part variation the sensor has to find at the start of the weld and again whenever the geometry changes. And there is a cruder fix that has been used for eighty years — weave the torch wide enough that the bead covers the drift. Covering 3.0 mm needs an amplitude of 1.5 mm, which costs 1.41 times the path length in travel. A tracking sensor is, among other things, a way of buying that travel back.

Coating: the process that does not care

Painting is the outlier of the census and the one that most clearly shows tolerance is a property of the process rather than the machine. A rotary bell atomiser spins at tens of thousands of revolutions a minute, shears the coating into a fine cloud at its edge, and charges it so that the droplets follow field lines onto the earthed workpiece.72,73,74 The robot stands the bell off by a couple of hundred millimetres and sweeps. A centimetre of positional error changes the film build by an amount the specification does not notice; what does matter is speed consistency, overlap, humidity, temperature and the charge on the part.75 The plate's band sits an order of magnitude to the right of everything else in its family, and that is not a criticism of paint robots. It is the correct engineering answer: do not buy precision a process cannot use.

Assembly: the chamfer as a machine

Insertion is where the tolerance argument becomes concrete. Take a peg of 9.980 mm entering a hole of 10.000 mm: the radial clearance is 0.010 mm, which is smaller than the robot's repeatability and very much smaller than the uncertainty of where the part is. Approached rigidly with no lead-in, the insertion succeeds only if the lateral error is under 0.0027 mm at one sigma, which nothing in a factory can deliver.

A chamfer 0.50 mm wide multiplies the tolerable error by 51. It is machined into the receiving part for no reason except to forgive the machine that will assemble it, it costs a tool path on an operation that was happening anyway, and it is the single highest-value design-for-assembly feature there is.55,52

But a chamfer only solves one of the two ways an insertion fails. The other is wedging: once the peg is in far enough for two-point contact, an angular misalignment beyond a critical value jams it, and pushing harder makes it worse.76,77 With the lab's parameters that ceiling sits at 8.0 per cent — no amount of lateral precision lifts a rigid insertion above it. The remote centre compliance, designed at Draper Laboratory in the nineteen-seventies, places the compliance centre at the peg tip so that contact forces themselves correct the angle, removing the jamming mode entirely.78,79 It adds no capture radius at all.

The third strategy buys capture with time. A force-guided spiral search presses down and spirals outward until the contact force drops, which tolerates 300 times the lateral error of an unchamfered hole. It costs 0.049 s on average at a quarter-millimetre of uncertainty, with a ninety-fifth percentile of 0.147 s, and 3.53 s in the worst case at the edge of the search. That is the move from D2 to D5, and the exchange rate is explicit: seconds for millimetres.40,80,81,82,83,84,85,86,87

A chamfer and a compliance look like alternatives. They fix different failures, and a cell that has one still has the other.

Handling: where tolerance stops mattering

Threaded fastening is the insertion problem with a helix: the screw point finds the hole through its own chamfer and a floating spindle, and the result is judged by a torque-angle curve rather than a position. Cross-threading is the characteristic failure, and it is characteristic because it looks like a successful cycle until somebody pulls the joint apart.88,89 Palletising sits at the far end of the scale: a stored pattern, a pallet corner, cases that are themselves compliant, and tolerances measured in centimetres. What constrains a palletising cell is reach, payload, stack stability and the arithmetic of how boxes tile a layer — problems of packing, not of position.90,91 High-rate packaging is the third regime again: a delta over a moving belt, a camera that saw each item, and a cycle time so short that settling time is the binding constraint.21,92

08Tending machines, and being one

Machine tending is the largest non-welding application of industrial robots and the one where the reference argument is easiest to see. The robot does not have to place the part accurately. It has to get the part inside the capture range of the chuck, the vice or the locating nest, which then establishes the position itself. A cell that loads a lathe is running a D2 reference at the destination and can tolerate millimetres at the approach.

What actually ruins such cells is not position but debris. A chip on a seating face lifts the part by a tenth of a millimetre, the machine cuts a part that is out of tolerance, and nothing in the robot's world observes it. Air blast, seat-check sensors and presence confirmation are the standard answers, and they are all instruments of the same principle: if the reference is mechanical, verify the mechanics, not the robot.

Robotic machining inverts the whole argument and is worth including for that reason. Here the robot is not placing a part against a datum; it is the machine tool, and the cutting force pushes it off the commanded path. A machining centre has a structural loop stiffness on the order of tens of newtons per micrometre; an articulated arm has roughly a fiftieth of that, and its stiffness varies with pose.93,94,95 The resulting deviation is a D0 quantity in the sense used here: it is referred to nothing, because the encoder that reports joint position sits upstream of the deflection. Compensation strategies exist — stiffness models, force measurement, mirror milling — and they all amount to giving the deflection a reference it did not have.16,96

The integration itself is more mundane and more often the source of trouble. A machine tool speaks G-code and a set of discrete signals; a robot speaks its own language and another set of discrete signals.97,98 Handshakes, door interlocks, chuck confirmation and part-present sensing are all state, not geometry, and state is the PLC's business. Richer interfaces exist — an information model of the machining process rather than a stream of axis moves99,100, or a common address space over which the cell's devices publish what they are doing101 — but the majority of installed cells still coordinate through wires carrying booleans, for the same reason teach pendants persist. The industrial view of where robot control has and has not moved over that period is worth reading from someone who built the controllers.102

09Measuring: buying a reference back

Everything above D2 on the ladder is a purchase of information. A sensor measures something the cell had previously arranged to already know, and the purchase is justified exactly when arranging to know it mechanically is more expensive than measuring it.

Two-dimensional vision is the workhorse. A camera at a known standoff finds features on a part lying in a plane and the stored path is offset by the difference. The engineering is almost entirely lighting37,36; the arithmetic is almost entirely calibration, and the calibration has two halves that are routinely confused. Camera calibration relates pixels to millimetres. Hand-eye calibration relates the camera's frame to the robot's, and is the solution of a homogeneous transform equation from a set of paired motions.34,35,103 A cell whose camera calibration is good and whose hand-eye calibration is stale will produce measurements that are precise, repeatable and wrong.

Three-dimensional bin picking is the hardest problem in the census and the one most often oversold. A point cloud of a heap is matched against a model, a grasp pose is proposed, and the arm reaches in. The pose estimate is good enough to lift with and not good enough to assemble with, which is why nearly every deployed bin-picking cell ends with a re-referencing step: the part is dropped onto a nest, or presented to a camera, or pushed against a stop, before anything precise happens to it.104,105,106 The picking cell buys its way up to D4 and then deliberately falls back to D2, because D2 is tighter.

Force sensing is the only reference in the article that is established where the work is actually done, and the only one with no line of sight problem.40,81 Its cost is time, and at high rates time is the entire business case.

The inspection paradox

An inspection station measures a part and decides whether it conforms. The decision is not a measurement: it is an inference from a measurement that has its own uncertainty, and the standards are explicit that the uncertainty is charged to whoever is making the claim.107,108 In practice the acceptance interval is shrunk by the expanded uncertainty at each end, which takes the uncertainty out of the manufacturer's tolerance.

With an expanded uncertainty of a tenth of the tolerance width, 80 per cent of the nominal tolerance survives; at three tenths, 40 per cent survives and the scheme becomes unusable, scrapping 39 per cent of conforming parts. On a process where 0.55 per cent of parts are genuinely out of tolerance, guarded acceptance at the tenth-of-tolerance level discards 2.7 per cent of production, which is 4.9 good parts thrown away for every bad one present. Accept without a guard band instead and 22 per cent of the nonconforming parts ship.109,110,111,112,113

The measurement is subtracted twice: once from the tolerance, and again from the yield.

This is not an argument against in-line inspection. It is an argument for treating the measuring system as a contributor to the stack, with its own line in the budget, rather than as an oracle standing outside it. A robot carrying a sensor is a measuring instrument whose uncertainty includes the robot.114

10The control layer, which references nothing

The programmable controller, the fieldbus and the clock that synchronises them do not know where anything is. They carry other people's references. On the ladder used here they all sit at D0, and that is not a slight: it is the reason the control layer is designed the way it is.

The PLC's job is state, not geometry. It arbitrates: the gate is closed, the fixture is clamped, the part is present, the machine is ready, the robot may proceed. It does this on a scan — read inputs, solve the program, write outputs — and the scan plus the handshake gives a granularity of tens of milliseconds.115,116,117 At the reference tool speed that is 8.7 mm of travel, which is why no one has ever put a PLC inside a motion loop and why the interlock architecture of a cell is deliberately arranged so that nothing moves while the handshake is in progress.118,119

The languages of that layer reward a second look, because they are artefacts of the same argument. Ladder diagram survives because it was drawn to be read by people who maintained relay panels; the standard that codified it alongside structured text, function blocks, instruction lists and sequential function charts was an attempt to let each part of a cell be written in the notation that fits it.115,120,121 Sequential function charts are state machines because cell coordination is a state machine. Nothing in any of the five languages has a notion of a coordinate frame.

Timing is a length

The moment a tool is moving, a delivery time becomes a distance. If a set-point arrives with an uncertainty of J seconds and the tool is travelling at v, the position is uncertain by vJ. That one line converts the entire industrial-network literature into the same units as the rest of this article.

At the reference speed, three sigma of jitter-induced error is 8.7 mm for a PLC handshake, 0.87 mm for an ordinary cyclic bus running a ten-millisecond period, 0.087 mm at a millisecond, and 0.0009 mm for a synchronised motion bus distributing a clock to every node. Read the other way: an ordinary ten-millisecond bus exhausts the whole 0.500 mm tolerance on its own at 577 mm/s, a one-millisecond bus at 5774 mm/s, and a PLC handshake at 58 mm/s — walking pace.

This is the entire case for the third generation of field-level networks. The first generation put a serial bus where a wire loom had been; the second put Ethernet where the serial bus had been; the third attacked jitter directly, by scheduling the medium and distributing time.122,123,124,125 The specific mechanisms differ — a summation frame clocked through the slaves, a reserved cyclic phase with isochronous transmission, hardware timestamping against a shared grandmaster — but the figure of merit is the same, and it is jitter rather than bandwidth or latency.126,127,128 Time-sensitive networking generalises the last of those into standard bridges, with a synchronisation protocol and a gated scheduler, so that a control traffic class can share infrastructure with everything else and still arrive when it said it would.129,130,131,132

The network is not a datum. It is what makes a datum arrive on time.

Safety travels on the same wire and behaves differently. A safety protocol assumes the network is a black channel — possibly corrupting, delaying, repeating, losing or reordering messages — and defends with sequence numbers, timestamps, cross-checks and a watchdog.133 The watchdog is the point: if the expected message does not arrive within the interval, the receiver goes to the safe state. That interval is dead travel before a stop begins, tens of milliseconds of it, and it becomes the first term of every safety distance in the next section. A safety network never fails silently, which is why its errors show up as nuisance stops rather than as accidents — and why the temptation to widen the watchdog is the characteristic way such systems are degraded in service.

11Keeping people out of it

Safety distances are millimetres too. They are three decades larger than everything else in the census, which is why they are usually discussed in a different room by different people, but the arithmetic is continuous with the arithmetic of a tolerance stack: a set of times, multiplied by speeds, added up, with a margin for what is not known.134,135

The classical form is one line. The distance from a detection plane to the hazard is the approach speed times the sum of the detection and stopping times, plus an intrusion allowance that depends on how coarse the detection is: S = K(t1 + t2) + C, with K taken as 1600 mm/s for a normal approach.135,136,137 For a light curtain of 20 mm resolution in front of a machine that stops in 350 ms, that is 656 mm of floor. Every 100 ms of stopping time costs 160 mm of it. This is why stopping performance, not speed, is the parameter a cell layout is actually sensitive to.

Speed and separation monitoring generalises the same equation to a distance that is recomputed continuously while a person and a robot share space.138,139,140 The protective separation distance is the operator's approach during the reaction and stopping time, plus the robot's own travel during both, plus intrusion and position uncertainties for both parties. At 1 m/s with a 300 ms stop the lab gets 1490 mm; halving the robot's speed to 500 mm/s gives 1365 mm, a reduction of only 8.4 per cent.

The reason halving robot speed does so little is that 640 mm of the distance — 43 per cent of it — is the operator walking towards the machine while the system notices and stops. That term does not contain the robot at all. It is the strongest argument in the census for the position taken by the current edition of the robot safety standards: that collaboration is a property of an application, not of a machine, and cannot be certified into a product.141,3,142

Power and force limiting takes the opposite route: accept the contact and make it tolerable. The transferred energy in a collision is half the effective mass times the square of the relative speed, where the effective mass is dominated by the smaller of the robot's reflected mass and the body region's.143,144,145 Speed enters as a square, so the limits bite hard: on the lab's illustrative energy limits the permissible relative speed lands around 539 mm/s. Giving up 46 per cent of process speed removes 75 per cent of the cell's floor area, from 14.0 m² down to 3.5 m². That is the trade, stated in the units each side is paid in.146,147,148

What the arithmetic of a safety function rewards

Behind every one of these distances is a control function that has to be trusted to detect and to stop, and its trustworthiness is a number: a probability of dangerous failure per hour, assigned a performance level or a safety integrity level.149,150,151 The lab prices a two-channel function in the underlying reliability arithmetic rather than through the standard's own tables, in order to see which term dominates.

From a starting point of a hundred-year mean time to dangerous failure and sixty per cent diagnostic coverage, doubling the mean time improves the figure 3.3 times, raising the coverage to ninety per cent improves it 9.9 times, and halving the common-cause factor improves it 1.10. Redundancy without diagnostics is close to worthless, because an undetected dangerous failure in one channel simply waits for the other. And the more the diagnostics improve, the more of what remains is the single failure that takes both channels together: the common-cause share rises from 21 per cent at sixty per cent coverage to 91 per cent at ninety-nine.152 Past a certain point, buying better components buys nothing; the only thing left to buy is independence — separated cabling, diverse technology, different failure modes.

The 2025 editions of the robot safety standards absorbed most of the collaborative technical specification into the part that governs cells, made the functional safety requirements explicit rather than implied, added requirements on safety-related cybersecurity, and introduced default performance levels that a designer may depart from only with a fuller risk assessment.141,3,153,138 The United States adoption followed in September 2025 and renamed the safety-rated monitored stop to a monitored standstill.153 In Europe the two parts were cited in the Official Journal in September 2026, giving them presumption of conformity.154,155 For a practitioner the important structural point is unchanged and worth restating: the robot is partly completed machinery and the cell is the machine. Nothing about a robot's own conformity tells you whether the application built from it is safe.156,157,158

12The other robot

Robotic process automation is on the list of topics this article was asked to cover, and it does not belong to the same subject. That is worth demonstrating rather than asserting, and the ladder demonstrates it cleanly.

J01Robotic process automationD0
refers toa screen position or an element selector, not a physical datum
paid indays, not months — which is the whole of its appeal
drifts whenwhen the application it drives is updated, on someone else's schedule
leavesan automation with no tolerance at all: it works or it does not

An RPA tool drives business applications through their user interfaces or their scripting surfaces, replaying a recorded sequence of interactions.159,160,161 The analogy with an industrial robot is real and it is exactly one layer deep: both replay a recorded sequence against a world they assume has not moved. Devol's patent and a recorded click share that much.

The analogy breaks at the tolerance. Every other mechanism in this census has a quantity that can be a little bit wrong: a position, a distance, an angle, an interval. Degradation is gradual and detectable, and the whole engineering discipline consists of budgeting it. A selector either resolves or it does not. There is no half-millimetre of slack in an element identifier, no chamfer to funnel a mistaken click into the right control, and no sensor that reports that the automation is drifting.

The consequence is a different failure profile. Forty-two addressed elements, each with a six per cent chance of changing in a year, give a 93 per cent chance of at least one break — 2.5 breaks a year, about one every 4.8 months. And the detection story inverts: a physical cell finds its faults with a sensor on the cycle they happen, while a software robot is found out by somebody downstream, later, after the bad transactions have accumulated.162,160

None of this makes RPA a bad technology. It makes it a different one, with a governance problem rather than a metrology problem: the reference it depends on is owned by somebody else, changes on somebody else's schedule, and gives no notice. The right comparison is not to a welding cell but to a screen-scraping integration, and the right engineering response is the one for brittle integrations — an interface contract, a monitored canary, and a plan for the day the vendor ships a release.

13What the census shows

Forty mechanisms, six rungs, five validity horizons, and one axis of length spanning 5.3 decades from a synchronised clock to a safety separation distance. The distribution is not uniform: 10 plates sit on the mechanical-datum rung, 9 reference nothing positional, 8 rely on a stored point, and only 3 measure the workpiece while the motion is under way. The mean rung is 1.95.

Fourteen of the thirty rung-by-horizon cells are occupied, and the empty ones are structural rather than accidental. Nothing establishes a reference during a motion and then keeps it for a shift, because a reference measured during a motion is consumed by that motion. Nothing uses a mechanical datum for a single shot and then discards it, because the cost of a mechanical datum is entirely in making it and nothing in using it again.

The ladder does not buy precision

The expected result would be that residual uncertainty falls as you climb: better references, tighter numbers. It does not. Setting the safety family aside, because its lengths are separation distances rather than workpiece tolerances, the geometric mean of the bands runs 0.487 mm at D0, 0.643 at D1, 0.187 at D2, 0.430 at D3, 0.281 at D4 and 0.084 at D5. The correlation between rung and log magnitude is -0.218 — real but weak, and the line is not monotonic.

The dip is the finding. A machined datum leaves less uncertainty than a calibrated model above it and less than a camera above that. A piece of located steel is the second tightest thing in the census; the only rung that beats it is the one that measures contact during the motion itself, and that rung has two members. So what are D3 and D4 for, if they are looser than the rung below?

The validity axis answers it. D2's horizon is per part: the fixture holds the part for as long as the part is in it, and holds the next one the same way, and holds it without being told what the part is. What it cannot do is hold a different part. Every rung above D2 is bought not for precision but for permission to stop holding the part still — to let it arrive on a belt, in a heap, in a different orientation, or as a different part number altogether. The ladder is a scale of flexibility priced in millimetres and seconds, and the census says the price is real: the flexible rungs are looser, and the mechanisms that recover the tightness — a nest, a chuck, a stop at the destination — are all D2 again.

You do not climb the ladder to get more accurate. You climb it to stop needing a fixture, and you pay in millimetres.

The rung that references nothing is the widest

D0 has the largest spread of any rung: 1.38 decades, from a synchronised motion bus at thousandths of a millimetre to a safety watchdog at tens. 6 of its 8 measurable plates exceed the reference tolerance. This is not a contradiction. ‘References nothing positional’ says nothing whatever about magnitude; it says only that the quantity is not answerable to any datum, and the consequence is that D0 mechanisms are never put in the position chain. The synchronised bus is not a datum; it is what lets a datum somewhere else be believed. The watchdog is not a distance; it is a distance's first term. Drivetrain compliance and cutting deflection are not errors anyone budgeted; they are errors that arrive downstream of the instrument that was supposed to see them.

Against the reference tolerance

Of the 40 plates, 15 have bands entirely inside the 0.500 mm reference tolerance, 13 straddle it, and 11 lie entirely outside it. Figure 14 puts all of them on one axis, which is the single most useful page in the article for anyone laying out a cell: it shows at a glance which mechanisms can be asked to hold a half-millimetre and which must be arranged around.

Does the coding earn its place?

Across the 780 unordered pairs of plates, the rung alone distinguishes 83.1 per cent, rung crossed with validity 92.7 per cent, and subject matter — the nine families this article is organised into — 88.6 per cent. On separation alone the two codings are close, and that is the wrong test. The interesting number is where they disagree: 54 pairs share a family but sit on different rungs, and 97 pairs sit on the same rung in different families — 151 pairs in all, 19.4 per cent, where the ladder says something subject matter does not.

Those are the pairs worth looking at. A taught arc-welding path and a seam tracker are both welding and are two rungs apart; the difference is the whole of why one of them exists. A chamfer, a chuck, a light curtain and a bowl feeder are four different trades and one rung: all four are assertions in steel about where something is, and all four fail the same way, by being knocked, worn or bypassed. A cell designer who notices that has learnt something a topic list would never have taught them.

14What follows for a cell

Six consequences, each of which is a restatement of something the census showed rather than advice added on top.

Specify the task, not the robot. The datasheet quotes repeatability, which owned 1.6 per cent of the variance in the example stack. Write down the chain from datum to tool point, assign a number to every link, and the purchase decision usually turns out to be about the fixture and the gripper.

Prefer a datum to a measurement, and a measurement to a hope. D2 is tighter, cheaper and more diagnosable than D3 or D4. Climb only when the flexibility is the thing you actually want. When a reference has been lost in transit, re-establish it at the destination rather than trying to carry it.

Design the part for the machine that will assemble it. A chamfer 0.50 mm wide bought a factor of 51 in tolerable error for the cost of a tool path. Nothing else in the article has that return.

Know which failure a fix addresses. A chamfer and a compliance device look interchangeable and fix different things; the jamming ceiling of 8.0 per cent is invisible until somebody separates the two mechanisms.

Convert every interval into a length before arguing about networks. A bus is fast enough or not fast enough only with respect to a tool speed and a tolerance. At 250 mm/s almost anything works; at 2 m/s a millisecond of jitter is a visible defect.

Treat the inspection station as part of the stack. Its uncertainty is subtracted from the tolerance the shop can use, and then again from the yield. A measuring system consuming a tenth of the tolerance width threw away 4.9 conforming parts for every nonconforming one present.

And one that is not about millimetres at all: when a technology is brought into a cell by analogy — because it is called a robot, or because it automates something — ask what its tolerance is. If there is no quantity that can be slightly wrong, the engineering practices of this discipline do not transfer, and the ones that do transfer come from software integration instead.

16The lab

Every number quoted in this article comes from the program below. It uses nothing outside the Python standard library, takes a fixed seed of 20260921, and produces byte-identical output under different interpreter hash seeds — which had to be checked, because a traversal over an unordered collection is reproducible within a run and not across runs.

The eleven experiments are: a forward-kinematic model of a generic six-revolute arm with geometric error, joint compliance and gear transmission error, used to separate accuracy from repeatability and to measure what a 26-parameter calibration can and cannot remove; a tolerance stack combined three ways; the two independent failure modes of a peg entering a hole; jitter converted into length; the growth of weld distortion along a seam; separation distances and the energy limit behind power and force limiting; the reliability arithmetic of a two-channel safety function; a vibratory feeder against a camera; guard banding and the two error rates it trades; the brittleness of a selector-based automation; and the census statistics of the forty plates.

# tiny linear algebra (dense, list-of-lists; sizes here are at most ~30x30)

def mat_eye(n):
    return [[1.0 if i == j else 0.0 for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def mat_mul(a, b):
    n, k, m = len(a), len(b), len(b[0])
    out = [[0.0] * m for _ in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        ai = a[i]
        oi = out[i]
        for t in range(k):
            v = ai[t]
            if v == 0.0:
                continue
            bt = b[t]
            for j in range(m):
                oi[j] += v * bt[j]
    return out


def solve_spd(a, b):
    """Solve a x = b for symmetric positive definite a by Cholesky.

    Returns None if the matrix is not positive definite (rank deficient),
    which is the honest signal that a parameter set is unidentifiable.
    """
    n = len(a)
    L = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1):
            s = a[i][j] - sum(L[i][k] * L[j][k] for k in range(j))
            if i == j:
                if s <= 0.0:
                    return None
                L[i][i] = math.sqrt(s)
            else:
                L[i][j] = s / L[j][j]
    y = [0.0] * n
    for i in range(n):
        y[i] = (b[i] - sum(L[i][k] * y[k] for k in range(i))) / L[i][i]
    x = [0.0] * n
    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        x[i] = (y[i] - sum(L[k][i] * x[k] for k in range(i + 1, n))) / L[i][i]
    return x


def mean(xs):
    return sum(xs) / len(xs)


def stdev(xs):
    if len(xs) < 2:
        return 0.0
    m = mean(xs)
    return math.sqrt(sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in xs) / (len(xs) - 1))


def pct(xs, q):
    """Linear-interpolated percentile of a list (does not mutate the input)."""
    s = sorted(xs)
    if len(s) == 1:
        return s[0]
    k = (len(s) - 1) * q
    lo = int(math.floor(k))
    hi = min(lo + 1, len(s) - 1)
    return s[lo] + (s[hi] - s[lo]) * (k - lo)


def norm_cdf(x):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))

# kinematics of a generic six-revolute arm  (standard Denavit-Hartenberg)
# ==========================================================================
#
# Link i is described by (a_i, alpha_i, d_i, theta_offset_i) in mm / radians.
# Proportions are those of a mid-size industrial arm: ~1.5 m reach, wrist
# offset 65 mm.  These are nominal; the "real" robot of E1 differs from them
# by small errors that the controller does not know about.

NOMINAL_DH = [
    # a (mm)   alpha (deg)   d (mm)    theta offset (deg)
    (150.0,   -90.0,        486.5,     0.0),
    (700.0,     0.0,          0.0,   -90.0),
    (115.0,   -90.0,          0.0,     0.0),
    (0.0,      90.0,        600.0,     0.0),
    (0.0,     -90.0,          0.0,     0.0),
    (0.0,       0.0,         65.0,     0.0),
]

D2R = math.pi / 180.0


def dh_transform(a, alpha, d, theta):
    ca, sa = math.cos(alpha), math.sin(alpha)
    ct, st = math.cos(theta), math.sin(theta)
    return [
        [ct, -st * ca,  st * sa, a * ct],
        [st,  ct * ca, -ct * sa, a * st],
        [0.0,      sa,       ca,      d],
        [0.0,     0.0,      0.0,    1.0],
    ]


def forward(dh, q, tool=(0.0, 0.0, 0.0)):
    """Position of the tool point, in mm, for joint vector q (radians)."""
    T = mat_eye(4)
    for i in range(6):
        a, alpha_deg, d, off_deg = dh[i]
        T = mat_mul(T, dh_transform(a, alpha_deg * D2R, d,
                                    q[i] + off_deg * D2R))
    tx, ty, tz = tool
    p = [T[r][0] * tx + T[r][1] * ty + T[r][2] * tz + T[r][3] for r in range(3)]
    return p


def gravity_torques(q, payload_kg):
    """Crude static torque on joints 2 and 3 from a payload at the flange.

    Only the sign and the order of magnitude matter: the torque falls as the
    arm folds in, which is what makes compliance error configuration
    dependent -- and therefore, unlike repeatability, removable by a model.
    """
    a2 = NOMINAL_DH[1][0] / 1000.0
    a3 = (NOMINAL_DH[2][0] + NOMINAL_DH[3][2]) / 1000.0
    g = 9.81
    r2 = a2 * math.cos(q[1] - math.pi / 2.0) + a3 * math.cos(q[1] + q[2] - math.pi / 2.0)
    r3 = a3 * math.cos(q[1] + q[2] - math.pi / 2.0)
    return (payload_kg * g * r2, payload_kg * g * r3)


def apply_errors(dh_nom, err):
    """err: dict of parameter index -> delta.  Returns a perturbed DH table."""
    out = []
    for i in range(6):
        a, al, d, th = dh_nom[i]
        out.append((a + err["a"][i], al + err["alpha"][i],
                    d + err["d"][i], th + err["theta"][i]))
    return out


def random_config(rng):
    """A reachable configuration inside a working envelope."""
    lim = [(-150, 150), (-60, 80), (-70, 60), (-160, 160), (-100, 100), (-180, 180)]
    return [rng.uniform(lo, hi) * D2R for lo, hi in lim]

# E1  repeatability, accuracy, and what calibration actually converts
# ==========================================================================


def e1():
    head(1, "Repeatability, accuracy, and what calibration converts")
    rng = random.Random(SEED + 1)

    # ---- the "real" robot: systematic geometric error, drawn once ---------
    err = {"a": [], "alpha": [], "d": [], "theta": []}
    for _ in range(6):
        err["a"].append(rng.gauss(0.0, 0.25))        # mm
        err["d"].append(rng.gauss(0.0, 0.25))        # mm
        err["alpha"].append(rng.gauss(0.0, 0.040))   # deg
        err["theta"].append(rng.gauss(0.0, 0.040))   # deg
    real_dh = apply_errors(NOMINAL_DH, err)

    # joint compliance, rad per N.m, on the two gravity-loaded joints
    compliance = [0.0, 3.1e-7, 5.4e-7, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
    payload = 6.0                                     # kg at the flange
    tool = (0.0, 0.0, 120.0)                          # 120 mm tool offset

    # Gear transmission error: a reducer's output angle oscillates against its
    # input over one revolution.  Half an arc-minute of amplitude is ordinary
    # for a production reducer.  It is periodic in the joint angle, so no
    # Denavit-Hartenberg parameter can absorb it -- this is the unmodelled
    # term that decides what calibration cannot reach.
    te_amp = [0.5 / 60.0 * D2R] * 3 + [0.25 / 60.0 * D2R] * 3   # rad
    te_phase = [rng.uniform(0.0, 2.0 * math.pi) for _ in range(6)]
    te_order = [1.0, 1.0, 2.0, 1.0, 2.0, 1.0]

    # repeatability source: servo settling + encoder quantisation, per joint
    sigma_joint = 0.00135 * D2R                       # rad, 1 sigma

    def measure(q, with_noise=True):
        qq = list(q)
        if with_noise:
            qq = [qi + rng.gauss(0.0, sigma_joint) for qi in qq]
        for i in range(6):
            qq[i] += te_amp[i] * math.sin(te_order[i] * qq[i] + te_phase[i])
        t2, t3 = gravity_torques(qq, payload)
        qq[1] -= compliance[1] * t2
        qq[2] -= compliance[2] * t3
        return forward(real_dh, qq, tool)

    # ---- ISO 9283-style test: five poses in the test cube, 30 cycles ------
    sub("ISO 9283 pose accuracy AP and pose repeatability RP (5 poses x 30 cycles)")
    poses = [random_config(rng) for _ in range(5)]
    ap_list, rp_list = [], []
    cloud = None
    for pi, q in enumerate(poses):
        cmd = forward(NOMINAL_DH, q, tool)            # where the controller thinks it is
        pts = [measure(q) for _ in range(30)]
        if pi == 0:
            cloud = (cmd, [list(p) for p in pts])
        bx = mean([p[0] for p in pts])
        by = mean([p[1] for p in pts])
        bz = mean([p[2] for p in pts])
        ap = math.dist(cmd, (bx, by, bz))
        li = [math.dist(p, (bx, by, bz)) for p in pts]
        rp = mean(li) + 3.0 * stdev(li)
        ap_list.append(ap)
        rp_list.append(rp)
        row("pose P%d   AP = %.3f mm" % (pi + 1, ap), "RP = %.4f mm" % rp)
    ap_mean, rp_mean = mean(ap_list), mean(rp_list)
    row("mean pose accuracy AP", "%.3f" % ap_mean, "mm")
    row("mean pose repeatability RP", "%.4f" % rp_mean, "mm")
    row("ratio AP / RP", "%.1f" % (ap_mean / rp_mean), "x")

    # ---- validation set before calibration --------------------------------
    sub("Absolute position error over 1000 arbitrary configurations")
    val = [random_config(rng) for _ in range(1000)]
    pre = []
    for q in val:
        cmd = forward(NOMINAL_DH, q, tool)
        act = measure(q, with_noise=False)
        pre.append(math.dist(cmd, act))
    row("mean absolute error, uncalibrated", "%.3f" % mean(pre), "mm")
    row("max  absolute error, uncalibrated", "%.3f" % max(pre), "mm")

    # ---- identification: 26 parameters, damped least squares --------------
    sub("Calibration: 26-parameter fit to 60 measured configurations")
    cal_q = [random_config(rng) for _ in range(60)]
    cal_p = [measure(q, with_noise=True) for q in cal_q]   # measured with noise

    # parameter vector: 6 a, 6 d, 6 alpha(deg), 6 theta(deg), 2 compliance
    def model(params, q):
        e = {"a": params[0:6], "d": params[6:12],
             "alpha": params[12:18], "theta": params[18:24]}
        dh = apply_errors(NOMINAL_DH, e)
        qq = list(q)
        t2, t3 = gravity_torques(qq, payload)
        qq[1] -= params[24] * 1e-7 * t2
        qq[2] -= params[25] * 1e-7 * t3
        return forward(dh, qq, tool)

    npar = 26
    p = [0.0] * npar
    lam = 1e-3
    step = [1e-3] * 24 + [1e-2, 1e-2]
    rms_hist = []
    for it in range(14):
        res, J = [], []
        for q, meas in zip(cal_q, cal_p):
            f0 = model(p, q)
            for k in range(3):
                res.append(meas[k] - f0[k])
            rows = [[0.0] * npar for _ in range(3)]
            for j in range(npar):
                pp = list(p)
                pp[j] += step[j]
                f1 = model(pp, q)
                for k in range(3):
                    rows[k][j] = (f1[k] - f0[k]) / step[j]
            J.extend(rows)
        n = len(res)
        rms = math.sqrt(sum(r * r for r in res) / (n / 3.0))
        rms_hist.append(rms)
        JTJ = [[sum(J[r][i] * J[r][j] for r in range(n)) for j in range(npar)]
               for i in range(npar)]
        for i in range(npar):
            JTJ[i][i] += lam * (1.0 + JTJ[i][i])
        JTr = [sum(J[r][i] * res[r] for r in range(n)) for i in range(npar)]
        delta = solve_spd(JTJ, JTr)
        if delta is None:
            say("    normal equations not positive definite -- stopping")
            break
        p = [p[i] + delta[i] for i in range(npar)]
    row("residual RMS, iteration 1", "%.4f" % rms_hist[0], "mm")
    row("residual RMS, final iteration", "%.4f" % rms_hist[-1], "mm")
    row("iterations", "%d" % len(rms_hist), "")

    # ---- validation after calibration -------------------------------------
    post = []
    for q in val:
        cmd = model(p, q)                # what the calibrated controller aims at
        act = measure(q, with_noise=False)
        post.append(math.dist(cmd, act))
    row("mean absolute error, calibrated", "%.3f" % mean(post), "mm")
    row("max  absolute error, calibrated", "%.3f" % max(post), "mm")
    row("mean error reduction", "%.2f" % (mean(pre) / mean(post)), "x")
    row("calibrated mean error / RP", "%.1f" % (mean(post) / rp_mean), "x")

    # ---- the part calibration cannot touch --------------------------------
    sub("What is left")
    floor = rp_mean / 3.0    # 1-sigma equivalent of the repeatability figure
    row("repeatability floor (RP)", "%.4f" % rp_mean, "mm")
    row("residual error after fit", "%.3f" % mean(post), "mm")
    row("share of uncalibrated error removed by the fit",
        "%.1f%%" % (100.0 * (1.0 - mean(post) / mean(pre))), "")
    row("residual / repeatability", "%.1f" % (mean(post) / rp_mean), "x")
    say("")
    say("    The residual is not measurement noise and not repeatability.  It")
    say("    is the gear transmission error: periodic in the joint angle, and")
    say("    therefore invisible to a model whose parameters are constants.")

    REC["e1"] = {
        "ap_mean": ap_mean, "rp_mean": rp_mean, "ap_over_rp": ap_mean / rp_mean,
        "pre_mean": mean(pre), "pre_max": max(pre),
        "post_mean": mean(post), "post_max": max(post),
        "reduction": mean(pre) / mean(post),
        "post_over_rp": mean(post) / rp_mean,
        "systematic_share": 100.0 * (1.0 - mean(post) / mean(pre)),
        "rms_first": rms_hist[0], "rms_last": rms_hist[-1],
        "n_params": npar, "n_cal": len(cal_q), "n_val": len(val),
        "sigma_joint_deg": sigma_joint / D2R, "floor": floor,
        "cloud_cmd": cloud[0], "cloud_pts": cloud[1],
        "ap1": ap_list[0], "rp1": rp_list[0],
    }

# E2  the tolerance stack: worst case, root-sum-square, and who owns it

STACK = [
    # name                                    half-width (mm)   rung
    ("robot pose repeatability",                     0.050, "D1"),
    ("taught point transfer to the real part",       0.100, "D1"),
    ("tool centre point definition",                 0.200, "D3"),
    ("fixture location on the table",                0.080, "D2"),
    ("part seating in the fixture",                  0.150, "D2"),
    ("grasp uncertainty in the gripper",             0.250, "D2"),
    ("thermal growth over a shift",                  0.100, "D0"),
]


def e2():
    head(2, "The tolerance stack: worst case, RSS, and who owns the variance")
    rng = random.Random(SEED + 2)
    TOL = 0.500      # mm, the reference task tolerance used throughout

    sub("Contributors (uniform, +/- half-width)")
    for name, hw, rung in STACK:
        row("%s  [%s]" % (name, rung), "%.3f" % hw, "mm")

    hws = [hw for _, hw, _ in STACK]
    wc = sum(hws)
    # variance of a uniform on [-h, h] is h^2/3
    var = sum(h * h / 3.0 for h in hws)
    rss_sigma = math.sqrt(var)
    rss_3s = 3.0 * rss_sigma

    sub("Combination")
    row("worst-case sum", "%.3f" % wc, "mm")
    row("statistical 1 sigma", "%.4f" % rss_sigma, "mm")
    row("statistical 3 sigma", "%.3f" % rss_3s, "mm")
    row("worst case / 3 sigma", "%.2f" % (wc / rss_3s), "x")
    row("reference tolerance", "%.3f" % TOL, "mm")

    # Monte Carlo, one dimension, 200 000 draws
    N = 200000
    over = 0
    draws = []
    for _ in range(N):
        e = sum(rng.uniform(-h, h) for h in hws)
        draws.append(e)
        if abs(e) > TOL:
            over += 1
    p_over = over / N
    row("P(|error| > tolerance), 200k draws", "%.4f%%" % (100.0 * p_over), "")
    row("equivalent parts per million", "%.0f" % (1e6 * p_over), "ppm")
    row("99.73 percentile of |error|", "%.3f" % pct([abs(d) for d in draws], 0.9973), "mm")

    sub("Variance shares")
    shares = []
    for name, hw, rung in STACK:
        s = (hw * hw / 3.0) / var
        shares.append((s, name, hw, rung))
    shares.sort(key=lambda t: -t[0])
    for s, name, hw, rung in shares:
        row("%s" % name, "%.1f%%" % (100.0 * s), "of variance")
    top = shares[0]
    row("largest contributor share of half-width sum",
        "%.1f%%" % (100.0 * top[2] / wc), "")
    row("largest contributor share of variance", "%.1f%%" % (100.0 * top[0]), "")

    # what removing the top contributor buys
    hws2 = [h for h in hws if h != top[2]]
    rss2 = 3.0 * math.sqrt(sum(h * h / 3.0 for h in hws2))
    row("3 sigma after removing the largest contributor", "%.3f" % rss2, "mm")
    row("improvement", "%.1f%%" % (100.0 * (1.0 - rss2 / rss_3s)), "")

    # how many identical contributors fit inside the tolerance
    sub("How many contributors the budget will carry")
    for h in (0.05, 0.10, 0.20):
        n_rss = (TOL / (3.0 * h / math.sqrt(3.0))) ** 2
        n_wc = TOL / h
        row("half-width %.2f mm: worst case allows" % h, "%.1f" % n_wc, "terms")
        row("half-width %.2f mm: statistical allows" % h, "%.1f" % n_rss, "terms")

    REC["e2"] = {
        "tol": TOL, "wc": wc, "sigma": rss_sigma, "three_sigma": rss_3s,
        "wc_over_3s": wc / rss_3s, "p_over": p_over, "ppm": 1e6 * p_over,
        "top_name": top[1], "top_hw": top[2],
        "top_var_share": 100.0 * top[0], "top_hw_share": 100.0 * top[2] / wc,
        "three_sigma_wo_top": rss2,
        "improvement_wo_top": 100.0 * (1.0 - rss2 / rss_3s),
        "n_contrib": len(hws),
        "shares": [(nm, 100.0 * s) for s, nm, _, _ in shares],
    }

def e3():
    head(3, "Peg in hole: the price of a capture radius")
    rng = random.Random(SEED + 3)

    D_HOLE = 10.000      # mm
    D_PEG = 9.980        # mm
    CHAMFER = 0.500      # mm, radial width of the lead-in
    c_rad = (D_HOLE - D_PEG) / 2.0

    sub("Geometry")
    row("hole diameter", "%.3f" % D_HOLE, "mm")
    row("peg diameter", "%.3f" % D_PEG, "mm")
    row("radial clearance", "%.4f" % c_rad, "mm")
    row("chamfer radial width", "%.3f" % CHAMFER, "mm")

    # Two independent failure mechanisms, and they are not fixed by the
    # same thing.  Lateral miss: the peg lands outside the capture radius.
    # Angular jam: the peg enters but wedges on two-point contact.
    THETA_JAM_DEG = 0.35     # deg, wedging limit for a rigid, stiff mount
    sigma_theta = 0.20       # deg, angular uncertainty of the presented hole
    p_jam = 1.0 - (2.0 * norm_cdf(THETA_JAM_DEG / sigma_theta) - 1.0)

    strategies = [
        ("rigid, no chamfer",            c_rad,           True,  "D2"),
        ("rigid, chamfered hole",        c_rad + CHAMFER, True,  "D2"),
        ("chamfer + passive compliance", c_rad + CHAMFER, False, "D2"),
        ("force-guided spiral search",   3.000,           False, "D5"),
    ]

    sub("Failure mechanism 1: the lateral miss")
    say("    A two-dimensional positioning error with standard deviation s in")
    say("    each axis gives a radial miss with a Rayleigh distribution, so")
    say("    the capture radius R is reached 99.9 % of the time when")
    say("    s = R / sqrt(2 ln 1000) = R / 3.717 .")
    results = []
    for name, cap, rigid, rung in strategies:
        s999 = cap / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.log(1000.0))
        results.append((name, cap, s999, rigid, rung))
        row("%s  [%s]" % (name, rung), "capture %.3f mm" % cap,
            "-> sigma 99.9%% = %.4f mm" % s999)
    base = results[0][2]
    for name, cap, sig, rigid, rung in results[1:]:
        row("lateral gain over an unchamfered hole: %s" % name,
            "%.0f" % (sig / base), "x")

    sub("Failure mechanism 2: the angular jam")
    row("wedging limit, two-point contact", "%.2f" % THETA_JAM_DEG, "deg")
    row("angular uncertainty of the presented hole", "%.2f" % sigma_theta, "deg")
    row("jam rate, rigid mount", "%.2f%%" % (100.0 * p_jam), "")
    row("jam rate, compliance centre at the peg tip", "%.2f%%" % 0.0, "")
    say("")
    say("    The chamfer buys 51 times the lateral tolerance and does nothing")
    say("    at all about the angle.  A compliance whose centre sits at the")
    say("    peg tip removes the angular failure and does nothing about the")
    say("    lateral capture.  They are not alternatives; they are two")
    say("    different failures, and a cell that fixes one still has the other.")

    sub("Combined success rate, by strategy")
    for sig_pos in (0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.40):
        say("")
        row("positioning sigma", "%.2f" % sig_pos, "mm")
        for name, cap, s999, rigid, rung in results:
            p_lat = 1.0 - math.exp(-(cap ** 2) / (2.0 * sig_pos * sig_pos))
            p = p_lat * ((1.0 - p_jam) if rigid else 1.0)
            row("   %s" % name, "%.4f" % p, "= %.0f ppm failures" % (1e6 * (1 - p)))

    # ---- spiral search cost ------------------------------------------------
    curves = []
    for name, cap, s999, rigid, rung in results:
        pts = []
        for i in range(61):
            sg = 10 ** (-2.0 + 1.8 * i / 60.0)
            p_lat = 1.0 - math.exp(-(cap ** 2) / (2.0 * sg * sg))
            pts.append((sg, p_lat * ((1.0 - p_jam) if rigid else 1.0)))
        curves.append((name, rung, pts))

    sub("Spiral search: the cycle time a capture radius costs")
    pitch = 0.40         # mm per turn
    feed = 20.0          # mm/s along the path

# E7  the arithmetic of a safety function
# ==========================================================================


def e7():
    head(7, "The arithmetic of a safety function")

    sub("Model")
    say("    A two-channel (1oo2) safety function with diagnostics, priced in")
    say("    the IEC 61508 style: dangerous failure rate lambda_D = 1/MTTFd,")
    say("    split by the diagnostic coverage DC into detected and undetected")
    say("    parts, with a common-cause factor beta applied to both channels.")
    say("")
    say("        PFH = 2[(1-b) lD_u]^2 (T1/2 + MTTR)")
    say("              + 2(1-b)^2 lD_u lD_d MTTR")
    say("              + b lD_u")
    say("")
    say("    This is not ISO 13849's own bar chart; it is the underlying")
    say("    reliability arithmetic that the bar chart summarises.  The point")
    say("    is which term dominates, and that is robust to the difference.")

    T1 = 20.0 * 8760.0 * 3600.0     # 20 year mission time, in seconds
    MTTR = 8.0 * 3600.0             # 8 hours

    def pfh(mttfd_years, dc, beta):
        lam_d = 1.0 / (mttfd_years * 8760.0 * 3600.0)
        ldd = dc * lam_d
        ldu = (1.0 - dc) * lam_d
        t1 = 2.0 * ((1 - beta) * ldu) ** 2 * (T1 / 2.0 + MTTR)
        t2 = 2.0 * (1 - beta) ** 2 * ldu * ldd * MTTR
        t3 = beta * ldu
        return t1 + t2 + t3, (t1, t2, t3)

    def pl_of(p):
        # PL bands of ISO 13849-1, in PFH per hour
        ph = p * 3600.0
        # ISO 13849-1 Table 3: PL e is 1e-8 <= PFH < 1e-7, PL d 1e-7 to 1e-6,
        # PL c 1e-6 to 3e-6, PL b 3e-6 to 1e-5, PL a 1e-5 to 1e-4.
        for lim, name in ((1e-7, "e"), (1e-6, "d"), (3e-6, "c"),
                          (1e-5, "b"), (1e-4, "a")):
            if ph < lim:
                return name, ph
        return "-", ph

    sub("PFH per hour, and the performance level it reaches")
    say("    %-12s %-8s %-8s %-14s %-6s" % ("MTTFd (y)", "DC", "beta",
                                            "PFH / h", "PL"))
    grid = []
    for mttfd in (30.0, 60.0, 100.0, 200.0):
        for dc in (0.60, 0.90, 0.99):
            p, parts = pfh(mttfd, dc, 0.02)
            pl, ph = pl_of(p)
            grid.append((mttfd, dc, 0.02, ph, pl, parts))
            say("    %-12.0f %-8.2f %-8.2f %-14.3e %-6s" % (mttfd, dc, 0.02, ph, pl))

    sub("Which term is doing the work (MTTFd 100 y, beta 2 %)")
    for dc in (0.60, 0.90, 0.99):
        p, (t1, t2, t3) = pfh(100.0, dc, 0.02)
        row("DC %.0f%%: common-cause share of PFH" % (100 * dc),
            "%.1f%%" % (100.0 * t3 / p), "")
    p99, (_, _, t3_99) = pfh(100.0, 0.99, 0.02)
    p60, _ = pfh(100.0, 0.60, 0.02)

    sub("What each lever buys, from MTTFd 100 y / DC 60 %")
    base, _ = pfh(100.0, 0.60, 0.02)
    a, _ = pfh(200.0, 0.60, 0.02)
    b, _ = pfh(100.0, 0.90, 0.02)
    c, _ = pfh(100.0, 0.60, 0.01)
    row("double MTTFd to 200 y", "%.2f" % (base / a), "x better")
    row("raise DC from 60 % to 90 %", "%.2f" % (base / b), "x better")
    row("halve beta from 2 % to 1 %", "%.2f" % (base / c), "x better")

    sub("The floor set by common cause alone")
    for beta in (0.05, 0.02, 0.01):
        lam_d = 1.0 / (100.0 * 8760.0 * 3600.0)
        ldu = 0.01 * lam_d
        row("beta %.0f%%: PFH floor at DC 99%%" % (100 * beta),
            "%.3e" % (beta * ldu * 3600.0), "/h")

    REC["e7"] = {
        "grid": [(m, d, bt, ph, pl) for m, d, bt, ph, pl, _ in grid],
        "cc_share_60": 100.0 * pfh(100.0, 0.60, 0.02)[1][2] / pfh(100.0, 0.60, 0.02)[0],
        "cc_share_90": 100.0 * pfh(100.0, 0.90, 0.02)[1][2] / pfh(100.0, 0.90, 0.02)[0],
        "cc_share_99": 100.0 * t3_99 / p99,
        "gain_mttfd": base / a, "gain_dc": base / b, "gain_beta": base / c,
    }

def e9():
    head(9, "The inspection robot eats the tolerance it is checking")

    sub("Model")
    say("    A characteristic with two-sided tolerance T (here +/- 0.250 mm)")
    say("    is measured by a system with expanded uncertainty U (k = 2).")
    say("    ISO 14253-1 shrinks the acceptance interval by U at each end.")
    say("    Parts are produced with standard deviation sigma_p, centred.")
    say("    Integrating the joint density of true and measured value gives")
    say("    the two error rates exactly.")

    T = 0.250
    sigma_p = 0.090

    def rates(U, guard):
        u = U / 2.0                 # standard uncertainty
        A = T - (U if guard else 0.0)
        if A <= 0:
            return 1.0, 0.0, 0.0
        # numeric integration over the true value
        n = 4001
        lo, hi = -6.0 * sigma_p, 6.0 * sigma_p
        h = (hi - lo) / (n - 1)
        fr = fa = good = bad = 0.0
        for i in range(n):
            x = lo + i * h
            w = (1.0 if i in (0, n - 1) else (4.0 if i % 2 else 2.0)) * h / 3.0
            dens = math.exp(-0.5 * (x / sigma_p) ** 2) / (sigma_p * math.sqrt(2 * math.pi))
            p_acc = norm_cdf((A - x) / u) - norm_cdf((-A - x) / u)
            if abs(x) <= T:
                good += w * dens
                fr += w * dens * (1.0 - p_acc)
            else:
                bad += w * dens
                fa += w * dens * p_acc
        return fr / good, fa / max(bad, 1e-12), bad

    sub("Simple acceptance (no guard band)")
    say("    %-12s %-16s %-16s" % ("U / 2T", "false reject", "false accept"))
    simple = []
    for ratio in (0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30):
        U = ratio * 2.0 * T
        fr, fa, bad = rates(U, guard=False)
        simple.append((ratio, U, fr, fa))
        say("    %-12.2f %-16.4f %-16.4f" % (ratio, fr, fa))

    sub("Guarded acceptance (ISO 14253-1)")
    say("    %-12s %-14s %-16s %-16s" % ("U / 2T", "A (mm)", "false reject",
                                         "false accept"))
    guarded = []
    for ratio in (0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30):
        U = ratio * 2.0 * T
        fr, fa, bad = rates(U, guard=True)
        guarded.append((ratio, U, T - U, fr, fa))
        say("    %-12.2f %-14.3f %-16.4f %-16.4f" % (ratio, T - U, fr, fa))

    sub("The tolerance the measurement takes away")
    for ratio, U, A, fr, fa in guarded:
        row("U/2T = %.2f: usable tolerance" % ratio,
            "%.1f%%" % (100.0 * A / T), "of nominal")
    sub("Absolute rates, on a process with sigma_p = 0.090 mm")
    base_bad = 2.0 * (1.0 - norm_cdf(T / sigma_p))
    row("parts genuinely out of tolerance", "%.3f%%" % (100.0 * base_bad), "")
    for ratio, U, A, fr, fa in guarded:
        good = 1.0 - base_bad
        row("U/2T = %.2f: good parts scrapped" % ratio,
            "%.3f%%" % (100.0 * fr * good), "of all parts")
        row("U/2T = %.2f: bad parts shipped" % ratio,
            "%.4f%%" % (100.0 * fa * base_bad), "of all parts")
    g10 = [g for g in guarded if abs(g[0] - 0.10) < 1e-9][0]
    scrap10 = g10[3] * (1.0 - base_bad)
    row("at U/2T = 0.10, good parts scrapped per bad part present",
        "%.1f" % (scrap10 / base_bad), "")
    r10 = [g for g in guarded if abs(g[0] - 0.10) < 1e-9][0]
    r30 = [g for g in guarded if abs(g[0] - 0.30) < 1e-9][0]
    s10 = [s for s in simple if abs(s[0] - 0.10) < 1e-9][0]
    row("scrap rate with no measurement error at all",
        "%.4f" % (2.0 * (1.0 - norm_cdf(T / sigma_p))), "")

    fine = []
    for i in range(25):
        ratio = 0.02 + (0.38 - 0.02) * i / 24.0
        U = ratio * 2.0 * T
        frg, fag, _ = rates(U, guard=True)
        frs, fas, _ = rates(U, guard=False)
        fine.append((ratio, frg, fag, frs, fas))

    REC["e9"] = {
        "T": T, "sigma_p": sigma_p, "fine": fine,
        "simple": simple, "guarded": guarded,
        "fr10": r10[3], "fa10_simple": s10[3],
        "usable10": 100.0 * r10[2] / T, "usable30": 100.0 * r30[2] / T,
        "fr30": r30[3],
        "base_scrap": 2.0 * (1.0 - norm_cdf(T / sigma_p)),
        "base_bad": base_bad, "scrap10": scrap10,
        "scrap_per_bad10": scrap10 / base_bad,
    }

            dr = a["rung"] != b["rung"]
            dv = a["validity"] != b["validity"]
            if dr:
                sep_rung += 1
            if dv:
                sep_val += 1
            if dr or dv:
                sep_both += 1
            if a["family"] != b["family"]:
                sep_fam += 1

    widest = max(ramp, key=lambda t: t[3])
    row("rung with the widest spread", "%s" % widest[0],

    sub("Against the 0.500 mm reference tolerance")
    clears = [p for p in sized if p["hi"] <= 0.5]
