01The space that is not yours

An industrial robot works inside a cell. The cell is a space the robot owns. It is fenced, surveyed, lit to a specification, emptied of people by interlock, and supplied with a workpiece presented in a known pose at a known instant. Every one of those conditions is a promise made to the machine by whoever built the cell, and the robot's programme is a bet on those promises. The fence is what makes the bet safe: when a promise fails, the failure is contained inside a volume nobody is standing in.

A service robot has no cell. The international vocabulary defines it as a robot performing useful tasks for people or equipment, excluding industrial automation applications, which is a definition by exclusion and tells you almost nothing.1 The useful definition is spatial. A service robot works in a space that belongs to somebody else: a kitchen floor, a hospital corridor, a dairy barn, a pavement, a hotel landing, an orchard, a sewer, a collapsed apartment block, a classroom. Nobody laid that space out for a machine. Nobody will empty it on request. The people in it did not sign anything, have not been briefed, and are under no obligation to behave predictably. The space changes hourly and tells the robot nothing when it does.

Most writing about this field is organised around what the machines can do, and the doing is genuinely impressive. But the question that decides whether a service robot is in daily use or in a store cupboard is almost never a question about the machine. It is a question about the space, and it has a specific form:

What had to change around it?

Three examples, chosen because the answer is physical and undeniable. A robotic lawnmower works because somebody buried a wire around the entire perimeter of the garden and around every flowerbed inside it; the machine is a cheap consequence of a landscaping job. A hospital courier works because the lifts were fitted with radios that let a machine call them, and because the fire doors were given actuators; without those it stands at the lift lobby waiting for a passer-by. A pavement delivery robot works because a legislature created a statutory category called a personal delivery device and said what it was allowed to do, which is to say that the law changed before the pavement did.91,87

None of these is a footnote to the engineering. Each is the load-bearing part of the deployment, and each is paid for by somebody other than the robot's designer. This article is an attempt to take that seriously and count it. It catalogues thirty-eight functions that service robots actually perform in the field, in the nine families the subject is conventionally divided into, and codes each one on a single scale: what had to be different before the machine could work, from nothing at all up to a change in the law.

The finding that organises everything after it is short. Across the thirty-eight, only three required nothing beyond a change of schedule. All three are search and rescue. Their mean residual — the share of encounters handled by neither the robot nor any accommodation, and therefore falling back on a person — is 0.467, against 0.069 for the other thirty-five. You may have an unmodified space or a working robot. It is difficult to have both.

Alongside the census there is a small computational laboratory: ten deterministic experiments in the Python standard library, run at build time, whose transcript and source are printed in full at the end. Every number quoted in this article comes out of that transcript or out of a cited source, and where a number is a coding judgement rather than a measurement the text says so. Several of the experiments were built to test the thesis rather than illustrate it, and one of them — the search-and-rescue model in the eleventh section — produces a result that cuts against the article's own emphasis.

02A history of removing the scaffolding

The field's history reads better as a history of accommodations withdrawn than as a history of capability added. At every stage the machines worked first inside a heavily prepared space, and progress consisted of taking some of the preparation away.

The prepared room

Grey Walter's Elmer and Elsie, built in Bristol in 1948 and 1949, were the first autonomous mobile robots in the modern sense, and Walter's argument was that complex, apparently purposive behaviour could come from two valves' worth of circuitry.8,9,10 They ran on the floor of a domestic room, which is exactly the setting a robotic vacuum occupies today, and they did no useful work whatever. The accommodation was total: a beacon light, a charging hutch and an observer who picked them up when they got stuck.

Shakey, built at Stanford Research Institute between 1966 and 1972, is remembered as the first robot to reason about its actions.12,11 It is less often remembered that Shakey ran in a suite of rooms built for it, with uniform painted baseboards to give its vidicon a reliable line to track, large blocks of high contrast, and doorways of a convenient width. The reasoning was real. So was the room. A decade later the Stanford Cart crossed a chair-strewn room at roughly one metre every fifteen minutes, and its author's account is frank about what the experiments needed in the way of lighting and patience.13

Meanwhile the commercially successful mobile robots of the same decades did no reasoning at all. Automated guided vehicles had been following buried wires around factories since the 1950s, and they worked, at scale, profitably, because the factory floor had a wire in it. That is the purest form of the trade this article is about: the machine can be made simple in exactly the proportion that the space is made complicated.

Leaving the laboratory

The behaviour-based turn of the mid-1980s cut the dependence on a modelled world rather than on a prepared one.14 The first service robot to run a real duty cycle in a real building was HelpMate, a hospital courier deployed from the late 1980s, which navigated corridors by dead reckoning corrected against ceiling features and — the point — was given an interface to the lifts.16,15 Joseph Engelberger, who had built the first industrial robot company, published the book that named the field in 1989 and spent the rest of his career arguing for exactly this application.2

The museum tour guides of 1997 and 1998 were the first machines to work all day in a crowd. RHINO ran for six days in the Deutsches Museum in Bonn; MINERVA ran for a fortnight in the Smithsonian National Museum of American History.17,18 Both worked, and both needed a map made in advance, a set of hand-entered invisible obstacles for glass cases and stairwells that the sensors could not see, and staff who could rescue them. The probabilistic methods those projects produced are the foundation of everything mobile that followed.44,45,46

The products

The first commercially successful domestic robot, launched in 2002, did not map and did not localise. It covered a room by driving straight until it hit something and then choosing a new direction, and its designer's own account of the development is a study in cost constraints rather than in autonomy.19 The cleaning family had a longer prehistory than that suggests.20 The first commercial robotic milking installation dates from 1992 in the Netherlands, and required the barn around it to be rebuilt for voluntary cow traffic.75,76 Robotic surgery reached general clearance in 2000 on the back of two decades of stereotactic and orthopaedic work, and never claimed autonomy at all.62,63,65

The driving challenges of 2004 to 2007 changed the ambition of the whole field, and they did so on closed courses with surveyed route files.21,23,22 Pavement delivery began in 2014. The three-year underground competition that ended in 2021 was the most serious attempt yet to work in a space nobody had prepared, and its results are discussed in the eleventh section.112,113

The standard reference works of the field record the same split from the other side: the handbook chapters on domestic and field robotics are organised around environments rather than around mechanisms, because the environment is what differs.3,4,5

Put the sequence together and the direction is unmistakable, but so is what has not moved. In 2026 the professional service-robot market is dominated by transport and logistics, at about 102,900 units of some 199,000 sold in the previous year, and those are overwhelmingly machines working indoors in buildings their operators control.6,7 The scaffolding has been removed in the laboratory. In the field it has been rebuilt in a different shape.

03The four things you can change, and the one you cannot

Anything a deployment changes in order to work falls into one of a small number of kinds, and they differ in who pays, how long the change lasts, and how hard it is to undo. Ordering them by that cost gives a six-rung scale, used throughout this article.

The rung recorded for a plate is the highest accommodation without which the mainstream deployment does not work. Lesser accommodations are recorded as ghost cells on the gauge, because most deployments make several. The scale measures what had to be different, not whether the difference was worth making: a deployment at S5 is not worse than one at S2, it is dearer to arrange and harder to reverse. Nor is it a scale of sophistication. Some of the most technically advanced machines in this survey sit at S0 because they work in places that cannot be prepared at all, and they pay for the privilege in the last column of the gauge.

The second axis: who answers

Every fielded service robot keeps a channel open to a person. The channel is the thing that makes the deployment viable, because no machine in this census handles everything it meets. What varies is how far away that person is, and the distance is a property of what the site can promise rather than of how good the robot is.

This is a second axis rather than a refinement of the first, and the human-factors literature has been building taxonomies along it since the beginning: by who is in the loop and at what level, by the ratio of operators to robots, and by whether the human is commanding, supervising or merely monitoring.24,31,32,25 The supervisory-control framing is the one that survives contact with the field, because it puts the question as one about the joint system rather than about the machine.28

A surgeon holding a console is at zero distance and is not supervising at all: the machine is an instrument, and there is no autonomy to fail. A ward orderly is a few metres away and can be summoned in a minute. A remote operator sits in a building somewhere else, sees the robot through a camera, and can take the wheel within seconds but cannot pick anything up. A field engineer is a van journey away and will arrive tomorrow. Those four positions have been studied under the heading of fan-out and span of control since the early 2000s, and the finding that a single operator's capacity is set by the ratio of neglect time to interaction time is the ancestor of the second experiment below.33,34,35

The cover bar, and what it is not

Each plate carries a bar divided into three: the share of encounters the machine would handle in the space as it stood before deployment; the share it handles only because something was changed; and what is left over for a person. The third figure is the one that matters, and it is drawn separately on a logarithmic scale because it ranges over three orders of magnitude across the census.

These three numbers are a coding against a stated rubric, not a measurement, and it is important to be plain about that. Operators do not publish intervention rates in a comparable form, and the few figures that exist are marketing summaries. Where a published or derived number pins a plate, it is given in the plate's note and in the text: the fetch rate at a milking box, the harvest success of fruit-picking rigs, the artefact count in the underground final, the autonomy percentage claimed by a pavement operator. Everywhere else the triple is the author's judgement, calibrated against the pinned cases, and the article's quantitative claims are stated in terms that a moderate recoding would not overturn.

Scope

Where a performance figure is quoted it is worth knowing that the field has standard test methods for navigation and for defined-area coverage, and that almost nobody publishes against them.130,135 The European machinery regulation that applies from January 2027 is the first major instrument to name machinery with self-evolving behaviour as its own conformity-assessment category, which will change what a manufacturer can assert without a notified body.134

Three things are out of scope. First, industrial robots working inside cells, and industrial mobile robots working inside warehouses their operator owns: those are the contrast class, and they are governed by a separate standards family.132,133 Second, military and defence applications, which have a different relationship to consent and to risk than anything here. Third, physical safety engineering as such, which has its own literature and its own standards, currently being rewritten to widen the 2014 personal-care scope into a general service-robot scope.127,128,129,131 Safety appears here only where a safety requirement is itself the accommodation.

04The floor you did not design

Start with the simplest service task there is: cover a floor. Every point of the reachable floor must be visited at least once, by a machine whose knowledge of the floor is whatever it can build while moving over it. The problem has an exact literature going back to the boustrophedon decomposition and its successors, and the theory is settled.42,41,43 What the theory assumes, and what a home does not provide, is that the robot knows where it is.

The first experiment lays out a room of 12 by 8 metres with six pieces of furniture in it — 71.25 square metres of reachable floor at a quarter-metre resolution — and drives three different control regimes over it, measuring metres travelled rather than steps so that the regimes are directly comparable.

The planner with perfect knowledge of its own pose needs 291 metres to reach ninety-nine per cent of the floor, against 285 metres for a hypothetical path that never retraces a step: an overhead of two per cent. The random walk needs 1,437 metres, a factor of 4.94, and the shape of its cost is the interesting part. It reaches ninety per cent of the floor in 612 metres, which is only 2.31 times the planner. The last nine per cent costs it 57.4 per cent of its total distance, against 8.8 per cent for the planner. A machine that covers by chance is cheap up to the point where the customer starts looking at corners.

The third regime is the one that matters for the thesis. It is the same planner, with the same map and the same algorithm, except that once every two hundred and fifty steps it loses its place and has to discard its coverage map. That is not an exotic failure: it is what happens when a robot is picked up and put down, when a door that was open is closed, when the sofa moves, when the light changes. The cost is 7,126 metres, 24.5 times the well-localised planner and nearly five times the random walk. The algorithm did not get worse. The space stopped holding still.

This is the whole argument in one number. The domestic family's accommodation — virtual walls, docking stations, a request to tidy before a run, the manufacturer's advice to keep the furniture where it was when the map was made — is not a set of usability workarounds. It is the purchase of the assumption on which the good algorithm depends. The extensive ethnographic literature on living with these machines finds households reorganising themselves around exactly this, and doing it willingly.55,56,57

The companion plate in this family sits on a different footing from the other three and should be read with more scepticism. Socially assistive robotics has a clear definition and a substantial research programme, and the systematic reviews of effects in elderly care have consistently found the evidence base thin and the study designs weak, while the ethical literature has been sceptical about substitution for human contact since before the products existed.61,60,58

The pool cleaner is the closest thing in the whole census to a machine that needed no accommodation, and it earns that position by working in a space that was already built to a specification: a sealed basin of fixed geometry, empty of people and of anything that can be harmed. It is worth remembering when a domestic robot looks easy. The easy case is the one where somebody else already did the preparing, decades earlier, for their own reasons.

05The residual, and who answers it

No machine in this census handles everything it meets. The design question is therefore not whether there will be a call for help but how often, and who is close enough to answer. Those two quantities multiply into the only number that decides whether a fleet is a business.

How many robots one person can hold

The right model is older than the field. A fleet of robots calling for help is a set of machines failing at some rate and being repaired by a limited number of attendants, and a robot that is stuck raises no further calls while it waits — which is exactly the finite-source repair queue.36 The second experiment solves it exactly for a three-minute handling time, and reports the largest fleet whose availability still reaches ninety-five per cent of its own no-waiting ceiling, so that the irreducible cost of being helped is separated from the cost of queueing for help.

Two results come out of it, and both are non-linear in the direction that surprises operators. Pooling: eight supervisors hold 12.9 times what one supervisor holds, not eight times, because a shared queue absorbs coincidence. Autonomy: cutting the call rate by a factor of sixteen, from one call per fifteen minutes to one per four hours, multiplies the fleet one person can hold by thirty-six. Reliability compounds against staffing faster than it improves.

The second panel shows why fleets fail suddenly rather than gradually. At one call per robot-hour, a single supervisor's knee is at thirteen robots. Adding half as many again moves the mean wait from 3.11 minutes to 8.33, a factor of 2.68; at thirty robots the wait is 27.8 minutes and the supervisor is busy 99.2 per cent of the time. Nothing about the machines changed. The operator ran out.

What a headline autonomy figure costs per event

Operators publish autonomy as a percentage of errands. One pavement operator describes its robots as ninety-nine per cent autonomous over more than ten million deliveries, with a fleet above three thousand machines in eight countries, some twenty-two million autonomous kilometres and roughly two hundred million road crossings.94,95 Those crossing and delivery figures together imply about twenty decisive events per errand, which makes the percentage convertible into an engineering requirement.

An errand is a chain of independent decisive events, so the errand-level success is the product. Ninety-nine per cent of errands finishing unaided, over twenty events, requires a per-event failure rate of 5.02 in ten thousand. Over forty events it requires 2.51 in ten thousand: holding the errand-level claim fixed, doubling the route length halves the permitted per-event failure exactly. The arithmetic is unforgiving in a way that a single headline number conceals, and it is the reason campus routes were the first thing to work.

Chaining the two experiments prices the difference. At twenty events an errand and three errands per robot-hour, a per-event failure of one in a hundred supports twenty-five robots per supervisor; one in a thousand supports 342; one in ten thousand supports 3,509. A tenfold improvement in per-event reliability buys roughly a tenfold larger fleet per person. That is the whole economic case for autonomy research, stated in staff.

It is worth being explicit about what the residual is made of, because the composition explains why it shrinks so slowly. What falls back on a person is disproportionately the part of the task that nobody can write down: recognising that a situation is unusual, deciding what a half-blocked corridor means, knowing which of two plausible readings of a scene is the one this building would produce. That is the old observation that we know more than we can tell, and its mechanical corollary that the things easiest for people are hardest for machines.37,38 It is also why the labour-economics literature on automation keeps finding reallocation rather than replacement: the tasks that resist automation are complements to the ones that do not.39,40

The queueing bound is an upper bound and nothing more: real operations are limited by charging, by loading, by weather and by the fact that a stuck robot on a pavement is a public nuisance long before it is a queueing problem. But the direction holds, and it explains a pattern visible across the census. Where the site could not be modified, operators bought the residual back by putting a person nearer: remote operations centres for pavement and road delivery, a therapist in the room for a rehabilitation exoskeleton, a crew at the manhole for a sewer crawler. The oldest observation in the automation literature applies without amendment — the more reliable the automation, the worse the job left for the person who must catch what it misses.27,29,30,26

06The most governed interior

A hospital is the most procedurally controlled space in this survey and the most willing to change itself. Both facts follow from the same cause: harm is expensive, so the institution already runs on written procedure, and a written procedure is a thing that can be amended to include a machine. That makes healthcare the family where accommodation is easiest to buy and most visible when bought.

The case with no autonomy at all

Robot-assisted surgery is the largest service-robotics business in the world and contains no autonomy whatever. The machine reproduces a surgeon's hand motions inside the patient with tremor filtering and motion scaling; the surgeon is the controller, continuously, and the residual is zero by construction because there is no unsupervised behaviour to fail.64,65 More than twenty million patients have now been operated on with one manufacturer's systems; 2025 saw about 3,153,000 procedures on an installed base that reached 11,106 systems at the end of that year.66,67

What had to change was the theatre and the people in it: a console surgeon trained to a specific certification, a scrubbed assistant at the patient side, a team drilled in emergency undocking, and a room re-laid-out around a cart. This is the clearest instance in the census of an accommodation made entirely of human procedure, and it is worth noticing that the most commercially successful machine here bought its success by giving up on autonomy completely.

What lamp power cannot buy

Ultraviolet terminal-room disinfection is the opposite case: a fully autonomous cycle in an empty room, and a hard geometric limit. The fifth experiment puts a point source in a 6 by 5 metre room with four pieces of furniture, samples 390 surface points on the walls and on the furniture's own faces, and computes fluence under inverse-square attenuation with hard shadowing.

From the central stand, 51.0 per cent of surface points are in line of sight. Raising the lamp power from the reference to double it lifts coverage from 35.6 per cent to 51.0. Raising it sixty-four-fold lifts it to 51.0. Dwelling sixty-four times as long lifts it to 51.0. Power and time are the same purchase, and they stop at the same wall, because a shadowed point receives nothing and no multiple of nothing is anything.

Moving the machine helps, and costs time: three stands sharing the room time reach 46.2 per cent at the reference total and 68.7 per cent at eight times it, against a three-stand ceiling of 69.0. Clearing the furniture and staying at one stand reaches 61.8 per cent in the reference time and 100 per cent in twice it. The only intervention that removes the ceiling is the one performed on the room, and it is the only one that costs a person's time rather than the machine's — which is precisely why the largest randomised trial of enhanced terminal disinfection added the ultraviolet arm to standard manual cleaning rather than substituting for it.70,71

Two families of evidence

Hospital logistics is the oldest service-robot application in a working building and the best documented as an operations problem rather than a robotics one: the early case studies measured transport tasks, corridor congestion and lift contention, and found the building to be the system under study.68,69,15

Rehabilitation exoskeletons are the family where the accommodation is most clearly a licence. The devices are regulated medical devices, cleared for use with a trained therapist present, and the clinical evidence is correspondingly careful: early feasibility work established that ambulation was possible for people with motor-complete injury, scoping reviews found heterogeneous designs and small samples, and the Cochrane synthesis of electromechanical gait training after stroke reports benefit concentrated in particular patient groups rather than across the board.72,73,74

The healthcare family has the lowest mean as-found share in the census, 0.140. Almost nothing here works in the space untouched. It also has the second-lowest mean residual, 0.030. That combination is the article's thesis in a single family: the hospital is a place that buys its low residual by changing a great deal, and can do so because it is already in the business of writing down what people must do.

07Changing the animal, the field and the crop

Agriculture is where the accommodation is most obviously biological. A field can be reshaped, a planting geometry can be chosen, and — uniquely in this survey — the living things in the space can be selected and trained. The family has the third-highest mean rung in the census at 3.60, and a mean residual of 0.106, behind only search and rescue and education.

The box that waits

Voluntary robotic milking is the oldest continuously successful autonomous service robot in commercial use, dating from 1992, and its constraint is not mechanical.75,76 The machine identifies a cow, cleans and attaches to four teats, milks and releases, all unsupervised. It cannot make a cow come. The sixth experiment simulates a single box with a seven-minute box time, twenty-two hours of daily availability and cows seeking the box at a mean interval consistent with 2.8 milkings a day, with a two-hour patience limit past which a cow gives up and must be fetched by a person.

The arithmetic capacity is 67.3 cows. The simulation shows why advisers recommend around fifty-five.77 At 55 cows the herd achieves 2.647 milkings a day with 0.030 fetches per cow-day — about one and a half cows collected by hand each day, consistent with the under-five-per-cent guidance in the field literature. At 70 cows the achieved frequency has fallen only 5.1 per cent, to 2.513, but the fetch load has risen 1.90 times, to about four cows a day. The headline production figure barely moves while the human labour attached to it nearly doubles, which is exactly the kind of degradation a throughput metric hides.

Note where the accommodation sits. The barn was rebuilt for free cow traffic. Concentrate feeding was moved into the box as an inducement, so that the animal has a reason to attend. Cows are selected on udder conformation and on temperament, and trained. The machine did not learn to fetch cows; the herd was changed until fewer needed fetching, and a person absorbs what is left.

The pass that does not go away

Selective harvesting is the family's hard case and has been for thirty years. The canonical review of harvesting robots for high-value crops reports a mean localisation success of 85 per cent, a mean detachment success of 75 per cent, a resulting harvest success of 66 per cent and a mean cycle time of 33 seconds per fruit, and no commercial system has displaced hand picking at scale since.78,79

The seventh experiment prices what that means for labour, and the result was not the one expected. Hand picking divides into two terms that scale differently: traversing the block and looking at every tree, which is proportional to the fruit present, and reaching and detaching, which is proportional to the fruit actually picked. For a block of 100,000 fruit at 1.4 and 2.2 seconds respectively, the hand-only baseline is 100 picker-hours: 38.9 hours of walking and looking, 61.1 hours of picking.

A robot removes picking work from the second pass and none at all from the first. At the published 66 per cent field success the gleaning pass still costs 59.7 per cent of the hand-only block. At a hypothetical 99 per cent it costs 39.5 per cent. Perfecting the machine from the state of the art to near-perfection — a fifty per cent improvement in harvest success — buys 20.2 percentage points of hand labour, and cannot buy more than 38.9, because somebody still has to walk every row.

The implication runs against the usual framing. The large saving is not available from a better robot at all; it is available from cancelling the second pass, which is worth 59.7 per cent of the block immediately, and which costs the 34 per cent of the crop the machine did not take. That is a decision about what the farm is willing to leave on the tree — a change to the task, rung S3 — and it is an agronomic and commercial decision rather than a robotics one. For completeness: at 33 seconds a fruit and twenty hours a day, matching one ten-person hand crew working eight hours requires 36.7 machines.

Weeding is the family's cleanest accommodation and the one that goes least remarked. Machines that discriminate crop from weed are viable because the crop is planted in rows at a spacing chosen to make the discrimination tractable, at a growth stage chosen for the same reason.81,82 The agronomy moved first. The economics of the whole family turn on the same point: the published analyses of autonomous arable equipment find the gains in machine size and field logistics rather than in the autonomy itself.83,84,80

08The pavement belongs to everybody

Delivery has the highest mean rung in the census, 4.75 of a possible 5, and the reason is jurisdictional rather than technical. Every other family in this survey works in a space that somebody owns and can therefore modify. A footway cannot be modified by the operator, and the people on it are, in the words of the international technical report that established the vocabulary for this class of machine, bystanders who are inattentive, uninvolved, unprotected and untrained relative to what the device is doing.90 That report also makes the historical claim squarely: this is the first time machines designed to work without a proximate human attendant have been put among such bystanders at scale.

The accommodation is a statute

So the first accommodation had to be legal. Virginia created a statutory category for personal delivery devices in 2017 and widened it in 2020, raising the permitted device weight from fifty pounds to five hundred; other states followed with their own weight, speed and right-of-way limits, and a robot that is a pedestrian in one state is an unclassified object in the next.91,87 Trackers disagree on how many states now have such a law — published counts through 2026 range from the mid-twenties to over thirty depending on whether bills in committee and general-purpose vehicle-code amendments are included — and the disagreement is itself informative: there is no single thing being counted, because each legislature drew a different object.

The airborne case is the same pattern at a larger scale. Routine flight beyond the operator's visual line of sight in the United States has run on individual waivers and air-carrier certificates for years. The replacement framework was published as a proposed rule on 7 August 2025, the comment period closed on 6 October 2025, the final rule was filed for regulatory review on 10 July 2026, and it remains unpublished as this is written.92,93 An entire application family is waiting on a document.

The one modification an operator can buy

Indoors, the operator can modify the building, and the fourth experiment prices the single most common modification: giving the lift an interface the robot can call. Without it, a robot at a lift lobby must wait for a passer-by who happens to be going its way and is willing to help; the model puts that at half of passers-by going the right way and eighty per cent of those helping.

In a busy building at sixty people an hour, the interface multiplies one robot's throughput by 1.55. At two people an hour it multiplies it by 26.35. The retrofit is worth least when the building is full and most when it is empty — which is to say, most in exactly the hours a building most wants a machine rather than a person doing the fetching. An operator who pilots at noon and prices the retrofit on what they saw will get the answer badly wrong.

The freezing robot

The technical residual on a pavement has a name. A planner that treats people as obstacles with uncertain future positions will, as the crowd thickens, find that every action has some probability of collision, and will conclude that the safest thing available is to stop. It then stays stopped, because stopping does not reduce the uncertainty. The failure was identified and named in 2010, and the resolution is not better prediction but modelling the interaction: people cooperate, and a planner that assumes they will not is wrong about the physics of a crowd.50,51

That insight is the foundation of a substantial literature on socially aware navigation, and the surveys of it are candid that the field lacks agreed benchmarks and that most systems are evaluated in the very conditions they were tuned in.52,53 The one long-running exception is instructive: a robot operated for kilometres through a busy pedestrianised city centre, and its published account is mostly about the map, the weather and the failure cases.54,49 Meanwhile the transport-research literature has been measuring the other half of the question — what these machines do to kerb space, to energy use, to pedestrians with mobility impairments, and to the people who have to share a footway with them.85,86,88,89

Delivery also has the lowest mean residual in the census at 0.020, which looks like a triumph and is better read as a consequence. When a space cannot be modified and the law has been changed at enormous cost to permit the machine at all, the operator is left with only one lever for the residual: put a person on a screen within seconds of every robot. The low residual and the remote operations centre are the same fact seen from two sides.

09The space that has to look untouched

Hospitality and commercial cleaning share a constraint that no other family has. The customer is standing in the space, and the accommodation must not be visible, because the accommodation is a defect in the product. A hotel corridor with a magnetic guide strip down the middle of the carpet has already failed.

Hospitality

The industry survey puts hospitality second among professional application classes at more than 42,000 units in 2024, and it is the only large class that contracted, by eleven per cent.6,7 The contraction is the interesting number. A family whose machines are visible to paying customers gets a verdict quickly, and the service-marketing literature has been unusually clear-eyed that the frontline robot is judged as part of the service encounter rather than as a tool.99,98,97,96

What the successful deployments have in common is that the machine runs the corridor and a person runs the last metre. A room-service robot travels to the door and telephones the room; the guest takes the item out of the bin. A restaurant runner carries plates down the aisle; a server lifts them onto the table. Placing a plate in front of a seated diner is not attempted at scale by anything in this census, and the reason is not manipulation research. It is that a machine reaching across a stranger's arm is a different kind of event from a machine driving past them.

Cleaning

Professional cleaning was the fastest-growing class in the same survey, up about a third to more than 25,000 units, and it grew for a reason visible on the gauge: its accommodation is a schedule.6 Commercial floors are large, owned by the operator, mapped once, and — crucially — empty at three in the morning. The machine is not solving the hard problem of working among people. It is being run at the hours when there are none.

That is rung S1 or S2 work, which is cheap, and it explains why this family scaled while hospitality did not. It also explains the residual that remains: edges, corners, stairs, spills that appear after the route was taught, and everything above floor level. The deployed machine does the large flat middle of the job and a person does the perimeter, which is roughly the division of labour the first survey of cleaning robots predicted a quarter of a century ago.20

Facade and solar-panel cleaning is the family's clearest economic lesson. The machine is inexpensive and the building modification is not; on new-build work the facade grid is specified with the machine in mind, and on a retrofit the anchor points are the entire cost. When people say a robot is not yet economic, they usually mean this: the machine is economic and the space is not.

10The space nobody wants to enter

Inspection is the family with the best case for robots and the least public attention, and the two facts are related: the spaces are ones people should not be in. It has the second-highest mean rung at 3.80, and its accommodations are older than the machines.

A space designed around a tool that did not exist yet

In-line pipeline inspection is the oldest large-scale service robot in commercial use and the most thoroughly accommodated. A tool travels inside a pressurised line carried by the product flow, logging wall thickness and geometry. It works because the pipeline was built to let it work: launcher and receiver barrels, minimum bend radii, full-bore valves, consistent internal diameter. Lines that were not built that way are called unpiggable and constitute a standing industry problem, which is the cleanest possible demonstration of what this article is about.101,100

Nobody in the 1960s laid out those specifications for the benefit of robots. They did it for cleaning pigs and for batching, and the inspection tool inherited a space already shaped to admit a travelling machine. Half a century of accommodation had been paid for in advance.

Permits, tethers and legs

Confined-space entry shows the accommodation as a rule rather than a fitting. Entry into a vessel is governed by a permit regime designed to stop people dying, and the robot has been fitted into that regime rather than exempted from it. Sewer crawlers show it as a crew: a team at the surface launches, recovers and codes the survey, and the tether is power, communications and recovery line at once.102,103

The legged patrol robots that arrived in industrial plant in the last decade are worth a moment because they invert the usual direction. Plants are built for people on foot, with stairs, grating, kerbs and door handles at human dimensions. The legged form is not a performance choice; it is an accommodation to a building that was never going to be re-laid-out for wheels.104,105 The accommodation that remains is the round itself: a route taught once, fiducials and docks fitted, and the readings restricted to ones a machine can take. What is left over is every reading that requires an inspector to decide where to look.

11The space that cannot be prepared

Search and rescue is the exception that shows the rule, and it is the only family in this census where the article's argument produces a verdict worth acting on rather than a description.

Three of the thirty-eight plates sit at S0 or S1 — nothing changed, or only a schedule. All three are here. Their mean residual is 0.467 against 0.069 for the other thirty-five: a factor of 6.8. A collapsed building cannot be surveyed, cannot be lit, cannot be marked with fiducials, cannot be made to hold still, and contains people whose time is running out. Every other family in this survey bought its low residual with a modification that this family cannot make.

The founding survey of the field catalogues thirty-four documented robot deployments to disasters up to 2014 and is unusually candid that the machines are used far less often, and far less successfully, than the research literature implies.108 The World Trade Center response in 2001 remains the most carefully documented, and its human-factors analysis found that the operators, not the robots, were the limiting element.107,106 Fukushima, Mirandola, Amatrice and the German task-force deployments since have all reported the same shape of result: the machine gets in, the picture is valuable, and the tether, the dust, the communications and the operator's workload dominate everything.109,110,111

The most serious attempt to change this was a three-year, eighty-two-million-dollar competition in underground environments, whose final event ran a purpose-built course combining tunnel, urban and cave sections. The winning team located twenty-three of forty artefacts in a sixty-minute run, with exactly one human permitted to communicate with the robots.112,113,114 That is the state of the art, achieved by the best-resourced teams in the world, on a course that had been surveyed in advance so that scoring was possible. Fifty-seven and a half per cent, on a prepared course, with a human supervisor.

What the clock does to the argument

The eighth experiment was built to test whether better robots are the right thing to want here, and the answer it gives is only half comfortable. It models a 4,000 square metre search area under random-search detection, with a sweep width of 1.2 metres and an advance of 0.1 metres per second, against an exponentially decaying survival curve of twenty-four-hour half-life, with the robots on site six hours after the collapse. The survival model is illustrative and is not fitted to any incident; the empirical literature on time-to-rescue is much more complicated than any exponential.118,115,116,117

With a six-hour start, the best conceivable search — infinitely fast, finding everyone the instant it begins — recovers 0.841 of an initially living population, because that is what survival at six hours is. The baseline search already achieves 0.664, which is 79.0 per cent of that ceiling. The entire speed axis is worth 17.7 percentage points. Doubling the coverage rate is worth 11.9 per cent; arriving two hours earlier is worth 5.9 per cent, and is equivalent to a 1.36-fold coverage rate.

That is the half that cuts against the article. Within the window, improving the machine is worth rather more than improving the logistics, and about twice as much as the two hours of arrival time that dominate most after-action discussion. The uncomfortable inference is that this is the family where robot capability research has the highest marginal value, precisely because the space cannot be accommodated and there is nothing else to buy.

The other half restores the balance. The ceiling itself is set entirely by the start time and cannot be raised by any amount of speed: at six hours it is 0.841, at zero hours it is 1.0. And sensitivity is not free. A sensor that doubles the effective sweep width but generates false alarms costing a quarter-hour of verification each stops paying above four alarms an hour — an exact consequence of the ratio rather than of any physics, but a real constraint on what a detection improvement is worth in a place where every alarm must be checked by a person on rubble.

Notice what the gauges show about who answers. Two of these four plates put the person at arm's length: the robot is an instrument in an expert's hands, tethered, with the operator watching a screen two metres away. That is not a failure to achieve autonomy. In a space that offers no promises at all, a very short channel to a person is the only accommodation available, and the family has correctly chosen it.

12Where the residual is the product

Educational robots invert every relationship in this article, and the inversion is worth stating precisely. In the other eight families the residual is a cost: the share of encounters that falls back on a person, to be minimised by engineering or bought off by accommodation. In education the residual is the curriculum. A machine that never failed would teach nothing.

The lineage runs back to the tethered floor turtle of the late 1960s and the pedagogy built around it, whose central claim was that children learn by debugging a model they made themselves, and that the model's failures are the informative part.119,120 The ninth experiment takes that claim literally and measures the failure.

A differential-drive turtle drives 0.30 metres per command with a heading noise of 0.9 degrees and a distance noise of one per cent, executing a closed polygon; 4,000 trials at each length. Mean position error grows as the number of commands to the power 1.448. That exponent is the lesson. An unbiased random walk in position would give 0.5. Integrating a random walk in heading gives 1.5, because a heading error is not an error in where you are but an error in where every subsequent step points. The measured 1.448 is that, and it is why dead reckoning fails in a way that feels sudden.47,48

The pedagogical consequence is a specific number. At sixteen commands the turtle misses its starting mark by more than its own body width in 7.1 per cent of runs. At thirty-two commands it does so in 64.4 per cent — about 9.6 metres of driving, or one long classroom exercise. The teacher who plans a twelve-command activity gets a machine that appears to work; the teacher who plans a forty-command activity gets a machine that visibly does not, and a lesson about accumulated error that no amount of explanation delivers as well. The choice of sequence length is a choice about which lesson the class receives.

The accommodation in this family is correspondingly odd. The floor is cleared and a gridded mat is laid down, not to help the robot but to make its error legible. The teacher is part of the system, and the curriculum around a construction kit is most of the product. The best-known kit of all was discontinued at the end of 2022 after twenty-four years, which says a good deal about distribution and very little about pedagogy.126

One plate here deserves its place for a reason outside education. Open research platforms work in laboratories deliberately arranged to be legible — flat floors, controlled lighting, fiducial markers, known obstacles — and that arrangement is an accommodation as real as a buried perimeter wire. It is the reason a stack that works in the lab meets the world and produces the entire census above. The standard review of social tutor robots finds effects comparable to human tutoring on restricted tasks, attributed largely to physical presence, and is equally clear that child speech recognition remains a hard limit on anything open-ended.121,122,124,125,123

13What the census says

Thirty-eight plates, nine families, six rungs and four intervention distances. The tenth experiment computes the summary statistics, and three of them bear on the thesis.

The mean rung is 3.13: the median fielded service robot works because the task was narrowed or the space was modified. Fourteen of the thirty-eight sit at S3, the task changed, which is the single largest cell in the census and by some distance the cheapest accommodation available to a vendor, since it requires the agreement of nobody outside the contract. Eight sit at S4 and six at S5. Only three sit at S0 or S1.

Thirteen of the twenty-four rung-by-distance cells are occupied, and the empty ones are informative. Both plates at S0 put the operator's hands on the machine, because a space that promises nothing cannot promise that the robot will still be where it was when you last looked at it. No plate sits at S4 or S5 with a hands-on operator, because if a person is holding the machine there was never any need to rebuild the space or change the law to let it work alone.

The test the thesis could have failed

If the article's claim were wrong — if service robotics were really a story about machine capability — then the residual would track how much the machine manages in an untouched space, and the accommodation would be incidental. It does not.

The correlation between a plate's as-found share and its log residual is +0.180, an r-squared of 0.032: essentially nothing, and with the wrong sign for the capability story, since plates that do more unaided have slightly larger residuals rather than smaller ones. The correlation between the accommodation share and the log residual is −0.598, an r-squared of 0.358. What the robot manages in the space as it stands predicts almost nothing about what is left over. What was changed predicts a third of it.

The coding separates 91.7 per cent of the 703 distinct pairs on rung and distance together, against 91.0 per cent on subject matter alone, which is barely an improvement and should be reported as such. Adding the residual band lifts it to 96.9 per cent. The honest reading is that rung is not orthogonal to family: 44.2 per cent of the variance in rung is explained by family alone.

That last figure is not a weakness in the coding, it is the second finding. The accommodation rung is largely a property of where the work happens rather than of what the machine does. Delivery averages 4.75 because the pavement belongs to everybody and the only available lever was the statute book. Search and rescue averages 1.50 because a collapsed building will not hold still for anybody. Between them sit seven families whose spaces are owned by somebody who could be persuaded to change them, and those are the families with working products.

What this does not establish

The cover triples are a coding, not a measurement, and although the pinned cases anchor them the quantitative claims should be read as claims about the shape of the relationship rather than about its magnitude. The families are the conventional ones, which are marketing categories as much as engineering ones; a different partition would move the family-variance figure. The census is of functions that are actually fielded, so it is survivorship-biased by construction — the deployments that failed because nobody would make the accommodation are invisible here, and there is reason to think they are numerous. The residual is treated as a single scalar, which hides the difference between a robot that stops safely and one that fails in a way somebody has to notice.

The laboratory has narrower limits still. Every experiment is a lumped model with parameters chosen to be plausible rather than fitted: the survival curve in the eighth is illustrative and explicitly not tied to any incident, the coverage model in the first ignores sensor footprint and turning cost, the queue in the second assumes exponential everything, and the ultraviolet model is a point source with hard shadows and no reflection, which will understate real coverage in a bright-walled room. What each experiment establishes is a direction and a rough magnitude, not a design figure.

The question to ask of a proposal

The practical value of all this is a question to put to any service-robotics proposal, and it is not the one usually asked. Not what can it do, and not how autonomous is it, but: what is this deployment asking somebody to change, who is that somebody, and what happens on the day they stop agreeing?

The wire in the lawn has to be repaired. The lift radio has to survive the next lift refurbishment, undertaken by a contractor who has never heard of the robot. The trained nurse who loads the cart has to still be on the ward next year. The statute has to survive the first serious injury. The cow has to keep coming. Every accommodation in this census is a standing agreement with a person or an institution, and the thing that fails in the field is almost never the machine.

Which is the reason the three plates at the bottom of the ladder are the ones that repay attention. In a collapsed building there is nobody to make an agreement with, no schedule to shift, no space to modify and no statute that helps. What is left is the machine, alone, in a place that promises it nothing — and a residual of forty-seven per cent is an honest measure of how much of this field's apparent success has been borrowed from somewhere else.

# E1  COVERAGE OF A ROOM THAT WAS NOT LAID OUT FOR THE ROBOT
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Plan-view occupancy grid, 0.25 m cells.  Three control regimes:
#   RANDOM      no map, no localisation; drive straight, re-draw heading on
#               contact (the 2002-generation domestic vacuum).
#   SYSTEMATIC  perfect localisation and a persistent coverage map; travel to
#               the nearest uncovered free cell by breadth-first search.
#   LOSSY       the same planner, but the coverage map is discarded whenever
#               re-localisation fails (hazard q per step).
# Measured: steps to 90 / 95 / 99 per cent of reachable floor.

def e1():
    head("E1", "COVERAGE WHEN THE FLOOR PLAN IS NOT YOURS")
    CELL = 0.25
    W, H = 48, 32                       # 12.0 m x 8.0 m at 0.25 m
    blocked = [[False] * W for _ in range(H)]
    for y in range(H):
        blocked[y][0] = blocked[y][W - 1] = True
    for x in range(W):
        blocked[0][x] = blocked[H - 1][x] = True
    rects = [(6, 4, 14, 8),    # sofa
             (24, 6, 34, 10),  # dining table
             (8, 18, 12, 24),  # armchair
             (30, 18, 44, 26), # bed
             (18, 14, 22, 16), # side table
             (38, 4, 44, 8)]   # sideboard
    for (x0, y0, x1, y1) in rects:
        for y in range(y0, y1):
            for x in range(x0, x1):
                blocked[y][x] = True

    start = (2, 2)
    seen = {start}
    dq = collections.deque([start])
    while dq:
        cx, cy = dq.popleft()
        for dx, dy in ((1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1)):
            nx, ny = cx + dx, cy + dy
            if not blocked[ny][nx] and (nx, ny) not in seen:
                seen.add((nx, ny))
                dq.append((nx, ny))
    reach = sorted(seen)
    nreach = len(reach)
    reachset = set(reach)
    p("room %s x %s m, cell %s m, %d furniture footprints"
      % (f(W * CELL, 1), f(H * CELL, 1), f(CELL, 2), len(rects)))
    p("reachable floor %d cells = %s m2; cleaning head one cell wide"
      % (nreach, f(nreach * CELL * CELL, 2)))
    p("distance is metres of travel, so the regimes are directly comparable")

    CAP_M = 60000.0
    MARKS = (0.90, 0.95, 0.99)

    def run_random(seed):
        """continuous drive-straight-until-contact, re-draw heading (2002 vacuum)"""
        rnd = random.Random(seed)
        x, y = (start[0] + 0.5) * CELL, (start[1] + 0.5) * CELL
        th = 0.7
        dstep = 0.05
        covered = {start}
        out = {}
        dist = 0.0
        while dist < CAP_M:
            nx, ny = x + dstep * math.cos(th), y + dstep * math.sin(th)
            cx, cy = int(nx / CELL), int(ny / CELL)
            if cx < 0 or cy < 0 or cx >= W or cy >= H or blocked[cy][cx]:
                th = rnd.random() * 2 * math.pi
                continue
            x, y = nx, ny
            dist += dstep
            if (cx, cy) in reachset and (cx, cy) not in covered:
                covered.add((cx, cy))
                frac = len(covered) / nreach
                for m in MARKS:
                    if m not in out and frac >= m:
                        out[m] = dist
                if len(out) == len(MARKS):
                    break
        return out, len(covered) / nreach

    def bfs_path(src, targets):
        if src in targets:
            return [src]
        prev = {src: None}
        dq = collections.deque([src])
        while dq:
            cur = dq.popleft()
            for dx, dy in ((1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1)):
                nb = (cur[0] + dx, cur[1] + dy)
                if blocked[nb[1]][nb[0]] or nb in prev:
                    continue
                prev[nb] = cur
                if nb in targets:
                    path = [nb]
                    while prev[path[-1]] is not None:
                        path.append(prev[path[-1]])
                    path.reverse()
                    return path
                dq.append(nb)
        return None

    def run_planned(seed, q_loss):
        rnd = random.Random(seed)
        pos = start
        truth = {start}
        belief = {start}
        out = {}
        dist = 0.0
        guard = 0
        while dist < CAP_M and guard < 200000:
            guard += 1
            targets = reachset - belief
            if not targets:
                if len(truth) >= nreach:
                    break
                belief = {pos}
                continue
            path = bfs_path(pos, targets)
            if path is None:
                belief = {pos}
                continue
            for cell in path[1:]:
                dist += CELL
                pos = cell
                belief.add(cell)
                if cell not in truth:
                    truth.add(cell)
                    frac = len(truth) / nreach
                    for m in MARKS:
                        if m not in out and frac >= m:
                            out[m] = dist
                if q_loss > 0.0 and rnd.random() < q_loss:
                    belief = {pos}
                    break
                if len(out) == len(MARKS) or dist >= CAP_M:
                    break
            if len(out) == len(MARKS):
                break
        return out, len(truth) / nreach

    r_rand, cov_rand = run_random(SEED)

# E2  HOW MANY ROBOTS CAN ONE PERSON HOLD
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Finite-source queue (machine-repairman, M/M/c//N).  A robot that is stuck
# generates no further calls, which is exactly the physical situation.
#   birth  (a robot gets stuck)   lambda_n = (N-n) * lam
#   death  (an operator finishes) mu_n     = min(n,c) * mu

def repairman(N, lam, mu, c):
    r = lam / mu
    terms = [1.0]
    for n in range(1, N + 1):
        t = terms[-1] * (N - n + 1) * r / min(n, c)
        terms.append(t)
    tot = sum(terms)
    pn = [t / tot for t in terms]
    En = sum(n * pn[n] for n in range(N + 1))
    Lq = sum(max(n - c, 0) * pn[n] for n in range(N + 1))
    avail = 1.0 - En / N
    lam_eff = lam * (N - En)
    Wq = Lq / lam_eff if lam_eff > 0 else float("inf")
    busy = sum(min(n, c) * pn[n] for n in range(N + 1)) / c
    return {"avail": avail, "Wq_h": Wq, "Lq": Lq, "util": busy}

def seg_blocked(ax, ay, bx, by, rects, eps=1e-9):
    """does segment a-b cross any axis-aligned rectangle interior?"""
    for (x0, y0, x1, y1) in rects:
        # slab clipping (Liang-Barsky)
        t0, t1 = 0.0, 1.0
        dx, dy = bx - ax, by - ay
        ok = True
        for (pp, qq) in ((-dx, ax - x0), (dx, x1 - ax),
                         (-dy, ay - y0), (dy, y1 - ay)):
            if abs(pp) < eps:
                if qq < 0:
                    ok = False
                    break
            else:
                t = qq / pp
                if pp < 0:
                    if t > t1:
                        ok = False
                        break
                    if t > t0:
                        t0 = t
                else:
                    if t < t0:
                        ok = False
                        break
                    if t < t1:
                        t1 = t
        if ok and t1 - t0 > 1e-6:
            return True
    return False


def e5():
    head("E5", "ULTRAVIOLET DOSE IN A ROOM THAT WAS NOT EMPTIED")
    RW, RH = 6.0, 5.0
    furn = [(0.6, 0.6, 2.6, 1.5),    # bed
            (3.4, 0.5, 4.6, 1.1),    # over-bed table
            (0.5, 3.2, 1.1, 3.8),    # chair
            (4.6, 2.6, 5.6, 4.4)]    # cabinet
    pts = []
    stepm = 0.10
    n = int(RW / stepm)
    for i in range(n):
        x = (i + 0.5) * stepm
        pts.append((x, 0.02)); pts.append((x, RH - 0.02))
    n = int(RH / stepm)
    for i in range(n):
        y = (i + 0.5) * stepm
        pts.append((0.02, y)); pts.append((RW - 0.02, y))
    for (x0, y0, x1, y1) in furn:
        nx = max(1, int((x1 - x0) / stepm))
        ny = max(1, int((y1 - y0) / stepm))
        for i in range(nx):
            x = x0 + (i + 0.5) * (x1 - x0) / nx
            pts.append((x, y0 - 0.02)); pts.append((x, y1 + 0.02))
        for i in range(ny):
            y = y0 + (i + 0.5) * (y1 - y0) / ny
            pts.append((x0 - 0.02, y)); pts.append((x1 + 0.02, y))
    pts = sorted(set((round(a, 4), round(b, 4)) for (a, b) in pts))
    stands = [(3.0, 2.5), (1.6, 4.2), (4.9, 1.6)]
    P0 = 100.0
    TREF = 900.0
    DREF = 3.0
    DREQ = P0 / (4.0 * math.pi * DREF * DREF) * TREF

    def irr(stand, pt, power, obst):
        d2 = (stand[0] - pt[0]) ** 2 + (stand[1] - pt[1]) ** 2
        if d2 < 0.01:
            d2 = 0.01
        if seg_blocked(stand[0], stand[1], pt[0], pt[1], obst):
            return 0.0
        return power / (4.0 * math.pi * d2)

    p("room %s x %s m, %d furniture footprints, %d target surface points"
      % (f(RW, 1), f(RH, 1), len(furn), len(pts)))
    p("point source, inverse square, hard shadow; dose = irradiance x dwell")
    p("required dose = what one nominal lamp delivers at %s m in %s s"
      % (f(DREF, 1), f(TREF, 0)))
    p("")
    los = []
    for s_ in stands:
        v = sum(1 for q in pts if not seg_blocked(s_[0], s_[1], q[0], q[1], furn))
        los.append(v / len(pts))
        p("line of sight from stand (%s, %s): %s%% of points"
          % (f(s_[0], 1), f(s_[1], 1), f(100.0 * v / len(pts), 1)))
    any_los = sum(1 for q in pts
                  if any(not seg_blocked(s_[0], s_[1], q[0], q[1], furn) for s_ in stands))
    ceil1 = los[0]
    ceil3 = any_los / len(pts)
    p("line of sight from at least one of the three stands: %s%%" % f(100.0 * ceil3, 1))
    p("never in line of sight from any stand: %s%% of points"
      % f(100.0 * (1 - ceil3), 1))
    p("")
    p("those ceilings are hard.  No lamp power and no dwell time moves them,")
    p("because a shadowed point receives nothing at all.")
    p("")

    def frac(cfg, obst):
        got = 0
        for q in pts:
            d = sum(irr(s_, q, pw, obst) * t for (s_, pw, t) in cfg)
            if d >= DREQ:
                got += 1
        return got / len(pts)

    p("A. buying with POWER, one stand, %s s dwell" % f(TREF, 0))
    p("%-30s %12s %14s" % ("lamp power", "dose met", "share of ceiling"))
    powres = []
    for m in (1, 1.2, 1.5, 2, 4, 16, 64):
        v = frac([(stands[0], m * P0, TREF)], furn)
        powres.append([m, v, v / ceil1, DREF * math.sqrt(m)])
        p("%-30s %12s %14s" % (f(m, 1) + "x nominal  (reach " + f(DREF * math.sqrt(m), 2)
                               + " m)", f(100 * v, 1) + "%", f(100 * v / ceil1, 1) + "%"))
    p("power saturates at the single-stand line-of-sight ceiling of %s%%"
      % f(100 * ceil1, 1))
    p("")
    p("B. buying with TIME, one stand, nominal power")
    p("%-30s %12s %14s" % ("total dwell", "dose met", "share of ceiling"))
    timeres = []
    for m in (1, 1.5, 2, 4, 16, 64):
        v = frac([(stands[0], P0, m * TREF)], furn)
        timeres.append([m, v, v / ceil1])
        p("%-30s %12s %14s" % (f(m * TREF, 0) + " s", f(100 * v, 1) + "%",
                               f(100 * v / ceil1, 1) + "%"))
    p("time and power are interchangeable in this model, and both stop at the")
    p("same wall")
    p("")
    p("C. buying with POSITION: three stands, total room time split between them")
    p("%-30s %12s %14s" % ("total room time", "dose met", "share of 3-stand ceiling"))
    posres = []
    for m in (1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 16):
        v = frac([(s_, P0, m * TREF / 3.0) for s_ in stands], furn)
        posres.append([m, v, v / ceil3])
        p("%-30s %12s %14s" % (f(m * TREF, 0) + " s", f(100 * v, 1) + "%",
                               f(100 * v / ceil3, 1) + "%"))
    p("")
    p("D. buying by MOVING THE FURNITURE: one stand, empty room")
    emptyres = []
    for m in (1, 2, 4):
        v = frac([(stands[0], P0, m * TREF)], [])
        emptyres.append([m, v])
        p("   %-27s %12s" % (f(m * TREF, 0) + " s", f(100 * v, 1) + "%"))
    p("")
    # crossing points
    one_at_1x = powres[0][1]
    three_at_3x = [r for r in posres if r[0] == 3][0][1]
    p("a single stand at nominal power and %s s reaches %s%% of surfaces"
      % (f(TREF, 0), f(100 * one_at_1x, 1)))
    p("sixty-four times the power raises that to %s%% and no further"
      % f(100 * [r for r in powres if r[0] == 64][0][1], 1))
    p("three stands at eight times the room time reach %s%%"
      % f(100 * [r for r in posres if r[0] == 8][0][1], 1))
    p("clearing the furniture and staying at one stand, same %s s, reaches %s%%"
      % (f(TREF, 0), f(100 * emptyres[0][1], 1)))
    p("clearing the furniture and doubling the time reaches %s%%"
      % f(100 * emptyres[1][1], 1))
    p("")
    p("the only intervention that removes the ceiling is the one performed on")
    p("the room, and it is the only one that costs a person's time rather than")
    p("the machine's")
    J["e5"] = {"npts": len(pts), "los": los, "ceil1": ceil1, "ceil3": ceil3,
               "never": 1 - ceil3, "TREF": TREF,
               "power": powres, "time": timeres, "pos": posres, "empty": emptyres,
               "one_at_1x": one_at_1x, "three_at_3x": three_at_3x,
               "three_at_8x": [r for r in posres if r[0] == 8][0][1],
               "empty_at_2x": emptyres[1][1],
               "empty_at_1x": emptyres[0][1],
               "pow64": [r for r in powres if r[0] == 64][0][1], "DREF": DREF}

# E6  THE MILKING BOX: A CEILING SET BY THE ANIMAL, NOT THE MACHINE
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def e6():
    head("E6", "VOLUNTARY ATTENDANCE AT A SINGLE MILKING BOX")
    box_min = 7.0
    avail_h = 22.0
    target_freq = 2.8
    patience_h = 2.0
    p("box time %s min, robot available %s h/day, cows seek the box at a mean"
      % (f(box_min, 1), f(avail_h, 1)))
    p("interval of %s h (a target of %s milkings/cow/day); a cow that has queued"
      % (f(24.0 / target_freq, 2), f(target_freq, 1)))
    p("more than %s h gives up and must be fetched by a person" % f(patience_h, 1))
    theo = (60.0 / box_min) * avail_h / target_freq
    p("theoretical capacity = (60/%s) x %s / %s = %s cows"
      % (f(box_min, 0), f(avail_h, 0), f(target_freq, 1), f(theo, 1)))
    p("")

    def sim(ncows, days=40, warm=10, seed=SEED):
        rnd = random.Random(seed + ncows)
        svc = box_min / 60.0
        want = [rnd.random() * (24.0 / target_freq) for _ in range(ncows)]
        ev = [(want[i], i) for i in range(ncows)]
        heapq.heapify(ev)
        queue = collections.deque()
        free_at = 0.0
        milkings = 0
        fetches = 0
        waits = []
        T = days * 24.0
        busy = 0.0
        while ev or queue:
            if ev and ev[0][0] > T and not queue:
                break
            # advance: next arrival or next service completion
            if queue:
                cow, tarr = queue[0]
                startt = max(free_at, tarr)
                if not ev or startt <= ev[0][0]:
                    queue.popleft()
                    # is the box shut? 2h wash window each 11h -> availability
                    day_frac = startt - 24.0 * math.floor(startt / 24.0)
                    shut = (avail_h < 24.0) and (day_frac >= avail_h)
                    if shut:
                        startt = 24.0 * math.floor(startt / 24.0) + 24.0
                    w = startt - tarr
                    if w > patience_h:
                        if startt > warm * 24.0:
                            fetches += 1
                        heapq.heappush(ev, (startt + 24.0 / target_freq, cow))
                        continue
                    free_at = startt + svc
                    if startt > warm * 24.0:
                        milkings += 1
                        waits.append(w)
                        busy += svc
                    heapq.heappush(ev, (free_at + rnd.expovariate(target_freq / 24.0), cow))
                    continue
            if not ev:
                break
            t, cow = heapq.heappop(ev)
            if t > T:
                continue
            queue.append((cow, t))
        span = (days - warm) * 1.0
        freq = milkings / (ncows * span)
        return {"freq": freq, "fetch_per_cow_day": fetches / (ncows * span),
                "mean_wait_min": (sum(waits) / len(waits) * 60) if waits else 0.0,
                "util": busy / (span * avail_h)}

    REPS = 6
    p("each row is the mean of %d independent 40-day runs (10-day warm-up)" % REPS)
    p("")
    p("%-8s %12s %14s %14s %12s" % ("cows", "milk/cow/d", "fetches/cow/d",
                                    "mean wait min", "box busy"))
    out = []
    for n in (40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 63, 65, 70, 75, 80):
        acc = [0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
        for rep in range(REPS):
            d = sim(n, seed=SEED + 977 * rep)
            acc[0] += d["freq"]; acc[1] += d["fetch_per_cow_day"]
            acc[2] += d["mean_wait_min"]; acc[3] += d["util"]
        acc = [a / REPS for a in acc]
        out.append([n] + acc)
        p("%-8d %12s %14s %14s %12s"
          % (n, f(acc[0], 3), f(acc[1], 4), f(acc[2], 1), f(acc[3], 3)))
    n55 = [r for r in out if r[0] == 55][0]
    n70 = [r for r in out if r[0] == 70][0]
    p("")
    p("from 55 to 70 cows: frequency %s -> %s (%s%%), fetch load %s -> %s per cow-day (%sx)"
      % (f(n55[1], 3), f(n70[1], 3), f(100 * (n70[1] / n55[1] - 1), 1),
         f(n55[2], 4), f(n70[2], 4),
         f(n70[2] / n55[2], 2) if n55[2] > 0 else "inf"))
    herd = 70
    p("at %d cows one person fetches %s cows a day; at 55, %s"
      % (herd, f(n70[2] * herd, 1), f(n55[2] * 55, 1)))
    J["e6"] = {"theo": theo, "rows": out, "box_min": box_min, "avail_h": avail_h,
               "target_freq": target_freq, "patience_h": patience_h,
               "n55": n55, "n70": n70}

# E8  SEARCH AND RESCUE: THE CLOCK BEATS THE SENSOR
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def e8():
    head("E8", "SEARCHING A COLLAPSE AGAINST A FALLING SURVIVAL CURVE")
    A = 4000.0        # m2 of searchable rubble surface
    Wsweep = 1.2      # m effective sweep width
    v = 0.10          # m/s advance
    cov_rate = Wsweep * v * 3600.0 / A   # fraction of area swept per hour
    T0 = 6.0          # h before robots are on site, baseline
    HL = 24.0         # h survival half-life under rubble
    HORIZON = 120.0
    p("area %s m2, sweep width %s m, advance %s m/s -> coverage %s /h"
      % (f(A, 0), f(Wsweep, 1), f(v, 2), f(cov_rate, 4)))
    p("random-search detection: POD(t) = 1 - exp(-coverage)")
    p("survival modelled as exponential decay, half-life %s h (illustrative,"
      % f(HL, 0))
    p("not fitted to any incident); start of robot search T0 = %s h" % f(T0, 1))
    p("")

    def expected(T0, mult, fp_cost=0.0, fp_rate=0.0):
        """fraction of an initially-live victim population found alive."""
        lam_s = math.log(2.0) / HL
        eff = cov_rate * mult / (1.0 + fp_rate * fp_cost)
        dt = 0.01
        t = T0
        tot = 0.0
        while t < HORIZON:
            dP = eff * math.exp(-eff * (t - T0)) * dt
            tot += dP * math.exp(-lam_s * t)
            t += dt
        return tot

    base = expected(T0, 1.0)
    p("%-44s %12s %10s" % ("regime", "found alive", "vs base"))
    variants = [("baseline", T0, 1.0),
                ("twice the coverage rate", T0, 2.0),
                ("four times the coverage rate", T0, 4.0),
                ("on site 2 h earlier", T0 - 2.0, 1.0),
                ("on site 4 h earlier", T0 - 4.0, 1.0),
                ("on site at once (T0 = 0)", 0.0, 1.0)]
    res = []
    for name, t0, m in variants:
        v_ = expected(t0, m)
        res.append([name, v_, v_ / base])
        p("%-44s %12s %10s" % (name, f(v_, 4), f(v_ / base, 3) + "x"))
    p("")
    # equivalence: how much extra coverage rate buys the same as 2h earlier
    target = expected(T0 - 2.0, 1.0)
    lo, hi = 1.0, 40.0
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if expected(T0, mid) < target:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    eq = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
    p("arriving 2 h earlier is worth a %sx coverage rate at the baseline start"
      % f(eq, 2))
    p("")
    ceil6 = 2.0 ** (-T0 / HL)
    p("the ceiling on searching FASTER is fixed by the start time alone: with an")
    p("infinitely fast search beginning at T0 = %s h, the best attainable is"
      % f(T0, 1))
    p("survival at T0 = %s.  The baseline already realises %s%% of that ceiling,"
      % (f(ceil6, 4), f(100 * base / ceil6, 1)))
    p("so the whole speed axis has %s points of headroom, while moving T0 to zero"
      % f(100 * (ceil6 - base), 1))
    p("raises the ceiling itself to 1.0")
    # sensitivity with false positives
    p("")
    p("a more sensitive sensor doubles sweep width but raises false alarms;")
    p("each alarm costs verification time")
    p("%-24s %14s %12s" % ("false alarms per hour", "found alive", "vs base"))
    fps = []
    for fr in (0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0):
        v_ = expected(T0, 2.0, fp_cost=0.25, fp_rate=fr)
        fps.append([fr, v_, v_ / base])
        p("%-24s %14s %12s" % (f(fr, 1), f(v_, 4), f(v_ / base, 3) + "x"))
    lo, hi = 0.0, 200.0
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if expected(T0, 2.0, 0.25, mid) > base:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    fp_be = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
    p("")
    p("doubling sweep width stops paying above %s false alarms per hour"
      % f(fp_be, 2))
    J["e8"] = {"ceil6": 2.0 ** (-T0 / HL), "base_over_ceil": base / (2.0 ** (-T0 / HL)),
               "cov_rate": cov_rate, "base": base, "variants": res,
               "equiv_mult_for_2h": eq, "fps": fps, "fp_breakeven": fp_be,
               "T0": T0, "HL": HL, "A": A}

# E9  THE CLASSROOM TURTLE: ERROR AS THE CURRICULUM
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def e9():
    head("E9", "DEAD RECKONING IN A FLOOR TURTLE")
    L = 0.30          # m per forward command
    sig_head = 0.9    # deg, per command
    sig_dist = 0.010  # fractional, per command
    TRIALS = 4000
    body = 0.12       # m
    p("turtle drives %s m per command; heading noise sigma %s deg, distance"
      % (f(L, 2), f(sig_head, 1)))
    p("noise sigma %s%% per command; %d trials; body width %s m"
      % (f(100 * sig_dist, 1), TRIALS, f(body, 2)))
    p("")

    def walk(nsteps, turn_every, rnd):
        x = y = 0.0
        th = 0.0
        turn = 360.0 / max(1, (nsteps // max(1, turn_every)))
        for i in range(nsteps):
            th += rnd.gauss(0.0, sig_head)
            d = L * (1.0 + rnd.gauss(0.0, sig_dist))
            x += d * math.cos(math.radians(th))
            y += d * math.sin(math.radians(th))
            if turn_every and (i + 1) % turn_every == 0:
                th += turn
        return math.hypot(x, y)

    p("%-14s %14s %14s %14s" % ("commands n", "mean err m", "err / n*L", "P(err>body)"))
    rows = []
    for n in (4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256):
        rnd = random.Random(SEED + n)
        errs = [walk(n, n // 4 if n >= 4 else 0, rnd) for _ in range(TRIALS)]
        m = sum(errs) / len(errs)
        pb = sum(1 for e in errs if e > body) / len(errs)
        rows.append([n, m, m / (n * L), pb])
