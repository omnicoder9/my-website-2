Limitations and Extensions of Game Theory

Bounded rationality, behavioural and social preferences, risk and ambiguity, robust and stochastic models, experiments, computational complexity, equilibrium selection, and the ethics of incentive design

Technical article · References in the Vancouver style

Abstract. Classical non-cooperative game theory rests on strong idealisations. Players are assumed to reason without limit, to care only about their own material payoffs, to evaluate lotteries by expected utility with known probabilities, to share a correct model of the game, and to coordinate on one equilibrium that can be computed. Each assumption has been challenged by evidence from psychology, the laboratory and the field, and by results from computer science. This article surveys those challenges and the extensions developed to meet them. It covers bounded rationality, behavioural game theory, social preferences, risk and ambiguity, robust and distributionally robust games, stochastic games with uncertain dynamics, experimental and empirical methods, the computational complexity of equilibrium, equilibrium selection, and the welfare and ethical side of incentive design. For each topic it gives the history, the formal ideas, worked examples with diagrams, and the main open problems.

1. The classical idealisations

The theory founded by von Neumann and Morgenstern and completed by Nash describes players who maximise expected utility, and it predicts that play will be an equilibrium in which no player can gain by deviating alone [1,2]. Textbook treatments make the background assumptions explicit. The game, including every player's payoffs, is common knowledge. Every player is rational, knows that the others are rational, and so on without limit. Players form beliefs about one another's strategies that turn out to be correct [3,4].

These assumptions make the theory sharp, but each one fails in identifiable ways. Real decision makers have limited time, memory and computing power [5]. They care about fairness and about the intentions of others, not only about money [6,7]. They treat unknown probabilities differently from known ones [8]. They often do not know the game they are playing, and analysts rarely know it precisely either [9,10]. Even when the model is right, an equilibrium can be intractable to compute [11], and there may be many equilibria with no principled way to choose among them [12]. Finally, the rules of a game are often designed by someone, and badly designed incentives can lower welfare or crowd out the motives they were meant to harness [13,14].

The response of the field has not been to abandon game theory but to extend it. Figure 1 maps each classical idealisation to the family of extensions discussed in this article.

2. Historical development

Doubts about the rational-choice core are older than game theory itself. In 1738 Daniel Bernoulli resolved the St Petersburg paradox by proposing that people value money by a concave utility function, which is the origin of the formal theory of risk aversion [16]. In 1921 Frank Knight separated measurable risk from unmeasurable uncertainty, a distinction that became central to the later theory of ambiguity [17]. Von Neumann and Morgenstern's axioms of expected utility in 1944 gave game theory its standard model of choice under risk [1], and Savage extended it to subjective probability in 1954 [18].

Challenges followed quickly. In January 1950 Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at the RAND Corporation ran a repeated game experiment, the game later named the Prisoner's Dilemma, and found that the two subjects cooperated far more often than equilibrium predicts [19]. Maurice Allais showed in 1953 that many people violate the independence axiom of expected utility [20]. In 1955 Herbert Simon proposed replacing global maximisation with "a kind of rational behavior that is compatible with the access to information and the computational capacities" that organisms actually have [5]. Daniel Ellsberg's 1961 urn experiments showed that people avoid bets with unknown odds in ways no single probability can explain [8]. Vernon Smith's market experiments, published in 1962, established the experimental method in economics [21].

Behavioural evidence accumulated through the 1970s and 1980s. Kahneman and Tversky's work on heuristics and biases and their 1979 prospect theory gave an alternative descriptive model of choice under risk [22,23]. Güth, Schmittberger and Schwarze's 1982 ultimatum game experiment showed that people reject unfair offers at a cost to themselves [24]. The 1990s produced formal extensions: models of fairness and reciprocity [6,7,25], quantal response equilibrium [26], models of iterated reasoning [27,28], and learning models of equilibrium selection [29,30]. Computer science later supplied two new kinds of limit: proofs that equilibria are hard to compute [11,31] and bounds on how much welfare selfish behaviour can lose [13,32]. Operations research supplied robust and distributionally robust formulations of games under model uncertainty [9,33].

Several of these programmes received the Nobel memorial prize in economic sciences: Simon in 1978 "for his pioneering research into the decision-making process within economic organizations" [34], Kahneman and Smith in 2002 for integrating psychological research into economics and for establishing laboratory experiments as a tool of empirical analysis [35], and Richard Thaler in 2017 "for his contributions to behavioural economics" [36].

3. Bounded rationality and limited computational resources

3.1 Simon's critique

Simon argued that the "global rationality" of economic man requires knowledge and computational capacity that real decision makers lack. He proposed that they "satisfice": they search until they find an alternative that meets an aspiration level, instead of optimising over all alternatives [5,46]. Later research on "fast and frugal" heuristics showed that simple rules that ignore most information can perform well in suitable environments [47,48]. In games the problem is sharper than in single-person decisions, because a player must reason about other players' reasoning as well as about the environment [49].

3.2 Approximate optimisation: ε-equilibrium

The simplest relaxation lets players ignore small gains. A strategy profile is an ε-equilibrium if no player can gain more than ε by deviating. Radner showed that in a finitely repeated oligopoly, collusive behaviour that unravels under exact equilibrium can be sustained as an ε-equilibrium when the horizon is long [50]. The same idea underlies the analysis of approximate equilibria in computer science (Section 10).

3.3 Strategies as machines

A second approach measures the complexity of strategies. In repeated games a strategy can be implemented by a finite automaton, and its complexity can be measured by the number of states. Rubinstein studied players who care about payoffs but also prefer simpler machines [51]. Neyman showed that if the players of a finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma are restricted to automata with fewer states than the number of rounds, cooperative outcomes can be equilibria, even though backward induction rules them out when strategies are unrestricted [52]. Papadimitriou and Yannakakis obtained a similar result when the bound on automaton size grows only polynomially with the length of the game [53]. More recently, Halpern and Pass built a general framework in which players choose algorithms and pay for computation, and showed that it can explain behaviour such as cooperation in the finitely repeated dilemma [54].

Limited attention offers a related view. In Sims's model of rational inattention, decision makers have a finite capacity to process information, measured in Shannon entropy, so they respond to signals only partly [55].

3.4 Backward induction in long games

Long games of perfect information expose the limits of iterated reasoning most clearly. In Rosenthal's centipede game two players alternately decide whether to take a growing pot or pass it on. Backward induction implies that the first player takes immediately [56]. In McKelvey and Palfrey's experiments, few players took at the first opportunity, and most games ended in the middle [57]. Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts and Wilson showed that a small amount of incomplete information, such as a small probability that the opponent is a committed cooperator, can make cooperation rational for most of a finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma [38]. Such results show that the logic of backward induction depends delicately on common knowledge of rationality.

4. Behavioural game theory and psychological biases

Behavioural game theory uses experimental evidence to build models of how people actually play, keeping the formal apparatus of game theory but replacing its strongest assumptions with ones that fit data [15]. Its models fall into three families: models of limited strategic thinking, models of noisy best response, and models of learning.

4.1 Level-k and cognitive hierarchy

Keynes compared professional investment to a newspaper beauty contest in which competitors pick the faces they expect others to pick, so that one reaches "the third degree where we devote our intelligences to anticipating what average opinion expects the average opinion to be" [58]. Nagel turned this into an experiment, the p-beauty contest. Each player picks a number between 0 and 100, and the winner is the one closest to p times the average. With p = 2/3, iterated elimination of dominated strategies leaves only 0, which is the unique Nash equilibrium. First-round choices were far from 0, and they clustered near 33 and 22, the answers produced by one and two steps of reasoning from a naive average of 50 [27,59].

Level-k models formalise this. A level-0 player chooses at random or follows a salient rule, and a level-k player best responds to level k−1 [28,60]. The cognitive hierarchy model of Camerer, Ho and Chong assumes instead that each level best responds to a Poisson-distributed mixture of all lower levels. Its average depth of reasoning is estimated at about one and a half steps across many games [44]. These models predict systematic deviations from equilibrium in one-shot games, and they converge toward equilibrium predictions as players gain experience or as the game becomes simple [61].

4.2 Quantal response equilibrium

McKelvey and Palfrey's quantal response equilibrium (QRE) keeps equilibrium consistency but replaces exact best responses with noisy ones: better strategies are chosen more often, but not always [26]. In the logit version, player i chooses action a with probability

σi(a) = exp(λ·Ui(a, σ−i)) / Σb exp(λ·Ui(b, σ−i)),

where λ ≥ 0 measures precision. At λ = 0 play is uniformly random, and as λ → ∞ the logit equilibria converge to a Nash equilibrium [26]. The idea goes back to Harsanyi's justification of mixed equilibria as the limit of play when payoffs are slightly perturbed [62].

QRE explains a well-known anomaly in mixed-strategy games. In a Nash equilibrium of a 2×2 game with a unique mixed equilibrium, each player's mixture is set to make the other indifferent, so a player's own payoffs do not affect that player's own choice probabilities. In the laboratory they do. Goeree and Holt ran asymmetric matching pennies with the row player's payoff in one cell raised from 80 to 320 cents, and the share of row players choosing that row rose from about half to 96 per cent [63]. QRE also explains Basu's traveller's dilemma. Two travellers each claim between 80 and 200; both receive the lower claim, with a reward added for the lower claimant and a penalty for the higher. The unique Nash equilibrium is for both to claim the minimum whatever the size of the reward or penalty. In experiments, claims were high when the penalty was small and fell toward the minimum only when it was large, as QRE predicts [64,65].

4.3 Learning and psychological biases

A third family describes how play changes with experience. In reinforcement learning, actions that earned more in the past are chosen more often [66]. In belief learning, players best respond to the empirical frequency of opponents' past play [67]. Camerer and Ho's experience-weighted attraction model nests both and fits experimental data better than either [68].

Biases documented in individual decision making also enter strategic settings. Framing changes choices between equivalent options [69]. Overconfidence leads to excess entry: in Camerer and Lovallo's experiments, subjects entered markets too often when payoffs depended on their own skill, and the result was average losses [70]. Loss aversion and probability weighting from prospect theory are discussed in Section 6 [23,71].

5. Social preferences: fairness, reciprocity and trust

5.1 The evidence

Three simple games, each with an obvious selfish prediction, have become the standard evidence on social preferences.

  • Ultimatum game. A proposer offers a split of a sum, and a responder accepts or rejects. Rejection leaves both with nothing. If players care only about money, the responder accepts any positive offer and the proposer offers the minimum. Güth and colleagues found that many proposers offered half and that low offers were rejected [24]. Across later studies, offers below about 20 per cent are rejected roughly half the time [15,72]. A meta-analysis of 37 studies found an average offer of about 40 per cent and an average rejection rate of about 16 per cent [73].
  • Dictator game. The responder has no veto, so generous offers cannot be explained by fear of rejection. Offers fall, but they do not disappear [74]. A meta-study of more than 600 treatments found that dictators give away about 28 per cent on average [75].
  • Trust game. An investor sends part of an endowment to a trustee, the amount is tripled, and the trustee decides how much to return. Berg, Dickhaut and McCabe found that most investors sent money and that many trustees returned it [42]. A meta-analysis of 162 replications found that investors send about half their endowment and trustees return about 37 per cent of what they hold [76].

Public goods games show the dynamics of cooperation. Contributions typically start at 40 to 60 per cent of the endowment and decline with repetition [77]. Fehr and Gächter showed that when players can pay to punish low contributors, many do so even in the last round, when punishment cannot pay off, and contributions rise and stay high [78]. Cross-cultural studies in 15 small-scale societies found that no society behaved as the selfish model predicts, and that offers varied with how much daily economic life depended on cooperation and markets [79]. Fehr and Fischbacher's review concluded that strong reciprocity, a willingness to reward and punish at personal cost, is common among humans [80].

5.2 Distributional models

Distributional models add the payoffs of others to a player's utility. In Fehr and Schmidt's model of inequity aversion, with n players and material payoffs x,

Ui(x) = xi − αi/(n−1) · Σj≠i max(xj − xi, 0) − βi/(n−1) · Σj≠i max(xi − xj, 0),

where αi measures envy, βi measures guilt, and the authors assume βi ≤ αi and 0 ≤ βi < 1 [6]. In the ultimatum game, a responder offered a share s < ½ of a unit pie gets utility s − α(1 − 2s) from accepting and 0 from rejecting, so the responder rejects any offer below α/(1 + 2α). The same model, applied to a population that mixes selfish and fair-minded types, explains why competitive market games produce very unequal outcomes while the threat of punishment sustains cooperation in public goods games [6]. Bolton and Ockenfels's ERC model, in which utility depends on one's own payoff and one's relative share, makes similar predictions [25]. Charness and Rabin found that many subjects also care about total surplus and about the worst-off player [81]. Andreoni's "warm glow" model adds the private satisfaction of giving itself [82].

5.3 Intentions and reciprocity

Distributional models ignore why an outcome came about. Yet people respond differently to the same unequal split depending on whether the proposer chose it freely. Psychological game theory, introduced by Geanakoplos, Pearce and Stacchetti, lets payoffs depend on beliefs as well as actions, which makes it possible to model emotions such as surprise, guilt and gratitude [83]. Rabin used this framework to define a fairness equilibrium in which players are kind to those they believe intend to be kind to them and unkind to those they believe intend harm [7]. Dufwenberg and Kirchsteiger extended reciprocity to sequential games [84]. These models explain why rejection rates in ultimatum games fall when the unfair split is chosen by a computer or forced on the proposer [15].

Interpretation. Social-preference models change the payoffs, not the solution concept: once utilities are redefined, players are still assumed to play an equilibrium. This makes the models portable but also flexible enough to fit almost any data, so the literature emphasises out-of-sample prediction across games with one set of parameters [6,81].

6. Risk aversion and ambiguity

6.1 Risk

Under expected utility, risk attitudes are captured by the curvature of the utility function. A risk-averse player prefers the expected value of a lottery to the lottery itself, and the difference between the expected value and the certainty equivalent is the risk premium. Arrow and Pratt defined the coefficient of absolute risk aversion, −u″(w)/u′(w), as a measure of local risk aversion [85,86]. Holt and Laury's lottery-choice experiments found that most subjects are risk averse even at low stakes, and that risk aversion increases when stakes are scaled up [87]. Rabin's calibration theorem shows a limit of the expected-utility account: modest risk aversion over small stakes implies absurd risk aversion over large ones, which suggests that small-stakes risk aversion has another source, such as loss aversion [88].

In games, risk attitudes matter because mixed strategies are lotteries. Payoffs in a game are von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, so a change in risk attitude changes the game itself [1,3]. Goeree, Holt and Palfrey found that risk aversion helps explain deviations from Nash predictions in generalised matching pennies games [89]. Prospect theory replaces utility of wealth with a value function over gains and losses that is steeper for losses, and it replaces probabilities with decision weights that overweight small probabilities [23,71].

6.2 Ambiguity

Knight's distinction between risk and uncertainty became testable with Ellsberg's experiments [8,17]. In the two-urn version, urn A contains 50 red and 50 black balls, while urn B contains 100 red and black balls in an unknown proportion. Many people prefer to bet on red from urn A rather than red from urn B, and also prefer black from A to black from B. No single probability for urn B can explain both preferences. The behaviour is called ambiguity aversion [8,90].

Several decision theories accommodate ambiguity. Gilboa and Schmeidler's maxmin expected utility evaluates each act by its minimum expected utility over a set of priors [39]. Schmeidler's Choquet expected utility uses non-additive probabilities [91]. The smooth model of Klibanoff, Marinacci and Mukerji separates the perception of ambiguity from the attitude toward it [92].

These theories have game-theoretic counterparts. Dow and Werlang defined a Nash equilibrium under Knightian uncertainty, with beliefs represented by non-additive probabilities, and showed that it can break down the backward-induction argument in finite games such as the repeated Prisoner's Dilemma [93]. Eichberger and Kelsey developed equilibrium for players with non-additive beliefs more generally and showed that ambiguity can produce equilibria that differ from Nash equilibria, with players acting as if pessimistic about their opponents' choices [94]. The maxmin approach to ambiguity about others' strategies or payoffs leads directly to the robust models of the next section.

7. Robust game theory under model uncertainty

7.1 The problem

Harsanyi's theory of games of incomplete information handles uncertainty about payoffs by assuming a common prior over types that every player knows [95]. Wilson criticised the dependence of mechanism-design results on such common knowledge assumptions, and argued that useful theory should rely on them less, a position now called the Wilson doctrine [96]. The concern is practical. Rubinstein's electronic mail game shows that equilibrium predictions can change discontinuously when common knowledge is replaced by "almost common knowledge" [97], and Kajii and Morris characterised which equilibria are robust to small amounts of incomplete information [98].

7.2 Robust-optimisation equilibrium

Aghassi and Bertsimas proposed a distribution-free alternative based on robust optimisation [9,99]. Each player knows only that payoffs lie in an uncertainty set and chooses a strategy that maximises the worst-case expected payoff over that set, given the others' strategies. A robust-optimisation equilibrium is a profile in which each strategy is such a worst-case best response:

σi ∈ argmaxτi minui ∈ 𝒰i ui(τi, σ−i) for every player i.

They proved that such equilibria exist in finite games with bounded uncertainty sets, and showed how to compute them for polyhedral sets using formulations from robust optimisation [9]. When the uncertainty sets shrink to single points, the definition reduces to Nash equilibrium.

7.3 Robust mechanism design and prediction

Bergemann and Morris recast the Wilson doctrine for mechanism design. They asked when a social choice function can be implemented for all beliefs that agents might hold, and showed that in many environments this is equivalent to ex post incentive compatibility [10]. In a later paper they defined Bayes correlated equilibrium, the set of outcomes consistent with equilibrium play under some information structure, which gives predictions that are robust to what players might know [100]. Carroll showed that when a principal knows only some of the actions available to an agent and maximises the worst case, linear contracts are optimal, which offers an explanation for the simplicity of real contracts [101].

8. Distributionally robust and stochastic games

8.1 A spectrum of uncertainty models

Robust optimisation assumes no distributional information, while Bayesian analysis assumes the distribution is known exactly. Distributionally robust optimisation (DRO) sits between them. The decision maker considers an ambiguity set of distributions, such as those that match given moments or lie within a Wasserstein distance of the empirical distribution, and optimises against the worst distribution in that set [102–104]. This is formally the maxmin expected utility of Gilboa and Schmeidler [39], applied with sets built from data.

In a distributionally robust game each player maximises the worst-case expected payoff over an ambiguity set of distributions of the random parameters. Liu, Xu, Yang and Zhang defined distributionally robust Nash equilibrium and the corresponding Stackelberg model for continuous games, and established existence and computational approaches for ambiguity sets of practical interest [33]. The approach generalises earlier stochastic formulations. In chance-constrained games, introduced by Charnes, Kirby and Raike, players require their payoffs to exceed a target with given probability [105]. In stochastic Nash equilibrium problems, players maximise expected payoffs when the distribution is known but can only be sampled, and sample average approximation gives consistent estimates of equilibria [106].

8.2 Stochastic games with uncertain dynamics

Shapley's stochastic games let the state of the game change over time according to transition probabilities that depend on the players' actions. Shapley proved that two-player zero-sum discounted stochastic games have a value and stationary optimal strategies [107]. The theory is surveyed in [108,109]. Even the simplest versions are computationally subtle: Condon showed that deciding the value of a simple stochastic game is in NP ∩ coNP, and no polynomial-time algorithm is known [110].

Standard stochastic games assume the transition probabilities are known. In practice they are estimated, and errors can compound over time. Robust Markov decision processes address this for one decision maker by letting nature choose transition probabilities from an uncertainty set at each step [111,112]. Kardeş, Ordóñez and Hall extended the idea to games. In a discounted robust stochastic game, each player maximises worst-case discounted payoff over uncertain payoffs and transition probabilities. They proved that equilibria in stationary strategies exist for finite games with bounded uncertainty sets, gave mathematical programming methods to compute them, and applied the model to queueing control [113].

These models connect to multi-agent reinforcement learning, where agents learn equilibria of stochastic games from sampled experience. The distributionally robust formulations give one way to account for estimation error in the learned model [33,104].

9. Experimental and empirical game theory

9.1 The laboratory

Laboratory experiments allow game theory to be tested under controlled conditions. Smith's theory of induced value set out the method: subjects are paid in money according to the payoffs of the game, so that the experimenter controls their incentives [114]. The handbook edited by Kagel and Roth surveys the results of the first decades [115]. Laboratory findings divide roughly into two groups. Predictions from equilibrium are often good in competitive markets, in zero-sum games and after experience, while they fail systematically in one-shot games, in games that need many steps of reasoning, and in games where social preferences matter [15,21,63].

Zero-sum games are a useful test because their equilibrium is unique and the minimax theorem guarantees it can be computed. O'Neill's laboratory test found aggregate play close to the minimax prediction [116]. In the field, Walker and Wooders analysed serves in Wimbledon finals and found that win rates were equal across serve directions, as minimax requires, although servers switched direction more often than independent randomisation implies [117]. Chiappori, Levitt and Groseclose found penalty kicks in professional football consistent with mixed-strategy equilibrium [118].

9.2 External validity and replication

Two concerns qualify laboratory results. Levitt and List argued that lab measures of social preferences may overstate their strength in the field because subjects know they are observed, stakes are small and the choice set is artificial [119]. Replicability has also been checked directly. A coordinated effort to replicate 18 laboratory experiments published in two leading economics journals found a significant effect in the original direction for 11 of them, about 61 per cent [120].

9.3 Structural estimation and empirical game theory

Empirical industrial organisation uses game-theoretic models to estimate unobserved primitives from observed equilibrium behaviour. Bresnahan and Reiss inferred how competition changes with the number of firms from the populations needed to support one, two or more firms in isolated markets [121]. Guerre, Perrigne and Vuong showed how to recover bidders' value distribution from first-price auction bids by inverting the equilibrium bidding function [122]. Multiple equilibria are a central difficulty: when a model predicts several outcomes, it does not give a unique distribution of observables. Tamer showed that such models may only partially identify the parameters, which gives bounds rather than point estimates [123]. This links empirical work to the selection problem of Section 11.

In computer science, empirical game-theoretic analysis builds a game model from simulation. The analyst selects a set of candidate strategies, estimates the payoff matrix among them by repeated simulation, and analyses the resulting game, adding strategies as needed [124]. The method is used to study complex games that cannot be written down in closed form, such as trading agents and large poker variants. Libratus, the system that defeated four top professionals at heads-up no-limit Texas hold'em in 2017, computed approximate equilibria of an abstracted game and refined them during play [45].

10. Computational complexity of equilibrium computation

10.1 Easy cases

Some equilibria are easy to compute. Von Neumann's minimax theorem implies that a two-player zero-sum game can be solved by linear programming, in time polynomial in the size of the matrix [1,125]. Correlated equilibria of any normal-form game are also solutions of a linear program and can be computed in polynomial time [40,125]. Papadimitriou and Roughgarden extended this to many compactly represented multiplayer games [126]. Simple adaptive procedures such as regret matching converge to the set of correlated equilibria [127].

10.2 Nash equilibrium and PPAD

Nash's theorem guarantees an equilibrium in every finite game, so the problem of finding one is a total search problem, and NP-completeness is not the right notion of hardness for it. Papadimitriou introduced the class PPAD for problems whose solutions are guaranteed by a parity argument on a directed graph [128]. The Lemke–Howson algorithm for two-player games is a path-following method of exactly this kind [129], and Savani and von Stengel constructed games on which it takes exponentially many steps [130]. Daskalakis, Goldberg and Papadimitriou proved that computing a Nash equilibrium of a general normal-form game is PPAD-complete [11], and Chen, Deng and Teng showed that this remains true for two-player games [31]. Unless PPAD collapses into polynomial time, no efficient algorithm finds Nash equilibria of general games.

Harder problems lie nearby. Deciding whether a game has a Nash equilibrium with a given property, such as a payoff above a threshold or a particular strategy in its support, or whether the equilibrium is unique, is NP-hard [40,131]. In games with three or more players, equilibria can require irrational probabilities, and computing them exactly is complete for the class FIXP [132]. Pure Nash equilibria of congestion games always exist, but finding one is PLS-complete [133].

Approximation helps only partly. Lipton, Markakis and Mehta showed that an ε-Nash equilibrium of a two-player game exists with supports of logarithmic size, which gives a quasi-polynomial-time algorithm [134]. Aviad Rubinstein showed that, under a plausible hardness assumption for PPAD, quasi-polynomial time is also necessary for some constant ε [135].

10.3 What hardness means for the theory

Complexity results bear on the interpretation of equilibrium. If no efficient algorithm can find an equilibrium, it is doubtful that boundedly rational players or markets find one either, which weakens the case for equilibrium as a prediction in large games [11,125]. The same point arises for learning. Hart and Mas-Colell showed that there are no uncoupled dynamics, in which each player's adjustment depends only on that player's own payoffs, that are guaranteed to converge to Nash equilibrium in all games [136]. Hart and Mansour showed that the communication needed for uncoupled procedures to reach a Nash equilibrium can be exponential in the number of players [137]. Correlated equilibria, by contrast, are both computable and learnable, which is one reason they are often preferred as a solution concept in computer science [126,127].

11. Equilibrium selection when multiple equilibria exist

11.1 The problem

Many games have several equilibria, and Nash equilibrium alone does not say which will be played. Coordination games are the clearest case. In Rousseau's stag hunt, as formalised in the game-theory literature, two hunters do best if both hunt stag, but each can secure a smaller sure payoff by hunting hare alone [138]. Both (Stag, Stag) and (Hare, Hare) are equilibria. Bank runs have the same structure: if depositors expect others to withdraw, withdrawing is a best response, so a healthy bank can fail [139]. Aumann argued that even the efficient equilibrium may not be self-enforcing. A player who intends to play Hare benefits if the other plays Stag, so an agreement to play (Stag, Stag) does not reveal true intentions [140].

11.2 Refinements and selection theories

The refinement programme removed equilibria that rely on implausible behaviour off the equilibrium path. Its tools include Kohlberg and Mertens's strategic stability [141] and, for signalling games, Cho and Kreps's intuitive criterion [142]. Refinements do not resolve coordination problems such as the stag hunt, because both equilibria there are strict and survive every standard refinement [4].

Harsanyi and Selten proposed a complete theory that selects one equilibrium in every game. It compares equilibria by payoff dominance, which favours an equilibrium better for everyone, and by risk dominance, which favours the equilibrium with the larger product of deviation losses. Their theory gives priority to payoff dominance [12]. In the stag hunt above, (Stag, Stag) is payoff dominant, but (Hare, Hare) is risk dominant, because (3 − 0)(3 − 0) = 9 exceeds (4 − 3)(4 − 3) = 1. Equivalently, Stag is a best response only if the other player plays Stag with probability above ¾. Harsanyi later proposed a different theory that does not give payoff dominance priority [143].

11.3 Learning, evolution and global games

Two influential programmes derived selection from more primitive assumptions, and both favour risk dominance. Kandori, Mailath and Rob and, independently, Young studied adaptive play with occasional random mistakes. In the long run, as mistakes become rare, the population spends almost all its time at the stochastically stable equilibrium, which in 2×2 coordination games is the risk-dominant one [29,30]. Carlsson and van Damme introduced global games, in which players observe the payoffs only through slightly noisy private signals. Iterated dominance then selects a unique equilibrium, again the risk-dominant one in 2×2 games [41]. Morris and Shin applied global games to currency attacks, which gave a unique equilibrium in a model that had previously had many [144]. Evolutionary game theory more broadly studies which equilibria are stable under replicator and related dynamics [145].

11.4 Evidence and focal points

Schelling argued that people often coordinate on focal points, outcomes that stand out for reasons of salience, precedent or culture rather than payoffs [146]. Mehta, Starmer and Sugden confirmed experimentally that people coordinate far better than chance when asked to match one another's answers to open questions, by choosing salient labels [147]. Payoffs also matter. Cooper and colleagues found that subjects in coordination games often settle on a Pareto-inferior equilibrium [148]. In Van Huyck, Battalio and Beil's minimum-effort game, payoffs depend on the lowest effort in the group, and large groups converged quickly to the least efficient equilibrium [149]. Selection is therefore an empirical question, influenced by risk, history, salience and the size of the group, as well as a theoretical one.

12. Ethics, welfare and unintended consequences of incentive design

12.1 The welfare cost of selfish behaviour

Equilibria need not be efficient. The Prisoner's Dilemma is the textbook case, and Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" applied the logic to shared resources, arguing that individual incentives lead to overuse [150]. Ostrom documented many communities that manage commons sustainably through institutions they design themselves, which shows that the tragedy is not inevitable [151]. Ostrom shared the 2009 economics prize for this analysis of economic governance [152].

Computer science gave a quantitative measure of inefficiency. Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou defined the ratio between the worst equilibrium's cost and the optimal cost, later named the price of anarchy [32]. Roughgarden and Tardos showed that in routing games with linear latency functions, the price of anarchy is exactly 4/3 whatever the network [13]. The bound is attained by Pigou's example of two parallel roads [153,154].

Braess's paradox shows that adding capacity can make everyone worse off [37,155]. In Figure 11, one unit of traffic travels from s to t. Each route has one congestible link, whose travel time equals the share of traffic using it, and one fixed link with time 1. In equilibrium, traffic splits evenly and everyone's travel time is 1.5. Adding a free shortcut from v to w makes the route s→v→w→t a dominant choice, all traffic uses it, and everyone's travel time rises to 2 [154].

12.2 Limits on what design can achieve

Mechanism design asks how to set the rules so that self-interested behaviour produces good outcomes, and Hurwicz formalised the incentive compatibility constraints it must respect [156]. Impossibility theorems mark its limits. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem shows that every voting rule that can select any of at least three outcomes and cannot be manipulated by strategic voting is a dictatorship [157,158]. Myerson and Satterthwaite showed that no mechanism for bilateral trade can be efficient, incentive compatible, individually rational and budget-balanced at the same time [159]. Design is therefore always a matter of trade-offs among goals that cannot all be met.

Design has also produced real welfare gains, which shows what is at stake in getting it right. Kidney exchange mechanisms increase the number of transplants by arranging swaps among incompatible donor–patient pairs [160], and redesigned school choice mechanisms remove incentives for families to misreport their preferences [161]. Shapley and Roth received the 2012 prize for this work on stable allocation and market design [162].

12.3 Unintended consequences

Incentives designed for an imagined self-interested agent can backfire on real ones.

  • Crowding out. Gneezy and Rustichini introduced a fine for late pickup at Israeli day-care centres. Late arrivals increased, and they stayed high after the fine was removed, because the fine turned a moral obligation into a price [43]. Frey and Oberholzer-Gee found that offering compensation to Swiss residents for hosting a nuclear waste facility lowered their stated willingness to accept it [163]. Bowles reviewed many experiments in which explicit incentives reduced pro-social behaviour, so that incentives and moral motives acted as substitutes rather than complements [14].
  • Gaming the measure. Kerr described organisations that reward one behaviour while hoping for another [164]. Holmström and Milgrom showed formally that when an agent performs several tasks and only some can be measured, strong incentives on the measured tasks draw effort away from the others, so weak incentives may be optimal [165]. Goodhart's observation about monetary targets, which Strathern generalised as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure", describes the same effect [166,167]. In machine learning, strategic classification studies how people alter their features to obtain favourable predictions, and how to design classifiers that anticipate this [168].
  • Perverse bounties. During the 1902 plague scare in Hanoi, French colonial authorities paid a bounty for each rat tail. Residents cut off tails and released the rats, and some reportedly bred rats for the bounty [169].

12.4 Ethical questions

Some ethical questions concern the theory itself. Sen criticised the model of the purely self-interested agent as a "rational fool", unable to represent commitment or sympathy [170], and the experimental evidence of Section 5 supports the view that the model is incomplete. Bowles warned that policies designed for self-interested citizens may make citizens more self-interested, so the behavioural assumption can become self-fulfilling [14]. Others concern the use of the theory. Roth showed that some transactions are blocked because people find them repugnant, as with markets for organs, and that designers need to treat such constraints as real [171]. The welfare criterion itself is a value judgement: designs that maximise total surplus, revenue or worst-case outcomes can differ sharply, and choosing among them is a normative decision, not a technical one [154,159].

14. Summary

Each idealisation of classical game theory has a well-documented failure and a body of work that repairs it. Bounded rationality and behavioural models replace unlimited reasoning with limited depth, noise and learning. Social-preference models add fairness, reciprocity and trust to the payoffs. Theories of risk and ambiguity change how players evaluate uncertain prospects, and robust and distributionally robust games change what players are assumed to know about the game. Experiments and structural estimation test the resulting predictions. Complexity theory shows that equilibrium can be intractable to compute and so questions its value as a prediction in large games. Selection theories based on noise, learning and salience address multiplicity. The welfare and ethical analysis of incentive design shows that the rules of a game carry values and can backfire when they ignore real motivation.

The extensions share a method. Each keeps the formal structure of players, strategies and payoffs, changes one assumption, and asks which predictions survive. Their combinations, such as quantal response with social preferences, or robust design with boundedly rational agents, are active areas of research [15,61,100].
