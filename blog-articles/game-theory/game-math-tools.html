Mathematical Tools of Game Theory

Probability, utility, optimisation, fixed points, convexity, Bayesian updating, dynamic programming, stochastic processes, dynamical systems, networks and computation

Technical article · References in the Vancouver style · Part of a series with Representations of Games, Classification of Games, Limitations and Extensions and Game Theory in Computer Science and AI

Abstract. Game theory borrows almost all of its machinery from other branches of mathematics. Probability theory gives it mixed strategies and chance moves. Expected utility turns uncertain outcomes into numbers that can be compared. Constrained optimisation and mathematical programming describe best responses and compute equilibria. Fixed-point theorems and convex analysis prove that equilibria exist. Bayesian inference handles private information, dynamic programming handles time, and Markov chains, differential equations and dynamical systems describe how play evolves. Graph theory models who interacts with whom, and computational search and simulation solve games too large for closed-form analysis. This article treats twelve such tools in turn. For each it gives the history, the core definitions and results, a worked game-theoretic example, and a diagram. It closes with a comparison of the tools and of the questions each one answers.

1. Why game theory needs a mathematical toolkit

A game is a model of interdependent decisions: each player's payoff depends on what the others do as well as on that player's own choice [1,2]. To analyse such a model, three kinds of question have to be answered. The first is descriptive: what can each player do, what does each know, and how are outcomes valued? The second concerns existence: is there a stable outcome, such as an equilibrium, at all? The third is computational and dynamic: how can that outcome be found, and will players who learn or adapt reach it [3,4]?

No single branch of mathematics answers all three. Mixed strategies are probability distributions, and comparing risky outcomes needs a theory of expected utility [5,6]. A best response is the solution of an optimisation problem, so equilibrium is a system of coupled optimisation problems [7,8]. Nash's existence proof rests on a fixed-point theorem from topology [9,10], while von Neumann's minimax theorem is at heart a statement about convex sets [11,12]. Incomplete information calls for Bayes' rule [13], sequential play calls for dynamic programming [14,15], and evolutionary and learning models are stochastic processes or differential equations [16,17]. Large games, finally, are solved by algorithms whose cost is studied with the tools of computer science [18,19].

The sections that follow take the tools in roughly the order in which a game-theoretic analysis needs them. The formal objects they act on (payoff matrices, game trees, Bayesian games and stochastic games) are described in the companion article Representations of Games, and the families of games they apply to are surveyed in Classification of Games.

2. Historical development

Several of the tools are older than game theory itself and grew out of the study of gambling. The correspondence between Pascal and Fermat in 1654 on how to divide the stakes of an interrupted game, and Huygens's treatise of 1657, introduced the idea of the expected value of a gamble [20]. Daniel Bernoulli's paper of 1738 resolved the St Petersburg paradox by arguing that people value money by its utility rather than its amount [21]. Bayes's essay, published after his death in 1763, and Laplace's memoir of 1774 founded inverse probability, the ancestor of Bayesian inference [22,23]. Lagrange introduced his multipliers for constrained problems in the Mécanique analytique of 1788 [24], and Cournot's duopoly model of 1838 already defined an equilibrium as a pair of mutual best responses [25,26].

The twentieth century supplied the rest. Zermelo applied set theory to chess in 1913 [27,28]; Brouwer proved his fixed-point theorem in 1911 [29]; Borel defined mixed strategies in 1921 [30]; and von Neumann proved the minimax theorem in 1928 [31]. Kolmogorov's axioms of 1933 put probability on a measure-theoretic footing [32]. The Theory of Games and Economic Behavior of von Neumann and Morgenstern appeared in 1944, and its second edition of 1947 added the axiomatic derivation of expected utility [1,5]. Around 1950 the RAND Corporation and Princeton became centres for linear programming, nonlinear programming, dynamic programming and differential games, much of it funded by military research [33,34,35]. Later decades added Bayesian games, evolutionary dynamics, network models and, from the 1990s, a complexity theory of equilibrium computation [13,36,37,38].

3. Probability distributions and conditional probability

3.1 Foundations

Kolmogorov's axioms describe uncertainty by a probability space (Ω, 𝓕, P): a set of states Ω, a σ-algebra 𝓕 of events, and a countably additive measure P with P(Ω) = 1 [32,61]. A random variable is a measurable function on Ω, and its distribution is the probability measure it induces on its range. The conditional probability of an event A given an event B with P(B) > 0 is

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B),

and two events are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B) [61]. These definitions enter game theory in four places: in chance moves, in mixed strategies, in beliefs about other players, and in the distributions of privately known characteristics.

3.2 Mixed and behavioural strategies

Borel described players who randomise over their pure strategies in 1921 [30], and von Neumann's minimax theorem showed that randomisation guarantees every two-person zero-sum game a value [31]. A mixed strategy of player i is a probability distribution σi ∈ Δ(Si) over that player's finite set of pure strategies. Because players randomise independently, a profile of mixed strategies induces the product distribution

P(s1, …, sn) = σ1(s1) σ2(s2) ⋯ σn(sn)

over pure profiles [4,10]. Nash's theorem guarantees an equilibrium in mixed strategies for every finite game [9]. In extensive games a player may instead randomise separately at each information set. Kuhn showed that, in games of perfect recall, these behavioural strategies are equivalent to mixed strategies, a result that greatly reduces the number of probabilities that must be specified [15].

Dropping independence gives a different solution concept. Aumann's correlated equilibrium is a joint distribution over pure profiles, for example one generated by a public signal, such that no player gains by deviating from the recommended action after seeing it. Every Nash equilibrium is a correlated equilibrium, but the converse fails, and correlated equilibria can give higher payoffs to all players [62,63]. 3.3 Worked example: the indifference principle

In Matching Pennies, player 1 wins 1 from player 2 when the coins match and loses 1 when they differ. If player 2 shows Heads with probability q, player 1's expected payoff is 2q − 1 from Heads and 1 − 2q from Tails. Player 1 is willing to mix only if the two are equal, which requires q = 1/2; the same argument gives p = 1/2. In any mixed equilibrium, each player's mixture makes the other player indifferent among the strategies that the other uses [4,64]. Harsanyi showed that such equilibria can be read as the limit of pure-strategy play in games where each player's payoffs are perturbed by small, privately observed random terms, which answers the objection that people rarely toss coins to choose [65]. Random payoff perturbations are also the basis of the quantal response equilibrium used in experimental work [66].

3.4 Continuous distributions

Many games have a continuum of possible types or actions. In auction theory each bidder's value is a random variable with a distribution function F, and the analysis uses order statistics: with n bidders whose values are independent and uniform on [0, 1], the probability that a given value v is the highest is vn−1, and the symmetric equilibrium bid in a first-price auction is β(v) = (n − 1)v / n [67]. Games with infinitely many types or actions require measure theory to define strategies and expected payoffs properly, and their equilibrium existence results rely on topological properties of spaces of probability measures [68,69].

4. Expected values and expected utility

4.1 From expected value to utility

The expected value of a random payoff X taking values xk with probabilities pk is E[X] = Σk pk xk, a concept that goes back to Huygens [20]. Expected value alone is a poor guide to choice. In the St Petersburg game a fair coin is tossed until it first shows heads; if that happens on toss k, the player receives 2k ducats. The expected prize, Σ 2−k · 2k, is infinite, yet few people would pay more than a small sum to play. Daniel Bernoulli resolved the paradox by proposing that the value of money is its utility, which grows more slowly than its amount; with logarithmic utility the game is worth only a few ducats [21].

4.2 The von Neumann–Morgenstern theorem

Von Neumann and Morgenstern needed numerical payoffs whose expectations are meaningful, because a mixed strategy produces a lottery over outcomes. In the 1947 edition of their book they showed that a preference relation over lotteries that is complete, transitive, continuous and satisfies the independence axiom can be represented by an expected utility

U(L) = Σk pk u(xk),

where u is unique up to a positive affine transformation a·u + b with a > 0 [5,70]. Savage extended the idea to subjective probabilities, deriving both a utility function and a personal probability from axioms on preferences over acts [6]. In game theory the numbers in a payoff matrix are therefore von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, and the expected payoff of a mixed profile is

ui(σ) = Σs ∈ S (Πj σj(sj)) ui(s).

Because utilities are affine-invariant, rescaling one player's payoffs by a positive affine map leaves the set of Nash equilibria unchanged [4].

4.3 Risk attitudes

A player is risk-averse if a sure amount equal to a lottery's expected value is preferred to the lottery itself, which by Jensen's inequality holds for every lottery exactly when u is concave [70]. The certainty equivalent of a lottery L is the sure amount c with u(c) = U(L), and the risk premium is E[L] − c. Pratt and Arrow measured local risk aversion by the coefficient

r(x) = −u″(x) / u′(x),

which does not change under affine transformations of u [71,72]. Figure 2 works an example. Risk attitudes change game-theoretic predictions. In first-price auctions, for instance, risk-averse bidders bid more than risk-neutral ones, which breaks the revenue equivalence between first- and second-price auctions [67].

4.4 Worked example: a first-price auction

Take two risk-neutral bidders with values independent and uniform on [0, 1], and suppose bidder 2 bids half of their value. If bidder 1 with value v bids b ≤ 1/2, bidder 1 wins when v2/2 < b, which has probability 2b. The expected payoff is (v − b) · 2b, maximised at b = v/2, so bidding half one's value is a symmetric Bayesian equilibrium. The seller's expected revenue is the expected highest bid, E[max(v1, v2)]/2 = 1/3. In a second-price auction bidding one's value is a dominant strategy and the expected revenue is the expected lower value, also 1/3 [67,73]. Myerson's revenue equivalence theorem explains this coincidence: any two auctions that allocate the object the same way and give the lowest type the same payoff earn the same expected revenue [74].

Limits of the theory. Experiments by Allais and Ellsberg showed systematic violations of the independence axiom and of subjective expected utility, and prospect theory proposed an alternative descriptive model [75,76,77]. These departures and their consequences for games are surveyed in the companion article Limitations and Extensions of Game Theory.

5. Constrained optimisation

5.1 Lagrange multipliers and the KKT conditions

A best response is the solution of an optimisation problem, usually with constraints: probabilities must sum to one, quantities must be non-negative, and capacities and budgets must be respected. The general problem is

maximise f(x) subject to gk(x) ≤ 0 (k = 1, …, m), hl(x) = 0 (l = 1, …, p).

Lagrange treated equality constraints in 1788 by introducing a multiplier for each constraint [24]. Inequality constraints were handled by Karush in a master's thesis of 1939 and, independently, by Kuhn and Tucker in 1951 [34,41,44]. Under a constraint qualification, a local maximum x* has multipliers λk ≥ 0 and μl satisfying the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions:

∇f(x*) = Σk λk ∇gk(x*) + Σl μl ∇hl(x*), λk gk(x*) = 0 for all k.

The second condition, complementary slackness, says that a constraint which does not bind has a zero multiplier. When f is concave and the constraints are convex, the KKT conditions are also sufficient for a global maximum [7,78]. A multiplier measures how fast the optimal value rises as its constraint is relaxed, and economists call it a shadow price [70].

5.2 Best responses and the indifference principle

The KKT conditions explain the indifference principle of Section 3.3. Player i's best-response problem is to maximise Σk σk vk, where vk is the expected payoff of pure strategy k against the others' strategies, subject to Σ σk = 1 and σk ≥ 0. Stationarity gives vk = μ − λk, and complementary slackness gives λk = 0 whenever σk > 0. So every strategy used earns the same payoff μ, and no unused strategy earns more [4,64]. A Nash equilibrium is a point that satisfies every player's KKT system at the same time.

When the players' feasible sets depend on one another, as when firms share a pipeline or a pollution cap, the result is a generalised Nash equilibrium problem. Debreu proved existence for such "social equilibria" in 1952, a result later used in the Arrow–Debreu existence proof [48,79]. Rosen studied concave games with coupled constraints and gave a condition, diagonal strict concavity, that guarantees a unique equilibrium [80]. Such problems are now often written as variational inequalities or complementarity problems and solved with specialised algorithms [8,81].

5.3 Worked example: Cournot duopoly with a capacity limit

Two firms choose quantities q1, q2 ≥ 0, the market price is 12 − q1 − q2, and production is free. Firm i's profit is qi(12 − q1 − q2), and the first-order condition gives the best response qi = (12 − qj)/2. The equilibrium, which Cournot found in 1838, is q1 = q2 = 4 [25,70]. Now suppose firm 1 cannot produce more than 3 units. Its problem has the constraint q1 − 3 ≤ 0, and its best response becomes min{3, (12 − q2)/2}. The new equilibrium is q1 = 3 and q2 = 4.5. The multiplier on the capacity constraint is the marginal profit at the constraint, λ = 12 − 2(3) − 4.5 = 1.5, the amount by which firm 1's profit would rise per unit of extra capacity, holding firm 2's output fixed. 6. Linear and nonlinear programming

6.1 Linear programming and duality

A linear program maximises a linear function subject to linear inequalities: maximise cTx subject to Ax ≤ b and x ≥ 0. Its dual is to minimise bTy subject to ATy ≥ c and y ≥ 0. The strong duality theorem says that if either problem has a finite optimum then both do, and the optimal values are equal [7,82]. Kantorovich formulated planning problems of this type in 1939 [40], and Dantzig developed the simplex method in 1947 [43,83]. Dantzig recalled that when he described the problem to von Neumann in October 1947, von Neumann immediately conjectured the duality theorem by analogy with his work on zero-sum games [43]. Gale, Kuhn and Tucker published a proof in 1951 [84].

6.2 Zero-sum games as linear programs

Consider a two-person zero-sum game in which the row player receives ajk when row j meets column k. The row player chooses a mixed strategy p to maximise the payoff guaranteed against every column:

maximise v subject to Σj pj ajk ≥ v for every column k, Σj pj = 1, p ≥ 0.

The column player's problem of minimising the row player's best payoff is the dual of this program, so strong duality is the minimax theorem [84,85]. Adler later showed that the equivalence also runs the other way: every linear program can be reduced to a zero-sum game, with a small gap in Dantzig's original argument repaired [86].

The simplex method can take exponential time on specially constructed examples [87], but Khachiyan's ellipsoid method and Karmarkar's interior-point method solve linear programs in polynomial time [88,89]. Two-person zero-sum games can therefore be solved efficiently. For zero-sum games in extensive form, Koller, Megiddo and von Stengel's sequence form gives a linear program whose size is linear in the size of the game tree, instead of exponential as in the normal form [90].

Linear programming also characterises other solution concepts. The incentive constraints of a correlated equilibrium are linear in the joint distribution, so correlated equilibria are the feasible points of a linear program [18,62]. In cooperative games, the Bondareva–Shapley theorem uses LP duality to show that the core is non-empty exactly when the game is balanced [91,92].

6.3 Worked example

Let the row player choose between rows R1 and R2 with payoffs (3, −1, 2) and (−2, 2, 0) against columns C1, C2 and C3. If p is the probability of R1, the three columns give the row player 5p − 2, 2 − 3p and 2p. The row player maximises the lower envelope of these lines, which peaks where the first two cross, at p* = 1/2, with value v = 1/2. Column C3 is never needed. The column player mixes C1 and C2 with probabilities 3/8 and 5/8, which holds the row player to 1/2 whichever row is played. 6.4 Nonlinear programs and complementarity

Games that are not zero-sum lead to nonlinear problems. Mangasarian and Stone showed that the Nash equilibria of a bimatrix game are exactly the global maxima, with optimal value zero, of a quadratic program with linear constraints [93]. Equivalently, an equilibrium solves a linear complementarity problem: find vectors whose componentwise products with their slack vectors are zero [94]. The Lemke–Howson algorithm of 1964 solves this problem by complementary pivoting and gave a constructive proof that every bimatrix game has an equilibrium [49]. Savani and von Stengel later constructed games on which it takes exponential time [95]. Practical solvers also use support enumeration [96], mixed-integer programming [97] and homotopy methods [98,99], and several are implemented in the open-source Gambit package [100,101].

For games with continuous actions, each player's problem is a nonlinear program, and equilibrium is a set of coupled nonlinear programs. When payoffs are concave in a player's own action, gradient-based and interior-point methods from nonlinear programming apply, and Rosen's diagonal strict concavity ensures that a continuous-time gradient process converges to the unique equilibrium [78,80].

7. Fixed-point theorems

7.1 The theorems

A fixed point of a map f is a point with f(x) = x. Existence proofs in game theory work by constructing a map whose fixed points are exactly the equilibria and then appealing to a theorem that such a map must have a fixed point [4,102]. Four theorems do most of the work.

Brouwer (1911). Every continuous function from a non-empty, compact, convex subset of ℝn to itself has a fixed point [29].
Kakutani (1941). Let K be a non-empty, compact, convex subset of ℝn and F a correspondence from K to K whose values are non-empty and convex and whose graph is closed. Then some x satisfies x ∈ F(x) [42].
Banach (1922). A contraction on a complete metric space, that is, a map with d(f(x), f(y)) ≤ β d(x, y) for some β < 1, has exactly one fixed point, and iterating the map from any starting point converges to it [103].
Tarski (1955). Every order-preserving map on a complete lattice has a fixed point, and its fixed points themselves form a complete lattice, with a smallest and a largest element [104].

Kakutani stated that his theorem was intended to simplify a fixed-point result that von Neumann had proved in 1937 for a model of economic growth [42,105]. Glicksberg extended Kakutani's theorem to locally convex topological vector spaces, which yields equilibria for games with compact, infinite strategy sets and continuous payoffs [68].

7.2 Nash's two proofs

Nash's note of 1950 applied Kakutani's theorem to the best-response correspondence BR(σ) = ×i BRi(σ−i). The set of mixed profiles is a product of simplices and so compact and convex. Each player's set of best responses is non-empty and convex because expected payoff is linear in the player's own mixed strategy, and the graph of BR is closed because payoffs are continuous. A fixed point is a profile in which every player is best-responding, which is a Nash equilibrium [9]. His 1951 paper used Brouwer's theorem instead, applied to a continuous map that shifts probability toward pure strategies that do better than the current mixture:

σ′i(s) = [σi(s) + gi,s(σ)] / [1 + Σt gi,t(σ)], where gi,s(σ) = max{0, ui(s, σ−i) − ui(σ)}.

The map's fixed points are exactly the Nash equilibria [10]. Debreu's social equilibrium theorem, proved with a similar argument, became the basis of the Arrow–Debreu proof that a competitive economy has an equilibrium [48,79]. Geanakoplos later gave a single Brouwer-based argument that covers both Nash and Walrasian equilibrium [106]. Figure 5 illustrates the hypotheses.

7.3 Contractions and lattices

Banach's theorem gives uniqueness and an algorithm as well as existence. It underlies the value of discounted stochastic games and of dynamic programs (Sections 10 and 11), because the Bellman and Shapley operators are contractions with modulus equal to the discount factor [47,107]. Tarski's theorem underlies the theory of supermodular games, in which each player's best response increases in the others' actions. Topkis showed that such games have pure-strategy equilibria, and Milgrom and Roberts showed that the largest and smallest equilibria bound the strategies that survive iterated deletion of dominated strategies and the limits of adaptive learning [108,109].

7.4 Computing fixed points

Brouwer's theorem does not say how to find the fixed point. Sperner's combinatorial lemma of 1928 implies Brouwer's theorem [110], and Scarf turned a related path-following argument into the first general algorithm for approximating fixed points in 1967 [52]. Papadimitriou defined the complexity class PPAD for problems, such as finding a Sperner triangle, whose solutions exist by a parity argument on a directed graph [38]. Computing a Nash equilibrium was shown to be PPAD-complete for games with three or more players by Daskalakis, Goldberg and Papadimitriou, and for two-player games by Chen, Deng and Teng [58,59]. Exact equilibria of games with three or more players can be irrational numbers, and Etessami and Yannakakis showed that computing them is complete for a further class, FIXP [111]. The consequences of these results for the predictive power of equilibrium are discussed in Limitations and Extensions and Game Theory in Computer Science and AI.

8. Convex analysis

8.1 Basic objects

A set is convex if it contains the line segment between any two of its points, and a function is convex if its epigraph is a convex set. The convex hull of a set is the smallest convex set containing it. The central result is the separating hyperplane theorem: two disjoint convex sets, one of them compact and the other closed, can be strictly separated by a hyperplane [7,12]. Fenchel's conjugate f*(y) = supx {⟨x, y⟩ − f(x)} turns this geometry into a duality theory for optimisation [12,112]. Convexity is what makes mixed strategies useful: the set of mixed strategies is a simplex, expected payoffs are linear on it, and the set of payoff vectors that randomisation can achieve is the convex hull of the pure-strategy payoff vectors [4].

8.2 The minimax theorem

Von Neumann's 1928 proof of the minimax theorem was analytic and topological. Ville gave the first elementary proof in 1938, using convexity, and von Neumann and Morgenstern's book of 1944 based its proof on a theorem on supporting hyperplanes of convex sets [1,11]. The argument runs as follows. Suppose the column player can hold the row player to at most v. Then the convex set of payoff vectors the column player can generate does not meet the open orthant of vectors that exceed v in every coordinate. A separating hyperplane between them has non-negative normal coefficients which, scaled to sum to one, form a mixed strategy that guarantees the row player v. Sion generalised the theorem in 1958 to any function that is quasi-concave in one variable and quasi-convex in the other, on convex strategy sets of which one is compact [113].

8.3 Feasible payoffs, bargaining and folk theorems

The set of payoffs that players can obtain by randomising jointly over outcomes is the convex hull of the pure-strategy payoff vectors. Nash's bargaining solution is defined on a convex, compact feasible set, and his axioms single out the point that maximises the product of the players' gains over the disagreement point [114]. In infinitely repeated games, the folk theorem of Fudenberg and Maskin states that any feasible payoff vector that gives each player strictly more than that player's minmax payoff can be sustained by a subgame-perfect equilibrium when players are patient enough, given a full-dimensionality condition when there are three or more players [115]. 8.4 Approachability, regret and potential functions

Blackwell's approachability theorem extends the minimax theorem to vector payoffs. A closed convex set can be approached, in the sense that the long-run average payoff vector converges to it whatever the opponent does, if and only if every half-space containing it can be approached [116]. Taking the set to be the non-positive orthant of regret vectors gives learning rules with no regret, an idea that goes back to Hannan [117,118]. Hart and Mas-Colell's regret-matching procedure makes the empirical distribution of play converge to the set of correlated equilibria [119], and Freund and Schapire showed that the multiplicative-weights algorithm yields a constructive proof of the minimax theorem [120].

Convexity also appears in potential games, in which the change in any player's payoff from a unilateral deviation equals the change in a single function on strategy profiles. Monderer and Shapley showed that every finite potential game has a pure equilibrium at a maximiser of the potential, and that better-response dynamics converge [121]. Rosenthal's congestion games all have exact potentials [121,122], and when the potential is concave its maximisers can be found by convex optimisation [7].

9. Bayesian inference

9.1 Bayes' rule

Bayes's essay of 1763 and Laplace's independent work of 1774 established how to reason from observed effects to unknown causes [22,23]. In modern notation, a prior π(θ) over an unknown parameter and a likelihood P(s | θ) for an observed signal s give the posterior

π(θ | s) = P(s | θ) π(θ) / Σθ′ P(s | θ′) π(θ′).

When prior and likelihood come from conjugate families, the posterior has a closed form. A Beta prior on a probability updated by binomial observations gives a Beta posterior, and a Dirichlet prior over an opponent's mixed strategy gives a Dirichlet posterior [123]. With a Dirichlet prior, the predictive distribution is the empirical frequency of the opponent's past actions adjusted by prior pseudo-counts, so fictitious play can be read as Bayesian learning by a player who believes the opponent's strategy is fixed [124].

9.2 Harsanyi's Bayesian games

Harsanyi's three-part paper of 1967–68 showed how to analyse games of incomplete information, in which players are unsure of one another's payoffs. Each player's private information is summarised by a type, types are drawn by Nature from a common prior, and each player updates by Bayes' rule on learning their own type. A Bayesian Nash equilibrium is then a Nash equilibrium of the resulting game of imperfect information, in which each type maximises expected utility given its posterior [13,125,126]. Mertens and Zamir showed that a universal type space can represent any hierarchy of beliefs about beliefs consistently, which justifies the type-based formulation in general [69]. Bayesian games and their diagrams are covered in detail in Representations of Games.

Aumann proved in 1976 that if two agents share a common prior and their posteriors for an event are common knowledge, the posteriors must be equal: rational agents with common priors cannot "agree to disagree" [127]. Kalai and Lehrer showed that if players' beliefs assign positive probability to the strategies actually played, a condition sometimes called a grain of truth, Bayesian learning in repeated games eventually leads to play close to a Nash equilibrium [128].

9.3 Updating in dynamic games

In dynamic games with incomplete information, players update their beliefs as they observe actions. Kreps and Wilson's sequential equilibrium and the perfect Bayesian equilibrium of Fudenberg and Tirole both require strategies to be optimal given beliefs, and beliefs to follow Bayes' rule wherever it applies [129,130]. Bayes' rule says nothing about beliefs after an action that should occur with probability zero, which is why signalling games often have many equilibria. Refinements such as Cho and Kreps's intuitive criterion restrict these off-path beliefs [131]. Signalling models of education and adverse-selection models of markets with hidden quality are standard applications [132,133].

9.4 Worked example: inferring an opponent's type

A firm believes that a rival is "tough" with prior probability 0.3. A tough rival cuts prices with probability 0.9, and a weak rival does so with probability 0.4. After observing a price cut, Bayes' rule gives

P(tough | cut) = (0.9 × 0.3) / (0.9 × 0.3 + 0.4 × 0.7) = 0.27 / 0.55 ≈ 0.49.

One observation raises the belief from 0.30 to 0.49. If the firm sees no cut, the belief falls to 0.03 / 0.45 ≈ 0.07. The expected posterior, 0.55 × 0.49 + 0.45 × 0.07 = 0.30, equals the prior, as it must. Kamenica and Gentzkow built the theory of Bayesian persuasion on this property: a sender who designs what a receiver will learn can induce any distribution of posteriors whose mean is the prior, and the sender's best achievable payoff is given by the concave envelope of the sender's payoff as a function of the posterior [134]. 10. Dynamic programming

10.1 The principle of optimality

Bellman developed dynamic programming at the RAND Corporation in the early 1950s. He later explained that he chose the name partly because it sounded harmless to officials who disliked mathematical research [35]. His principle of optimality states that an optimal policy has the property that, whatever the initial state and decision, the remaining decisions form an optimal policy for the state that results [14,46]. For a discounted problem with states x, actions a, rewards r(x, a), transition probabilities P(x′ | x, a) and discount factor δ < 1, the value function satisfies the Bellman equation

V(x) = maxa { r(x, a) + δ Σx′ P(x′ | x, a) V(x′) }.

The right-hand side defines an operator that is a contraction with modulus δ, so the Bellman equation has a unique solution, and value iteration converges to it from any starting point [107,135]. Howard's policy iteration alternates between evaluating a policy and improving it, and usually converges in few steps [136]. The number of states grows exponentially with the number of state variables, which Bellman called the "curse of dimensionality" [14,137].

10.2 Backward induction

In a finite game of perfect information, dynamic programming becomes backward induction: solve the last decisions first, replace each final decision node by the payoff its mover would choose, and repeat until the root is reached. Zermelo's 1913 analysis of chess is often cited as the first use of the idea, although Schwalbe and Walker point out that his argument was different in detail [27,28]. Kuhn showed that backward induction proves every finite game of perfect information has an equilibrium in pure strategies [15]. Selten's subgame-perfect equilibrium, which requires equilibrium play in every subgame, generalises backward induction to games with imperfect information [51,138].

Rosenthal's centipede game shows both the power and the limits of the method. Two players alternately decide whether to take a growing pot or pass it on. Backward induction predicts that the first player takes at once, although both would gain by passing for a while [139]. Figure 8 shows a four-stage version.

10.3 Recursive methods in games

Shapley's stochastic games of 1953 predate Bellman's book. Shapley showed that a two-person zero-sum discounted stochastic game has a value, found by iterating an operator that solves a matrix game in every state, and that this operator is a contraction [47]. In repeated games, the one-shot deviation principle reduces the check for subgame perfection to single-period deviations, a consequence of the same dynamic-programming logic [3,107]. Abreu, Pearce and Stacchetti showed that the set of equilibrium payoffs of a discounted repeated game is the largest fixed point of a set-valued operator, which made such sets computable [140]. Rubinstein's alternating-offers bargaining model has a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium, derived by a stationary recursion: with common discount factor δ, the first proposer receives 1/(1 + δ) of the pie [141].

11. Markov chains and stochastic processes

11.1 Markov chains

A stochastic process is a family of random variables indexed by time. Markov introduced, in 1906, chains of random variables in which the distribution of the next state depends only on the current one, partly to show that the law of large numbers does not require independence [39]. A finite Markov chain is described by a transition matrix P with entries Pxy = P(Xt+1 = y | Xt = x). A stationary distribution is a probability vector with πP = π. If the chain is irreducible and aperiodic, the stationary distribution is unique, the distribution of Xt converges to it from any start, and the long-run fraction of time spent in each state equals its stationary probability [142]. Doob's theory of martingales, processes whose expected next value equals their current value, supplies convergence theorems used throughout the study of learning and beliefs; a Bayesian's sequence of posterior probabilities is a martingale [128,143].

11.2 Stochastic games and Markov perfect equilibrium

A stochastic game is a Markov chain controlled by several players: in each period the state determines a stage game, the players' actions determine payoffs and the distribution of the next state [47,144]. Mertens and Neyman proved that undiscounted two-person zero-sum stochastic games also have a value [145]. In applied work, attention is often restricted to Markov strategies, which depend on history only through the current state. A Markov perfect equilibrium is a subgame-perfect equilibrium in such strategies, and Maskin and Tirole developed its foundations [146]. Ericson and Pakes used Markov perfect equilibria of dynamic oligopoly to build a framework for empirical industrial organisation [147]. Stochastic games with unknown transition probabilities are the standard model of multi-agent reinforcement learning, following Littman [148]; that literature is discussed in Game Theory in Computer Science and AI.

11.3 Stochastic stability

Markov chains also select among equilibria. Foster and Young introduced stochastic stability for evolutionary dynamics with persistent noise [149]. Kandori, Mailath and Rob, and independently Young, studied populations that play a coordination game, mostly by best-responding to current play, but with a small probability ε of a random mistake. The resulting process is an irreducible Markov chain with a unique stationary distribution. As ε → 0, the stationary distribution concentrates on the stochastically stable states, which in 2×2 coordination games are those where everyone plays the risk-dominant equilibrium [54,55].

A two-state caricature shows the mechanism. Let state A be the risk-dominant convention and B the other. Suppose leaving A needs two simultaneous mistakes, which occur with probability of order ε2, while leaving B needs one, with probability of order ε. In a two-state chain the stationary distribution balances the flows, πAε2 = πBε, so πA = 1/(1 + ε), which tends to 1. Figure 9 shows the chain. The general theory computes the same comparison by counting the mistakes along the cheapest paths, called "resistances", between the recurrent classes of the noise-free process [55].

11.4 Continuous-time processes

Continuous-time stochastic processes are also used. Sannikov analysed repeated games in continuous time in which actions are observed only through a Brownian motion with a drift that depends on them, and characterised the boundary of the set of equilibrium payoffs by an ordinary differential equation [150].

12. Differential equations and dynamical systems

12.1 Dynamical systems

An autonomous system of ordinary differential equations ẋ = f(x) defines a flow on a state space. Its rest points satisfy f(x) = 0. A rest point is Lyapunov stable if trajectories that start nearby stay nearby, and asymptotically stable if they also converge to it. Lyapunov's second method proves stability by finding a function that decreases along trajectories [151,152]. Game theorists use these ideas to ask whether equilibria are reached by players who adjust their behaviour over time.

12.2 Learning and evolutionary dynamics

The first such models were designed to compute equilibria of zero-sum games. Brown and von Neumann wrote down a system of differential equations whose solutions converge to the set of optimal strategies [45], and Brown proposed fictitious play, in which each player best-responds to the empirical frequencies of the opponent's past play [153]. Robinson proved that fictitious play converges in two-person zero-sum games [154], but Shapley gave a 3×3 game in which it cycles forever [155].

Evolutionary game theory gave the subject a biological interpretation. Maynard Smith and Price defined an evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) as one that, once adopted by a population, cannot be invaded by a small group of mutants [53,156]. Taylor and Jonker proposed the dynamics now called the replicator equation, a name given by Schuster and Sigmund [36,157]:

ẋk = xk [ (Ax)k − xTAx ],

in which the population share of each strategy grows at a rate equal to the difference between its payoff and the population average. The "folk theorem" of evolutionary game theory links the dynamics to equilibrium: Nash equilibria are rest points, stable rest points are Nash equilibria, interior trajectories that converge do so to a Nash equilibrium, and an ESS is asymptotically stable [16,158]. The replicator equation is also equivalent to the Lotka–Volterra equations of population ecology [16]. Other dynamics, such as best-response, logit and pairwise comparison dynamics, are studied in a common framework by Sandholm [17,159].

12.3 Worked examples: Hawk–Dove and Rock–Paper–Scissors

In the Hawk–Dove game a resource is worth V and a fight costs C > V. Hawks meeting Hawks get (V − C)/2, Hawks meeting Doves get V, Doves meeting Hawks get 0, and Doves meeting Doves get V/2 [53,156]. If x is the share of Hawks, the replicator equation reduces to

ẋ = x(1 − x)(V − Cx)/2,

which has rest points at 0, 1 and V/C. The interior point is asymptotically stable and is the mixed ESS. In Rock–Paper–Scissors with payoff 1 for a win, −1 for a loss and 0 for a draw, the product xRxPxS is constant along trajectories, so interior trajectories are closed orbits around the equilibrium (1/3, 1/3, 1/3), which is stable but not asymptotically stable [16]. Figure 10 draws both phase portraits.

12.4 Differential games

In a differential game the state evolves according to ẋ = f(x, u1, …, un), where ui are the players' controls, and each player maximises an integral of running payoffs. Isaacs began the theory at RAND with pursuit–evasion problems and summarised it in his book of 1965. In the zero-sum case the value function satisfies a partial differential equation now called the Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs equation [50]. Pontryagin's maximum principle, published in English in 1962, gives necessary conditions for open-loop optimal controls and so for open-loop Nash equilibria [160,161]. Başar and Olsder distinguish open-loop equilibria, in which controls depend only on time, from feedback equilibria, in which they depend on the current state; the two generally differ, and only feedback equilibria are subgame perfect [161].

13. Graph theory and network analysis

13.1 Graphs in game theory

Graph theory began with Euler's 1736 solution of the Königsberg bridges problem [162]. A graph consists of nodes and edges, and is described by an adjacency matrix G with gij = 1 when i and j are linked. Graphs enter game theory in three roles. Game trees are directed trees, and their analysis is a graph search [15]. Graphs record who interacts with whom: in Kearns, Littman and Singh's graphical games, each player's payoff depends only on the actions of that player's neighbours, which can make the description of a large game exponentially smaller and allows efficient equilibrium algorithms on trees [163]. And networks are themselves the outcome of strategic choice [164,165].

13.2 Games on networks

Ballester, Calvó-Armengol and Zenou studied a game with linear-quadratic payoffs in which each player's effort is complementary to that of their neighbours. When the strength of complementarity φ is below the reciprocal of the largest eigenvalue of G, the unique Nash equilibrium sets each player's effort proportional to that player's Bonacich centrality, the weighted count of walks starting at the node given by the vector (I − φG)−11 [166,167]. The result links equilibrium analysis to the spectral analysis of networks and identifies a "key player" whose removal reduces total activity most. Galeotti and coauthors extended network games to settings in which players know only the number of their own neighbours [168]. In evolutionary models, spatial structure changes outcomes: Nowak and May showed that cooperators can persist in the Prisoner's Dilemma played on a lattice [169], and Ohtsuki and coauthors found that, under weak selection, cooperation is favoured when the benefit-to-cost ratio exceeds the average number of neighbours [170].

13.3 Network formation

Jackson and Wolinsky modelled networks formed by players who add or sever links. Their pairwise stability requires that no player wants to cut a link and no unlinked pair both want to form one. They showed that stable and efficient networks often differ [37]. Bala and Goyal studied a noncooperative model in which each player pays for the links they form. With one-way flow of information, strict Nash networks are either wheels or empty; with two-way flow they are either centre-sponsored stars or empty [171].

13.4 Routing, congestion and matching

Traffic networks are games in which drivers choose routes. Wardrop's first principle of 1952 states that, in equilibrium, all used routes between two points have equal travel time and no unused route is faster [172]. Rosenthal's congestion games, in which the cost of a resource depends on how many players use it, always have pure equilibria [122]. Braess observed in 1968 that adding a road to a network can increase every driver's equilibrium travel time [173]. Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou proposed measuring such inefficiency by the ratio of the worst equilibrium cost to the optimum, later called the price of anarchy, and Roughgarden and Tardos proved that it is at most 4/3 in routing networks with linear latency functions [174,175].

The standard example has 4000 drivers travelling from S to E by way of either A or B. The links S→A and B→E take T/100 minutes when T drivers use them, and S→B and A→E take 45 minutes. In equilibrium the drivers split evenly and each takes 65 minutes. Adding a zero-time link from A to B makes the route S→A→B→E a dominant choice; everyone takes it and each journey lasts 80 minutes [165,173]. Figure 11 shows the network.

Bipartite graphs model matching markets. Gale and Shapley's deferred-acceptance algorithm finds a stable matching in every two-sided market with strict preferences, which proves that one always exists [176].

14. Computational search and simulation

14.1 Game-tree search

When a game is too large for analysis by hand, its tree must be searched by computer. Shannon's paper of 1950 proposed that a chess program search a limited number of moves ahead, evaluate the resulting positions with a heuristic function, and back the values up by the minimax rule. He estimated the number of possible chess games at about 10120, which rules out complete search [177]. Alpha–beta pruning skips branches that cannot affect the minimax value. Knuth and Moore showed that, with perfect move ordering, it examines roughly the square root of the number of leaves that plain minimax examines, which allows a search about twice as deep for the same cost [178,179]. Figure 12 shows an example.

Combined with large databases and improved hardware, search has solved some games outright. Schaeffer and coauthors proved in 2007 that checkers is a draw with perfect play by both sides [180].

14.2 Monte Carlo methods

Metropolis and Ulam named and described the Monte Carlo method in 1949: estimating a quantity by averaging over random samples [181]. By the central limit theorem, the error of a sample average shrinks in proportion to 1/√N regardless of the dimension of the problem [61], which makes simulation attractive for expected payoffs that cannot be computed exactly. Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), named by Coulom in 2006, builds a search tree incrementally and evaluates leaves by random playouts [57,182]. Kocsis and Szepesvári's UCT algorithm treats each node as a multi-armed bandit and selects the child j that maximises

X̄j + c √( ln n / nj ),

where X̄j is the child's average reward, nj its visit count and n the parent's visit count. They proved that the probability of selecting a suboptimal action converges to zero as the number of samples grows [56]. Silver and coauthors combined MCTS with deep neural networks for evaluation and move selection in AlphaGo, which defeated a professional Go player in an even match [183].

14.3 Games of imperfect information

Search over game trees does not directly apply when players have private information, because a player cannot tell which node of an information set is the true one. Counterfactual regret minimisation (CFR), introduced by Zinkevich and coauthors, runs no-regret learning at every information set in self-play; in two-player zero-sum games the average strategy converges to a Nash equilibrium [184]. A variant of CFR was used to compute a strategy for heads-up limit Texas hold'em so close to equilibrium that the game was declared essentially weakly solved [60]. Brown and Sandholm's Libratus beat leading professionals at heads-up no-limit hold'em, and their Pluribus did so in six-player no-limit hold'em, combining equilibrium computation with real-time search [185,186].

14.4 Simulation and empirical game-theoretic analysis

Simulation is also used when the strategy space is too rich to write down. Axelrod's two computer tournaments, held around 1980, pitted strategies for the repeated Prisoner's Dilemma submitted by researchers against one another; the simple strategy Tit for Tat won both tournaments [187]. Wellman's empirical game-theoretic analysis estimates the payoff matrix of a game restricted to a set of candidate strategies by simulating their interactions, and then analyses the estimated game with standard equilibrium tools [188]. For games that can be written down explicitly, software such as Gambit implements Lemke–Howson, support enumeration, homotopy and other algorithms surveyed by McKelvey and McLennan [100,101].

16. Summary

Game theory is a field defined by its questions rather than by a single method. Probability and expected utility turn strategic uncertainty into numbers that players can compare. Optimisation describes what each player does given what the others do, and fixed-point theorems and convex analysis guarantee that mutually consistent choices exist. Bayesian inference extends the analysis to private information, and dynamic programming to choices made over time. Markov chains, differential equations and dynamical systems describe how play changes when players learn, imitate or evolve, and graph theory describes the structure of who interacts with whom. Computational search, programming and simulation make the theory usable for games as large as poker and Go, while complexity theory marks where exact computation stops being feasible. A working game theorist moves between these tools as the questions change from whether an equilibrium exists, to what it looks like, how it can be found, and whether real players would reach it.
