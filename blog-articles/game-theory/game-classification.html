Classification of Games

Timing, cooperation, payoffs, information, duration, chance, players, strategy spaces, communication and participation

Technical article · References in the Vancouver style · Companion to Representations of Games

Abstract. Game theory does not study one kind of game but a family of them, and the first step in any analysis is to say which member of the family is at hand. This article sets out ten standard dimensions along which games are classified: timing, cooperation, payoff structure, information, duration, the role of chance, the number of players, the size and shape of strategy spaces, communication, and participation. For each dimension it gives the formal definitions, the history of the idea, worked examples with diagrams, and the consequences for which solution concepts apply. Particular care is taken over the four information categories (perfect, imperfect, complete and incomplete), which are often confused. The article closes by showing how the dimensions constrain one another and by classifying a set of well-known games along all ten at once.

1. Why classify games?

A game, in the technical sense, is a model of interactive decision-making in which each participant's outcome depends on the choices of the others as well as on its own [1,2]. The same mathematical framework covers chess, wage bargaining, auctions, arms races and the evolution of animal behaviour [3,4]. These situations differ in ways that matter for analysis. Some are pure conflict and some leave room for mutual gain. Some are played once and some repeat indefinitely. In some every move is visible, and in others players must reason about what they cannot see [5,6].

Classifying a game answers three practical questions. First, it tells the analyst which representation to use: a payoff matrix, a game tree, a model with types, or a coalitional function (see the companion article, Representations of Games). Second, it tells the analyst which solution concepts are appropriate, for example minimax for two-person zero-sum games, subgame perfection for sequential games, Bayesian equilibrium for games of incomplete information, and the core or Shapley value for cooperative games [3,5,6]. Third, it tells the analyst which theorems are available. Existence of equilibrium, uniqueness of value and the efficiency of computation all depend on where a game sits in the classification [7–9].

The dimensions used here are the ones that recur across the standard graduate texts [3,5,6,10]. They are not mutually exclusive categories but independent axes, so that a single game is described by a position on each. Section 14 shows that the axes are not fully independent: some combinations are impossible and others are interchangeable by a change of model.

2. Historical development

The classification of games grew with the theory itself, and many distinctions entered the literature as the titles of the papers that introduced them. Cournot's 1838 analysis of duopoly is usually read as the first equilibrium analysis of a simultaneous game with a continuous strategy space [5,11]. Bertrand's 1883 review of Cournot argued that firms set prices rather than quantities, an early example of how the choice of strategy variable changes the conclusion [12]. Zermelo's 1913 note on chess studied a finite, two-person game of what would later be called perfect information [13,14]. Borel in 1921 and von Neumann in 1928 studied two-person games of pure opposition, and von Neumann proved the minimax theorem for them [8,15].

Von Neumann and Morgenstern's Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944) organised the field by number of players and by payoff structure. It treated the two-person zero-sum game first, then zero-sum games with three or more players, in which coalitions become essential, and finally general (non-zero-sum) games. It also defined games "with perfect information" [1]. Stackelberg's 1934 model of a leader and a follower had already shown how sequential timing changes a duopoly outcome [16].

John Nash introduced the distinction between cooperative and non-cooperative games. His 1951 paper described its theory as "based on the absence of coalitions in that it is assumed that each participant acts independently, without collaboration or communication with any of the others" [7]. His bargaining papers of 1950 and 1953 treated the cooperative side and proposed reducing cooperative solutions to non-cooperative models, an agenda later called the Nash program [17–19]. Kuhn's 1953 paper generalised perfect information to arbitrary information sets [20], and the same volume contained Shapley's value for cooperative games [21]. Shapley's stochastic games appeared that year as well [22]. Gillies defined the core in 1959 [23], and Schelling's The Strategy of Conflict (1960) put tacit communication and focal points at the centre of mixed-motive games [24].

Harsanyi's three-part paper of 1967 and 1968 drew the line between incomplete and imperfect information and showed how to convert the first into the second [25]. The theory of repeated games developed from folk results of the 1950s through Friedman's 1971 paper and Aumann and Shapley's work to the discounted folk theorem of Fudenberg and Maskin in 1986 [26–28]. Crawford and Sobel's 1982 model of strategic information transmission made costless communication a formal object of study [29]. Three Nobel memorial prizes mark this history. Nash, Harsanyi and Selten shared the 1994 prize "for their pioneering analysis of equilibria in the theory of non-cooperative games" [30]. Aumann and Schelling shared the 2005 prize "for having enhanced our understanding of conflict and cooperation through game-theory analysis" [31]. Shapley and Roth shared the 2012 prize "for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design" [32].

4. Timing: simultaneous and sequential

4.1 Definitions

Simultaneous-move game. Each player chooses without observing the choices of the others. "Simultaneous" is a statement about information, not about clock time: a sealed-bid auction is simultaneous even if bids are submitted days apart [3,50].

Sequential-move game. Players move in an order, and at least some players observe at least some earlier moves before choosing. Such games are naturally represented as trees in extensive form [5,20].

The distinction is visible in the extensive form. A simultaneous game can be drawn as a tree in which the second mover's decision nodes are joined in a single information set, so that the second mover cannot tell which branch it is on (Figure 2a). Removing the information set turns the same tree into a sequential game (Figure 2b) [3,20].

4.2 Consequences

Sequential games call for refinements of Nash equilibrium. In a tree, a Nash equilibrium can rest on a threat that the threatening player would not actually carry out once the moment came. Selten's subgame-perfect equilibrium excludes such threats by requiring equilibrium behaviour in every subgame [39]. In finite games of perfect information, subgame-perfect equilibria are found by backward induction, and at least one always exists in pure strategies [3,20].

The order of moves also changes outcomes in economic models. In Cournot's model two firms choose quantities simultaneously. In Stackelberg's model a leader commits to a quantity first, and the follower responds. With linear demand the leader produces more and earns more than in the Cournot equilibrium, and the follower produces less (Figure 9 in Section 11) [5,11,16]. Moving first is not always an advantage, however. In Matching Pennies the player who moves second and observes the first move wins every time [50].

5. Cooperation: cooperative and non-cooperative

5.1 What the distinction is

Non-cooperative game. A model whose primitives are the actions available to individual players. Any agreement among players must be self-enforcing, which means each player must find it in its own interest to comply [3,7].

Cooperative (coalitional) game. A model whose primitives are the outcomes that groups of players can achieve together. It assumes that agreements can be enforced, for instance by contract, and asks which agreements will form and how the gains will be divided [1,3,37].

The names can mislead. A non-cooperative game may well predict cooperation, as the repeated games of Section 8 show, and a cooperative game may predict that some players are left with nothing. The difference lies in the modelling unit and in whether binding agreements are possible, not in the behaviour that results [3,37]. Nash's own definition of the non-cooperative case also ruled out communication, a point taken up in Section 12 [7].

5.2 Coalitional games and their solutions

A coalitional game with transferable utility is a pair (N, v), where N is the set of players and the characteristic function v assigns to each coalition S ⊆ N the total payoff v(S) its members can secure and share among themselves, with v(∅) = 0 [1,6]. When payoffs cannot be freely transferred, the game has non-transferable utility and v(S) becomes a set of attainable payoff vectors [53].

Two solution concepts dominate. The core is the set of allocations that distribute v(N) so that no coalition could do better on its own, that is, Σi∈S xi ≥ v(S) for every S [23]. The core can be empty. Bondareva and Shapley independently characterised the games with non-empty cores as the "balanced" games [54,55]. The Shapley value gives each player its average marginal contribution over all orders in which the grand coalition could be assembled [21]:

φi(v) = ΣS ⊆ N∖{i} [ |S|! (n − |S| − 1)! / n! ] · ( v(S ∪ {i}) − v(S) ).

Example: the glove game. Player 1 owns a left glove and players 2 and 3 each own a right glove. A matched pair is worth 1, and a single glove is worth nothing [6]. Figure 3 shows the characteristic function. The core contains a single allocation, (1, 0, 0). If player 1 offered player 2 any positive amount, player 3 would be willing to accept less, so competition between the two right-glove owners drives their share to zero. The Shapley value is (2/3, 1/6, 1/6). Player 1 is pivotal in four of the six orderings and each right-glove owner in one. The two concepts encode different ideas of fairness and stability.

5.3 Bridging the two approaches

Nash proposed that cooperative solutions should be justified by non-cooperative models of the negotiation that produces them [18]. Rubinstein's alternating-offers model of 1982 is the best-known success of this program. Two players alternate proposals for dividing a surplus, and delay is costly. The game has a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium, in which agreement is immediate [35]. Binmore, Rubinstein and Wolinsky showed that as the time between offers shrinks, this outcome converges to the Nash bargaining solution [19].

6. Payoffs: zero-sum, constant-sum and non-zero-sum

6.1 Definitions

Zero-sum. The players' payoffs sum to zero at every outcome: Σi ui(s) = 0 for every strategy profile s.
Constant-sum. The payoffs sum to the same constant c at every outcome.
Non-zero-sum (general-sum). The sum varies across outcomes, so that some outcomes are better or worse for everyone together [1,40].

A constant-sum game is strategically identical to a zero-sum game. Von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities are unique only up to positive affine transformations, so subtracting c from one player's payoffs changes no player's preferences over strategies [1,40]. Zero-sum and constant-sum games are therefore usually treated as one class, the games of pure conflict. Conversely, whether a game is zero-sum depends on the utility scale chosen. Moulin and Vial characterised the "strategically zero-sum" games, which have the same best responses as some zero-sum game even though their payoffs do not sum to a constant [56].

6.2 Consequences

Two-person zero-sum games have the most complete theory in the subject. Von Neumann's minimax theorem states that every finite two-person zero-sum game has a value V, and that each player has a mixed strategy that guarantees it, so that

maxσ₁ minσ₂ u₁(σ₁, σ₂) = minσ₂ maxσ₁ u₁(σ₁, σ₂) = V [8].

Equilibrium strategies are interchangeable, all equilibria give the same payoff, and an equilibrium can be computed in polynomial time by linear programming [1,57]. None of this survives in non-zero-sum games. Equilibria can be multiple, as in Battle of the Sexes, and can give different payoffs. They can also be inefficient: in the Prisoner's Dilemma the unique equilibrium (1, 1) is worse for both players than (3, 3) [5,40]. Finding a Nash equilibrium of a general two-player game is PPAD-complete, so no polynomial-time algorithm is expected [58,59].

The payoff structure also shapes the other dimensions. In a two-person zero-sum game there are no joint gains to share, so binding agreements and communication have nothing to offer either player [1,40]. Cooperation and communication matter only in non-zero-sum games, which Schelling called games of "mixed motive" [24].

7. Information: perfect, imperfect, complete and incomplete

Information has two separate dimensions, and confusing them is one of the most common errors in the subject. One dimension concerns what players observe during play: earlier moves and the current state. The other concerns what players know about the game itself: the rules, the other players' payoffs, and their characteristics [5,10,25].

Perfect information. Every player, whenever it moves, observes all previous actions and the relevant state of the game. In extensive form, every information set contains exactly one node [1,20].

Imperfect information. Some player, at some point, does not observe some earlier action or the current state. In extensive form, some information set contains more than one node [20].

Complete information. The structure of the game, including the players, their available actions and their payoff functions, is known to all players. The standard formulation strengthens this to common knowledge: everyone knows the structure, knows that everyone knows it, and so on [25,60,61].

Incomplete information. Some player does not know some characteristic of another player, such as its preferences, costs, capabilities or beliefs. Following Harsanyi, each player's private characteristics are summarised by its type [25].

The four categories form a two-by-two grid, and every combination occurs (Figure 5).

7.1 Perfect and imperfect information

Von Neumann and Morgenstern singled out games in which "perfect information" prevails [1], and Kuhn's information sets gave the general framework [20]. Perfect information has strong consequences. Every finite game of perfect information has a subgame-perfect equilibrium in pure strategies, found by backward induction [20]. In particular, chess is determined: either White can force a win, or Black can, or both can force at least a draw. This conclusion goes back to Zermelo, although historians note that his argument was not backward induction in the modern sense [13,14]. Knowing that chess is determined does not reveal which case holds, because the game tree is far too large to search. Strong programs for perfect-information games such as Go therefore combine tree search with learned evaluation [62].

Imperfect information arises from simultaneous moves, from hidden actions and from hidden moves of chance. In poker, the cards dealt to an opponent are a move by Nature that the player does not observe. Kuhn's simplified three-card poker was an early model, and its equilibrium already requires bluffing with positive probability [63]. Heads-up no-limit Texas hold'em was played at a superhuman level in 2017 by Libratus, which relied on algorithms built for imperfect-information games [64]. A related property is perfect recall: no player ever forgets what it knew or did earlier. Under perfect recall, Kuhn's theorem guarantees that mixed and behaviour strategies are equivalent [20].

7.2 Complete and incomplete information

Before Harsanyi, a game in which players were unsure of one another's payoffs seemed to require an infinite regress: beliefs about the other's payoffs, beliefs about the other's beliefs, and so on [25]. Harsanyi's solution was to treat each player's private characteristics as a type drawn by Nature from a common prior distribution. Each player observes its own type but not the others' [25]. The result is a Bayesian game, in which a strategy is a map from types to actions and equilibrium requires each type to best-respond given its beliefs about the other types [5,25].

The Harsanyi transformation converts incomplete information into imperfect information. A new initial move by Nature selects the types, and each player's information sets are drawn so that it sees its own type but not the others' (Figure 6). The resulting game has complete but imperfect information, so the existing tools for imperfect information apply [2,25]. This is why the distinction between incomplete and imperfect is sometimes described as a matter of interpretation rather than mathematics. Incomplete information describes a player's uncertainty about the game, and imperfect information describes its uncertainty within the game [5,10].

Terminology. Some older and popular sources use "complete information" to mean what is defined here as perfect information, or treat poker as a game of incomplete information. In the modern technical usage, poker is a game of complete but imperfect information: the rules and payoffs are common knowledge, and what is hidden is the outcome of a chance move [5,10,63]. Rasmusen also distinguishes certain games, in which Nature does not move after any player, and symmetric information, in which no player has information others lack [10].

8. Duration: one-shot and repeated

8.1 Definitions

One-shot game. The game is played once, and players have no future interaction to consider.
Finitely repeated game. A fixed stage game is played T times, with T known to all, and each round's actions are observed before the next.
Infinitely or indefinitely repeated game. The stage game is repeated without a known final round. Future payoffs are discounted by a factor δ ∈ (0, 1), which can be read either as time preference or as the probability that the game continues for another round [38,50].

The interpretation of δ as a continuation probability is what makes the infinite model applicable to real relationships, which always end at some point. What matters is that no player knows that a given round is the last. A game that ends with probability 1 − δ after each round is "indefinitely" repeated, and its expected payoff Σt δt ut is formally identical to that of a discounted infinite game [38,71].

8.2 The finite-horizon problem

If the Prisoner's Dilemma is repeated a known, finite number of times, defection in the last round is dominant. Given that, defection in the second-to-last round is also optimal, and so on back to the first round. Luce and Raiffa discussed this unravelling argument in 1957 [40]. More generally, if the stage game has a unique Nash equilibrium, every subgame-perfect equilibrium of the finitely repeated game plays it in every round. Benoit and Krishna showed that cooperation can be sustained when the stage game has several equilibria, which can be used as rewards and punishments [72]. Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts and Wilson showed that cooperation can also emerge if there is a small probability that one player is a type committed to tit-for-tat, a result that combines finite duration with incomplete information [73]. Selten's chain-store paradox makes the same point for entry deterrence [74].

8.3 The infinite horizon and the folk theorem

Without a known last round, the threat of future punishment can sustain cooperation. Under the grim trigger strategy a player cooperates until anyone defects and then defects forever. Cooperating forever yields R/(1 − δ). Deviating yields T once and then P forever, a total of T + δP/(1 − δ). Cooperation is therefore an equilibrium when

δ ≥ (T − R) / (T − P),

which equals ½ with Axelrod's payoffs [26,38]. The folk theorem generalises this. For sufficiently patient players, any feasible payoff vector that gives each player strictly more than its minmax payoff can be sustained by a subgame-perfect equilibrium [27,28]. It is called a folk theorem because versions of it circulated among game theorists before anyone published them [27,28]. Aumann's contribution to this theory was cited in his 2005 prize [31].

8.4 Evidence

Axelrod's computer tournaments of the repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, in which tit-for-tat performed best, brought the theory to a wide audience [48]. Laboratory experiments support the distinction between finite and indefinite repetition. Dal Bó found that cooperation increased with the probability of continuation, and that it was higher in indefinitely repeated games than in finitely repeated games of the same expected length [71].

9. Outcomes: deterministic and stochastic

Deterministic game. The outcome is fully determined by the players' actions; there are no moves by chance.
Stochastic game (in the broad sense). Chance influences the outcome, through moves by Nature, random payoffs or random transitions between states [10,20].

Chess and Go are deterministic; backgammon, with its dice, and poker, with its shuffled deck, are not [13,63,75]. In extensive form, chance is represented by a fictitious player, Nature, who chooses among branches with fixed, commonly known probabilities [20]. Players evaluate the resulting lotteries by expected utility, which von Neumann and Morgenstern justified axiomatically [1]. This randomness comes from the environment, and it should be distinguished from mixed strategies, in which the players themselves randomise [3]. TD-Gammon, a backgammon program trained by temporal-difference learning, showed in the early 1990s that self-play learning could reach expert level in a game with chance moves [75].

The term stochastic game also has a narrower, technical meaning, due to Shapley [22]. A stochastic game has a set of states. In each state the players play a normal-form game, and their joint action determines both their current payoffs and a probability distribution over the next state. Shapley proved that two-person zero-sum stochastic games with discounting have a value and that the players have optimal stationary strategies [22]. Fink extended existence of stationary equilibria to n-person discounted games [76], and Mertens and Neyman proved that undiscounted zero-sum stochastic games have a value [77]. Stochastic games include repeated games (one state) and Markov decision processes (one player) as special cases. They are the framework for multi-agent reinforcement learning, where they are called Markov games [78], and for Markov-perfect models of dynamic oligopoly [47,79].

10. Players: two-player and multiplayer

A game with one player is a decision problem, so game theory begins with two. Von Neumann and Morgenstern organised their book around the number of players. They found that the two-person zero-sum case has a complete solution, while with three or more players coalitions become central even in zero-sum games, because two players can gang up on the third [1].

Several properties change as the number of players grows.

  • Coalitions. With three or more players, subsets of players can coordinate against the rest. This is why cooperative game theory is essentially a theory of multiplayer games. In the three-player majority game, any two players can split a prize of 1, so v(S) = 1 whenever |S| ≥ 2, and the core is empty: any division leaves some pair able to do better together [1,6].
  • Computation. Two-player zero-sum games are solvable by linear programming [57]. General two-player games are PPAD-complete [59]. With three or more players, equilibria can involve irrational probabilities, and exact computation is complete for the harder class FIXP [80].
  • Size. A normal-form game with n players and m strategies each requires n·mn payoff numbers, which grows exponentially with the number of players. Compact representations such as congestion and graphical games exploit structure to avoid this [9,81].
  • Large games. When players are many, it is often simpler to model a continuum of them, so that each individual is negligible. Aumann modelled markets with a continuum of traders [41], Schmeidler proved existence of equilibrium in such "nonatomic" games [82], and mean-field games extend the idea to dynamic settings [42]. Evolutionary game theory studies large populations in which strategies spread by success rather than by deliberate choice [4].

Nash's existence theorem was stated for n-person games from the start, and this generality was one of its main advances over the minimax theorem [7,33].

11. Strategy space: finite, infinite, discrete and continuous

11.1 Definitions

Finite game. The number of players and each player's set of pure strategies are finite.
Infinite game. Some player has infinitely many pure strategies. The set may be discrete (countable and without continuity, such as the positive integers) or continuous (a continuum such as an interval of prices or quantities) [3,5].

The two pairs of terms are not independent. A finite set is always discrete, so the meaningful types are finite, countably infinite and continuous. Even in a finite game, the set of mixed strategies is a continuous simplex, and this is what makes Nash's fixed-point proof possible [7,33]. Repeated games and infinite-horizon bargaining games have infinite strategy sets even when every stage game is finite, because a strategy must specify a response to every possible history [35,38].

11.2 Existence of equilibrium

Nash proved that every finite game has an equilibrium in mixed strategies [7,33]. For infinite games existence needs extra conditions. Glicksberg showed that a mixed-strategy equilibrium exists when strategy sets are compact and payoffs are continuous [43]. Debreu, Fan and Glicksberg independently showed that a pure-strategy equilibrium exists when, in addition, strategy sets are convex and each player's payoff is quasi-concave in its own strategy [43,44,83]. Sion extended the minimax theorem to infinite zero-sum games of this kind [84]. Without compactness, existence can fail. If two players each name a positive integer and the larger number wins, every strategy can be beaten by naming a larger number, so the game has no equilibrium [5]. Many economic games have discontinuous payoffs, such as auctions and price competition, in which a tiny change in a bid or price can flip the winner. Dasgupta and Maskin and later Reny gave existence results for such games [85,86].

11.3 Example: Cournot, Bertrand and Stackelberg

In Cournot's model two firms choose quantities q1, q2 ≥ 0 from a continuum. With inverse demand p = a − q1 − q2 and zero cost, firm i's best response is qi = (a − qj)/2. The equilibrium is where the two reaction curves cross, at q1 = q2 = a/3 [5,11]. If firm 1 moves first, as in Stackelberg's model, it chooses the point on firm 2's reaction curve that maximises its own profit, which is q1 = a/2 and q2 = a/4 [5,16]. If the firms instead choose prices, as Bertrand proposed, the firm with the lower price takes the whole market. With identical costs, the unique equilibrium then has both firms pricing at marginal cost, the "Bertrand paradox" [5,12].

12. Communication

Non-communicative game. Players cannot exchange messages, other than through their moves in the game. Nash's original definition of a non-cooperative game assumed this [7].
Communicative game. Players can send messages before or during play. Messages that are costless, non-binding and unverifiable are called cheap talk [29,87].

Communication should be kept separate from cooperation. Cheap talk lets players coordinate their expectations, but it does not make promises binding, so a game with cheap talk is still non-cooperative [37,87]. It also differs from signalling, in which an informed player conveys information through a costly action, as in Spence's job-market model, where education is credible because it is costly [69].

12.1 When talk matters

Whether talk can change the outcome depends on how far the players' interests are aligned.

  • Pure conflict. In two-person zero-sum games, any information a player volunteers can only help its opponent, so cheap talk has no effect on the value [1,87].
  • Coordination. When interests coincide, talk can select among equilibria. Farrell showed how pre-play messages can coordinate entry decisions [88]. Even without talk, Schelling argued that players often coordinate on focal points made salient by shared culture or context [24].
  • Partial conflict. Talk may or may not be believed. In the Stag Hunt, Aumann argued that a promise to hunt stag is not self-enforcing, because a player who intends to hunt hare also wants the other to hunt stag and so would make the same promise [51,52].
  • Private information. In Crawford and Sobel's model an informed sender advises an uninformed receiver whose preferred action differs by a bias. All equilibria are partitional, meaning the sender reveals only which interval the state lies in. The closer the preferences, the finer the partition and the more information is transmitted [29].

12.2 Mediated communication and evidence

Communication can also pass through a mediator. Aumann's correlated equilibrium allows a device to send each player a private recommendation, and it is an equilibrium if every player wants to follow its recommendation. The set of correlated equilibria contains the convex hull of the Nash equilibria and can give higher payoffs [34]. Forges and Myerson extended this idea to communication equilibria in games with private information and in multistage games [45,89].

Experiments show that communication matters in practice. Cooper, DeJong, Forsythe and Ross found that pre-play cheap talk changed how often players coordinated in coordination games, with effects that depended on whether one or both players could send messages [90]. Sally's meta-analysis of social-dilemma experiments run between 1958 and 1992 found that allowing discussion substantially raised cooperation rates [91].

13. Participation: fixed players, entry and exit

Fixed player set. The set of players N is given and known to all, which is the standard assumption of both normal and extensive forms [3].
Entry and exit. Whether, when and how many players take part is itself part of the game. Potential players decide whether to enter, existing players may leave, and the number of participants may be random or unknown [36,46,47].

Variable participation arises in several ways.

  • Entry deterrence. An incumbent's actions can discourage potential entrants. Dixit showed how investment in capacity can serve as a credible commitment that deters entry [46]. Selten's chain-store game asks whether an incumbent facing a sequence of entrants should fight early entrants to discourage later ones [74]. Kreps and Wilson and Milgrom and Roberts showed that predation can be rational if entrants are unsure whether the incumbent is "tough", which links participation to incomplete information [65,66].
  • Endogenous entry in auctions. When bidding is costly, the number of bidders is determined in equilibrium. Levin and Smith analysed auctions in which potential bidders randomise over whether to enter [92].
  • Industry dynamics. Hopenhayn modelled long-run competitive equilibrium with firm entry and exit [93], and Ericson and Pakes built a Markov-perfect framework for oligopolies whose firms enter, invest and exit over time [47].
  • Overlapping generations. Kandori showed that cooperation can be sustained in repeated games when each player lives only a finite time but the game continues as new players replace old ones [94].
  • Population uncertainty. In elections and large markets, players may not know how many others will take part. Myerson's Poisson games model the number of players of each type as a Poisson random variable [36].

The fixed-player assumption is partly a modelling convenience. A game with entry can be rewritten as a game with a fixed, larger set of potential players, each with an option to stay out. That rewriting is what entry-deterrence models do [46,74]. It breaks down when the number of potential players is itself uncertain or very large, which is the case Poisson games address [36].

14. How the dimensions interact

The ten dimensions are logically related in several ways. Some combinations cannot occur, some imply others, and some can be converted into one another by remodelling.

  • Perfect information implies sequential moves. A game with simultaneous moves has, by definition, an information set with more than one node, so it has imperfect information [3,20].
  • Incomplete information can be rewritten as imperfect, stochastic information. The Harsanyi transformation adds a chance move, so a game of incomplete information becomes one with imperfect information and a move by Nature [25].
  • Repetition changes the strategy space. Repeating a finite stage game infinitely often gives an infinite strategy space, and the folk theorem shows that this enlarges the set of equilibrium outcomes dramatically [28,38].
  • Zero-sum games make cooperation and communication irrelevant for two players, since there are no mutual gains [1].
  • Finite perfect-information games have pure equilibria, while finite games in general need mixed strategies [7,20].
  • Communication can substitute for a mediator, and repetition for enforcement. Correlated equilibria can be reached through communication [34,45], and repeated play can sustain agreements that would need a contract in a one-shot game [26,37].

15. Other classifications

Several further distinctions are common in the literature, though they are less often listed as primary dimensions.

  • Symmetric and asymmetric games. A game is symmetric if all players have the same strategy sets and their payoffs do not depend on who is who. Nash proved that every finite symmetric game has a symmetric equilibrium [7].
  • Potential and congestion games. In a potential game, every player's incentive to change strategy is captured by one global function. Such games always have pure equilibria and best-response dynamics converge [95]. Congestion games, in which players choose resources whose costs depend on how many use them, are the leading example [81].
  • Combinatorial games. Two-player games of perfect information with no chance and a win/loss outcome, such as Nim and Go endgames, are studied with algebraic methods [96].
  • Differential games. Continuous-time games in which the state evolves according to differential equations, such as pursuit–evasion problems [97].
  • Evolutionary games. Strategies are inherited behaviour, and payoffs measure reproductive fitness [4].

16. Summary

Classifying a game is a way of asking a fixed list of modelling questions. Who moves when, and what does each mover see? Can agreements be enforced, and can players talk? Are interests opposed, aligned or mixed? Does the game repeat, and does it involve chance? How many players are there, how many choices do they have, and can players come and go? Each answer selects a representation, a family of solution concepts and a set of theorems. The minimax theorem applies to two-person zero-sum games, backward induction to finite games of perfect information, Bayesian equilibrium to games of incomplete information, folk theorems to indefinitely repeated games, and the core and Shapley value to cooperative games. The four information categories deserve particular care. Perfect and imperfect information concern what players observe during play, while complete and incomplete information concern what they know about the game and about each other, and Harsanyi's transformation connects the two.
