Game Theory in Computer Science and AI

Adversarial search, learning in games, multi-agent reinforcement learning, mechanism design, security games, distributed protocols, and the algorithmic study of strategic outcomes

Technical article · References in the Vancouver style · Companion to Representations of Games

Abstract. Computer systems increasingly consist of many autonomous programs, each acting for a different owner with its own objectives. Game theory supplies the mathematics for reasoning about such systems, and computer science in turn has changed game theory by asking how strategic outcomes can be computed, learned and designed. This article surveys the ten areas where the two fields meet most directly for software engineers and AI researchers. They are multi-agent systems, adversarial search, learning in games, multi-agent reinforcement learning, algorithmic game theory, the price of anarchy, mechanism design and auctions, security games, distributed systems, and automated negotiation. For each area it gives the history, the central definitions, a worked example with a diagram, and the landmark systems. It closes with a comparison table and a list of open problems.

1. Why game theory matters for computing

Game theory is the mathematical study of interactive decision-making, in which each participant's outcome depends on the choices of others as well as on its own [1,2]. Classical computer science assumed that the components of a system follow their specification. The Internet broke that assumption. Its routers, servers, advertisers and users belong to different parties who act in their own interest, and Papadimitriou argued in 2001 that game theory was therefore the natural framework for studying it [3]. Artificial intelligence reached the same point from another direction. An agent that plays chess, negotiates a price or patrols an airport faces opponents and partners who are themselves reasoning about it [4,5].

The exchange runs in both directions. Game theory gives computer science models of strategic behaviour and solution concepts such as Nash equilibrium. Computer science asks questions that classical game theory set aside. Can an equilibrium be computed in reasonable time? Can agents learn one by playing? How much efficiency is lost when no one is in charge? Can a protocol be designed so that self-interested participants behave well? [6,7] The answers have produced a distinct field, usually called algorithmic game theory, and a set of deployed systems ranging from superhuman game-playing programs to airport patrol schedules and online advertising auctions [6,8,9].

This article assumes the basic representations of games: payoff matrices, game trees and Bayesian games. They are covered in the companion article Representations of Games.

2. Historical development

Game theory and computing grew up together. John von Neumann proved the minimax theorem for two-person zero-sum games in 1928 [10]. With Oskar Morgenstern he wrote Theory of Games and Economic Behavior in 1944 [1], and in the same decade he helped design the stored-program computer. John Nash proved in 1950 and 1951 that every finite game has an equilibrium in mixed strategies [11,12].

Game playing was one of the first tasks proposed for computers. In 1950 Claude Shannon described how a computer could play chess by searching a tree of moves and evaluating positions with the minimax rule [13]. Alan Turing's hand-simulated chess program was published in 1953 [14], and Arthur Samuel's checkers program, which improved by playing against itself, was described in 1959 [15]. The same years saw the first learning algorithms for games. George Brown proposed fictitious play in 1951, and Julia Robinson proved in the same year that it converges in two-person zero-sum games [16,17]. James Hannan's 1957 paper and David Blackwell's 1956 approachability theorem laid the foundations of what is now called no-regret learning [18,19]. Lloyd Shapley's stochastic games of 1953 later became the standard model for multi-agent reinforcement learning [20,21].

From the 1980s, distributed artificial intelligence studied how separately designed agents could cooperate and negotiate, and it borrowed bargaining theory and mechanism design for the purpose [22–24]. In the late 1990s the theoretical computer science community began to study the Internet in game-theoretic terms. Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou's paper "Worst-case equilibria" (1999) began the study of what became known as the price of anarchy, and Nisan and Ronen's paper (presented in 1999) founded algorithmic mechanism design [25,26]. These papers, together with Roughgarden and Tardos's analysis of selfish routing, received the 2012 Gödel Prize [27,28]. In 2006 to 2009, computing a Nash equilibrium was shown to be complete for the complexity class PPAD, even for two players [29,30].

In AI, game-playing programs reached and then passed human strength. IBM's Deep Blue defeated the world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a match in 1997 [31]. Checkers was weakly solved in 2007 [32]. DeepMind's AlphaGo defeated the professional Lee Sedol at Go in 2016 [33,34]. Programs based on regret minimisation essentially solved heads-up limit Texas hold'em poker in 2015 and beat top professionals at no-limit variants in 2017 and 2019 [35–37]. Game-theoretic schedulers have been in operational use for security since 2007, starting at Los Angeles International Airport [8,38].

3. Core concepts in brief

A game in normal form lists the players N = {1, …, n}, each player's strategy set Si, and payoff functions ui on strategy profiles [52]. A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure strategies. The solution concepts used most in computing are the following.

  • Nash equilibrium. A profile in which no player can gain by changing its own strategy alone. Every finite game has one in mixed strategies [12].
  • Minimax. In a two-person zero-sum game, each player can guarantee the game's value by maximising its worst-case payoff, and the minimax strategies are exactly the equilibrium strategies [1,10].
  • Correlated equilibrium. A distribution over strategy profiles, from which a mediator draws a profile and privately recommends each player its part. It is an equilibrium if no player gains by disobeying [53]. The coarse correlated equilibrium weakens this: no player gains by ignoring the recommendation altogether and committing in advance to a fixed strategy [6,54].
  • Stackelberg equilibrium. A leader commits to a strategy first; a follower observes it and best-responds. The leader chooses the commitment that is best for it given that response [55,56].
  • Dominant-strategy incentive compatibility. In a mechanism, telling the truth about one's private information is optimal whatever the others do [6,57].

Computer science adds three questions to each concept. Is it guaranteed to exist? Can it be computed efficiently? Can players reach it through a simple, decentralised learning process? [6,54] The sections below keep returning to these questions.

4. Multi-agent systems

A multi-agent system consists of several autonomous agents that act in a shared environment, each pursuing its own goals or those of the party it represents [5,58]. Wooldridge defines an agent as a computer system that can act independently on behalf of its user or owner. A multi-agent system is a set of such agents that must cooperate, coordinate and negotiate because their actions affect one another [58]. Game theory supplies the standard way of modelling what the agents want and what they will do. Each agent is treated as maximising its expected utility given its beliefs about the others [5,59].

4.1 Cooperative, competitive and mixed settings

Multi-agent settings are usually classified by how the agents' objectives relate [60,61]:

  • Fully cooperative. All agents share one reward, as in a team of warehouse robots. The game-theoretic difficulty is coordination: several equilibria may be optimal, and the agents must pick the same one [62].
  • Fully competitive. One agent's gain is another's loss. Two-player zero-sum games are the clean case, and minimax reasoning applies [10,21].
  • Mixed or general-sum. Interests are partly aligned and partly opposed. Social dilemmas such as the Prisoner's Dilemma belong here. Leibo and colleagues extended them to sequential social dilemmas, Markov games in which cooperative and defecting behaviour are policies rather than single actions [63].

Axelrod's computer tournaments of iterated Prisoner's Dilemma strategies were an early meeting of game theory and computation. They found that the simple reciprocating strategy Tit for Tat did well against a wide field of programs [64]. Research on cooperative AI now asks how to build agents that find mutually beneficial outcomes with humans and with other agents [65].

4.2 Coalitions and the Shapley value

Cooperative game theory models what groups of agents can achieve together. A characteristic function v(C) gives the value that coalition C can secure on its own [1]. The Shapley value divides the total value by giving each player its average marginal contribution over all orders in which the coalition could form [40]:

φi(v) = ΣC ⊆ N∖{i} |C|! (n − |C| − 1)! ⁄ n! · [ v(C ∪ {i}) − v(C) ].

Two computing applications stand out. Coalition structure generation partitions agents into groups that maximise total value, which is computationally hard in general; Sandholm and colleagues gave anytime algorithms with worst-case guarantees [66]. In machine learning, the SHAP method explains a model's prediction by treating input features as players and computing approximate Shapley values [67].

5. Adversarial search

Two-player, zero-sum, perfect-information games such as chess, checkers and Go are the classical domain of adversarial search. Such a game has a value that each player can guarantee with a pure strategy [4,68]. In principle a program could find the value by searching the entire game tree. In practice the tree is far too large. Shannon estimated about 10120 possible chess games, and proposed searching to a limited depth and scoring the frontier positions with an evaluation function [13].

5.1 Minimax

The minimax value of a node is defined recursively. At a leaf it is the evaluation (or the true game result). At a node where MAX moves it is the maximum of the children's values. At a node where MIN moves it is the minimum [4]. Searching to depth d in a tree with branching factor b takes O(bd) time.

5.2 Alpha-beta pruning

Alpha-beta pruning computes the same value while skipping branches that cannot affect it. The search carries two bounds: α, the best value MAX can already guarantee on the current path, and β, the best value MIN can guarantee. Once a node's value is known to fall outside (α, β), its remaining children need not be examined [4,42]. Knuth and Moore traced the idea to several researchers of the late 1950s and early 1960s. They proved its correctness and showed that with perfect move ordering it examines about b⌈d/2⌉ + b⌊d/2⌋ − 1 leaves. That is roughly the square root of the minimax cost, so the program can search about twice as deep in the same time [42]. Pearl later proved that alpha-beta is asymptotically optimal among game-searching algorithms on random trees [69].

5.3 From Deep Blue to AlphaZero

Deep Blue combined alpha-beta search on custom hardware with a hand-tuned evaluation function, and it defeated Kasparov in 1997 [31]. Schaeffer's team weakly solved checkers in 2007. They proved, by combining retrograde endgame databases with deep forward search, that perfect play by both sides leads to a draw [32].

Go resisted alpha-beta methods because of its large branching factor and the difficulty of writing an evaluation function. Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) estimates the values of positions by simulating random games and grows the tree selectively [48]. The UCT algorithm treats the choice of which branch to explore as a multi-armed bandit problem. It uses an upper confidence bound to balance exploring new moves against exploiting good ones, and it converges to the minimax move as the number of simulations grows [47]. AlphaGo combined MCTS with deep neural networks for move selection and position evaluation, and it beat the European champion Fan Hui 5–0 in 2015 [33] and Lee Sedol in 2016 [34]. AlphaZero dropped human game records and learned chess, shogi and Go entirely through self-play [70].

5.4 Imperfect information and chance

Backgammon adds dice. The expectiminimax rule handles chance by averaging over chance nodes [4]. Card games add hidden information, which breaks plain tree search, because the value of a position depends on beliefs about the opponent's cards and on the opponent's strategy. These games are modelled as extensive-form games with information sets [68]. Koller, Megiddo and von Stengel showed that the sequence form lets a two-player zero-sum extensive game be solved by a linear program whose size is linear in the game tree, rather than exponential [71]. Poker has become the main test bed for these methods (section 6.4).

6. Learning in games

Equilibrium concepts say where play ends up but not how players get there. Learning in games studies simple adaptive rules that players can follow while playing repeatedly, and asks what the rules converge to [72,73]. The question matters to computing in two ways. It explains how agents without a central planner might reach an equilibrium, and it gives practical algorithms for computing equilibria of very large games.

6.1 Fictitious play

In fictitious play, each player assumes that opponents are playing the empirical frequency of their past actions, and best-responds to that mixture [16]. Robinson proved that in two-person zero-sum games the empirical frequencies converge to equilibrium strategies [17]. Convergence fails in general: Shapley gave a 3 × 3 general-sum game in which fictitious play cycles forever [74]. Heinrich, Lanctot and Silver extended the idea to large extensive-form games as fictitious self-play, with best responses and average strategies approximated by machine learning [75].

6.2 Regret and no-regret learning

Suppose a player chooses mixed strategies p1, …, pT over T rounds against an arbitrary, even adversarial, sequence of opponent actions. Its external regret is the gap between what it would have earned by playing the best single fixed action in hindsight and what it actually earned [73]:

RT = maxa ∈ A Σt=1T u(a, yt) − Σt=1T u(pt, yt).

An algorithm is no-regret (or Hannan-consistent) if RT/T → 0. Hannan constructed the first such procedure in 1957 [18]. The multiplicative-weights family is the standard modern one. It includes Littlestone and Warmuth's weighted majority algorithm and Freund and Schapire's Hedge [76,77]. Hedge keeps a weight for each action and multiplies it by exp(η · payoff) after every round. With a suitable step size η, its regret against n actions is O(√(T log n)) [77,78].

No-regret learning connects to equilibrium through three results:

  • In a two-player zero-sum game, if both players use no-regret algorithms, their time-averaged strategies converge to minimax strategies. This gives a learning-based proof of the minimax theorem [73,77].
  • In any finite game, if every player has vanishing external regret, the empirical distribution of joint play converges to the set of coarse correlated equilibria [6,54].
  • If every player has vanishing internal (or swap) regret, play converges to the set of correlated equilibria. Hart and Mas-Colell's regret matching achieves this. Each player plays actions with probability proportional to their positive accumulated regret [45].

6.3 Worked example: regret matching in Rock–Paper–Scissors

In Rock–Paper–Scissors a win pays 1, a loss −1 and a tie 0. The unique equilibrium is for each player to mix uniformly. Suppose player 1 played Rock and player 2 played Paper in round 1. Player 1 earned −1. Had it played Paper it would have earned 0, and Scissors would have earned 1. Its regrets are therefore 0 for Rock, 1 for Paper and 2 for Scissors. Regret matching plays each action with probability proportional to its positive regret, so in round 2 player 1 plays Paper with probability 1/3 and Scissors with probability 2/3. Repeating the update after each round, the time-averaged strategies of both players converge to the uniform equilibrium, as the zero-sum result above guarantees [45,81].

6.4 Counterfactual regret minimisation and poker

Zinkevich and colleagues introduced counterfactual regret minimisation (CFR) in 2007. It decomposes the overall regret in an extensive-form game into separate regrets at each information set, weighted by the probability of reaching that set, and minimises each locally with regret matching [49]. In two-player zero-sum games the average strategy converges to a Nash equilibrium. Its memory use is proportional to the number of information sets, not to the number of strategies, which is far smaller [49]. A variant, CFR+, was used to compute a strategy for heads-up limit Texas hold'em so close to equilibrium that a lifetime of play could not distinguish it from optimal with statistical confidence. The game was reported as essentially weakly solved in 2015 [35,82]. DeepStack and Libratus extended these methods with real-time re-solving of subgames and beat professional players at heads-up no-limit hold'em [36,83]. Pluribus beat professionals at six-player no-limit hold'em in 2019. It is a multiplayer game, in which equilibrium no longer guarantees good results, but the methods still worked well in practice [37].

6.5 Learning dynamics in machine learning

Game-theoretic learning now appears inside mainstream machine learning. A generative adversarial network trains a generator and a discriminator against each other as a two-player minimax game [84]. Plain simultaneous gradient descent can cycle in such games instead of converging. Daskalakis and colleagues showed that optimistic variants of gradient descent, drawn from the no-regret literature, converge in bilinear zero-sum games and improve GAN training [85].

7. Multi-agent reinforcement learning

Reinforcement learning (RL) trains an agent to maximise cumulative reward in a Markov decision process. When several learning agents share an environment, the right model is a stochastic game, also called a Markov game. It is defined by a set of states, an action set for each agent, a transition function that depends on the joint action, and a reward function for each agent [20,21]. A Markov decision process is the one-agent case, and a repeated normal-form game is the one-state case.

7.1 Game-theoretic MARL algorithms

Littman's minimax-Q (1994) adapted Q-learning to two-player zero-sum Markov games. At each state it solves a small matrix game by linear programming in place of the usual maximum over actions [21]. Hu and Wellman's Nash-Q extended the approach to general-sum games by computing a Nash equilibrium of the stage game at each update. Its convergence guarantees hold only under restrictive conditions on the stage games encountered [86]. Bowling and Veloso's WoLF ("win or learn fast") principle varies the learning rate according to whether the agent is doing better or worse than its equilibrium payoff, which helps simultaneous learners converge [87].

7.2 Core difficulties

  • Non-stationarity. Each agent's environment includes the other learners, so the dynamics it sees keep changing and single-agent convergence guarantees do not carry over [61,88].
  • Equilibrium selection and coordination. Claus and Boutilier showed that even in cooperative games, independent learners can settle on a suboptimal equilibrium [62].
  • What counts as success. Shoham, Powers and Grenager argued that the field needed to say whether its goal was to compute equilibria, describe behaviour, or achieve high reward against particular opponents, because these require different algorithms [89].
  • Scale. The joint action space grows exponentially with the number of agents [61,90].

7.3 Deep MARL

Deep MARL methods commonly use centralised training with decentralised execution. MADDPG trains each agent's actor with a critic that sees all agents' observations and actions, while each actor acts only on local information [91]. QMIX learns a joint action value for a cooperative team as a monotonic combination of per-agent values, so that each agent can act greedily on its own value [92]. Policy-space response oracles (PSRO) generalise fictitious play and the double-oracle method. They repeatedly train a best response to a game-theoretic mixture of earlier policies, which counters the tendency of plain self-play to overfit to its current opponent [93]. AlphaStar used a league of agents trained in this spirit, including "exploiter" agents that targeted weaknesses of the main agents, and it reached Grandmaster level in StarCraft II [50]. Textbooks and surveys now treat game theory as the foundation of the field [90,94].

8. Algorithmic game theory and complexity

Algorithmic game theory studies strategic interaction with the tools of theoretical computer science, chiefly algorithms, complexity and approximation [6]. Its starting point is that a solution concept which cannot be computed efficiently is a doubtful prediction of behaviour. Papadimitriou quotes Kamal Jain's summary of the point: "If your laptop can't find it, neither can the market" [6].

8.1 What is easy

  • Two-player zero-sum games can be solved in polynomial time, because the minimax problem is a linear program. Dantzig noted the equivalence of the two problems in 1951 [5,95].
  • Correlated equilibria of a normal-form game are the solutions of a linear feasibility problem, so one can be found, and even one that maximises total welfare, in time polynomial in the size of the payoff matrices. Papadimitriou and Roughgarden extended this to many succinctly represented multiplayer games [80].
  • Stackelberg commitment: the leader's optimal mixed strategy to commit to in a two-player normal-form game can be found by solving one linear program per follower action [56].

8.2 What is hard

A Nash equilibrium always exists, so the usual NP-completeness framework, built for decision problems whose answer can be "no", does not fit. Papadimitriou defined the class PPAD in 1994 for total search problems whose solutions are guaranteed by a parity argument on a directed graph, the argument behind Sperner's lemma and Brouwer's fixed-point theorem [96]. Daskalakis, Goldberg and Papadimitriou proved that computing a Nash equilibrium is PPAD-complete for games with three or more players. Chen, Deng and Teng extended the result to two players [29,30]. No polynomial-time algorithm for any PPAD-complete problem is known, and none is expected.

The classic Lemke–Howson algorithm finds an equilibrium of a two-player game by following a path of pivots [97]. Savani and von Stengel constructed games on which every such path is exponentially long [98]. Asking for an equilibrium with extra properties makes the problem NP-hard. Examples include an equilibrium with welfare above a threshold, a unique equilibrium, or one in which a given strategy is played [99,100].

These results make the outer sets in figure 3 more attractive as predictions. Correlated and coarse correlated equilibria are tractable to compute and reachable by simple learning, which a Nash equilibrium in general is not [54,79]. Much modern work studies succinct representations, such as graphical, congestion and action-graph games, in which the payoff tables would be exponentially large but structure can be exploited [6,102,103].

9. The price of anarchy

When self-interested users share a resource such as a network, the equilibrium they reach may be worse for everyone than an outcome a central planner would choose. Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou proposed measuring this loss by the worst-case ratio between the cost of an equilibrium and the optimal cost [25]. Papadimitriou named the ratio the price of anarchy [3]:

PoA = maxequilibria σ Cost(σ) ⁄ minoutcomes s Cost(s)  ≥ 1.

The price of stability uses the best equilibrium instead of the worst. It measures how much is lost even if the players can be steered to the most favourable stable outcome [104]. For a utility-maximisation game the ratios are inverted, so that they still measure loss [6].

9.1 Selfish routing: Pigou's example

The ideas predate computer science. Pigou described a two-road example in 1920 [105], and Wardrop in 1952 formulated the equilibrium condition for traffic, in which all used routes have equal and minimal travel time [106]. Consider one unit of traffic travelling from s to t on two parallel links. The upper link always takes time 1. The lower link takes time x, where x is the fraction of traffic using it. The lower link is never worse than the upper link, so at equilibrium all traffic uses it and everyone's travel time is 1. A planner would send half the traffic each way, for an average time of ½ · 1 + ½ · ½ = ¾. The price of anarchy is therefore 1 ⁄ ¾ = 4/3 [28].

Roughgarden and Tardos proved that 4/3 is the worst case for every network whose link latencies are affine functions, however large or complicated it is. For general continuous, non-decreasing latencies, the equilibrium is never worse than the optimum for twice as much traffic [28]. Results like this are what made the 2012 Gödel Prize committee say that, "surprisingly often", the cost of selfishness is not that high [27].

9.2 Braess's paradox

Braess showed in 1968 that adding a road to a network can make every driver's journey slower at equilibrium [107]. In figure 6(b), one unit of traffic splits evenly between two routes, each taking ½ + 1 = 1.5. Adding a zero-cost link from v to w creates the route s–v–w–t. Every driver now prefers it, and at the new equilibrium every journey takes 1 + 0 + 1 = 2. Studies of road networks in Boston, London and New York have identified streets whose closure could reduce travel times in this way [108].

9.3 Beyond routing

Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou's original model was load balancing, in which selfish jobs choose which of several machines (or network links) to use. They showed, for example, a price of anarchy of 3/2 for two identical links [25]. Rosenthal's congestion games, in which a resource's cost depends on how many players use it, always have pure equilibria and give a general framework for these settings [103]. Johari and Tsitsiklis showed that when users bid for shares of a link's bandwidth under a simple proportional rule, at least 75% of the optimal total utility is achieved at equilibrium [109]. Roughgarden's smoothness framework explained why many such bounds hold. A single smoothness inequality yields a bound that applies to pure and mixed Nash equilibria, and to correlated and coarse correlated equilibria. The last case means the bound also applies to outcomes of no-regret learning [110]. Anshelevich and colleagues showed that in network design games with fair cost sharing the price of anarchy can be as large as the number of players, but the price of stability is at most the n-th harmonic number, H(n) = 1 + ½ + ⋯ + 1/n [104].

10. Mechanism design and auctions

Most of game theory takes the game as given and predicts the outcome. Mechanism design works in reverse. It chooses the rules of the game so that self-interested agents, acting on private information, produce an outcome the designer wants [6,57]. Hurwicz, Maskin and Myerson shared the 2007 Nobel prize for laying its foundations [111].

10.1 Truthfulness, the revelation principle and VCG

The revelation principle lets designers restrict attention to direct mechanisms in which agents are asked for their private information and telling the truth is optimal [112,113]. Its limits are set by impossibility results. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem shows that with three or more alternatives and no money, every onto, strategy-proof voting rule is a dictatorship [112,114]. With money and quasi-linear utilities the picture is far brighter. The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism chooses the outcome that maximises total reported value. It then charges each agent the harm its presence imposes on the others, which is the others' best total value without it minus their total value in the chosen outcome. Truth-telling is then a dominant strategy for every agent [41,115,116].

10.2 Worked example: the second-price auction

For a single item, VCG is Vickrey's second-price sealed-bid auction. The highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid [41].

A pays 8, which is exactly its externality: without A, B would have won and gained 8. A's bid only decides whether it wins, never what it pays. Bidding above 10 risks winning at a price above its value, and bidding below 10 risks losing an item it would have bought at a profit. Truthful bidding is therefore a dominant strategy [41,117]. Myerson's theory of optimal auctions shows how a seller who knows the distribution of bidders' values can raise expected revenue, for example by adding a reserve price [118].

10.3 Algorithmic mechanism design

Nisan and Ronen observed that mechanisms run on computers must be computationally efficient as well as incentive-compatible, and named the combined study algorithmic mechanism design [26]. The tension is clearest in combinatorial auctions, where bidders value bundles of items. Choosing the allocation that maximises reported value (winner determination) is NP-hard [101,119]. VCG needs the exact optimum to remain truthful. Replacing it with an approximation algorithm generally breaks truthfulness, so researchers design approximation algorithms that are themselves truthful, such as Lehmann, O'Callaghan and Shoham's greedy mechanism for single-minded bidders [26,120]. Automated mechanism design treats the mechanism itself as the output of an optimisation [121]. More recent work learns auctions with neural networks trained to maximise revenue subject to penalties for incentive violations [122].

10.4 Mechanisms in practice

  • Sponsored search. Search engines sell advertising slots with the generalised second-price (GSP) auction. Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz, and independently Varian, showed that GSP is not truthful but has an equilibrium whose outcome matches the VCG outcome [9,123].
  • Spectrum auctions. The US Federal Communications Commission's 2016–17 incentive auction bought broadcast spectrum from television stations through a descending clock auction and resold it to mobile carriers. Each step needed hard feasibility checks on station repacking, solved by computer [124]. Milgrom and Wilson received the 2020 Nobel prize for improvements to auction theory and inventions of new auction formats [125].
  • Matching markets. Gale and Shapley's deferred-acceptance algorithm finds a stable matching, and it is strategy-proof for the proposing side [126,127]. Roth showed that the US medical residency match had independently adopted an equivalent algorithm. Roth and Shapley shared the 2012 Nobel prize for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design [128,129].

11. Security games

A defender with limited resources, such as police officers, patrol boats or inspection teams, must protect many targets against an adversary who can watch the defence before striking. Deterministic schedules are exploitable because the adversary learns them. Security games model the problem as a Stackelberg game. The defender (leader) commits to a randomised allocation of resources, and the attacker (follower) observes the coverage probabilities and attacks the target that is best for it [8,130].

11.1 Model and solution

Each target t has four payoffs: the defender's and the attacker's utility if t is attacked while covered, and both utilities if it is attacked while uncovered. A defender strategy assigns each target a coverage probability ct, subject to the resource limits. The usual solution concept is the strong Stackelberg equilibrium, in which the attacker breaks ties in the defender's favour [130,131]. Committing to a strategy cannot hurt the leader compared with a simultaneous-move Nash equilibrium, and it often helps [132]. Korzhyk and colleagues showed that in a broad class of security games the defender's Stackelberg strategy is also a Nash equilibrium strategy, so it is a safe choice even if the attacker cannot observe the coverage [131]. With multiple attacker types, finding the optimal commitment becomes NP-hard [56]. The DOBSS algorithm solves such Bayesian Stackelberg games exactly as a mixed-integer linear program [133], and ERASER exploits the compact structure of security games to scale to large problems [130].

11.2 Worked example

Take one defender resource and two targets. An attack on target 1 yields the attacker +5 if it is uncovered and −1 if it is covered. An attack on target 2 yields +2 if uncovered and −1 if covered. The defender's payoffs are the negatives of these. If the defender covers target 1 with probability c and target 2 with probability 1 − c, the attacker's expected payoffs are

Ua(1) = 5(1 − c) − c = 5 − 6c,    Ua(2) = 2c − (1 − c) = 3c − 1.

The defender should make the attacker indifferent between the two targets, which happens at c = 2/3, where each attack is worth 1 to the attacker and −1 to the defender. Always guarding target 1 is worse. The attacker would then hit target 2 and the defender would get −2. Splitting coverage evenly is no better, because the attacker would then hit target 1 and the defender would again get −2. Randomisation tuned to the target values halves the defender's expected loss in this example.

11.3 Deployed systems

Tambe's group and their collaborators turned the theory into operational tools [8]:

  • ARMOR randomised the placement of vehicle checkpoints and canine patrols at Los Angeles International Airport from 2007 [38].
  • IRIS scheduled Federal Air Marshals on international flights [134].
  • PROTECT generated patrol schedules for the US Coast Guard in the port of Boston, with a model that allows for attackers who are not perfectly rational [135].
  • Green security games applied the approach to patrols against poaching and illegal fishing, where there are many attacks over time and data on past attacks can be learned from [136].

Human attackers do not always best-respond. Experiments with human subjects led to models of bounded rationality, such as quantal response, being built into the defender's optimisation [137]. Game-theoretic methods are also used for network and cyber security, such as intrusion detection and the placement of defences [138].

12. Distributed systems

Classical fault-tolerant distributed computing splits nodes into correct ones, which follow the protocol, and faulty ones. The worst faults are Byzantine: a faulty node can behave arbitrarily, even maliciously. Lamport, Shostak and Pease showed that agreement is possible with oral messages only if fewer than one third of the nodes are Byzantine [43]. Open systems such as peer-to-peer networks and blockchains add a third kind of participant. A rational node will deviate from the protocol whenever deviation pays [7,139].

12.1 Rational participants and the BAR model

Aiyer and colleagues' BAR model classifies nodes as Byzantine, Altruistic (following the protocol regardless) or Rational. A BAR-tolerant protocol must stay correct despite a bounded number of Byzantine nodes, and it must also make following the protocol a best response for rational nodes. The authors built a cooperative backup service on these principles [46]. Abraham, Dolev, Gonen and Halpern combined the two kinds of robustness formally. Their (k, t)-robust equilibria tolerate coalitions of up to k rational deviators together with up to t nodes of arbitrary behaviour [139]. Halpern and Teague showed that secret sharing and multiparty computation fail among rational players who prefer to learn the secret alone, and that randomised protocols restore them [140]. Feigenbaum and Shenker called the design of incentive-compatible protocols that are themselves computed by the participants distributed algorithmic mechanism design [141].

12.2 Peer-to-peer incentives

Measurements of the Gnutella file-sharing network in 2000 found that most users shared no files at all, a textbook free-rider problem [142]. BitTorrent addressed it with a tit-for-tat rule, in which a peer uploads preferentially to peers that upload to it, and occasional "optimistic unchoking" to discover new partners [143]. Piatek and colleagues then showed that a strategic client, BitTyrant, could obtain faster downloads by exploiting the protocol's allocation rule. Their result showed that the incentives were weaker than had been believed [144].

12.3 Blockchains and consensus incentives

Bitcoin secures consensus by rewarding miners with newly created coins and fees for extending the longest chain of proof-of-work blocks. Nakamoto argued that an attacker with most of the computing power would find it more profitable to mine honestly than to undermine the system [145]. Eyal and Sirer showed that the protocol is not incentive-compatible as stated. In selfish mining, a pool withholds the blocks it finds and releases them strategically, wasting honest miners' work and earning more than its share of rewards. Depending on network propagation, this can pay for a pool with well under half of the total hash power, as little as one third when honest miners never favour the attacker's block [146]. Analysing protocols as games in this way is now a standard part of evaluating consensus designs [7,146].

13. Communication and negotiation

Autonomous agents often have to reach agreements: dividing tasks, setting prices, or scheduling shared resources. Automated negotiation studies the protocols agents use (the rules of encounter) and the strategies they adopt within them [23,147]. Jennings and colleagues characterised the design space by three elements: the negotiation protocol, the objects under negotiation, and the agents' decision-making models [147].

13.1 Axiomatic and strategic bargaining

Nash's 1950 bargaining solution picks, from the set of feasible utility pairs, the point that maximises the product of the gains over the disagreement point d. It is the unique solution that satisfies his axioms of Pareto efficiency, symmetry, invariance to affine rescalings of utility, and independence of irrelevant alternatives [39]:

(u1*, u2*) = argmax(u1, u2) ∈ F, u ≥ d (u1 − d1)(u2 − d2).

Rubinstein's 1982 alternating-offers model gives a strategic foundation. Two players take turns proposing how to split a surplus of 1, and delay is costly through discount factors δ1, δ2. The unique subgame-perfect equilibrium has immediate agreement, with the first proposer receiving (1 − δ2) ⁄ (1 − δ1δ2) [44]. With δ1 = δ2 = 0.9, the first proposer gets 0.1/0.19 ≈ 0.526. As the players become more patient, the split approaches the Nash bargaining solution, which is ½ each in this symmetric case [44,52].

13.2 Protocols for agents

Smith's Contract Net protocol (1980) allocates tasks by an auction-like exchange. A manager announces a task, contractors submit bids, and the manager awards the contract [24]. Rosenschein and Zlotkin analysed negotiation protocols for software agents as games in Rules of Encounter. They studied which protocols are stable, efficient and simple, and when agents gain by lying about their tasks or preferences. Their analysis includes the monotonic concession protocol, in which agents alternately concede, with the Zeuthen strategy choosing who concedes next, and it reaches the Nash bargaining outcome [23]. Faratin, Sierra and Jennings defined families of time-, resource- and behaviour-dependent concession tactics for multi-issue negotiation [148]. Standard agent communication languages, such as FIPA ACL, give such protocols a shared vocabulary of performatives (for example propose, accept-proposal and reject-proposal) [149].

13.3 Cheap talk and language

When communication is costless and non-binding, it is called cheap talk. Crawford and Sobel showed that a better-informed sender whose interests differ from the receiver's can credibly convey only coarse information, and that less is conveyed as their interests diverge [150]. Negotiation in natural language is now a research frontier. Lewis and colleagues trained dialogue agents end to end to negotiate the division of items, and found that agents trained with reinforcement learning against each other drifted away from human language unless constrained [151]. Meta's CICERO played the seven-player game Diplomacy, in which players negotiate alliances in private messages. It combined a language model with strategic planning based on game-theoretic reasoning about other players' intentions, and it ranked in the top 10% of participants who played more than one game in an online league [51].

15. Open problems and summary

Several questions remain open across these areas.

  • Beyond two-player zero-sum. Equilibrium-finding methods are well understood for two-player zero-sum games. In multiplayer and general-sum settings, equilibria may be intractable, may not be unique, and may not guarantee good results against real opponents [29,37,89].
  • Bounded rationality. Deployed systems face people who do not best-respond. Behavioural models such as quantal response improve the fit, but no single model is accepted [8,137].
  • Learning mechanisms. Learned auctions and automatically designed mechanisms scale to settings where theory gives no closed-form answer, but their incentive guarantees are only approximate [121,122].
  • Cooperation among AI agents. As AI agents negotiate and transact with one another and with people, cooperation, commitment and communication become central research problems [51,65].

Game theory entered computing through chess programs and minimax search. It now underpins how computer scientists design, analyse and train systems of interacting agents. Adversarial search and learning in games compute equilibria of games too large to write down. Multi-agent reinforcement learning extends this to agents that learn from experience. Algorithmic game theory asks which strategic outcomes can be computed at all, and the price of anarchy quantifies what is lost without central control. Mechanism design, security games and incentive-aware protocols run the analysis in reverse and engineer the game so that self-interested behaviour produces good results. Automated negotiation, now including natural language, extends all of these to agents that have to talk to one another before they act.
