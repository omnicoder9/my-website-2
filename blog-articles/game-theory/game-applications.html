Applications of Game Theory

How models of strategic interaction are used in markets, security, artificial intelligence, politics, biology and engineered systems

Technical article · References in the Vancouver style · Companion to Representations of Games, Classification of Games, Limitations and Extensions and Game Theory in Computer Science and AI

Abstract. Game theory is used wherever the outcome for one decision-maker depends on what others choose. This article surveys its main fields of application. In economics and business it explains pricing and oligopoly, collusion, merger effects, bargaining, supply-chain contracts, auctions, platforms and market design. In security and defence it allocates scarce protective resources against adaptive attackers, and it models intrusion detection, deception and deterrence. In artificial intelligence it underpins multi-agent reinforcement learning, superhuman poker and strategy-game agents, automated negotiation and the planning of autonomous vehicles. In political science it is used to study voting, coalitions, crisis bargaining, arms races and treaty compliance. In biology and the social sciences it explains the evolution of cooperation, contests over resources, conventions and collective action. It also shapes routing, wireless networks, distributed computing, traffic, scheduling and blockchain protocols. For each area the article gives the history, the core model, a worked example with a diagram, and evidence from deployed systems or empirical studies.

1. Why game theory is applied so widely

Game theory is the mathematical study of interactive decision-making. Each participant's payoff depends on the choices of others as well as its own [1,2]. The definition is broad on purpose. A "player" can be a firm setting a price, a state deciding whether to arm, a security agency scheduling patrols, a software agent bidding for computing time, or a gene whose effect on behaviour is copied in proportion to the reproductive success it brings [3,4]. The same few ideas recur across these settings. Nash equilibrium is a profile of strategies from which no player gains by deviating alone [5,6]. Commitment and credibility, incomplete information and the design of rules recur as well.

The applications fall into two broad kinds. Positive applications explain or predict behaviour: why oligopolists price above cost, why arms races occur, why animals settle contests by display. Normative or engineering applications design the rules of an interaction so that self-interested behaviour produces a good outcome. Auctions, matching markets, security schedules and network protocols are examples [7–9]. The Nobel memorial prize in economic sciences has gone to work built substantially on game theory at least nine times since 1994. The awards span non-cooperative equilibrium, incentives and markets under asymmetric information, conflict and cooperation, mechanism design, the commons, market design, market power and regulation, and auctions [8,10–17].

This article is organised by domain. The formal objects it uses, such as payoff matrices, game trees and Bayesian games, are defined in the companion article Representations of Games. The taxonomy of game types is in Classification of Games. The computational side of many applications is treated in more depth in Game Theory in Computer Science and AI.

2. Historical development

Strategic models of markets came before game theory as a discipline. In 1838 Antoine Augustin Cournot analysed two producers of mineral water who each choose an output while taking the other's output as given. His equilibrium is, in modern terms, a Nash equilibrium in quantities [18,19]. In 1883 Joseph Bertrand objected that firms set prices, not quantities. With prices as the strategic variable, two firms with equal costs undercut each other down to marginal cost [20,21]. Heinrich von Stackelberg added a leader–follower structure in 1934 [22]. Émile Borel's notes of 1921 included a resource-allocation game that later became known as the Colonel Blotto game [23,24].

John von Neumann's minimax theorem (1928) and his book with Oskar Morgenstern (1944) founded the field and were written with economic behaviour explicitly in view [1,25,26]. John Nash's equilibrium (1950) and his axiomatic bargaining solution (1950) supplied tools that work outside zero-sum conflict [5,27]. At the RAND Corporation in the 1950s game theory was applied to military and nuclear strategy. Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher's experiment there became the canonical Prisoner's Dilemma [26,28]. Thomas Schelling's The Strategy of Conflict (1960) brought commitment, focal points and threats into international relations [29]. Lloyd Shapley and Martin Shubik had already measured voting power in committees in 1954 [30].

Applications widened in the following decades. William Vickrey analysed auctions in 1961 [31]. John Maynard Smith and George Price introduced the evolutionarily stable strategy in 1973. Game theory became a branch of evolutionary biology that needs no assumption of conscious rationality [4,32]. Robert Axelrod's computer tournaments around 1980 showed how reciprocity can sustain cooperation [33,34]. Elinor Ostrom documented how communities govern common-pool resources [35]. From the 1990s game theory became an engineering discipline. The US Federal Communications Commission ran spectrum auctions designed by economists from 1994 [36,37]. Search engines sold advertising through position auctions [38,39]. Computer scientists quantified the cost of selfish routing [40,41]. Security agencies deployed randomised patrol schedules computed from Stackelberg games [42,43]. AI systems reached superhuman play in poker and strong play in multi-player strategy games [44–47].

3. The applied toolkit

A small set of models carries most applied work. Each is defined precisely in the companion articles. The list below records what each contributes to applications.

  • Nash equilibrium and its refinements. A prediction is a profile of mutual best responses [6]. In sequential settings, subgame perfection rules out threats that a player would not carry out when the time came [59].
  • Stackelberg (leader–follower) games. One player commits first and the others respond. Commitment can benefit the leader, which is the basis of entry deterrence and of security games [22,60].
  • Repeated games. When an interaction recurs, the threat of future punishment can sustain cooperation that would collapse in a single encounter [61,62].
  • Bayesian games. Players hold private information, or "types", and form beliefs about others' types. These games are the basis of auction, signalling and contract theory [63–65].
  • Bargaining and cooperative games. These models divide a surplus or allocate power within coalitions, using the Nash bargaining solution, Rubinstein's model, the core and the Shapley value [27,54,66].
  • Mechanism design. This reverses the usual analysis: the designer chooses rules so that equilibrium behaviour produces a target outcome [7,8,53].
  • Evolutionary game theory. Strategies spread in proportion to their payoff, and stability replaces rationality as the organising idea [4,67,68].

Three 2×2 games recur across domains. The Prisoner's Dilemma captures any situation in which individually rational choices produce a collectively worse outcome. Examples are cartel pricing, arms races, overfishing and cooperation between microbes [34,52,69,70]. Chicken, also called Hawk–Dove, captures confrontations in which each side prefers that the other back down. Examples are nuclear crises and animal contests [4,71]. The Stag Hunt captures coordination with a risky but better equilibrium. It appears in the security dilemma, in social conventions and in collective action [69,72]. Much applied work consists of recognising which of these structures, or which richer generalisation, a real situation has.

4. Economics and business

Economics is the oldest and largest field of application. Modern industrial organisation, auction theory, contract theory and market design are largely written in the language of non-cooperative games [21,62].

4.1 Pricing and market competition

Take two firms with constant marginal cost c that face the inverse demand P = a − q1 − q2. In Cournot's model each firm chooses its output. Firm i maximises (a − qi − qj − c) qi, which gives the best-response function

qi = BRi(qj) = (a − c − qj) / 2.

The two best responses intersect at q1 = q2 = (a − c)/3. Total output is therefore larger than the monopoly output (a − c)/2 and smaller than the competitive output a − c [18,21]. If the firms compete in prices with identical products, Bertrand's logic of undercutting drives price down to c. Two firms are then enough for the competitive outcome [20,21]. Kreps and Scheinkman reconciled the two models. When firms first choose capacities and then compete in price, the equilibrium is the Cournot outcome [73]. In von Stackelberg's version one firm commits to its output first. The leader produces (a − c)/2, the follower produces (a − c)/4, and the leader earns more than in the simultaneous game [21,22]. This first-mover advantage underlies the analysis of capacity investment and entry deterrence.

4.2 Collusion and repeated interaction

Figure 2 shows why a one-shot cartel is unstable: each member gains by expanding output, or cutting price, while the others hold back. When the same firms meet repeatedly, a deviation can be punished by a return to competitive behaviour. If firms value future profits enough, collusion can then be sustained as a subgame-perfect equilibrium [61,62]. Green and Porter showed that when firms cannot observe rivals' prices, a collusive scheme needs occasional "price wars" triggered by low market prices, even when no one has cheated [74]. Porter found patterns consistent with this in a nineteenth-century railway cartel, the Joint Executive Committee [75]. This line of research informs competition policy. A recent concern is that pricing algorithms may learn to collude. Calvano and co-authors found that simple Q-learning agents, repeatedly setting prices in a simulated market, learned to charge prices above the competitive level and to punish deviations. They did so without communicating and without being instructed to collude [76].

4.3 Mergers

Oligopoly models are used to predict how a merger will change prices. In a symmetric Cournot market with linear demand, Salant, Switzer and Reynolds showed a "merger paradox". A merger of two out of three firms lowers the merging firms' joint profit, because outsiders expand output in response. A merger is profitable only if it brings together a large share of the industry [77]. Farrell and Shapiro derived general conditions under which a horizontal merger raises price. Without sufficient cost savings it does, so the efficiencies a merger generates are central to judging it [78]. For markets with differentiated products, Werden and Froeb developed merger simulation. This method calibrates a Bertrand pricing game, often with logit demand, to market data, then solves for the post-merger equilibrium [79]. Antitrust agencies assess such "unilateral effects" when they review mergers [80].

4.4 Negotiation and bargaining

Nash's axiomatic solution predicts that two parties split a surplus so as to maximise the product of their gains over their disagreement payoffs (u1 − d1)(u2 − d2) [27]. Rubinstein's strategic model has the parties alternate offers and discount delay. It has a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium in which agreement is immediate. With common discount factor δ, the first proposer receives 1/(1 + δ) of the surplus [54]. As the time between offers shrinks, the Rubinstein outcome converges to the Nash solution. This gives the axiomatic solution a non-cooperative foundation and tells modellers how to set the disagreement point [81]. Both models show that patience and outside options are sources of bargaining power. Raiffa's prescriptive approach built on these ideas and turned them into practical negotiation analysis, which emphasises reservation values and the zone of possible agreement [82]. In applied industrial organisation, Horn and Wolinsky's framework of simultaneous bilateral Nash bargains is used to model negotiations between suppliers and buyers [83].

4.5 Supply chains

A supply chain is a game among independent firms whose decisions affect one another's profits [84]. Take a manufacturer that sells to a retailer at wholesale price w, with a retailer facing demand P = a − q. If each firm adds its own mark-up, the retail price is higher and the joint profit lower than under vertical integration. Spengler identified this "double marginalisation" in 1950 [85]. Figure 3 gives the numbers for a = 8 and zero production cost. Contracts that share risk or revenue can restore the integrated outcome. Examples are buy-back (returns) contracts and revenue-sharing contracts, the latter studied in the video-rental industry [86,87]. Strategic behaviour also helps explain the bullwhip effect, in which order variability grows as one moves upstream. One of its causes is a "shortage game": when supply is rationed, retailers inflate their orders [88].

4.6 Auctions

Auctions are Bayesian games: each bidder knows its own value but not the values of others. Vickrey showed in 1961 that in a sealed-bid second-price auction, bidding one's true value is a weakly dominant strategy. He also showed that, with independent private values, the English, Dutch, first-price and second-price formats yield the same expected revenue [31]. Myerson and, independently, Riley and Samuelson generalised this revenue-equivalence result and derived optimal auctions. These auctions use reserve prices that can exclude buyers with low values [53,89]. Milgrom and Weber extended the theory to interdependent ("affiliated") values. That extension explains the winner's curse and shows why open ascending auctions can raise more revenue than sealed bids [90].

Auction theory moved into practice in the 1990s. From 1994 the US Federal Communications Commission allocated radio spectrum through a simultaneous multiple-round ascending auction. Paul Milgrom, Robert Wilson and Preston McAfee designed it so that bidders could assemble packages of complementary licences while observing prices [36,37,92]. The United Kingdom's 2000 auction of third-generation mobile licences raised about £22.5 billion. Its designers attribute part of this success to choices made to attract entrants and discourage collusion [93]. Klemperer argued that these practical concerns matter more than the fine points of auction theory: collusion, entry deterrence and the robustness of rules [94]. Search advertising is sold through the generalised second-price auction. Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz, and independently Varian, characterised its equilibria [38,39]. Milgrom and Wilson received the 2020 Nobel memorial prize "for improvements to auction theory and inventions of new auction formats" [17].

4.7 Platforms and two-sided markets

Card networks, operating systems, video-game consoles and online marketplaces serve two groups whose participation benefits each other. Rochet and Tirole showed that in such two-sided markets the structure of prices matters as well as their level: how the total charge is split between the two sides. Platforms may rationally subsidise one side to attract the other [57,95]. Armstrong showed that when one side uses a single platform but the other uses several ("multi-homes"), platforms compete hard for the single-homing side. They then charge the multi-homing side for access to it, a pattern he called a "competitive bottleneck" [96]. Network effects also create coordination problems among users, which can tip a market to one standard [97]. Jean Tirole's 2014 Nobel memorial prize cited his "analysis of market power and regulation" [16].

4.8 Information, signalling and market design

Markets with asymmetric information are games of incomplete information. Akerlof showed that hidden quality can unravel a market for used cars, because good cars are withdrawn when buyers pay only average value [64]. Spence showed that costly education can act as a credible signal of productivity in a separating equilibrium [65]. Both shared the 2001 Nobel memorial prize with Joseph Stiglitz [12].

Market design applies game theory to build allocation systems where money cannot or should not be used. Gale and Shapley's deferred-acceptance algorithm always finds a stable matching: no doctor and hospital would both prefer each other to their assigned partners [50]. Roth showed that truthful reporting is a dominant strategy for the proposing side [98]. A variant designed by Roth and Peranson has run the US National Resident Matching Program since the 1998 match [56]. The same ideas have been used to design school-choice systems and kidney exchanges, in which incompatible donor–patient pairs swap donors [99,100]. Roth and Shapley received the 2012 Nobel memorial prize "for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design" [15].

5. Cybersecurity and defence

Security problems are games because the adversary adapts. A defence that is optimal against a fixed threat can be exploited once the attacker learns it. Game-theoretic security models therefore treat the attacker as a strategic player who observes, plans and responds [43,101,102]. The computational side of this work, including the algorithms for security games, is covered in Game Theory in Computer Science and AI, §11.

5.1 Attacker–defender models for infrastructure

Brown, Carlyle, Salmerón and Wood formulated the protection of critical infrastructure as sequential games. In the attacker–defender version an attacker chooses which components of a network (pipelines, power lines, bridges) to disable, and the defender then operates the damaged system as well as it can. In the defender–attacker–defender version the defender first chooses which components to harden. These are bilevel and trilevel optimisation problems. The authors solved them for US infrastructure systems to find which investments most reduce worst-case damage [103]. The approach treats the attacker's choice as a best response rather than a random event, which separates it from standard reliability analysis.

5.2 Stackelberg security games and resource allocation

In a Stackelberg security game a defender with limited resources commits to a randomised schedule for protecting a set of targets. The attacker observes the coverage probabilities, possibly through surveillance, and attacks the target that is best for it [43,104]. Committing to a mixed strategy can help the defender. Conitzer and Sandholm showed that an optimal strategy to commit to can be computed in polynomial time for normal-form games. The problem becomes NP-hard when the leader faces a Bayesian follower of uncertain type [60]. The worked example in Figure 5 shows the core idea: randomise so that the attacker is indifferent between targets.

Security games have been deployed. ARMOR has scheduled randomised vehicle checkpoints and canine patrols at Los Angeles International Airport since 2007 [42]. IRIS has scheduled US Federal Air Marshals on flights [105]. PROTECT has generated patrol routes for the US Coast Guard, starting in the port of Boston [106]. "Green security" games apply the same logic to patrols against poaching and illegal fishing, where attackers act repeatedly and the defender learns from data about past attacks [104,107].

When both sides allocate forces simultaneously across several fronts, the classic model is the Colonel Blotto game. Each colonel divides a fixed budget of troops among battlefields, and each battlefield goes to whoever commits more there [23]. Pure-strategy equilibria generally do not exist. Roberson characterised the mixed-strategy equilibria of the continuous game for any number of battlefields and for unequal budgets [24]. Blotto models are used to study the allocation of defensive effort across assets, and campaign spending across electoral districts.

5.3 Intrusion detection and network defence

Alpcan and Başar modelled intrusion detection as a game between an attacker who chooses which part of a system to target and an intrusion-detection system that chooses where to monitor and how to respond. Detection is imperfect, and both sides trade off costs against the risk of being caught or missing an attack [102,108]. Lye and Wing modelled the interaction between an attacker and an administrator on a small enterprise network as a two-player stochastic game. Network states change with each side's actions, and they computed Nash equilibria that gave defensive policies [109]. Manshaei and co-authors survey the wider literature, which covers physical and MAC-layer attacks, anonymity, privacy, trust and the economics of security investment [101].

5.4 Cyber deception

Defensive deception, such as honeypots, decoy data and moving-target defences, is naturally modelled as a game of incomplete information. The defender knows which systems are real and the attacker does not. Carroll and Grosu treated honeypots as a signalling game. The defender disguises normal systems as honeypots and honeypots as normal systems, and equilibrium analysis shows when such camouflage deters attacks [110]. Pawlick, Colbert and Zhu classified the game-theoretic deception literature into types such as perturbation, moving-target defence, obfuscation, mixing, "honey-x" and attacker engagement, each linked to the game models that suit it [111].

5.5 Deterrence, stealthy compromise and adversarial risk

Advanced persistent threats control a system covertly over long periods. van Dijk, Juels, Oprea and Rivest modelled this in FlipIt, a game in which two players repeatedly "flip" control of a shared resource at a cost. A player learns of the other's moves only when it flips itself. The analysis gives guidance on how often to reset passwords and rotate keys, and it shows when one side's best response is to drop out entirely [112]. In classical deterrence theory, defending a network or a state means making retaliation or denial credible, a theme examined in §7 [113].

Standard game theory assumes that payoffs and rationality are common knowledge, which is rarely true of real adversaries. Adversarial risk analysis, proposed by Rios Insua, Rios and Banks, replaces equilibrium with Bayesian decision analysis. The defender places a probability distribution over the attacker's possible decisions, obtained by modelling the attacker's own problem with uncertain utilities and beliefs [114]. Behavioural models of bounded rationality play a similar role in deployed security games. The Limitations and Extensions article discusses robust alternatives to exact equilibrium.

6. AI and multi-agent systems

When several learning or planning agents share an environment, each agent's best behaviour depends on what the others do, so the setting is a game [115,116]. This section summarises the main applications. The companion article Game Theory in Computer Science and AI treats them in more technical depth, in particular learning in games, multi-agent reinforcement learning, adversarial search and negotiation.

6.1 Multi-agent reinforcement learning

Littman proposed Shapley's stochastic games, or Markov games, as the framework for multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL). His minimax-Q algorithm learns optimal policies in two-player zero-sum Markov games [55]. Hu and Wellman's Nash-Q extended the approach to general-sum games by updating values towards a Nash equilibrium of each stage game. Its convergence guarantees, however, need restrictive conditions [117]. The central difficulty is non-stationarity. From any one agent's point of view the environment includes the other agents, and their policies change as they learn, so single-agent convergence results no longer apply [116]. Shoham, Powers and Grenager argued that the field must say which goal it is pursuing: computing equilibria, describing behaviour, or doing well against particular opponents [118].

Modern deep MARL often uses centralised training with decentralised execution. MADDPG gives each agent a critic that sees all agents' actions during training, which stabilises learning in mixed cooperative–competitive tasks [120]. QMIX factorises a team value function so that each agent can act greedily on its own local value [121]. At the population level, Policy-Space Response Oracles (PSRO) alternate between training best responses with reinforcement learning and solving an empirical "meta-game" among the policies found so far. This generalises the double-oracle method and fictitious play [122]. Leibo and co-authors used MARL to study sequential social dilemmas, Markov-game versions of the Prisoner's Dilemma in which whether cooperation emerges depends on resource abundance and on what agents can perceive [123].

6.2 Competitive agents

Two-player zero-sum games have a well-defined target, the minimax strategy, and this made them a proving ground for AI. In games of imperfect information such as poker, an agent cannot simply search the game tree. It must balance its play so that its hidden information is not revealed. Counterfactual regret minimisation (CFR) solves such games by self-play. It drives the average regret at every information set towards zero, which in two-player zero-sum games approximates a Nash equilibrium [124]. Using CFR variants, heads-up limit Texas hold'em was declared "essentially weakly solved" in 2015 [125]. DeepStack and Libratus beat professional players at heads-up no-limit hold'em in 2017 [44,126]. In 2019 Pluribus won against elite professionals in six-player no-limit hold'em. That game has no guarantee that equilibrium play is optimal, yet the self-play strategy worked in practice [45].

In large real-time video games, AlphaStar reached Grandmaster level in StarCraft II. It was rated above 99.8% of officially ranked human players. It was trained through a "league" of agents that included exploiters designed to find weaknesses in the main agents, a population approach rooted in game theory [46]. OpenAI Five defeated the world-champion team at Dota 2 in 2019 [127]. DeepNash learned to play the imperfect-information board game Stratego at human expert level using a regularised learning dynamic designed to converge to equilibrium [128]. Generative adversarial networks frame generative modelling as a two-player minimax game between a generator and a discriminator [129].

6.3 Autonomous coordination and strategic planning

Autonomous vehicles and robots must coordinate with other machines and with people. Dresner and Stone designed a reservation-based protocol for intersections in which autonomous vehicles request time–space slots from an intersection manager. In simulation it greatly reduced delay compared with traffic lights [130]. Sadigh and co-authors modelled the interaction between an autonomous car and a human driver as a leader–follower game. The robot plans its actions while anticipating how the human will respond, and so can influence the human's behaviour, for example by nudging forward to make a human driver yield [131]. More broadly, "cooperative AI" research asks how to build agents that find common ground and avoid destructive equilibria in mixed-motive settings [132]. Game-theoretic learning also has risks. As noted in §4.2, pricing agents can learn to collude without being told to [76].

6.4 Negotiating agents

Rosenschein and Zlotkin's Rules of Encounter applied game theory and mechanism design to the design of negotiation protocols for software agents. It aimed for protocols that are efficient, stable and resistant to manipulation [133]. Jennings and co-authors framed automated negotiation in terms of protocols, objects and agents' reasoning models, and compared game-theoretic, heuristic and argumentation-based approaches [134]. Learning-based negotiators came later. Lewis and co-authors trained dialogue agents end-to-end to bargain over items. They reported that the agents sometimes feigned interest in a worthless item so that they could later "concede" it [135]. CICERO combined a language model with strategic planning based on equilibrium search to play Diplomacy, a seven-player game of negotiation and alliance. In an anonymous online league it scored more than double the human average and ranked in the top 10% of participants who played more than one game [47].

7. Politics and international relations

Political actors such as voters, parties, legislators and states pursue goals under rules and in anticipation of one another's moves. This makes politics a natural field for game theory. Rational-choice political science and much of the formal theory of international relations are built on game-theoretic models [136,137]. Robert Aumann and Thomas Schelling received the 2005 Nobel memorial prize "for having enhanced our understanding of conflict and cooperation through game-theory analysis" [13].

7.1 Voting and elections

Arrow's impossibility theorem showed that no rule for aggregating three or more alternatives satisfies a small set of reasonable conditions at once [138]. Its strategic counterpart is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. Every non-dictatorial voting rule with at least three possible outcomes gives some voter, in some situation, an incentive to misreport preferences [139,140]. Voting is therefore a game, and outcomes depend on strategic behaviour as well as sincere preferences.

Electoral competition is modelled spatially. Black showed that when preferences over a one-dimensional issue are single-peaked, the median voter's ideal point defeats every alternative in a pairwise vote [141]. Downs, building on Hotelling's model of firms locating on a street, argued that two office-seeking parties converge on the median voter (Figure 7) [48,142]. Convergence is fragile. With two or more issue dimensions, McKelvey showed that majority rule generally has no equilibrium, and an agenda-setter can steer the outcome almost anywhere [143].

7.2 Coalition formation and voting power

Many political outcomes depend on which coalitions form. Riker's size principle predicts that, in zero-sum situations, rational actors form minimal winning coalitions, just large enough to win, so that the spoils are shared among as few as possible [144]. Baron and Ferejohn's non-cooperative model of legislative bargaining has a randomly recognised member propose a division, which a majority must accept. The proposer forms a minimal majority and keeps a disproportionate share [145].

Cooperative game theory measures how much influence a voting rule gives each member. The Shapley–Shubik index applies the Shapley value to voting. A member's power is the probability that it is pivotal, meaning it turns a losing coalition into a winning one when members join in a random order [30,66]. Banzhaf proposed a related index and used it to show that weighted voting in a New York county board gave some members no real power [146]. As an illustration, take the UN Security Council. A substantive decision needs nine of fifteen votes including all five permanent members [147]. Treating abstentions as votes against, a direct calculation gives each permanent member a Shapley–Shubik index of about 0.196. Each non-permanent member gets about 0.0019, so the permanent five hold about 98% of voting power, even though they cast a third of the votes. Shapley and Shubik applied their index to the Security Council, then of eleven members, in their original paper [30].

7.3 Diplomacy, crisis bargaining and war

Schelling recast strategy as the art of influencing an opponent's choices. Commitment, such as burning bridges or tying one's hands, can strengthen a bargaining position. Focal points let parties coordinate without communicating. A "threat that leaves something to chance" can be credible even when carrying it out deliberately would not be [29,113].

Fearon's rationalist analysis asked why states fight when war is costly and some negotiated settlement should leave both sides better off. He identified three mechanisms: private information about capabilities or resolve combined with incentives to misrepresent it, commitment problems, and indivisible issues [148]. In a related paper he argued that leaders who publicly escalate a crisis incur domestic "audience costs" if they back down. This makes their threats more credible and allows them to signal resolve [149]. Putnam's two-level game links international negotiation to domestic ratification. A negotiator constrained at home can sometimes extract more abroad [150].

7.4 Arms races and the security dilemma

Lewis Fry Richardson modelled arms races with coupled differential equations in which each state's arming responds to the other's stock of arms [151]. Game theory reframed the problem in strategic terms. Jervis argued that when weapons that defend a state also threaten its neighbours, states that only want security can still be driven into competition. He related this to the Prisoner's Dilemma and the Stag Hunt, and showed that the balance between offence and defence and the ability to tell them apart determine how severe the dilemma is [69]. Brams applied 2×2 games, including Chicken, to superpower arms races and crises [71].

7.5 Deterrence

Deterrence theory asks when a threat to retaliate is credible. In the simple entry-style game of Figure 8, a defender who would prefer to concede once challenged cannot deter a challenge. Subgame perfection rules out the empty threat. Credibility needs something that changes the defender's payoffs or information, such as audience costs, reputation, or a commitment device [59,113,149]. Powell analysed nuclear deterrence as a contest of brinkmanship under incomplete information [152]. Zagare and Kilgour's "perfect deterrence" theory restricts attention to threats that are credible in this sense [153].

7.6 Treaty compliance and international cooperation

States cooperate without a world government. Axelrod and Keohane explained this through features of the game: the payoff structure, the "shadow of the future" in repeated play, the number of actors, and institutions that improve monitoring and reciprocity [154]. Downs, Rocke and Barsoom warned that high observed compliance may simply mean treaties ask little of their members. Deeper cooperation needs stronger enforcement [155]. Barrett's models of self-enforcing environmental agreements show a paradox: where cooperation would yield the greatest gains, a stable treaty can sustain only a small number of members [156]. His comparison of treaties attributes the success of the Montreal Protocol on ozone to provisions such as trade restrictions with non-parties that changed states' incentives to participate [157]. Nordhaus applied similar reasoning to climate policy. He proposed "climate clubs" whose members impose uniform penalty tariffs on non-members, making membership individually rational [158].

8. Biology and the social sciences

Evolutionary game theory reinterprets the elements of a game. Strategies are heritable behaviours, payoffs are reproductive fitness, and equilibrium is reached by natural selection rather than by reasoning [4,159]. The same mathematics, read as cultural imitation or learning, applies to human norms and institutions [72,160].

8.1 Evolutionarily stable strategies and contests over resources

Maynard Smith and Price asked why animal contests over food, territory or mates are so often settled by display rather than all-out fighting. They introduced the evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS): a strategy that, once common in a population, cannot be invaded by any rare mutant [32]. Formally, strategy S is an ESS if for every alternative T, either E(S,S) > E(T,S), or E(S,S) = E(T,S) and E(S,T) > E(T,T) [4]. The standard illustration is the Hawk–Dove game. Two animals contest a resource of value V. Hawks escalate and risk an injury of cost C, while Doves display and retreat if attacked [4].

When C > V neither pure strategy is stable. A population of Doves is invaded by Hawks, and a population of Hawks by Doves. The ESS is a mixture with a proportion p* = V/C of Hawk behaviour [4]. Taylor and Jonker linked the ESS to the replicator dynamics, ṗ = p(1 − p)[fH(p) − fD(p)], in which a strategy's share grows in proportion to how far its fitness exceeds the population average. Field and laboratory systems have matched these models. Male side-blotched lizards come in three throat-colour morphs with different mating strategies. Each morph beats one other and loses to the third, producing the population cycles predicted by a rock–paper–scissors game [161]. Three strains of Escherichia coli, a toxin producer, a sensitive strain and a resistant strain, also play rock–paper–scissors. They coexist only when interactions are local [162]. The ideal free distribution predicts that foragers distribute themselves among patches so that no individual can gain by moving. It is the Nash equilibrium of a game of competition for resources [163]. Milinski found sticklebacks dividing themselves between two feeding sites in proportion to their food input, as the theory predicts [164]. Game theory also explains honest signalling. Zahavi's handicap principle, formalised by Grafen, shows that a signal can be reliable when it is more costly for low-quality signallers to produce [165,166].

8.2 The evolution of cooperation

Cooperation is puzzling because a cooperator pays a cost to benefit others, and in a Prisoner's Dilemma defection is always individually better. Hamilton's kin selection explains cooperation among relatives: an altruistic act is favoured when rb > c, where r is relatedness, b the benefit to the recipient and c the cost to the actor [167]. Trivers proposed reciprocal altruism for repeated interactions between non-relatives [168]. Axelrod tested this idea by inviting strategies for a computer tournament of the repeated Prisoner's Dilemma. Anatol Rapoport's Tit-for-Tat won both tournaments. Tit-for-Tat cooperates on the first move and then copies the opponent's previous move [33,34]. Axelrod and Hamilton argued that a population of reciprocators, once established, resists invasion when future interactions are likely enough [33].

Later work identified further routes to cooperation. With indirect reciprocity, helping builds a reputation that others reward [169]. With network or spatial reciprocity, cooperators survive in clusters [170]. Nowak summarised five mechanisms: kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity and group selection. Each gives a simple condition under which cooperation is favoured [171]. The same dilemmas arise in microbes. Turner and Chao showed that an RNA virus, the bacteriophage φ6, plays a Prisoner's Dilemma when multiple viruses co-infect a cell [70]. Gore, Youk and van Oudenaarden found that yeast cells that break down sucrose outside the cell play a "snowdrift" game with cheaters that do not. This sustains a stable mix of the two [172].

8.3 Social norms and conventions

Lewis analysed conventions, such as which side of the road to drive on, as solutions to recurring coordination games. They are equilibria that persist because everyone expects everyone else to follow them [173]. Ullmann-Margalit distinguished norms that solve coordination problems from norms that solve Prisoner's Dilemmas [174]. Evolutionary models explain which convention a society settles on. Young, and independently Kandori, Mailath and Rob, showed that when agents adapt with occasional random experimentation, the population spends almost all of its time at a "stochastically stable" equilibrium. In 2×2 coordination games this is the risk-dominant equilibrium, which is not always the efficient one [72,175]. Bicchieri defined social norms by agents' conditional preferences. People follow a norm when they expect others to follow it and believe others expect them to [160]. Behavioural experiments across fifteen small-scale societies found that offers in ultimatum and related games vary with local norms of cooperation and market exchange. Strategic behaviour is shaped by culture [176]. These deviations from purely self-interested play are discussed in Limitations and Extensions.

8.4 Collective action and the commons

Olson argued that large groups have difficulty providing public goods. Each member's contribution has little effect, so members are tempted to free-ride [177]. Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" described the overuse of an open-access resource by self-interested users, a multi-player Prisoner's Dilemma [52]. Granovetter's threshold model showed that whether collective action, such as a riot or a strike, takes off can depend on the distribution of individuals' thresholds for joining, not just on average attitudes [178].

Elinor Ostrom showed through field studies that communities often avoid the tragedy. Inshore fisheries, irrigation systems and common pastures are governed by locally devised rules, monitoring and graduated sanctions. She distilled the design principles that long-lived institutions share [35]. Laboratory experiments supported her: communication and the chance to sanction others greatly improve cooperation in common-pool resource games [179]. Fehr and Gächter found that in public-goods games contributions collapse over time without punishment. When subjects can pay to punish free-riders, cooperation is sustained, even when the punisher gains nothing [180]. Ostrom received the 2009 Nobel memorial prize "for her analysis of economic governance, especially the commons" [14].

9. Networks, infrastructure and computing

Engineered systems increasingly consist of independent, self-interested users: drivers, network flows, wireless devices, peers, cloud tenants and miners. Designers cannot dictate their behaviour, so they analyse the equilibria that selfish use produces and redesign the rules to make those equilibria good [9,181]. Sections 9 and 12 of the CS and AI article cover the theory behind this section.

9.1 Network routing and traffic

Wardrop stated in 1952 that in equilibrium all routes actually used between an origin and a destination have equal travel times, and none of the unused routes is faster [49]. Beckmann, McGuire and Winsten showed that this equilibrium solves a convex optimisation problem. This made large traffic networks computable and underpins traffic-assignment models in transport planning [182]. Selfish routing need not be efficient. Braess discovered in 1968 that adding a road to a network can make every driver's journey longer (Figure 10) [51,183].

Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou proposed the price of anarchy to quantify the worst-case loss from selfish behaviour [40]. Roughgarden and Tardos proved that with linear latencies selfish routing costs at most 4/3 of the optimum, whatever the network topology [41]. Youn, Gastner and Jeong estimated the price of anarchy in the road networks of Boston, London and New York. They found that closing certain roads could in principle reduce total travel time, as Braess's paradox predicts [184]. Orda, Rom and Shimkin studied the related case of a few large users, each controlling a large share of the traffic, in communication networks [185].

Congestion pricing makes equilibrium efficient. Pigou observed that a road user ignores the delay imposed on others. A toll equal to this marginal external cost aligns individual and social interests [186]. Vickrey extended the analysis to the timing of trips and argued for tolls that vary with congestion [187].

9.2 Telecommunications and wireless networks

Kelly, Maulloo and Tan interpreted Internet congestion control as a decentralised market. Users choose rates in response to "shadow prices" signalled by congestion, and the system converges to an allocation that is proportionally fair [188]. Johari and Tsitsiklis showed that if users anticipate how their bids affect prices, efficiency falls by at most 25% [189]. Akella and co-authors studied what happens when end hosts tune their TCP congestion control selfishly. They found that the resulting equilibria can be inefficient, depending on the loss-recovery mechanism and the queue management at routers [190]. In wireless networks, transmitters raising their power interfere with one another, a Prisoner's-Dilemma-like externality. Saraydar, Mandayam and Goodman showed that charging for transmit power yields a Nash equilibrium that is Pareto-better than the equilibrium without pricing [191]. MacKenzie and Wicker, and the monograph of Han and co-authors, survey applications to power control, medium access, spectrum sharing and cognitive radio [192,193].

9.3 Distributed computing and peer-to-peer systems

Classical fault-tolerant protocols assume that nodes are either correct or Byzantine (arbitrarily faulty). Aiyer and co-authors added rational nodes that deviate whenever it pays. Their BAR (Byzantine, altruistic, rational) model gave a cooperative backup service that tolerates all three kinds of node [194]. BitTorrent's "choking" algorithm applies tit-for-tat: a peer uploads preferentially to the peers that upload to it, which discourages free-riding [195]. Abraham, Dolev, Gonen and Halpern combined game theory and distributed computing in solution concepts that tolerate both coalitions of deviating players and faulty players. They applied these concepts to secret sharing and multi-party computation [196].

9.4 Resource allocation and scheduling

Nisan and Ronen founded algorithmic mechanism design with the problem of scheduling tasks on machines whose owners privately know their processing times. They showed that truthful mechanisms face limits on how well they can approximate the optimal schedule [181]. In cluster computing, Ghodsi and co-authors proposed dominant resource fairness for allocating several resource types, such as CPU and memory. It is strategy-proof, envy-free and Pareto-efficient, and it gives each user at least what it would get from an equal split. It was implemented in the Mesos cluster manager [197]. In electricity grids, Mohsenian-Rad and co-authors formulated household appliance scheduling as a game among consumers billed according to total load. The Nash equilibrium of their game coincides with the schedule that minimises the system's energy cost, and it can be reached by distributed best responses [198,199]. Supply-chain coordination problems are treated in §4.5.

9.5 Blockchain protocols

Permissionless blockchains rely on incentives to keep anonymous participants honest. Nakamoto's design assumed that rewards for mining make it more profitable to follow the protocol than to attack it, provided honest nodes control a majority of computing power [200]. Game-theoretic analysis has qualified this assumption. Eyal and Sirer showed that a "selfish mining" pool can earn more than its fair share by withholding blocks. The share of hashing power the pool needs depends on how often it wins races between competing blocks. It is one third if it never wins them, and it falls towards zero as its chance of winning approaches one [58]. Eyal's "miner's dilemma" showed that mining pools attacking one another by block withholding face a Prisoner's Dilemma [201]. Carlsten and co-authors argued that once block rewards disappear and miners earn only transaction fees, the protocol becomes unstable [202]. Daian and co-authors documented how bots on decentralised exchanges compete in priority-gas auctions for transaction ordering. The profits available from reordering transactions, which they called miner-extractable value, can threaten consensus [203]. Mechanism design is also used constructively. Roughgarden analysed Ethereum's EIP-1559 transaction-fee mechanism for its incentive properties [204]. Proof-of-stake protocols such as Ouroboros include incentive analyses showing that honest behaviour is an approximate equilibrium [205]. Budish argued that the cost of attacking a proof-of-work chain bounds the value it can secure, and that this places economic limits on its use [206].

11. Applying game theory in practice

Successful applications share a workflow. The analyst identifies the players, their feasible actions, the timing and information structure, and the payoffs. The analyst then chooses a representation and a solution concept that fit (see Representations), computes or characterises equilibria, and tests predictions against data or experiments [2,3]. Three practical lessons recur.

  • Details of the rules matter. Klemperer's review of auction failures and successes concluded that robustness to collusion and to entry deterrence mattered more than theoretical optimality [94]. Market designers likewise had to engineer complementarities and couples into the residency match [56].
  • Real players are boundedly rational. Laboratory evidence shows systematic departures from equilibrium, including limited depth of reasoning, social preferences and learning [207]. Deployed security games therefore incorporate behavioural models of attackers [104]. See Limitations and Extensions.
  • Equilibria may be many, or hard to compute. Coordination games have several equilibria, and selecting among them needs further theory or empirical calibration [72]. Computing equilibria in large games can be intractable, which pushes practitioners towards approximations and learning [9]. See equilibrium selection and complexity.

Game-theoretic designs also raise ethical questions about whose objective the designer optimises and who bears the cost when incentives change behaviour. These are discussed in Limitations and Extensions, §12.

12. Summary

Game theory began as a mathematics of parlour games and oligopoly. It has become a general-purpose language for any setting in which outcomes depend on interdependent choices [1,26]. In economics it explains competition, collusion, bargaining and information. It is also used to design auctions, platforms and matching markets that allocate billions of dollars of resources and thousands of medical residents [36,56]. In security it turns the adversary's adaptiveness into a design input. In AI it supplies both the objectives and the algorithms for agents that compete, cooperate and negotiate. In political science it gives precise meaning to credibility, voting power and the rational causes of war. In biology it explains stable behaviour without assuming conscious reasoning. In engineered networks it measures the cost of selfishness and guides the design of tolls, prices and protocols. In each case the value of the approach comes from making strategic interdependence explicit, so that its consequences can be predicted and the rules can be redesigned.
