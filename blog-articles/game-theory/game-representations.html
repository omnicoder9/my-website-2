Representations of Games

Normal form, extensive form, Bayesian games, and the compact, dynamic and design-oriented models built on them

Technical article · References in the Vancouver style

Abstract. A game-theoretic analysis begins with a choice of representation: a formal object that records who the players are, what they can do, what they know when they act, and how they value outcomes. This article surveys the main representations. It covers the normal (strategic) form with its payoff matrices, the extensive form with its trees and information sets, and Bayesian games with privately known types. It then turns to repeated games, stochastic games, graphical games and mechanism-design models. For each one it gives the history, the formal definition, worked examples with diagrams, the associated solution concepts, and the computational trade-offs. It ends by showing how the representations translate into one another.

1. Why representation matters

Game theory studies interactive decision-making, where each agent's outcome depends on the choices of others as well as its own [1,2]. Before any equilibrium can be computed, the strategic situation has to be written down as a mathematical object. Different objects suit different questions. A payoff matrix is enough to analyse a one-shot simultaneous choice. A tree is needed when the order of moves and the flow of information matter. A model with types is needed when players are unsure about one another's characteristics [3,4].

The choice also has practical consequences. Two representations of the same strategic situation can differ in size by an exponential factor, and they can make different solution concepts natural. The game tree of a sequential interaction, for example, exposes threats that are not credible, while its payoff-matrix version hides them [5,6]. Modern algorithmic game theory therefore treats the representation as a central design choice [7,8].

2. Historical development

Mathematical work on strategic games began early in the twentieth century. Zermelo's 1913 note on chess is usually cited as the first formal theorem of game theory. It analysed finite games of perfect information, although later scholars have shown that its argument differs from the backward-induction proof often attributed to it [9,10]. From 1921 Émile Borel published notes that formulated pure and mixed "methods of play" for two-person games [11]. In 1928 John von Neumann proved the minimax theorem for two-person zero-sum games [12].

The field's founding text, von Neumann and Morgenstern's Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944), set out both a sequential description of games, built from moves and information, and the "normalized" form. In the normalized form each player chooses a complete strategy once and for all. The book also introduced the characteristic-function form for coalitional games and an axiomatic theory of expected utility [1]. Harold Kuhn's 1953 paper recast the sequential description as the game tree with information sets that is used today, and it proved the equivalence of mixed and behaviour strategies under perfect recall [13].

John Nash proved in 1950 and 1951 that every finite game has an equilibrium in mixed strategies [14,15]. Reinhard Selten refined equilibrium for extensive-form games with subgame perfection in 1965 and trembling-hand perfection in 1975 [5,6]. John Harsanyi's three-part paper of 1967 and 1968 made games of incomplete information tractable [16]. Nash, Harsanyi and Selten shared the 1994 Nobel memorial prize in economic sciences "for their pioneering analysis of equilibria in the theory of non-cooperative games" [17].

The dynamic and design-oriented representations developed alongside these. They include Shapley's stochastic games (1953) [18] and the theory of repeated games, for which Robert Aumann shared the 2005 prize [19,20]. Mechanism design, founded by Hurwicz and developed by Maskin and Myerson, was recognised in 2007 [21–23]. Shapley shared the 2012 prize with Alvin Roth for work on stable allocations and market design [24]. Computer science later added compact representations such as graphical games (2001) [25] and complexity results for computing equilibria [26,27].

3. Common ingredients

Every representation of a non-cooperative game specifies four things [2,3]:

  • Players. A finite set N = {1, …, n} of decision-makers.
  • Actions or strategies. What each player can do. A strategy is a complete contingent plan that says what to do in every situation in which the player might have to act.
  • Information. What each player knows when choosing, about earlier moves, about chance events, and about the other players' characteristics.
  • Payoffs. A utility for each player over outcomes. Under the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, preferences over lotteries can be represented by expected utility, so payoffs can be averaged over randomness [1].

Representations differ mainly in how explicitly they record timing and information, and in how compactly they store payoffs. Throughout, the standard assumption is that the structure of the game is common knowledge: every player knows it, knows that the others know it, and so on [3].

4. Normal-form games

4.1 Definition

Definition (normal form). A normal-form or strategic-form game is a tuple G = (N, (Si)i∈N, (ui)i∈N). Here Si is player i's set of pure strategies and ui : S1 × ⋯ × Sn → ℝ is player i's payoff function [3,4].

The players are modelled as choosing simultaneously and independently. "Simultaneous" refers to information, not clock time: no player observes another's choice before making its own. For two players with finite strategy sets, the game is a bimatrix. Rows are player 1's strategies, columns are player 2's, and each cell lists the pair (u1, u2) [8]. Von Neumann and Morgenstern showed that any game described by its sequence of moves can be "normalized" into this form by treating whole strategies as single choices [1].

4.2 Mixed strategies and equilibrium

A mixed strategy σi ∈ Δ(Si) is a probability distribution over pure strategies, and payoffs extend to mixed profiles by expectation [12,15]. A strategy is a best response to the others' strategies σ−i if no other strategy gives player i a higher expected payoff. A profile σ* is a Nash equilibrium if every player's strategy is a best response to the others':

ui(σ*i, σ*−i) ≥ ui(σi, σ*−i)  for all i ∈ N and all σi ∈ Δ(Si).

Nash proved that every finite game has at least one such equilibrium [14,15]. In two-person zero-sum games, where u1 = −u2, equilibrium strategies coincide with von Neumann's minimax strategies, and the game has a unique value [1,12]. Related concepts defined on the normal form include elimination of dominated strategies and Aumann's correlated equilibrium. In a correlated equilibrium, players condition on signals from a common randomising device [36].

(a) Prisoner's Dilemma. Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher devised this game at the RAND Corporation in 1950, and Albert Tucker gave it the story of two prisoners and its name [28]. Defect strictly dominates Cooperate for each player, so (Defect, Defect) is the unique equilibrium. Yet both players would be better off at (Cooperate, Cooperate). The game is the standard model of the tension between individual rationality and collective welfare, and it is the usual starting point for the study of cooperation [37].

(b) Matching Pennies. This zero-sum game, discussed by von Neumann and Morgenstern [1], has no pure-strategy equilibrium. Its unique equilibrium has each player randomising ½–½, and the value of the game is 0. It shows why mixed strategies are needed for Nash's existence theorem to hold.

(c) Battle of the Sexes. This game, introduced by Luce and Raiffa [38], models coordination when the players disagree about which outcome is better. It has two pure equilibria, (B, B) and (S, S). It also has a mixed equilibrium in which player 1 plays B with probability ⅔ and player 2 plays B with probability ⅓. Each of these probabilities makes the other player indifferent: player 2 is indifferent when 1·p = 2(1 − p), so p = ⅔. 4.4 Strengths and limitations

The normal form is the most general and uniform representation, since every finite game can be written in it [1]. It is the natural home of Nash equilibrium and of linear-programming solutions for zero-sum games [8]. It has two weaknesses. First, its size grows exponentially in the number of players: a game with n players and m strategies each needs n·mn numbers [7,25]. Second, it suppresses timing and information. That makes it blind to whether a threat is credible, which motivates the extensive form [5].

5. Extensive-form games

5.1 Definition

Definition (extensive form). A finite extensive-form game, following Kuhn, has the following parts [3,13]:
  • a finite rooted tree whose non-terminal nodes are decision nodes and whose leaves are terminal nodes;
  • a player function that assigns each decision node either to a player in N or to Nature (chance), with fixed probabilities on Nature's branches;
  • for each decision node, a set of actions, one for each outgoing branch;
  • for each player, a partition of that player's nodes into information sets. The player cannot tell nodes in the same set apart, so every node in the set must offer the same actions;
  • a payoff vector at each terminal node.

A game has perfect information if every information set contains a single node, as in chess. Otherwise the information is imperfect. A game has perfect recall if no player ever forgets what it previously knew or did [13]. A pure strategy assigns an action to each of the player's information sets, including sets that the player's own earlier choices make unreachable. A behaviour strategy instead randomises independently at each information set. Kuhn's theorem states that in games with perfect recall, every mixed strategy has a payoff-equivalent behaviour strategy [13].

5.2 Example: sequential entry and credible threats

A potential entrant (E) decides whether to enter a market held by an incumbent (I). If E enters, I either fights a price war or accommodates. Selten used this structure in his "chain-store paradox" [39].

Converting this tree to the normal form gives a 2 × 2 matrix with rows {Out, In} and columns {Fight, Accommodate}. It has two pure Nash equilibria, (In, Accommodate) and (Out, Fight). The second relies on a threat to fight that the incumbent would never carry out once entry had occurred. Selten's subgame-perfect equilibrium requires strategies to form a Nash equilibrium in every subgame, and so rules out (Out, Fight) [5,6]. In finite games of perfect information, backward induction yields such an equilibrium in pure strategies, a result due to Kuhn [13]. This is the main reason to keep the extensive form: the matrix cannot tell the two equilibria apart.

5.3 Information sets: simultaneity inside a tree

Information sets let a tree represent moves that are simultaneous in the informational sense. Figure 3 shows Matching Pennies as a tree. Player 2 moves after player 1 but does not observe player 1's choice, so player 2's two nodes lie in one information set, drawn as a dashed line. The resulting normal form is exactly matrix (b) above. Different trees can therefore have the same normal form, and the extensive form contains strictly more information than the matrix built from it [3,13].

5.4 Chance moves and imperfect information: Kuhn poker

Nature's moves make it possible to model card deals, dice and other exogenous uncertainty. A classic example is Kuhn's simplified poker. Each of two players is dealt one card from a three-card deck, followed by a short round of betting [40]. The deal is a chance node. Each player's information sets group together the nodes that differ only in the opponent's hidden card. Kuhn solved the game analytically and showed that optimal play includes bluffing [40]. Extensive-form games with chance moves and imperfect information remain the standard formal model for poker and similar games [8].

5.5 Refinements and beliefs

When information sets contain several nodes, subgame perfection can lose its force, because a proper subgame cannot start inside a non-singleton information set. Kreps and Wilson's sequential equilibrium adds a belief system, a probability distribution over the nodes of each information set. It requires strategies to be optimal given those beliefs, and the beliefs to be consistent with the strategies [34]. Selten's trembling-hand perfection gives a related refinement by requiring robustness to small mistakes [6].

5.6 Size: normal form versus sequence form

A player's number of pure strategies is the product, over that player's information sets, of the number of actions at each. It therefore grows exponentially with the size of the tree, and "normalizing" a large game is often infeasible [8,41]. Koller, Megiddo and von Stengel introduced the sequence form, whose size is linear in the tree. With it, two-player zero-sum games of perfect recall can be solved by a linear program of polynomial size, and general two-player games by a linear complementarity problem [41,42]. This is a clear case of a choice of representation turning an intractable problem into a tractable one.

6. Bayesian games

6.1 The problem of incomplete information

In many interactions players do not know one another's payoffs, costs, valuations or capabilities. A seller may not know a buyer's willingness to pay, and a firm may not know a rival's costs. Before Harsanyi, such games of incomplete information seemed to require an infinite regress of beliefs: beliefs about payoffs, beliefs about those beliefs, and so on [16,43]. Harsanyi's solution was to summarise everything a player privately knows in that player's type, drawn from a commonly known prior distribution [16].

Definition (Bayesian game). A Bayesian game is a tuple (N, (Ai), (Θi), p, (ui)). Here Ai is player i's action set, Θi its set of types, p a common prior on Θ = Θ1 × ⋯ × Θn, and ui(a, θ) the payoff to i given the action profile a and the type profile θ. Each player learns its own type θi and forms beliefs p(θ−i | θi) about the others' types by Bayes' rule [2,4,16].

A strategy in a Bayesian game is a function si : Θi → Ai, or into mixed actions, that says what each type does. A Bayesian Nash equilibrium is a profile in which each type of each player maximises its expected payoff, given its beliefs about the others' types and the others' strategies [16].

6.2 The Harsanyi transformation

Harsanyi showed that a game of incomplete information can be recast as a game of imperfect information. An initial move by Nature draws the type profile from the prior p, and each player observes only its own component [16]. The result is an ordinary extensive-form game in which a Bayesian Nash equilibrium is simply a Nash equilibrium. Figure 4 applies this to a variant of Battle of the Sexes from Osborne's textbook. Player 1 does not know whether player 2 wants to meet player 1 or to avoid player 1, and assigns probability ½ to each [44].

Solving the example. Consider the profile in which player 1 plays B, type "meet" plays B, and type "avoid" plays S. Given that player 1 plays B, type "meet" gets 1 from B and 0 from S, and type "avoid" gets 0 from B and 2 from S, so both types are best-responding. Player 1's expected payoff from B is ½·2 + ½·0 = 1, and from S it is ½·0 + ½·1 = ½, so B is optimal. The profile (B; B if meet, S if avoid) is therefore a Bayesian Nash equilibrium [44].

6.3 Continuous types: auctions

Bayesian games with continuous types are the basic model of auctions. In a first-price sealed-bid auction with n bidders, independent private values drawn uniformly on [0, 1] and risk neutrality, the symmetric equilibrium bid is b(v) = (n − 1)v / n. Each bidder shades the bid below its value to trade a lower chance of winning against a larger surplus when it wins [30,45]. In a second-price auction, bidding one's true value is a weakly dominant strategy whatever the others' types are [30]. Myerson's revenue-equivalence and optimal-auction results build directly on this Bayesian representation [22].

6.4 Foundations and extensions

Mertens and Zamir showed that Harsanyi's types can be built rigorously from infinite hierarchies of beliefs, using a "universal belief space" [43]. When Bayesian games unfold over time, equilibrium needs beliefs at every information set and requires them to be updated by Bayes' rule where possible. This leads to sequential and perfect Bayesian equilibrium, used in signalling and reputation models [4,34].

7. Other representations

7.1 Repeated games

A repeated game consists of a fixed stage game G, usually in normal form, played in periods t = 0, 1, 2, …. Before each period the players observe the history of past action profiles. With discount factor δ ∈ (0, 1), a player's normalised payoff is [3,4]:

Ui = (1 − δ) Σt≥0 δt ui(at).

Strategies map histories to actions, so even a 2 × 2 stage game produces an infinite extensive-form tree. The representation is still compact, because only G and δ need to be specified.

Cooperation through repetition. In the Prisoner's Dilemma of table (a), consider the "grim trigger" strategy: cooperate until anyone defects, then defect forever. Cooperating forever yields −1 per period. A one-time deviation yields 0 today and then −2 forever, worth (1 − δ)·0 + δ·(−2) = −2δ. Cooperation is therefore sustainable in a subgame-perfect equilibrium exactly when −1 ≥ −2δ, that is, when δ ≥ ½. This follows the standard construction [4,46]. The folk theorem generalises the argument. For sufficiently patient players, any feasible payoff vector that gives each player strictly more than its minmax level can be sustained by a subgame-perfect equilibrium, under a full-dimensionality condition when there are three or more players [35]. The result is called a "folk" theorem because versions of it were widely known before anyone published them [4]. Axelrod's computer tournaments, in which the reciprocal strategy Tit for Tat did well, made the repeated Prisoner's Dilemma a central model of the evolution of cooperation [37]. Aumann and Maschler extended repeated games to incomplete information [19].

7.2 Stochastic games

Shapley's stochastic game lets the stage game change over time in response to the players' actions [18]. It is a tuple (N, S, (Ai), P, (ri), δ). Here S is a set of states, each with its own normal-form game, ri(s, a) is the stage payoff, and P(s′ | s, a) is the probability of moving to state s′ after action profile a in state s. A stochastic game with one state is a repeated game, and a stochastic game with one player is a Markov decision process [8].

Shapley proved that two-player zero-sum stochastic games with discounting, or equivalently with a positive stopping probability in every stage, have a value, and that both players have optimal stationary strategies, which depend only on the current state [18]. Fink extended the existence of stationary equilibria to discounted n-player games [47]. Mertens and Neyman proved that undiscounted zero-sum stochastic games have a value [48]. Stochastic games, which computer scientists call "Markov games", are the standard framework for multi-agent reinforcement learning, starting with Littman's minimax-Q algorithm [49].

7.3 Graphical games and other compact representations

In many large games each player's payoff depends on only a few others, such as neighbours in a network or competitors in a local market. Kearns, Littman and Singh's graphical games exploit this [25]. A graphical game consists of an undirected graph on the players and, for each player i, a local payoff table ui(ai, aN(i)) that depends only on i's own action and the actions of its neighbours N(i). With two actions per player and at most d neighbours, the representation needs on the order of n·2d+1 numbers instead of the n·2n required by the normal form. Kearns et al. gave a polynomial-time algorithm for computing approximate Nash equilibria when the graph is a tree [25].

Other compact representations exploit different kinds of structure. In congestion games, payoffs depend only on how many players use each resource. Rosenthal showed that such games always have a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium [50]. Action-graph games encode context-specific independence among actions rather than players [51]. Multi-agent influence diagrams represent extensive-form games as Bayesian networks with decision and utility nodes, so that their structure can be used to decompose computation [52].

7.4 Mechanism-design models

The representations above take the game as given and predict behaviour. Mechanism design reverses the question: given a goal, which game should be built? [2,7,21] A designer faces n agents with private types θi and wants to implement a social choice function f(θ), such as giving a good to the bidder who values it most. A mechanism (M1, …, Mn, g) specifies a message space for each agent and an outcome function g : M → X. Combined with the agents' types and the prior, it induces a Bayesian game among the agents. The mechanism implements f if an equilibrium of that game yields f(θ) for every type profile [2,7].

Hurwicz defined incentive compatibility, the requirement that agents find it in their interest to act as the mechanism intends [21]. The revelation principle states that any outcome that some mechanism implements in equilibrium can also be implemented by a direct mechanism, in which agents simply report their types and truth-telling is an equilibrium. Gibbard established it for dominant strategies, and Dasgupta, Hammond and Maskin, and Myerson, for Bayesian equilibrium [31–33]. Classic results stated in this representation include:

  • Vickrey's second-price auction, in which truthful bidding is a dominant strategy [30], and its generalisation to the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanisms for efficient public decisions [53,54];
  • Myerson's optimal auction, which characterises revenue-maximising mechanisms with independent private values [22];
  • the Myerson–Satterthwaite theorem, which shows that no bilateral trading mechanism can be at once efficient, incentive compatible, individually rational and budget balanced when the traders' valuation ranges overlap [55].

Hurwicz, Maskin and Myerson received the 2007 Nobel memorial prize "for having laid the foundations of mechanism design theory" [23].

7.5 A note on coalitional (cooperative) form

All the forms above are non-cooperative: they model individual choices. Von Neumann and Morgenstern also introduced the characteristic-function form (N, v). Here v(C) gives the total payoff that each coalition C ⊆ N can secure, and individual strategies are left out of the model [1]. Solution concepts such as the core and the Shapley value are defined on this form [29,56].

9. Computational considerations

The choice of representation determines the input size against which algorithmic complexity is measured, so it shapes what can be computed [7]. Some reference points:

  • Two-player zero-sum normal-form games can be solved in polynomial time by linear programming [8]. The same holds for two-player zero-sum extensive-form games with perfect recall when they are written in sequence form, rather than after an exponential normalization [41,42].
  • Computing a Nash equilibrium of a general normal-form game is PPAD-complete [26], and this remains true for two-player games [27]. So no polynomial-time algorithm is expected even for bimatrix games.
  • Compact representations such as graphical, congestion and action-graph games can be exponentially smaller than the equivalent normal form. When their structure is favourable, as with tree-structured graphs, equilibria can be computed or approximated efficiently [25,50,51].

10. Summary

The normal form reduces a game to strategies and payoffs. It is enough to define Nash equilibrium and to analyse one-shot dilemmas, conflict and coordination. The extensive form adds time, information sets and chance, and it is needed to separate credible strategic behaviour from incredible threats. Bayesian games, through Harsanyi's types and common prior, bring private information into the same framework. Repeated and stochastic games model interactions that continue over time, graphical and related representations make large games tractable, and mechanism design treats the game as something to be designed. A good analysis uses the most economical representation that still records the features that matter: who knows what, when they act, and whose actions affect whom.
