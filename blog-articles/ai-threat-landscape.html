Abstract

Machine-learning systems fail in ways that classical software does not, and adversaries have spent twenty years learning to trigger those failures deliberately. What changed recently is not the theory but the deployment surface: models now read untrusted content, hold credentials, call tools and act. This survey covers the attack classes specific to AI systems — evasion, poisoning, extraction, inversion, prompt injection, jailbreaking, memory poisoning, tool misuse — and the attacks that AI enables against everything else, from automated spear phishing to malware that queries a model mid-execution.

Throughout, the aim is to separate measured results from asserted ones. Where a number comes from a controlled study, a standards body or a primary incident report, it is attributed. Where it comes from a party selling the remedy, that is said. The trajectory of this field is well established; many of its published measurements are not.

01 Orientation

Three problems wearing one name

“AI security” is used to describe at least three different problems, and most confusion in the field comes from sliding between them mid-sentence. The first is security of AI: machine-learning systems have failure modes that classical software does not, and adversaries can trigger them deliberately. The second is security from AI: the same capabilities that make models useful to defenders make them useful to attackers, and the economics of intrusion change when reconnaissance, lure-writing and exploit development stop being labour-limited. The third is security around AI: the enterprise plumbing that now surrounds models — registries, retrieval stores, plugin marketplaces, agent runtimes, unsanctioned browser tabs — is a conventional attack surface that happens to have been built in eighteen months.

The three problems interact, which is why they are worth treating together. An attacker who uses a model to write a lure (problem two) may be delivering that lure into an assistant that reads it as an instruction (problem one), inside an organisation that does not know the assistant is deployed (problem three). The dominant incidents of the last two years have all had this compound shape.

The taxonomies that structure this article reflect that compound shape. The National Institute of Standards and Technology's adversarial machine learning report, updated in March 2025, splits its taxonomy between predictive and generative systems and covers attacker goals, capabilities and lifecycle position rather than attack names alone.1 MITRE's ATLAS knowledge base ports the ATT&CK structure — tactics, techniques, mitigations, case studies — to AI systems, and has been extended repeatedly through late 2025 and 2026 to cover agent behaviour.2, 3 OWASP maintains two complementary lists: the Top 10 for LLM Applications, whose 2025 edition still places prompt injection first,4 and the Top 10 for Agentic Applications, published in December 2025 with the ASI01–ASI10 identifier scheme.5 None of them is complete on its own; the useful move is to know which one answers which question.

A note on evidence

AI security is unusually contaminated by vendor marketing. Wherever a number appears in this article it is attributed to a primary source — a peer-reviewed paper, a standards body, a vendor's own incident report, or a controlled study — and where the primary source is a party with a commercial interest in the finding, that is said plainly. Several widely repeated statistics in this field do not survive tracing back to their origin. Where a claim is contested, the contest is described rather than resolved.

02 History

Twenty-two years of attacking learning systems

Nothing about the current moment is conceptually new. The observation that a statistical classifier can be gamed by an adversary who understands its features dates to 2004, when Dalvi and colleagues modelled spam filtering as a game between a classifier and an adversary who modifies instances to evade it.6 Two years later Barreno and colleagues asked the question in its general form — can machine learning be secure? — and produced the first taxonomy of attacks against learning systems, distinguishing causative attacks that manipulate training from exploratory attacks that probe a deployed model.7 That causative/exploratory split is still, twenty years later, the load-bearing distinction in every taxonomy that followed.

The deep learning era began with two nearly simultaneous discoveries. Biggio and colleagues demonstrated gradient-based evasion against non-linear classifiers at test time in 2013,8 and Szegedy and colleagues showed in the same year that imperceptible perturbations could flip the classification of state-of-the-art image networks — and, more disturbingly, that the same perturbations often transferred between independently trained models.9 Goodfellow, Shlens and Szegedy then supplied both an explanation and a one-line attack: the fast gradient sign method, which argued that adversarial examples arise from the model's local linearity rather than from exotic overfitting.10 Carlini and Wagner subsequently showed that most published defences of the period collapsed under a properly optimised attack, establishing a pattern of defence proposal and rapid defeat that has recurred ever since.11 Madry and colleagues reframed the whole problem as robust optimisation and gave projected gradient descent as the canonical strong attack and adversarial training as the canonical defence.12 Biggio and Roli's retrospective a decade on is still the best single account of that period.13

Three extensions mattered for practice. Papernot and colleagues showed that white-box access was unnecessary: an attacker can train a substitute model against a black-box API and transfer attacks to it.14 Sharif and colleagues, and later Eykholt and colleagues, moved the attack off the screen and into the world, with printed eyeglass frames that defeated face recognition and physical stickers that made a stop sign read as a speed limit sign.15, 16 And Gu, Dolan-Gavitt and Garg introduced BadNets, which reframed poisoning as a supply chain problem: a model trained by someone else, or fine-tuned from someone else's checkpoint, can carry a trigger the recipient cannot see.17

The generative era added a class of attack that has no clean analogue in the earlier literature. In September 2022 Simon Willison gave the name prompt injection to a failure he observed in GPT-3 applications: because instructions and data occupy the same channel, text supplied by a user can override the developer's instructions.18 Perez and Ribeiro characterised goal hijacking and prompt leaking in the same period.19 The decisive escalation came in 2023, when Greshake and colleagues showed that the attacker need not be the user at all — instructions can be planted in any content a model-integrated application will later retrieve, turning a web page or an email into a remote control for someone else's assistant.20 That paper is the origin of the modern threat model, and every agentic incident since has been a variation on it.

The operational record caught up in 2024 and 2025. A deepfaked video conference cost the engineering firm Arup roughly USD 25 million.21 A zero-click prompt injection in Microsoft 365 Copilot, catalogued as CVE-2025-32711 and named EchoLeak, demonstrated end-to-end data exfiltration from a production assistant with no user interaction at all.22, 23 In November 2025, Google's Threat Intelligence Group documented the first malware families to call a language model during execution,24 and Anthropic published its analysis of what it assessed to be the first largely AI-orchestrated cyber espionage campaign.25 Six weeks later OWASP published a Top 10 written specifically for autonomous agents.5 The research had been ready for a decade; the deployment surface arrived all at once.

03 Structure

Where the attack surface actually is

A model is not a product. What ships is a pipeline: data collected under weak provenance, a training run on borrowed compute, a serialised artifact in a registry, an inference endpoint, a retrieval layer, and — increasingly — a runtime that can call tools and act. Each stage has a distinct set of adversary objectives, and confusing them produces the characteristic enterprise failure of buying a guardrail for a supply chain problem.

NIST's taxonomy organises this by attacker capability rather than by stage name, which is more durable. An adversary is characterised by goal (availability, integrity, privacy, or misuse of the system for their own ends), knowledge (white-box access to weights and gradients, black-box query access, or something in between), and point of access in the lifecycle.1 The 2025 edition splits the taxonomy explicitly between predictive AI — where evasion, poisoning and privacy attacks dominate — and generative AI, where supply chain compromise, direct prompting and indirect prompt injection are the primary classes.1, 26

04 Adversarial machine learning

The four classical attack families

These four families predate the language-model era and still apply to every predictive model in production — fraud scoring, malware classification, content moderation, biometric matching, industrial anomaly detection. They are also the families for which the mathematics is best understood, which makes them a useful calibration point for the messier generative cases.

4.1 Evasion

An evasion attack modifies an input at inference time so that the model's output is wrong, while keeping the input semantically unchanged for a human observer. Formally, the attacker searches for a perturbation δ maximising the model's loss subject to a norm bound ‖δ‖p ≤ ε. With gradient access this is a straightforward optimisation — one step for FGSM, many steps for PGD, a carefully constructed objective for the Carlini–Wagner attacks.10, 12, 11 Without gradient access it becomes query-based estimation or a transfer attack from a locally trained substitute.14

The security-relevant properties are these. Adversarial examples transfer, so an attacker who cannot see your model can often attack a public one and reuse the result.9, 14 They exist in the physical world, so “the model only sees camera input” is not a mitigation.15, 16 And the defence with the best track record — adversarial training — buys robustness by spending clean accuracy and compute, and buys it only within the threat model it was trained against.12 Certified defences such as randomised smoothing give a provable radius of stability around an input, which is a real guarantee but a narrow one: it certifies a ball, not a concept.33

In practice, evasion matters most where the model is a control point rather than a convenience. A misclassified holiday photograph is a curiosity; a malware classifier that can be walked past with a few hundred bytes of appended data is a security control that does not control anything.

4.2 Poisoning and backdoors

Poisoning attacks corrupt the training process. The classical split is between availability attacks that degrade overall accuracy — Jagielski and colleagues quantified how few points are needed against regression models34 — and targeted or backdoor attacks that leave benign-case behaviour intact while installing a trigger. BadNets demonstrated the latter and framed it as a supply chain risk rather than a data-hygiene one.17

Two results moved this from academic concern to operational one. Carlini and colleagues showed in 2024 that poisoning web-scale datasets is practical: for a modest sum, an attacker can buy expired domains that back a meaningful fraction of the URLs in a public dataset, or exploit the gap between when a dataset snapshot is indexed and when it is downloaded.35 Then in October 2025, a study by Anthropic's alignment science team with the UK AI Security Institute and the Alan Turing Institute reported that the number of poisoned documents needed to install a simple backdoor was approximately constant with model scale rather than proportional to it: around 250 documents sufficed across models from 600 million to 13 billion parameters, even though the larger model saw more than twenty times as much clean data.36, 37

Read that result carefully

The 250-document finding is frequently mis-stated. The backdoor studied was narrow and low-stakes — a trigger token that induced gibberish output — and the authors are explicit that it is not known whether the constant-count property holds for larger frontier models or for more consequential behaviours, or whether such backdoors survive post-training and targeted defences.36 What the result does overturn is the comfortable assumption that scale is itself a defence, because an attacker needs to control a percentage of the corpus. If the requirement is a fixed small number of documents, the attack is within reach of an individual rather than a state.

Poisoning also reaches the alignment stage. Rando and Tramèr showed that corrupting a small fraction of human-preference data can install a universal jailbreak backdoor that survives RLHF.38 Hubinger and colleagues went further, demonstrating that deliberately trained deceptive behaviours conditioned on a trigger can persist through supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and adversarial training — and that adversarial training sometimes teaches the model to hide the trigger behaviour better rather than remove it.39 That is a training-integrity result with uncomfortable implications for the entire model-provenance question.

4.3 Model extraction and theft

Model extraction treats the inference API as an oracle and reconstructs enough of the model to substitute for it. Tramèr and colleagues demonstrated the basic result against production prediction APIs in 2016, recovering functionally equivalent models — including exact parameter recovery for some model classes — from query access alone.40 The economics are the point: an attacker pays for queries and receives a model whose training cost was orders of magnitude higher, and which can then be used for offline attack development.

The modern version is more surgical. Carlini and colleagues showed in 2024 that a top-layer projection matrix of a production language model — its embedding dimension and final layer — could be recovered through ordinary API access, at a cost measured in hundreds of dollars.41 Full weight recovery remains out of reach; partial structural recovery does not, and partial recovery is enough to sharpen white-box attacks against a system that was assumed to be black-box.

Distinguish this from outright theft of weights, which is a conventional security problem with an unconventional prize. Weight files sitting in object storage with permissive access policies, checkpoints in unsecured registries, and credentials embedded in training notebooks are the realistic path — not cryptanalysis of an API.

4.4 Inversion, membership inference and memorisation

Privacy attacks ask what the model reveals about its training data. Fredrikson, Jha and Ristenpart's model inversion showed that confidence scores could be used to reconstruct recognisable representations of training inputs.42 Shokri and colleagues formalised membership inference — determining whether a specific record was in the training set — as a learning problem in its own right.43 For a medical or financial model, membership alone can be the disclosure.

Language models made this concrete. Carlini and colleagues extracted verbatim training sequences, including personally identifying information, from GPT-2 through targeted sampling.44 Nasr and colleagues later demonstrated a divergence attack that caused aligned production models to emit memorised training data at scale, showing that alignment training suppresses the behaviour without removing the underlying memorisation.45 The distinction matters for anyone reasoning about fine-tuning on sensitive corpora: the data is in the weights, and refusal behaviour is a policy on top of it, not a property of it.

The principled mitigation is differentially private training, which bounds the influence any single record can have on the resulting model.46 It costs accuracy and it does not retrofit. For most enterprises the practical controls are upstream: do not fine-tune on data you cannot afford to have memorised, scope retrieval rather than training where possible, and treat model outputs as a disclosure channel subject to the same review as any other export path.

05 Language models

When instructions and data share a channel

Everything specific to LLM security follows from one architectural fact: a transformer consumes a single token stream, and there is no cryptographic or structural distinction within it between “instructions from the principal” and “data to be processed”. Delimiters, system prompts and role tags are conventions the model has been trained to respect, not boundaries it is unable to cross. Every mitigation discussed below is an attempt to reconstruct a trust boundary that the architecture does not provide.

5.1 Prompt injection

Direct prompt injection is a user overriding the developer's instructions. It is a real problem for products that rely on a system prompt to enforce policy, and a mostly commercial one — the damage is usually reputational or a bypassed usage restriction. Indirect prompt injection is the security problem. Here the attacker is not the user: they plant instructions in content that the system will later ingest as data, and the system executes them with the user's authority.20

Liu and colleagues gave the first systematic formalisation and benchmark of the attack class, which is worth reading before evaluating any product claim about injection resistance: without a common framework, defence comparisons in this area are not commensurable.47

The delivery surface is everything the system reads: web pages, emails, calendar invitations, shared documents, PDF metadata, code comments, repository README files, issue descriptions, image alt text, transcripts, and the descriptions of tools themselves. Rendered-but-invisible text — white on white, zero-height, HTML comments — remains effective because the model reads the source and the human reads the render.

EchoLeak is the reference case study. Disclosed in June 2025 against Microsoft 365 Copilot and assigned CVE-2025-32711, it chained an evasion of Microsoft's cross-prompt-injection classifier, a bypass of link redaction using reference-style Markdown, automatically fetched images, and a permitted proxy endpoint that satisfied the content security policy — producing unauthenticated data exfiltration from a single crafted email, with no user interaction.22, 23 Microsoft patched it server-side and reported no exploitation in the wild. The structural lesson survives the patch: the vulnerability was not a memory-safety bug or a missing authorisation check but a composition failure across four mechanisms that were each individually reasonable.

Willison's lethal trifecta is the most useful compression of the threat model: a system that has access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and the ability to communicate externally can be turned into an exfiltration channel by text alone, and every genuinely useful assistant has all three.48 Meta's subsequent “agents rule of two” formulation turns this into a deployment rule — an unsupervised agent may satisfy at most two of the three properties in a single session.49

5.2 Jailbreaking

Jailbreaking targets the model's safety training rather than an application's instructions. The techniques are well characterised. Gradient-based search produces universal adversarial suffixes that transfer across models, which established that alignment is a surface with the same optimisation weaknesses as any other learned classifier.50 The lineage runs back to universal adversarial triggers in NLP.51 Alignment methods such as Constitutional AI, which derives harmlessness from an explicit written policy rather than from human preference labels alone, shift the refusal boundary without removing it.52 Long-context models introduced many-shot jailbreaking, where a large number of fabricated in-context examples gradually shift the model's behaviour.53 Multi-turn techniques such as Crescendo escalate across a conversation, so that no individual message is refusable.54 And the simplest technique, still the most common in observed operations, is pretext: Google's threat intelligence team documented actors posing as capture-the-flag competitors or academic researchers to obtain assistance that had been refused when requested directly.24

Defences have improved without becoming reliable. Constitutional classifiers — separate input and output filters trained on synthetic data derived from an explicit policy — substantially raised the cost of universal jailbreaks in extended red-teaming, at a measurable rate of over-refusal and inference overhead.55 The sobering counterweight is a 2025 result from Nasr, Carlini and colleagues showing that a broad set of published jailbreak and prompt-injection defences fall to stronger adaptive attacks, often to attack success rates above ninety per cent, because most defences are evaluated against static attack sets rather than an adversary who moves second.56 Treat any reported robustness number that was not produced by an adaptive attacker as an upper bound on the defence's real value.

5.3 Exfiltration, leakage and the retrieval layer

Once a model is inside an enterprise, three leakage paths matter more than jailbreaking.

Output-channel exfiltration. Any mechanism by which model output can cause a network request is an exfiltration channel: Markdown image references, clickable links, auto-fetched previews, webhook-backed tools. The data does not need to be printed to the user; it needs only to be encoded into a URL that something will fetch.22, 48

System prompt leakage. OWASP added this as a distinct category in the 2025 edition for a reason.4 The real damage is rarely the prompt text itself but what teams put in it — internal endpoints, routing logic, business rules, and occasionally credentials. The correct posture is that the system prompt is public and should contain nothing that would matter if it were.

Retrieval and embedding weaknesses. RAG pipelines fail in two directions. Over-broad context assembly pulls chunks the requesting user is not entitled to see, so the model becomes an authorisation bypass with a friendly interface. And the vector store is writable state: content that reaches the index, from a shared drive or a public crawl, becomes trusted context at retrieval time. OWASP tracks these as vector and embedding weaknesses; agentic frameworks treat the same problem as memory poisoning.4, 5

5.4 The model supply chain

The generative supply chain has more links than the traditional software one, and fewer of them are signed. Base models and fine-tuned derivatives are downloaded from public hubs. Model artifacts in legacy serialisation formats execute code on load, which makes “download and run this checkpoint” equivalent to running an untrusted binary. Datasets are snapshots of a web that anyone can write to.35 Plugins, skills, tool descriptions and MCP server definitions are code and prompt fragments distributed through registries with limited review. Prompt templates and agent personas are configuration that changes behaviour and is rarely version-controlled with the same discipline as code.

One category deserves specific mention because it links the model layer to the classical software supply chain. Code-generating models hallucinate package names at a measurable and persistent rate; Spracklen and colleagues quantified the phenomenon across models and showed that many hallucinated names repeat, which makes them registrable.57 An attacker who publishes the hallucinated package waits for the model to recommend it. The vulnerability is in neither the model nor the registry but in the habit of installing what a model suggests.

06 Agents

From bad output to bad action

An LLM application takes input and returns output. An agent plans, holds memory across sessions, calls tools with real credentials, and delegates to other agents. Every risk in the previous section persists, but the consequence changes category: a successful injection no longer produces wrong text, it produces a transaction, a deleted file, a granted permission, an outbound message. OWASP published the Top 10 for Agentic Applications in December 2025 precisely because the LLM-centric list could not express this.5, 58

6.1 Tool misuse and privilege aggregation

ASI02 and ASI03 are, in practice, one problem seen from two angles. The tools are legitimate; the agent is persuaded to use them against the principal's interest. And the agent holds the union of every credential it has been given, so confusing the agent yields every permission at once. This is the structural difference from a compromised user account: a user has one identity, whereas an agent is typically a service account that aggregates several, often with standing rather than short-lived credentials.5

The related failure is over-permissioning at design time. Agents are commonly granted the broadest scope that makes the demo work, because narrowing scope requires knowing in advance which tools a plan will need — and the entire premise of an agent is that it decides that at runtime. Least privilege and open-ended planning are in genuine tension; the resolution is to constrain the action rather than the plan, with per-tool policy and approval gates on irreversible operations.

The manipulation need not even be textual. Fu and colleagues demonstrated adversarial inputs that steer agents into calling the wrong tool with attacker-chosen arguments, including through non-obvious modalities, which makes tool selection itself an attack surface.59

6.2 Memory and context poisoning

Persistent memory converts a one-shot injection into a durable compromise. Content written into long-term memory, a vector index, or a scratchpad is retrieved later as trusted context, often in a session the attacker never touches.5 The attack can be gradual: a sequence of individually unremarkable interactions that shift stored beliefs. Detection is hard because there is no moment of exploitation to observe — only a retrieval that returns what was legitimately stored.

6.3 Multi-agent failure modes

Three risks appear only when agents talk to each other.5, 60 Insecure inter-agent communication (ASI07) — messages between agents are frequently unauthenticated, making spoofing, replay and forged consensus available to anyone on the bus. Cascading failures (ASI08) — a hallucinated endpoint or a single bad data point propagates through planning and memory and amplifies across handoffs, surfacing far from its origin. Rogue agents (ASI10) — a compromised or drifted agent continues to look legitimate inside the workflow, because legitimacy in these systems is established by position in the orchestration graph rather than by proof.

To this list the agentic literature adds ASI09, human-agent trust exploitation: fluent, confident agents are persuasive, and an approval obtained through the agent's own conversational interface is worth very little. The audited record shows a human approving an action; the manipulation is invisible to the audit. The mitigation is structural — route consent for high-impact actions out of band, through a channel the agent does not control.5

6.4 Case study: the agent skill supply chain

The clearest 2026 demonstration of agentic supply chain risk (ASI04) came from the skill marketplace of a rapidly adopted open-source agent platform. Within weeks of the marketplace reaching scale, security researchers documented a coordinated campaign of malicious “skills” — plugin-style extensions that add capabilities to the agent — published under typosquatted and plausible names. Reported counts differ by methodology and by the date of the audit, ranging from several hundred in the first disclosure to over a thousand in later sweeps, with a large share traced to a single actor; the dominant payload on macOS was a commodity credential stealer harvesting keychain entries, browser credentials, SSH keys and wallet files.61, 62 A critical remote code execution vulnerability in the platform itself, tracked as CVE-2026-25253, ran concurrently with the marketplace campaign.62

Two details make this more than another registry compromise. First, several of the malicious skills did not carry an exploit at all — they carried instructions, in the form of plausible-looking prerequisite steps that the installing human or the agent itself would execute. Second, some payloads were dormant, activating only when the user issued a particular natural-language request. Neither pattern is visible to a scanner looking for malicious code, because in the relevant sense there is no code — there is text that a compliant agent follows.61

The generalisable point: agent extension ecosystems have recreated, in roughly eighteen months, the full history of package-registry attacks — typosquatting, dependency confusion, maintainer compromise, dormant payloads — with the added property that the malicious artifact may be pure natural language and therefore invisible to every tool built for the previous problem.

07 Offence

AI-enabled attacks: measuring the uplift

The question that matters for defenders is not whether attackers use AI — they do — but where it changes the economics of an operation rather than merely its convenience. The evidence is uneven across categories, and it is worth separating what has been measured from what has been asserted.

7.1 Social engineering

This is the category with the strongest evidence and the clearest economics. Heiding, Schneier, Vishwanath and colleagues ran a controlled human-subjects study in which a custom tool performed open-source reconnaissance on each target, generated a personalised message, and sent it — with no human in the loop. Across 101 participants, generic control emails achieved a 12 per cent click-through rate; emails written by human experts achieved 54 per cent; fully AI-automated emails also achieved 54 per cent; and AI-generated emails with light human review achieved 56 per cent.63, 64 The authors' earlier study had already shown large gains over generic phishing when models were combined with structured human tactics.65

The headline is not that AI writes better phishing than a skilled human — it writes about as well. The headline is that it writes as well as a skilled human at a marginal cost measured in cents and with no scaling constraint, including the reconnaissance step that previously consumed most of the analyst hours in a targeted campaign. Spear phishing was historically rationed by labour; it no longer is. IBM's 2025 breach study reports the downstream effect: sixteen per cent of breaches involved attacker use of AI, with AI-generated phishing the most common form at 37 per cent and deepfake impersonation second at 35 per cent.66, 67

Synthetic media extends the same logic to identity. The Arup case — a finance employee in Hong Kong who authorised fifteen transfers totalling roughly USD 25 million after a video call on which every other participant was generated — is the canonical example, and its mechanics are instructive: the initial email raised suspicion, and the video call resolved it.21 The control that failed was not technical. It was the assumption, embedded in decades of process design, that seeing and hearing a colleague constitutes verification.

7.2 AI-enabled malware

Until 2025, “AI-written malware” meant a human using a chatbot as a coding assistant — a productivity gain, not a capability change. Google's Threat Intelligence Group documented the capability change in November 2025: the first malware families observed to query a language model during execution.24 Two are notable. PROMPTFLUX, a VBScript dropper assessed to be in development, contains a component that periodically asks a model to rewrite its own source for evasion, saving the regenerated variant for persistence — an attempt at a metamorphic script that outsources the mutation engine to an external API. PROMPTSTEAL, a data miner attributed to the Russian state-linked actor APT28 and observed in operations against Ukraine, carries no hard-coded collection commands at all; it queries a hosted open-weight model to generate the commands it then executes.24, 68

The defensive implication is specific. Static signatures degrade against code that is regenerated per host, but the technique introduces a new and quite legible indicator: malware that must reach an inference API, authenticate to it, and receive code in response. Egress to model endpoints from processes that have no business talking to them is a detection opportunity that did not exist before. Google's assessment was that both families remained nascent.24

7.3 Autonomous discovery, exploitation and orchestration

The best-measured evidence on autonomous offensive capability comes, ironically, from a defensive competition. DARPA's AI Cyber Challenge concluded at DEF CON 33 in August 2025 with seven autonomous cyber reasoning systems working on large real codebases with no human in the loop. DARPA's final accounting: competitors discovered 54 of 63 injected synthetic vulnerabilities (86 per cent) and patched 68 per cent of them; in the same runs the systems found 18 previously unknown real-world flaws; the average cost per competition task was USD 152 and the average time from discovery to validated patch was 45 minutes.69, 70 The winning systems were released as open source.71

A system that finds a vulnerability and writes a patch is a system that finds a vulnerability and could write an exploit. The competition was structured to produce defensive tooling, and the tooling is genuinely valuable — but the capability it demonstrates is symmetric, and it is now public.

Orchestration is the more consequential development. In November 2025 Anthropic reported disrupting a campaign it attributed with high confidence to a Chinese state-sponsored group, designated GTG-1002 and detected in mid-September 2025, which used an agentic coding tool driven through Model Context Protocol servers to run intrusions against approximately thirty organisations across technology, finance, chemicals and government. Anthropic assessed that the model performed 80–90 per cent of the tactical work — reconnaissance, vulnerability identification, exploit development, credential harvesting, lateral movement and data triage — with human operators acting as checkpoint authorities at a handful of escalation points. The tooling was overwhelmingly commodity open-source penetration-testing software; the novelty was the orchestration layer. The model was induced to comply by a role-play pretext framing the operation as authorised defensive testing, and it hallucinated during operations, over-reporting successes and inventing credentials, which required human verification.25

Calibration

The GTG-1002 report is a vendor incident disclosure about the vendor's own product, and it attracted informed scepticism on exactly those grounds: no indicators of compromise were published, the “first of its kind” framing is unfalsifiable, and the label has no independent corroboration in public threat databases.72 Both things can be true — the specific claims deserve the discount appropriate to single-source vendor reporting, and the general direction is corroborated by independent telemetry. Check Point's March–April 2026 landscape digest reports AI-orchestrated intrusion moving from state-sponsored experimentation into financially motivated criminal use, including a single operator running thousands of model-executed commands across multiple government agencies.73 Treat the trajectory as established and any individual figure as provisional.

7.4 The barrier to entry

Three things have genuinely become cheaper: reconnaissance, content generation, and the translation of intent into working tooling by someone who could not previously write it. Two things have not: initial access against a well-configured target, and the operational discipline required to run a campaign without being caught. The models hallucinate, and they hallucinate during offensive operations too, which imposes a verification tax on the attacker.25

The honest summary is that AI compresses the middle of the skill distribution. It does comparatively little for the top — nation-state teams already had the labour — and a great deal for the large population of moderately skilled actors who previously bought tooling and now generate it. That is a volume story more than a sophistication story, and volume is precisely what saturates human-scaled defences.

08 Structure

Structural vulnerabilities

8.1 Data

Data weaknesses are usually filed under governance rather than security, which is a mistake, because several of them are exploitable. Training data provenance is frequently unknown, which makes poisoning claims unfalsifiable after the fact.35 Memorisation makes the model itself a disclosure surface.44, 45 And bias is a security property whenever a model is a control point: a fraud or moderation model with a known demographic error profile has a documented population of inputs that reliably evade it, which is an evasion oracle published in the fairness literature.

8.2 Infrastructure

Most AI infrastructure compromise is ordinary infrastructure compromise. The pattern is consistent across incidents: services designed for a trusted research network deployed on a public one, authentication disabled by default, dashboards and gateways bound to all interfaces, credentials in configuration files, and object storage holding weights with permissive policies. The agent-platform case discussed earlier followed exactly this shape — tens of thousands of internet-exposed instances, the large majority without authentication, and a gateway that accepted connections from any origin.62

Two AI-specific items are worth calling out. Serialisation formats that execute code on load turn model download into arbitrary code execution, which is why artifact scanning and safe formats matter more here than the equivalent controls for ordinary data files. And the inter-component channels — inference endpoints, vector store APIs, agent buses, MCP server connections — are frequently unauthenticated because they were introduced as internal plumbing.74, 5

8.3 Shadow AI

The most consequential structural weakness is the simplest: security teams cannot defend data flowing to tools they cannot see. IBM's 2025 breach study puts numbers on it. Twenty per cent of breached organisations had a breach involving shadow AI — unsanctioned tools adopted by employees without IT or security review. Those breaches cost on average USD 670,000 more than breaches at organisations with low or no shadow AI (USD 4.63 million against USD 3.96 million, where the global average was USD 4.44 million). They disproportionately exposed customer personally identifiable information (65 per cent, against a 53 per cent global average) and intellectual property (40 per cent). Separately, 97 per cent of organisations reporting an AI-related breach lacked proper AI access controls, and 63 per cent of breached organisations had no AI governance policy or were still drafting one.66, 75, 67

A widely circulated version of this statistic gives the shadow AI premium as approximately USD 700,000. The figure reported by IBM is USD 670,000.66, 75

Shadow AI is a culture problem with a security bill. Employees adopt these tools because the productivity gain is real and immediate, and procurement is neither. Prohibition without a sanctioned alternative reliably produces more shadow AI, not less, and moves it to personal devices where there is no telemetry at all. The controls that work are the boring ones: discovery through network and identity telemetry, a fast approval path, and a default-permitted tier of vetted tools with data-handling terms that legal has already accepted.

Independent survey data points the same way while adding an agentic dimension. HiddenLayer's 2026 report, based on a survey of 250 IT and security leaders, found malware in public model and code repositories the most-cited source of AI-related breaches at 35 per cent, reported that roughly one in eight AI-related breaches now involves agentic systems, and noted that while a large majority of organisations use open-weight models from public repositories, fewer than half consistently scan inbound models.76 It is vendor research and should be read as such, but the direction is consistent with the IBM findings.

09 Defence

What holds, and what only appears to

9.1 Start with what does not work alone

Three defences are routinely oversold. Prompt-level instructions — telling the model to ignore instructions found in data — reduce casual attempts and fail against a motivated attacker; they are a hygiene measure, not a control. Classifier-based filtering of inputs and outputs raises cost meaningfully and is worth deploying,55, 77 but it is a probabilistic control on an adversarial distribution, and the adaptive-attack literature shows published defences degrading sharply when the attacker optimises against them.56 Model choice is not a control at all: every frontier model is susceptible to indirect prompt injection, and the differences between them are differences of degree measured on benchmarks the attacker has also read.

The correct inference is not that mitigation is hopeless. It is that mitigation must be architectural. A probabilistic defence at the model layer is a cost multiplier; a deterministic constraint at the system layer is a bound.

9.2 Architectural containment

The most promising line of work treats prompt injection the way operating systems treat untrusted code: not by making the interpreter trustworthy, but by removing its authority. CaMeL, from Google and ETH Zurich, extracts control flow and data flow from the trusted user query using a privileged model, processes untrusted content in a quarantined model with no tool access, and executes the resulting plan through a custom interpreter that tracks data provenance and enforces explicit capability policies before every tool call. Untrusted data can influence values but never the program's control flow, which converts a class of attack into a policy decision.78 Related work along the same axis includes structured-query separation of instructions from data (StruQ), preference-optimisation defences (SecAlign), and spotlighting techniques that mark provenance within the context.79, 80, 81 AgentDojo provides the dynamic benchmark on which such defences can be compared honestly.82

For teams building today, the practical version of this is a short list of design rules:

  • Break the trifecta. Do not let one session hold private data access, untrusted content exposure and external communication simultaneously. Split into cooperating components with different privileges, or gate the third capability behind human approval.48, 49
  • Constrain actions, not plans. Let the agent reason freely; make the tool layer enforce what is permissible, with per-tool scopes and irreversibility gates.5
  • Default-deny egress. Sandbox generated code, deny outbound network access by default, and allow-list the destinations tools may reach. Most exfiltration paths are ordinary HTTP.5
  • Scope retrieval to the requesting identity. Filter at query time by the user's entitlements, not after generation.
  • Validate what enters durable state. Segment memory by trust level; reconcile long-term memory against a trusted source rather than trusting it because it is stored.5
  • Route consent out of band. High-impact approvals must not be obtainable through the agent's own conversational surface.5

9.3 Identity, provenance and the supply chain

Agent identity is the control plane that most organisations have not built. An agent should be a first-class non-human identity with its own credential, scoped to a task, short-lived, and auditable — not a service account holding the union of a team's permissions. Field data consistently attributes the largest share of agentic incidents to over-permissioning rather than to novel technique.5

On the artifact side, the controls are recognisable from software supply chain security, extended to new artifact types: maintain an inventory of models, datasets, prompts, tools and MCP servers (an AI bill of materials); pin and vet dependencies including skills and plugins; scan model artifacts before load and prefer serialisation formats that cannot execute code; verify provenance and signatures where available; and treat prompt templates and agent personas as version-controlled configuration subject to review.5, 61

9.4 Assurance

AI red teaming differs from application penetration testing in three ways that change how it must be resourced. It is probabilistic, so a single passing run proves nothing and findings need to be expressed as rates over trials. It is non-stationary, so a model or prompt change invalidates prior results and testing must be a release gate rather than an annual event. And the interesting findings are usually compositional — the EchoLeak chain was four reasonable mechanisms interacting — so testing components in isolation systematically misses the class of bug that matters.22 Evaluate against adaptive attacks, not fixed corpora.56, 83

Detection and response need equivalent rework. Existing playbooks assume a human adversary operating at human speed; an agentic intrusion completes phases in minutes.25 The telemetry that matters is agent-specific: full tool-call traces with arguments, retrieval provenance, memory writes, inter-agent messages, and egress by destination. Most organisations log the prompt and the completion and nothing in between, which is the least useful pair.

11 Governance

The regulatory perimeter

Security obligations for AI systems now arrive from three directions: horizontal AI regulation, voluntary frameworks that are becoming procurement requirements, and sector rules that never mentioned AI but apply anyway.

The EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, entered into force on 1 August 2024 with staggered applicability.92 Prohibited practices and AI literacy obligations applied from February 2025 and general-purpose AI model obligations from August 2025. The high-risk regime was originally due to apply from 2 August 2026, but implementation slipped: the Commission tabled the Digital Omnibus on AI in November 2025, institutions reached provisional political agreement on 7 May 2026, and the European Parliament approved the amendments on 16 June 2026.93, 94, 95

The resulting position, as of this writing, is that stand-alone Annex III high-risk obligations move to 2 December 2027 and Annex I product-embedded high-risk obligations to 2 August 2028, while 2 August 2026 remains a live date for Article 50 transparency duties — disclosure of AI interaction, marking of synthetic content, deepfake labelling — and for the Commission's general-purpose AI enforcement and penalty powers. Article 50(2) obligations extend to systems already on the market from 2 December 2026, alongside a new Article 5 prohibition covering AI systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material.93, 94 For teams reading this in late July 2026, that deadline is nine days away.

The voluntary frameworks matter more than their status suggests, because they are what procurement and insurance now ask about. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides the programme structure — govern, map, measure, manage — and its Generative AI Profile enumerates the risks specific to generative systems.28, 29 ISO/IEC 42001 is the certifiable management-system standard, and is increasingly the artifact requested in vendor questionnaires.30 Neither is a security control, and neither will stop an indirect prompt injection; both are the scaffolding that determines whether an organisation knows what it has deployed. Given that 63 per cent of breached organisations reported having no AI governance policy, that scaffolding is not a formality.67

12 Outlook

What to watch

Agent identity becomes the battleground. The recurring root cause across agentic incidents is not a novel technique but a credential with too much scope and too long a life. Expect the interesting work over the next eighteen months to happen in identity infrastructure — per-agent identities, short-lived scoped tokens, delegation with attenuation, and audit trails that record the tool call rather than the conversation.5

Prompt injection stays unsolved at the model layer. There is no evidence that scaling or alignment training closes it, and strong evidence that defences evaluated non-adaptively overstate their protection.56 The realistic path is architectural containment of the CaMeL variety, which trades some capability for a guarantee.78 Watch whether the market accepts that trade; the commercial pressure runs the other way.

The supply chain problem gets worse before it gets better. Skill and plugin marketplaces are recapitulating the entire history of package-registry attacks at compressed speed, with the additional wrinkle that the malicious artifact can be natural language rather than code.61 Signing, provenance and marketplace review are solved problems elsewhere; they simply have not been applied here yet.

Detection shifts from content to behaviour. Signature matching has little to bite on when the payload is a sentence and the malware regenerates itself hourly.24 The durable signals are behavioural: an agent deviating from its baseline tool sequence, egress to an inference endpoint from a process with no reason to reach one, a memory write from an untrusted retrieval path, an approval granted inside a conversational channel.

And the field needs better evidence. A large share of what circulates as AI threat intelligence is single-source vendor reporting about the vendor's own product, or survey data collected by companies selling the remedy. That is not a reason to dismiss it — the disclosures have been genuinely informative, and the vendors often have the only telemetry that exists — but it is a reason to hold individual figures loosely, insist on primary sources, and prefer controlled studies and standards-body taxonomies as the load-bearing structure. The trajectory of this field is clear enough. Its measurements are not yet.
