EMERGING TRENDS IN CONSTRUCTION

Where does the repetition come from?

Twenty technologies are supposed to be changing how buildings get made. Almost all of them are attempts to solve the same problem, and it is not a technical one: construction is an industry that builds a prototype every time, and learning curves need something to repeat. This is an audit of forty practices against that single question — what recurs, where the learning is kept, and how many repeats it takes before the carrier pays for itself.

SECTION 01The question underneath all twenty

A car plant and a building site do the same thing. Both convert drawings, components and labour into an object that did not exist before. They do it at wildly different costs per unit of output, and the gap has been widening for fifty years. In the United States, labour productivity in manufacturing rose steadily from 1950 to 2020 while construction productivity fell below where it started. That is not a rounding error or a measurement artefact; it survives every attempt to explain it away with deflators.106,111,112

The usual list of remedies is now familiar enough to have its own conference circuit. Building information modelling. Digital twins. Generative design. AI estimating and scheduling. Robots. Autonomous plant. Drones. LiDAR. Printed buildings. Modular and prefabricated construction. Mass timber. Smart buildings. Low-carbon concrete. Electrified equipment. Data platforms. Computer vision on site. Automated code checking. Resilience. Circularity. Design for disassembly. Twenty headings, each with its own vendors, its own pilot projects and its own disappointed clients.

Treated as twenty separate technologies they are hard to think about. Treated as twenty answers to one question they become tractable, and the question is this. A learning curve needs something to repeat. Where, in construction, does the repetition come from?

The learning curve is not a metaphor. Wright measured it in 1936 on airframes: unit cost fell by a fixed percentage every time cumulative output doubled.102 The relationship held across industries, across decades, and across every attempt to attribute it to something else.103,104,160 It has been measured inside construction too, on repeated operations within a single project.105 The mechanism is not mysterious. Doing a thing again is cheaper than doing it the first time, provided something survives between the two attempts to carry what was learned.

That proviso is where construction lives. The industry is organised, almost perfectly, to destroy the thing that survives. A project is a temporary coalition of firms assembled for one building, on one site, under one contract, which dissolves at practical completion. The next building has a different client, a different site, a different consultant team, a different set of subcontractors and a different set of statutory constraints. Whatever the coalition learned goes home in the heads of people who will not work together again.116,158

So the twenty trends are not twenty ideas about technology. They are twenty bids to manufacture a carrier: something that outlives the project and holds what the project learned. Some of those bids build a carrier out of documents, some out of files, some out of machinery, some out of a product with a type approval on it. They differ in how much of the work the carrier does, in what it costs to build, and above all in how many repetitions the carrier will actually get before somebody throws it away.

What follows audits forty named practices against that question. Each gets the same four fields, the same two-axis coding, and the same gauge: a break-even curve showing how many repeats the practice needs against how many it is likely to get. Nine experiments run at build time supply every number quoted. The source is at the end, and it reproduces exactly.

SECTION 02What the industry is actually short of

It is worth being precise about the productivity story, because the precision points at the answer. The best recent account documents a Kuznets curve: homes built per American construction worker were flat from 1900 to 1940, boomed from 1940 to 1970, and then fell continuously after 1970.45 The middle segment matters more than the ends. For thirty years construction got rapidly better at building houses. Nothing about the physics of putting up a building makes progress impossible, because progress demonstrably happened, within living memory, in the same country.

The explanation offered is structural rather than technological. Local land-use controls cap the size of projects. Capping project size caps the equilibrium size of the firms that build them. Small firms cannot capture scale economies and have weak incentives to invest in process innovation, because they will not be around to amortise it over enough buildings. The authors put a number on the counterfactual: if American homebuilders had the firm-size distribution of American manufacturers, homebuilding would be roughly sixty per cent more productive.45

Read that mechanism carefully and it is a statement about repetition. Regulation limits project size, project size limits firm size, and firm size limits the number of times any given method gets used by the organisation that paid to develop it. The productivity problem is a volume problem wearing a planning-permission costume.

Experiment 1 puts arithmetic on how severe that is. Take a bespoke baseline of 1.00 per unit and an industrialised alternative that costs p on its first unit and improves along a Crawford unit curve. The break-even is the cumulative volume at which the alternative has paid back everything the first unit cost extra.

The curves are steep and the axis is logarithmic. A carrier costing twice the conventional price on its first unit, improving at ten per cent per doubling, needs 277 repeats before it has paid for itself. The same carrier improving at twenty per cent per doubling needs 24. That is a factor of 11.5 from a change in the learning rate that no manager directly controls.

Push the first-unit penalty from twice to five times and the same ten-per-cent learner needs 117,329 repeats: 424 times as many for a 2.5-fold increase in the entry cost. And a slow learner at five per cent per doubling, which is what a practice looks like when every new adopter starts nearly fresh, needs 1,376.6 times the volume of a fast one at the same entry cost.

WHY THIS SHAPE MATTERS Break-even is not sensitive to the technology. It is violently sensitive to two things the technology does not control: how expensive the first instance is, and how fast the learning compounds. A practice can be technically excellent and still sit permanently on the wrong side of its own break-even because the population it can amortise over is too small.

The horizontal lines on the figure are the populations available. A single project supplies perhaps thirty repetitions of anything. A firm might supply a few hundred over the life of a method. A supply chain or a product line reaches ten thousand. A code or a standard reaches the whole industry. Those four ceilings, rather than any property of the technology, decide which practices can ever pay.

The wider productivity literature reaches the same place from a different direction. What separates productive firms from unproductive ones in the same industry is mostly managerial practice, learning and the durability of internal routines rather than access to technology.107 Nelson and Winter's account of the firm as a repository of routines is almost a definition of a carrier: a routine is knowledge that persists because it is enacted repeatedly.162 Rosenberg's point about learning by using is the same one again from the buyer's side.161 All three require the organisation to survive long enough, and at sufficient volume, for the routine to be enacted.

This is why the second axis of the coding below is not technical at all. It is a question about ownership: who holds the carrier, and how many times will they get to use it?

SECTION 03The repetition ladder

If the question is what survives between instances, the useful classification is by what the survivor is made of. Six rungs, ordered by how much of the work the carrier performs without anyone having to re-derive it.

R0 — nothing recurs. Every instance is reconstructed. This is the baseline condition of custom construction and it is the thing all twenty trends are trying to leave. It is worth noticing that no practice on the list below is coded R0, because leaving R0 is what makes something a trend in the first place.

R1 — the person recurs. The learning lives in the hands and judgement of a crew. This is the oldest carrier in the building trades and by far the most effective one per unit of investment. It is also uninsurable, unauditable and mortal. Polanyi called the general case tacit knowing: we know more than we can tell.163,164 A gang that has framed four hundred identical houses is a learning curve with legs, and it walks off site at the end of the job.

R2 — the procedure recurs. A method statement, a specification, a mix design, a checklist, a rule of measurement. Copyable, transmissible, cheap. It still requires someone competent to execute it, so it captures only the part of the learning that can be written down. Most of what construction has successfully industrialised in the past century sits here, and it is chronically underrated because a document is not exciting.

R3 — the digital asset recurs. A model, an object library, a rule set, a trained network, a dataset. Copyable at essentially zero marginal cost, which is the great advantage, and orphanable at essentially zero cost too, which is the matching defect. A file with no owner and no maintenance budget decays into a liability within one schema version.

R4 — the tool recurs. A jig, a mould, a press, a print head, a robot cell. The carrier now performs the motion rather than describing it, which removes an entire class of execution variance. It also has a purchase price, a footprint, a maintenance contract and an idle cost. Tools do not amortise on enthusiasm.

R5 — the product recurs. An identical physical unit coming off a line with a type approval attached, so that a designer can specify it without testing it and an inspector can accept it without inspecting its manufacture. This is the highest rung and the one that tolerates variety least. Everything above R3 trades flexibility for repeatability, and R5 trades the most.

The ladder is monotone in two respects that matter. Higher rungs carry more of the work. Higher rungs also cost more to build, which means their break-even volumes are larger. A practice that climbs the ladder without securing the volume to match has made itself worse off, and several of the forty have done exactly that.

The second axis: who gets to use it again

A carrier only earns out over the population that shares it. Four scopes, and the ceiling each one plausibly supplies:

Project (P) — the carrier dies at handover. Perhaps thirty repetitions. Firm (F) — it survives inside one organisation's order book. A few hundred. Supply chain (S) — it lives in a supplier, a product line or a trade body, and is shared across many clients. Ten thousand. Universal (U) — it is written into a code, a standard or a public model, and everybody who builds gets it whether they wanted it or not. A million and upwards.

Those ceilings are order-of-magnitude estimates, and they are stated as such. What they are not is arbitrary: they are separated by a decade or so each, which is the resolution at which the break-even arithmetic actually bites. The gauge on every plate below plots the practice's break-even against the ceiling of its scope, and marks whichever comes first.

SECTION 04A century of trying, and what it cost

None of this is new. The striking thing about the current wave is how closely it rhymes with previous ones, and how reliably the failures have the same shape.

Sears sold mail-order house kits from 1908 to 1940: tens of thousands of precut, numbered, catalogue-ordered homes, which is a genuine R5 carrier operating at supply-chain scope.43,120 The Lustron Corporation built porcelain-enamelled steel houses after the Second World War on a converted aircraft plant, reached about two and a half thousand units, and failed.119,121 Operation Breakthrough, the United States federal programme of 1969, funded twenty-two industrialised housing systems and produced almost no durable change.44 The post-mortem in every case is the same: the factory was fine, the order book was not.

The digital line has a similarly long run-up. Sketchpad demonstrated interactive constrained geometry in 1963.108 Eastman's Building Description System set out, in 1974, almost every argument made for BIM today: a single description from which drawings are derived, so that a change propagates instead of being redrawn.109 The phrase "building information model" appears in the literature in 1992.110 IFC became an ISO standard in 2013 and its infrastructure-capable version, IFC 4.3, was approved as a final standard in January 2024 and published as ISO 16739-1:2024.7,8 That is a sixty-year arc from working demonstration to universal schema, and the schema still is not uniformly implemented.

Two lessons come out of the history rather than out of any technology. The first is that carriers made of tools and products (above the line in the figure) fail from demand starvation, and carriers made of documents and files (below it) fail from orphaning. Different failure modes, same underlying cause: not enough repetitions reached the carrier before someone stopped paying for it.

The second is that the successful cases are unglamorous and almost always involve a change of scope rather than a change of rung. Portland cement did not win because it was better than lime mortar in a laboratory; it won because a specification and a testing regime made it orderable by anyone, anywhere, without the buyer knowing anything.128,129 Reinforced concrete spread through a licensed system with published design rules attached.130 Machine control on earthmoving plant went from novelty to invisible infrastructure in about fifteen years and nobody calls it an emerging trend any more. What those have in common is that the carrier ended up owned by somebody with a very large number of repetitions available.

One strand of the history is more successful than the rest and is almost never counted as technology. Lean construction took the Toyota production system113, reframed building as a flow process rather than a series of conversions114, and produced a planning method whose carrier is a weekly conversation and a written commitment.115 That is an R2 carrier at firm scope with a first-unit penalty close to nothing, which is exactly the combination Experiment 1 says should pay fastest. It has diffused further than most of the practices audited below and has cost the industry almost nothing to adopt.

The British reform reports of the 1990s are worth reading in this light.117,118 Both diagnosed fragmentation and both recommended longer-term relationships, standardised components and measured performance. Both are, in the language used here, arguments for widening amortisation scope. Neither was implemented in a form that changed who owned the carriers, and the productivity series did not move.

The most recent full-scale demonstration of the failure mode cost more than two billion dollars. Katerra, founded in 2015, set out to vertically integrate design, manufacture and assembly, built factories, and filed for Chapter 11 in June 2021 listing liabilities of up to ten billion dollars against assets between five hundred million and one billion.30,32 Observers of the collapse converge on a single diagnosis: the company could not persuade developers and contractors to abandon the subcontractors and suppliers they already used, so the factories never got a predictable order book. That is the volume problem again, and no amount of capital substitutes for it.

SECTION 05How the forty were coded, and what the lab does

Forty practices, grouped into seven families by what they act on: modelling the work, predicting it, making it off site, making it on site, measuring it afterwards, operating the result, and ending it. Every one gets the same four fields.

What recurs names the unit that repeats, which is frequently not the unit people assume. For robotic layout marking the repeating unit is the floor plate, not the wall. For computer-vision progress monitoring it is the tracked object, not the site. Getting this wrong is the single commonest error in the business cases, because it inflates the apparent volume by an order of magnitude.

Carrier names where the learning is kept between instances, and determines the rung. First one costs names what has to be spent before any repetition happens. Breaks when names the event that destroys the repetition, which is almost always organisational rather than technical.

Terminology for building elements follows the standard vocabulary146 where one exists, and is defined in the plate where it does not.

The numeric coding uses a deliberately coarse scale, because a finer one would imply precision that is not there. The first-unit penalty is drawn from {1.2, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0} times conventional cost. The learning rate is drawn from {95%, 90%, 85%, 80%}, with 95% reserved for practices where each new adopter genuinely starts near-fresh and 80% for physical operations with tight, fast feedback. Those two numbers determine the break-even through the Experiment 1 model. The scope determines the ceiling. The verdict is simply whether the break-even arrives before the ceiling does.

Two honesty notes. First, the break-even figures are model outputs from stated assumptions, not measurements; what is being compared across forty practices is the structure of their economics, not their actual costs, which are commercially confidential everywhere. Second, the penalty and learning-rate assignments are judgements. They are published in full in the ledger at Section 13 so that a reader who disagrees with one can see exactly which cell to change and what it would do to the verdict.

The lab

Nine experiments run when this document is built. They are written in the Python standard library with a fixed seed, they take a few seconds, and their complete source and complete output are reproduced in Section 15. Each one exists because a specific claim in the sections below needed a number behind it rather than an assertion.

  1. The price of the first one. Learning curves and break-even volumes, which supply the gauge on every plate.
  2. Where the error is caught. What a model-based check is worth, and which of its properties actually decides that.
  3. The schedule that cannot be beaten. Merge bias in the critical path, and why more parallel work makes the deterministic date more optimistic rather than less.
  4. How much clinker can leave. Substitution against a strength test, and what the date of the test is worth in kilograms.
  5. How many points prove a wall is plumb. Least-squares plane fitting on a synthetic scan, against a real verticality tolerance.
  6. Progress from pictures. Detector bias in percent-complete estimates, and the sample size needed to correct it.
  7. What fraction of a code a machine can decide. A runnable fifteen-clause rule engine over an explicit, deliberately defective building model.
  8. How deep you have to cut. Disassembly depth over a component dependency graph, for two connection schedules.
  9. The factory and the hole in the ground. Where off-site manufacture stops compressing a programme, and what variety costs.

The experiments are deliberately small. None of them is a simulation of a real project, and none is offered as one. They are arguments made in arithmetic, where the assumptions are visible and a reader who disputes one can change a constant and rerun.

A

Modelling the work before it exists

The first family produces descriptions. Its output is a model, a schema, a library, a file. That makes it the cheapest family to enter and the hardest to keep: a description costs almost nothing to copy and almost nothing to abandon.

The case for modelling has never rested on drawing. It rests on catching errors early, which is a claim about the cost of finding out late. The cost-of-change curve that everyone reproduces in slide decks138 is a real effect with a real literature behind it139,140,141, and it is the reason clash detection was the first part of BIM that anyone paid for voluntarily.

Experiment 2 tests the claim rather than repeating it. Four hundred latent interface defects escalate in remedy cost through six stages. A model-based check is inserted at detailed design. It sees only the share of defects that are visible as geometry, taken as 60 per cent, because a clash detector cannot see a missing fire damper or a wrong pipe schedule. It flags real defects at some recall, and its flags are real at some precision. Every flag, true or false, costs a human review.

The first result is a ceiling. Even a perfect check catches only 60 per cent of the population, so the most any model check can remove is 58.5 per cent of expected remedy cost, before a single flag has been looked at. A defect found on site costs 50 times one found in the model, and one found after handover 200 times, which is why the remaining 40 per cent hurts so much.

The second result is sharper and less comfortable. At eighty per cent recall and twenty-five per cent precision the check removes 33.7 per cent of expected remedy cost. At the same recall and two per cent precision, which is what raw unfiltered clash output looks like, it adds 116.3 per cent. Identical detector. Different filter.

EXPERIMENT 2, PRINCIPAL RESULT The break-even precision is 6.972 per cent, and it is independent of recall. Both the saving and the review burden scale with the number of true defects caught, so recall changes how much is on the table but not whether it is worth picking up. The filter decides that, and the filter is a tacit skill held by a coordinator, not a property of the software. The carrier for the most valuable part of clash detection is a person.

None of this is new statistics. Shewhart established in 1931 that an inspection regime is characterised by two error rates and that acting on the output without knowing both is how control charts get misused.142 Clash detection is an inspection regime. It has been sold for twenty years on its recall and almost never on its precision, which is the property that decides whether it makes money.

That result governs how plate A01 is coded. Federated clash coordination sits at R2 rather than R3, because the documented part of the practice is a procedure and the decisive part is not written down at all. The reflex to code it higher, on the grounds that there is software involved, is exactly the mistake the ladder exists to prevent.

The family also contains the clearest case in the whole set of a carrier that cannot amortise. Open schema exchange7,9 is scoped universally, which is the widest ceiling available, and still does not reach break-even, because the learning rate is so slow. Every new implementer of a large schema starts close to fresh, and partial implementations mean the carrier is shared in name rather than in fact. This is not an argument against open schemas. It is an argument that they are public infrastructure, to be funded like infrastructure, rather than investments that repay their backers.

At the other end, the operational digital twin is the only project-scoped practice in the forty. The concept has a clean lineage from Grieves in 2002 through the NASA formulation in 2012 to a formal manufacturing standard in 2021.12,13,14,15 The problem is not the concept. It is that a twin is created by the party that designs and builds, and used by the party that operates, and those are different organisations with different incentives meeting once, at handover. ISO 19650-3 exists precisely to address the operational phase5; the coding here reflects what typically happens rather than what the standard asks for.

B

Predicting cost, time and compliance

This family turns a record of finished work into a claim about unfinished work. Its carrier is always a dataset, which makes its economics unusual: the asset is worthless until it is large, and it only gets large if somebody records outturn on jobs that have already been paid for.

Construction is unusually bad at this, for a reason that is contractual rather than cultural. The programme is a contract document. Recording, in a durable form, that the programme was wrong is a discoverable admission. The data that would make estimating and scheduling models work is systematically not created, and the incentive not to create it is worth real money.

Experiment 3 shows what those models would be correcting. A milestone waits on k parallel chains of eight activities each. Every activity has the same unbiased, mildly right-skewed duration distribution. Deterministic critical-path planning uses the modal duration. Because the milestone takes the maximum over chains, it inherits the tail of every chain merging into it.21

A single chain finishes by the deterministic date 0.9 per cent of the time. With two chains that falls to 0.008 per cent, and beyond three it rounds to zero at three decimal places. The P80 date slips from 13.8 per cent over the deterministic date with one chain to 20.7 per cent with twenty.

The bias is structural. No care in estimating individual activities removes it, because nothing about individual activities is wrong. Only the max operator is new. That is a useful thing to know, because it means the correction is a single learned multiplier rather than a reform of estimating practice. The experiment prints the multiplier: about 1.138 on the deterministic date for an eighty per cent service level with one merging chain, rising to 1.207 with twenty.

Two responses to that arithmetic already exist and neither requires a model. Critical chain planning strips the contingency out of individual activities and pools it in a project buffer, which is the correct statistical move for exactly this reason.149 The parade-game demonstration shows the same effect running through a sequence of trades: variability in each hand-off starves the next one, and the loss compounds along the chain.150 The general result is older still and belongs to queueing theory, where the relationship between work in progress, throughput and cycle time is fixed regardless of anybody's intentions.147,148

That multiplier is a carrier, and it is an R2 or R3 one depending on whether it lives in a note or a model. Critically, it does not transfer from one firm to another without the underlying programme data, which is why plate B03 is coded to supply-chain scope: the only way the dataset gets large enough is if it is pooled across clients by someone who is not a party to any of the contracts.

The family also contains the sharpest natural experiment in the whole set. Rule-based automated code checking (B05) and model-based statutory submission (B06) are the same technology. Encoded clauses, executed against a model, producing a compliance verdict. The literature behind them runs back to Fenves in 196624 and has been reviewed repeatedly since.25,26,29 They differ in exactly one respect: who owns the rule set.

When a design firm encodes the code for its own use, the rule set is scoped to that firm, the encoding cost is borne once per firm, and the break-even sits far beyond the number of projects the firm will ever run. When a regulator encodes it, the rule set is mandatory, the encoding cost is borne once for the jurisdiction, and every building in the country is a repetition. Singapore's CORENET X is the live instance: it became mandatory for projects above thirty thousand square metres on 1 October 2025 and, on the published timetable, applies to all regulatory submissions from 1 October 2026.27,28 B05 does not reach break-even. B06 does, comfortably. Nothing technical distinguishes them.

Experiment 7, discussed under family F, bounds how much of a code can be handled this way at all.

C

Making it somewhere else

Off-site manufacture is the oldest and most direct answer to the repetition question: if the site cannot supply repetitions, move the work somewhere that can. It has been tried in every decade since the 1900s and the failures have been spectacular and instructive.33,120,121,165

The economics are unforgiving in a specific way. A factory is an R4 or R5 carrier with a large fixed cost, so it does not amortise on a learning curve alone; it amortises on a learning curve plus a utilisation rate. Experiment 1 quantifies the fixed-cost part: at a gentle first-unit penalty and a fifteen-per-cent learning rate, a carrier with a fixed cost equal to twenty-five conventional units breaks even at 67 units, and one with a fixed cost of two thousand units breaks even at 2,723. The fixed cost rises eightyfold and the break-even rises about fortyfold: sublinear, but not nearly sublinear enough to rescue an underused plant.

Experiment 9 sets out where the schedule benefit actually comes from and where it stops. Two hundred and forty modules, a fourteen-week substructure that must finish before the first module lands, a site that can install twelve a week, sixteen weeks of fit-out, against a ninety-six-week conventional benchmark.

The programme floor is 50 weeks: substructure plus installation plus fit-out, none of which the factory touches. That is a maximum compression of 47.9 per cent and it is reached at 8 modules a week. Every further unit of factory throughput past that point buys essentially nothing. The commonly quoted headline of a fifty-per-cent programme saving is therefore roughly right and also roughly the ceiling, and the way to go past it is to attack the substructure or the fit-out, neither of which is a modular problem.

The second half of Experiment 9 is the part that kills factories.

The same two hundred and forty modules, the same line, the same people, split across more and more distinct types. One type gives an average unit cost of 0.574 against bespoke. Forty types gives 1.250, which is 117.8 per cent worse than the single-type case and actually worse than not having a factory at all. The crossing happens somewhere between eight and sixteen types. Variety is not paid for in tooling. It is paid for in repeats, and the bill arrives on the learning curve.

WHY MODULAR KEEPS FAILING Every modular business has to hold two things at once: enough volume to amortise the line, and enough sameness that the volume is actually repetition. Clients supply neither for free. A developer who wants forty unit types has silently asked for the factory's economics to be switched off, and will usually be surprised by the price.

The comparison that gets made most often is with Japanese industrialised housing, where manufacturers have run genuine production lines for decades and offer customisation within a fixed component set.152,153 The instructive part is not the factories but the demand structure: a manufacturer sells directly to the household, holds the design platform, and repeats it thousands of times a year. Hong Kong's public housing programme achieved something similar through a single client with a standing programme.154 Both are cases where somebody large enough to supply the repetitions owned the carrier. Where that condition is absent, surveys of housebuilders find exactly the caution the arithmetic predicts.151,155

The internal fix that does work is measurement. Production tracking inside the factory converts a construction-style project into a manufacturing-style flow with known throughput156, and supply-chain integration practices are what let a systemic innovation diffuse at all in a fragmented industry.157 Both are moves to widen scope rather than to climb the ladder.

Against that, mass timber is the family's success story, and the reason is instructive: the decisive carrier was not the press. Cross-laminated timber was industrialised in Austria in the 1990s37, but it became specifiable at scale when product standards40 and then building codes38 let a designer use it without personally proving it. The 2021 International Building Code created explicit construction types permitting mass timber to eighteen storeys, and a run of tall buildings followed.39 The engineering changed less than the paperwork did, and the paperwork is what moved the practice from supply-chain scope to universal scope.

D

Making and placing it on site

This family changes what happens where material meets ground. It contains the most photographed practices in the sector and, on the coding here, the widest spread of outcomes.

Construction robotics has a long and largely disappointing history.51,131,132 Japanese contractors built integrated automated high-rise systems in the late 1980s and abandoned them. The recurring failure is that a site-based machine has to be commissioned for the site, and the site ends. That is a project-scoped carrier wearing a capital-equipment price tag, which is the worst combination on the ladder.

The practices that succeed in this family are the ones that escaped that trap by changing who owns the machine. Machine guidance and grade control (D04) is the most completely absorbed automation in construction, to the point that nobody calls it a trend: the carrier is a control package sold with the machine, the repetitions are every pass of every blade on every earthworks job in the world, and the scope is effectively universal.55,57 Robotic layout marking and blocklaying are sold as services by specialists who take the machine from site to site, which converts a project-scoped carrier into a supply-chain-scoped one.50,52,53

It is worth being careful with the published numbers in this area. For one bricklaying machine, secondary sources quote anything from two hundred to a thousand blocks an hour for the same product, depending on whether the figure is a tested rate, a rated peak or a target. The manufacturer's own tested figure is a 751-block structure completed at an effective rate above 285 blocks an hour.52,53,54 The spread between those numbers is itself evidence about how early the technology is.

Printed construction is the clearest published learning curve in the sector, which is why it is worth taking seriously even though it does not reach break-even on this coding. Contour crafting was proposed in 2004133 with precursors a decade earlier134, the process literature matured through the 2010s46,135,136, and a qualification standard arrived in 2023.47 The Wolf Ranch development of a hundred printed homes is the first run long enough to show a curve at all, and the developer reported roughly halving both cost and cycle time across it.48,49

A DERIVED NUMBER A hundred units is about 6.6 doublings of cumulative output. A halving of unit cost across 6.6 doublings implies a learning rate of roughly 90 per cent per doubling — squarely in the range this article assigns to a physical process with fast feedback, and a genuinely good result. It is also, on Experiment 1's arithmetic, nowhere near enough to clear a three-times first-unit penalty inside a single firm's order book. The technology is working. The scope is too narrow.

The most careful published cost-and-time study of a robotically built structure found that digital fabrication was competitive only where geometric complexity was high enough that conventional methods were themselves expensive.137 That is the learning curve seen from the other side: automation pays first where the bespoke baseline is worst, not where the volume is highest.

The materials end of this family is where the largest and least glamorous gains sit. Cement is responsible for a large share of global industrial emissions and the routes to cutting it are well mapped.123,124,125,126,127 The industry reported a roughly twenty-five per cent reduction in the carbon intensity of cement by late 2025.122 Most of that came from a change of specification rather than a change of process: the United States moved from ordinary Portland cement to almost exclusively portland-limestone cement, worth about a tenth of the emissions at a stroke, because a standard permitted it and then a procurement practice adopted it.63,64

Experiment 4 examines the next step, limestone calcined clay systems, and finds that the binding constraint is not chemistry either. A pozzolan only reacts with the calcium hydroxide that clinker hydration releases, so there is a hard cap on how much clay a given quantity of clinker can activate; past that point additional clay is expensive filler. Clay also reacts slowly, so its contribution at fifty-six days is materially higher than at twenty-eight.

At a strength target of 0.95 relative to an all-clinker binder, moving the acceptance test from twenty-eight to fifty-six days raises the permissible substitution from 14.5 per cent to 30.4 per cent, and deepens the CO₂ cut from 10.7 per cent to 22.3 per cent. That is 100 kilograms of CO₂ per tonne of binder, bought entirely by changing the date on which a cube is crushed.62,65

Which is to say: the most cost-effective decarbonisation lever in this family is a clause in a specification. That is an R2 carrier at universal scope, the cheapest and most powerful combination available, and it is available today to anyone willing to write the clause.

E

Measuring what was actually built

Measurement is the family where the physics is least negotiable, and therefore the one where the arithmetic is most useful. A measuring practice is only worth anything if it can settle a question somebody was going to argue about, and whether it can settle that question is determined by the instrument, not by the software.

Experiment 5 makes this concrete. A three-metre by six-metre wall leans nine millimetres out of plumb. The tolerance is height over four hundred, so 7.5 millimetres: the wall genuinely fails, by 1.5 millimetres. Points are sampled on the face with Gaussian range noise, a least-squares plane is fitted, and the lean is recovered from its gradient. The question is how many points, at what noise, before the confidence interval clears the tolerance and the scan can actually decide the case.

At two millimetres of noise, a hundred points settles it. At five millimetres it takes sixteen hundred. At ten millimetres, sixty-four hundred. At twenty millimetres, sixty-four hundred points still leaves an interval of 1.86 millimetres and the case is undecided. Points needed scale as the square of the noise, so a five-fold worse instrument costs twenty-five times the point count to hold the same interval.

The practical reading is that the scanner's range accuracy sets the tolerance class it can adjudicate at all, and no amount of density or post-processing moves that. This is why measurement practices are coded conservatively here and why the documented accuracy specifications66 matter more than the marketing. A scan that cannot resolve the tolerance is not evidence; it is an expensive opinion.

The same structure appears in computer-vision progress monitoring, which is the most oversold practice in this family and the one with the cleanest underlying statistics. A detector that reports objects as complete has a sensitivity and a specificity. The raw share it reports is not an estimate of progress; it is a mixture of true positives and false positives, and the mixture depends on the very quantity being estimated.

The bias crosses zero at exactly one prevalence, and is wrong everywhere else, in a direction that flips halfway through the job. A detector at ninety per cent sensitivity and ninety per cent specificity overstates progress by eight points when the floor is ten per cent done and understates it by eight points when it is ninety per cent done. On a weekly report that reads as a project that starts ahead and finishes behind, which is a story people are already inclined to believe.

Correcting it is old and simple statistics74: invert the apparent rate through the known sensitivity and specificity. The word doing the work is known. Establishing those two numbers requires a labelled sample from this site, this trade, this lighting. Experiment 6 prices the consequence: a detector at eighty per cent on both measures needs 6,670 tracked objects against 2,401 for a perfect one, a factor of 2.78, to reach the same plus-or-minus two-point interval. The cost of a weak detector is quadratic in Youden's J.73,75

That labelled calibration set is the carrier, and it is the part that does not travel. Hence the coding: an R3 asset at firm scope, with a break-even beyond what a firm can supply. The detector is not the problem. The detector is a commodity. The site-specific ground truth is the asset, and nobody is funding it as one.

F

Running and keeping the building

Operations is where repetitions are abundant and carriers are scarce. A building runs for decades and every hour is a repetition, which ought to make this the easiest family on the ladder. It is not, because the thing that repeats is the building and the thing that would carry the learning is a description of the building that nobody is contractually obliged to maintain.

The specific blockage is semantic. An application that detects a failing air-handling unit in one building cannot run on the next one unless both describe their points the same way, and they do not. Three conventions have competed for a decade79,80 and the unifying standard has been in development since 2018. As of its second independent-committee public review in April 2026, ASHRAE Standard 223P is still not published.78

EIGHT YEARS AND COUNTING A semantic tagging standard announced in 2018 reached a first advisory public review in March 2024, a first publication public review in June 2025, and a second committee public review in April 2026. That is the honest timescale on which a universal carrier arrives in this industry, and any business case that assumes otherwise is assuming its way past the hardest part.

Until it lands, portfolio fault detection81,82 and grid-interactive operation83,84 both carry a per-building mapping cost that has to be paid again for every building. The rule library is genuinely reusable; the binding of the rule library to the plant is not. That is the difference between an R3 carrier at supply scope and the same carrier stranded at firm scope, and it is worth several orders of magnitude on the break-even.

What a rule engine can and cannot settle

Experiment 7 belongs here as much as under family B, because it bounds the automatable fraction of regulation generally. Fifteen clauses of the recurring furniture of building control are written as Python predicates over a small, explicit, deliberately defective building model. The engine runs and reports.

Ten of the fifteen are decided from geometry alone: 66.7 per cent. Five return to a person, not because the checker is unfinished but because what the clause asks about is not in the model and could not be put there without having already made the decision. Whether a means of escape strategy is adequate is not a measurement. Of the ten decidable clauses, 5 pass and 5 fail on this model.

The experiment also shows the sensitivity that makes automation worth having. An exit door is 810 millimetres clear against a threshold of 815. Five millimetres decides whether the scheme complies, and five millimetres is precisely the kind of thing a human reviewer misses at four in the afternoon on a set of forty drawings. Two-thirds of the work is exactly like that, and it is the two-thirds that should be machine-owned.

The remaining third is not a temporary gap. Solihin and Eastman's classification of rule types29 makes the same point structurally: rules requiring derived structures and extended data processing are qualitatively harder than rules requiring only explicit model data, and some require information that no schema carries. A strategy for automated compliance that does not plan for a permanent human third is not a strategy.

G

Ending well

The last family decides what survives the building. It is the newest on the list and the one whose repetition problem is most extreme, because the interval between making the carrier and using it is measured in decades.

The scale is not in doubt. The United States generated some six hundred million short tons of construction and demolition debris in 2018, more than twice the municipal solid waste stream, and demolition accounted for over ninety per cent of it.95 Construction is the largest waste stream in the European Union on comparable accounting.96,97 Whatever else circularity is, it is not a marginal question.

Design for disassembly has a principled standard93 and an older research literature94, and the intuition it rests on is that reversible connections make a building recoverable. Experiment 8 tests that intuition on a twenty-component assembly specified twice: once with conventional cast, welded, glued and nailed joints, and once with the same geometry and demountable joints wherever a demountable joint actually exists. The foundation stays cast. The glazing stays bonded into its frame. The insulation stays adhered.

Recoverability moves as expected: from 3 of 20 components recoverable intact to 17 of 20. Destruction collapses, from a mean of 2.35 components destroyed per recovery attempt to 0.45, a reduction of 80.9 per cent. That is the result the standard predicts and it is real.

EXPERIMENT 8, PRINCIPAL RESULT The mean number of components that must be removed to reach a target is 2.90 in the conventional design and 2.90 in the demountable one. Identical. Reversibility changes what survives, not how much has to be taken apart. The sequence cost is a property of the dependency graph, and the dependency graph is set by the architecture, not by the connection schedule. A building designed for disassembly with the same topology as a conventional one is exactly as expensive to take apart, and merely less wasteful when you do.

Simon's account of near-decomposability is the right general frame: complex systems that can be taken apart are the ones organised into loosely coupled subsystems, and that property is decided when the architecture is set, not when the fixings are chosen.143

That has a design consequence that the standards do not emphasise. If the labour of disassembly is fixed by topology, then shallow, layered, independently accessible arrangements matter more than bolt specifications. Brand's shearing layers144,145 are the right frame: a building whose services are buried inside its structure is undismantlable however it is bolted together.

The experiment also identifies where the marginal money goes. Changing a single glued joint to a bolted one in the conventional design removes five destruction events across the twenty recovery attempts. The high-value joints are the ones with the most dependent components above them, which is a computable property of the assembly and not an aesthetic judgement.

The family's remaining practices turn on whether a market exists. Material passports have a developed framework90,91,92, reused structural steel has a testing and certification route98,99, and recycled aggregate has standards permitting it and a large characterisation literature.100,101 The binding constraint on all three is that a component with no buyer has no value, and there is no price signal for a second-hand beam until somebody creates one. Pre-demolition audit and harvesting is the only practice in the forty coded at R1, because in ordinary commercial practice the carrier really is a demolition foreman's eye for what is worth saving, and that is not written down anywhere.

SECTION 13What the coding shows

Forty practices, coded on two axes, with break-even computed from stated assumptions. 31 of 40 reach break-even inside the population they can amortise over. 9 do not.

The occupancy is not uniform, and the pattern is the point. The hollow markers do not scatter. They concentrate in one column and on one row: practices whose carrier is owned by a single firm, storing their learning in a file.

Finding one: almost every failure is a scope failure

Of the 9 practices that do not reach break-even, 7 would reach it at one scope wider. Only 2 do not pay at any scope available to them. Those two are open schema exchange (A04) and functional recovery targets (F05), and their reasons are opposite: the first has too many independent implementers starting fresh, the second has too few hazard events to learn from.

That is the single most useful result in the audit, because it converts nine apparently technical disappointments into one organisational question. The practices that are not working are not, mostly, failing on their merits. They are failing because the carrier is owned by a body too small to use it enough times.

Finding two: the digital rung has the worst record

The forty distribute across the ladder with a mean rung of 3.08 and a heavy concentration at R3: 17 of 40 practices store their learning in a file. That is where the failures concentrate too. 7 of the 17 practices at R3 do not reach break-even, a failure rate of 41 per cent, against 1 of 9 at R4, 1 of 10 at R2 and none at all at R5.

This is counterintuitive and worth sitting with. The rung that is cheapest to reach, easiest to copy and most enthusiastically funded is the rung that pays back least often. Three reasons, and they compound.

A digital asset is cheap to make, which means it gets made before anybody has established who will maintain it. A digital asset is cheap to abandon, so when the maintenance question arrives the honest answer is frequently to stop. And digital assets are overwhelmingly scoped to a single firm: of the 17 R3 practices, most sit at firm scope, where the ceiling is a few hundred repetitions and the break-even for anything with a three-times entry cost is several thousand.

A mould, by contrast, is expensive enough that nobody buys one without first asking how many castings it will see. The discipline that capital expenditure imposes is absent from software procurement, and the absence shows up here as a worse hit rate.

Finding three: the same technology, two owners, two outcomes

The cleanest pair in the set is B05 and B06. Rule-based code checking, owned by a design firm, does not reach break-even at firm scope. The same technique, owned by a regulator and made mandatory, reaches it comfortably at universal scope. There is no technical difference between them. Everything that distinguishes the outcomes is a question about who holds the carrier and how many repetitions they can compel.

The pattern recurs. Machine guidance (D04) succeeded when the carrier moved into the machine vendor's product. Mass timber (C04) succeeded when the carrier moved into a product standard and then a building code. Portland-limestone cement moved an entire national market by changing what a specification permits. In every case the technology was available for years before the scope change, and the scope change is what made it a trend.

Henderson and Clark's distinction between component innovation and architectural innovation explains why incumbents keep missing this.159 Most of the forty are component innovations: they replace one piece of the delivery process and leave the relationships between pieces intact. SECTION 14What follows

If the repetition question is the right one, several things that look like technology decisions turn out to be something else.

For anyone buying one of these

The first question is not what the technology does. It is: what is the repeating unit, who owns the carrier, and how many repetitions will they get? Those three answers determine the outcome more than any feature comparison. A practice whose carrier is scoped to your firm and whose break-even exceeds your pipeline will not pay, however good it is, and the honest options are to widen the scope, reduce the entry cost, or not do it.

The second question is whether the repeating unit has been counted honestly. Experiment 6's labelled calibration set is the standard trap: the vendor counts sites, the buyer counts sites, and the actual repeating unit is the site-trade-lighting combination, of which there are far fewer repetitions than anyone assumed.

For anyone building one

The strategic move, on this evidence, is almost never to climb the ladder. It is to widen the scope. Seven of the nine failures in this set are fixed by moving the carrier one population outward, and none of them is fixed by better engineering. Concretely that means selling the carrier as a service across many clients rather than licensing it to each; contributing it to a standard rather than defending it as proprietary; or persuading someone with statutory power to adopt it.

Giving the carrier away is frequently the profit-maximising move, because the returns accrue to whoever can execute against it rather than to whoever owns it. That is an uncomfortable conclusion for a venture-funded company and a fairly obvious one for a trade body.

For anyone writing the rules

Regulators and standards bodies are, on this analysis, the most powerful actors in construction innovation and the least likely to think of themselves that way. They own the only universal-scope carriers available. Three levers are underused.

Move acceptance criteria from composition to performance. Experiment 4 puts 100 kilograms of CO₂ per tonne of binder behind a single clause about when a cube gets crushed. A specification that forbids a mix by recipe forbids it before it can be tested.

Publish the rules as executable artefacts. If a third of a code cannot be automated, the other two-thirds can, and a regulator who publishes machine-readable clauses converts every designer in the jurisdiction into a repetition of the same carrier.

Fund open schemas as infrastructure. A04 does not reach break-even at any scope, which means it will never be delivered adequately by the parties who benefit from it. That is the textbook definition of a public good and the textbook remedy is public funding.

The uncomfortable part

The strongest available account of the productivity collapse attributes it to land-use regulation limiting project size and therefore firm size.45 If that is right, then the binding constraint on construction productivity is not in the industry at all. It sits in a planning system that has no view on productivity and no reason to acquire one.

That should temper the claims made for everything audited here. The forty practices above are, at best, ways of manufacturing repetition inside a structure designed to prevent it. Some of them work. The ones that work best are the ones that found a way to be owned by somebody large enough to use them often, which is a statement about industrial organisation rather than about robots.

The correct response to that is not resignation. It is to notice which lever is actually long. A specification clause costs nothing to write and can move a national market in a decade. A press costs a hundred million dollars and moves whatever its order book allows. Both are legitimate. Only one of them is routinely mistaken for the exciting option.

THE SHORT VERSION Construction does not have a technology problem. It has a repetition problem, and every trend on the list is a bid to solve it. The bids that work are the ones where the carrier ends up owned by somebody with enough repetitions to pay for it — a supplier, a standards body, a regulator. The bids that fail mostly fail for that reason and not for any other. Before asking whether a practice works, ask how many times its owner will get to use it.

# deterministic pseudo-random source (Numerical Recipes LCG + Box-Muller)
# --------------------------------------------------------------------------
class LCG:
    """Park-Miller-ish 32-bit LCG. Deterministic across platforms."""

    def __init__(self, seed):
        self.s = seed & 0xFFFFFFFF

    def next_u32(self):
        self.s = (1664525 * self.s + 1013904223) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        return self.s

    def uniform(self):
        # in (0,1), never exactly 0 or 1
        return (self.next_u32() + 0.5) / 4294967296.0

    def normal(self):
        u1 = self.uniform()
        u2 = self.uniform()
        return math.sqrt(-2.0 * math.log(u1)) * math.cos(2.0 * math.pi * u2)

    def triangular(self, lo, mode, hi):
        u = self.uniform()
        c = (mode - lo) / (hi - lo)
        if u < c:
            return lo + math.sqrt(u * (hi - lo) * (mode - lo))
        return hi - math.sqrt((1.0 - u) * (hi - lo) * (hi - mode))

# E1  THE PRICE OF THE FIRST ONE
#     Wright/Crawford unit learning curve. How many repeats before a
#     carrier with a first-unit penalty pays back against bespoke work?
# ==========================================================================
def unit_cost(n, p, b):
    """Crawford unit-curve cost of the n-th unit, bespoke baseline = 1.0."""
    return p * (n ** b)


def cumulative(N, p, b):
    return sum(unit_cost(n, p, b) for n in range(1, N + 1))


_SUM_CACHE = {}
_N0 = 100_000


def _partial_sums(b):
    """Exact prefix sums of n^b up to _N0, cached per exponent."""
    key = round(b, 12)
    if key not in _SUM_CACHE:
        acc = [0.0] * (_N0 + 1)
        s = 0.0
        for n in range(1, _N0 + 1):
            s += n ** b
            acc[n] = s
        _SUM_CACHE[key] = acc
    return _SUM_CACHE[key]


def sum_pow(N, b):
    """sum_{n=1..N} n^b -- exact below _N0, Euler-Maclaurin continuation above."""
    acc = _partial_sums(b)
    if N <= _N0:
        return acc[int(N)]
    N = float(N)
    n0 = float(_N0)
    return (acc[_N0]
            + (N ** (b + 1) - n0 ** (b + 1)) / (b + 1)
            + 0.5 * (N ** b - n0 ** b)
            + (b / 12.0) * (N ** (b - 1) - n0 ** (b - 1)))


CAP = 10 ** 15


def breakeven(p, b, fixed=0.0):
    """Smallest N with fixed + p*sum(n^b) <= N (bespoke total = N). None if never."""
    def f(N):
        return fixed + p * sum_pow(N, b) - N

    if f(CAP) > 0:
        return None
    lo, hi = 1, CAP
    if f(1) <= 0:
        return 1
    while lo + 1 < hi:
        mid = (lo + hi) // 2
        if f(mid) <= 0:
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo = mid
    return hi

def exp3():
    banner(3, "THE SCHEDULE THAT CANNOT BE BEATEN -- merge bias in CPM")
    print("""
Model. A milestone waits on k parallel chains. Each chain has m activities
with triangular durations (lo, mode, hi) = (0.85, 1.00, 1.45) x nominal --
the usual optimistic-mode, long-tail shape of construction estimates.
Deterministic CPM uses the mode, so the CPM date is m x 1.00. The milestone
is the MAXIMUM over chains, so it inherits the tail of every chain merging
into it.

Method. One chain is simulated DRAWS times to get its duration
distribution F. Under independence the k-chain milestone has CDF F(x)^k
exactly, so quantiles follow by inverting F at q^(1/k). No separate
simulation per k, and no Monte-Carlo noise in the k-dependence.
""".rstrip())

    DRAWS = 200_000
    m = 8
    lo, mode, hi = 0.85, 1.00, 1.45
    rng = LCG(SEED)
    chain = []
    for _ in range(DRAWS):
        chain.append(sum(rng.triangular(lo, mode, hi) for _a in range(m)))
    chain.sort()
    cpm = m * mode

    def F(x):
        # empirical CDF by binary search
        loi, hii = 0, len(chain)
        while loi < hii:
            mid = (loi + hii) // 2
            if chain[mid] <= x:
                loi = mid + 1
            else:
                hii = mid
        return loi / len(chain)

    def Q(q):
        i = min(len(chain) - 1, max(0, int(q * len(chain))))
        return chain[i]

    p_single = F(cpm)
    mean_chain = sum(chain) / len(chain)
    print()
    print(f"{DRAWS:,d} chain simulations, {m} activities per chain, seed {SEED}")
    print(f"deterministic CPM date = {cpm:.2f}; simulated single-chain mean = "
          f"{mean_chain:.3f}")
    print(f"P(one chain finishes by the CPM date) = {pct(p_single,2)}")
    print()

Model. A binder is clinker fraction c plus a supplementary fraction (1-c)
split between ground limestone (an inert filler with a small nucleation
contribution a_L) and calcined clay (a pozzolan). The pozzolan only reacts
with the calcium hydroxide that clinker hydration releases, so its
contribution is capped by the clinker present: usable clay is at most
KAPPA x c. Past that point more clay is just expensive filler. Clay is also
slow, so its activity is higher at 56 days than at 28.

    S(c, fQ) = c + a_L*fL*(1-c) + a_Q(t)*min(fQ*(1-c), KAPPA*c) + synergy

Embodied CO2 is clinker plus a calcining charge for the clay. The constants
are illustrative and chosen to reproduce the qualitative behaviour reported
for limestone calcined clay systems -- the experiment is about the SHAPE of
the trade-off and about what the acceptance age does to it.
""".rstrip())

    aL = 0.14
    aQ28, aQ56 = 0.88, 1.15
    KAPPA = 0.42            # tonnes of clay a tonne of clinker can activate
    syn = 0.10
    CO2_CLINKER = 860.0
    CO2_CLAY = 330.0
    CO2_LS = 25.0

    def strength(c, fQ, t28=True):
        fL = 1.0 - fQ
        aQ = aQ28 if t28 else aQ56
        scm = 1.0 - c

        active = min(clay, KAPPA * c)
        return (c + aL * fL * scm + aQ * active
                + syn * min(clay, KAPPA * c) * fL)

        scm = 1.0 - c

# E5  HOW MANY POINTS PROVE A WALL IS PLUMB
#     Least-squares plane fit on a synthetic scan; the sample size needed
#     to resolve a verticality tolerance at 95% confidence.
# ==========================================================================
def fit_plane(points):
    """Least squares z = a*x + b*y + c via 3x3 normal equations."""
    n = len(points)
    Sxx = Sxy = Sxz = Syy = Syz = Sx = Sy = Sz = 0.0
    for x, y, z in points:
        Sxx += x * x
        Sxy += x * y
        Sxz += x * z
        Syy += y * y
        Syz += y * z
        Sx += x
        Sy += y
        Sz += z
    A = [[Sxx, Sxy, Sx], [Sxy, Syy, Sy], [Sx, Sy, float(n)]]
    rhs = [Sxz, Syz, Sz]
    # Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting
    M = [A[i][:] + [rhs[i]] for i in range(3)]
    for col in range(3):
        piv = max(range(col, 3), key=lambda r: abs(M[r][col]))
        M[col], M[piv] = M[piv], M[col]
        pv = M[col][col]
        if abs(pv) < 1e-15:
            return None
        for r in range(3):
            if r == col:
                continue
            f = M[r][col] / pv
            for cc in range(col, 4):
                M[r][cc] -= f * M[col][cc]
    return [M[i][3] / M[i][i] for i in range(3)]

def dependents(asm):
    """map component -> list of things resting directly on it"""
    d = {k: [] for k in asm}
    for k, v in asm.items():
        if v["on"] is not None:
            d[v["on"]].append(k)
    return d


def recovery(asm, target):
    """
    To recover `target` intact, everything resting on it must come off first.
    Returns (survives, removed_count, destroyed_list). `removed` is the
    sequence cost -- set by the topology. `destroyed` is the material loss --
    set by the connection schedule.
    """
    dep = dependents(asm)
    removed = []
    destroyed = []

    def strip(node):
        for child in dep[node]:
            strip(child)
            removed.append(child)
            rev, wrecks = CONNECTION[asm[child]["conn"]]
            if (not rev) or wrecks:
                destroyed.append(child)

    strip(target)
    own_rev, own_wrecks = CONNECTION[asm[target]["conn"]]
    survives = own_rev and not own_wrecks
    return survives, len(removed), destroyed

def exp9():
    banner(9, "THE FACTORY AND THE HOLE IN THE GROUND -- limits of overlap")
    print("""
Model. A building of M modules. Substructure takes SUB weeks and must
finish before the first module lands. The factory produces modules at rate
FAB per week and can start at week 0, in parallel with the substructure.
Modules are delivered in batches of size B; site installs INST per week.
Fit-out and external works after the last module take FIT weeks. The
conventional in-situ programme is CONV weeks.
""".rstrip())

    M = 240
    SUBSTR = 14.0
    FIT = 16.0
    CONV = 96.0
    INST = 12.0

    def duration(fab, batch, sub=SUBSTR, inst=INST):
        # module m (1-indexed) leaves the factory at m/fab
        # a batch is delivered when its last module is finished
        # site can start installing at t >= sub
        t = sub
        installed = 0
        m = 0
        while installed < M:
            m = min(M, m + batch)
            ready = m / fab
            t = max(t, ready)
            t += (m - installed) / inst
            installed = m
        return t + FIT

    print()
    print(f"M = {M} modules, substructure {SUBSTR:.0f} wk, fit-out {FIT:.0f} wk,")
    print(f"install {INST:.0f} modules/wk, conventional benchmark {CONV:.0f} wk")
    print()
    print("Programme duration (weeks) by factory rate and delivery batch size")
    print(SUB)
    hdr = f"{'fab/wk':>8s}" + "".join(f"{b:>10d}" for b in [1, 6, 12, 24, 60])
    print(f"{'':>8s}{'batch size:':>40s}")
    print(hdr)
    best = {}
    for fab in [4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 24]:
        row = f"{fab:>8d}"
        for b in [1, 6, 12, 24, 60]:
            d = duration(float(fab), b)
            best[(fab, b)] = d
            row += f"{d:>10.1f}"
        print(row)
    print(SUB)

    print()
    print("Compression against the conventional programme, batch size 12:")
    print(SUB)
    print(f"{'fab/wk':>8s}{'weeks':>9s}{'saving':>10s}{'binding constraint':>24s}")
    for fab in [4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 24]:
        d = best[(fab, 12)]
        # which constraint binds: factory throughput or site capacity
        fab_limited = (M / fab) > (SUBSTR + M / INST)
        binding = "factory throughput" if fab_limited else "site + substructure"
        print(f"{fab:>8d}{d:>9.1f}{pct(1-d/CONV,1):>10s}{binding:>24s}")
    print(SUB)

    # the floor
    floor = SUBSTR + M / INST + FIT
    print()
    print(f"As factory rate goes to infinity the programme converges to")
    print(f"  substructure {SUBSTR:.0f} + installation {M/INST:.0f} + fit-out {FIT:.0f} "
          f"= {floor:.0f} weeks,")
    print(f"a maximum compression of {pct(1-floor/CONV,1)} against {CONV:.0f} weeks.")
    fab_star = None
    for fab in range(1, 400):
        if best.get((fab, 12)) is None:
            d = duration(float(fab), 12)
        else:
            d = best[(fab, 12)]
        if d <= floor * 1.02:
            fab_star = fab
            break
    print(f"Factory rate beyond {fab_star}/wk buys less than 2% more. Everything")
    print("past that point has to come out of the substructure or the fit-out.")

    # module variety vs learning, tying back to E1
    print()
    print("Module VARIETY against factory learning (E1 curve, LR = 85%, p = 1.6x):")
    print(SUB)
    b85 = math.log(0.85) / math.log(2.0)
    print(f"{'types':>7s}{'units/type':>12s}{'avg unit cost':>15s}{'vs bespoke':>12s}")
    variety = []
    for types in [1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 40, 120, 240]:
        per = M / types
        # each type runs its own learning curve from unit 1

        avg = tot / M
        variety.append((types, per, avg))
        print(f"{types:>7d}{per:>12.1f}{avg:>15.3f}{pct(avg-1,1):>12s}")
    print(SUB)
    one = variety[0][2]
    forty = [v for v in variety if v[0] == 40][0][2]
    print(f"Going from 1 module type to 40 raises average unit cost from")
    print(f"{one:.3f} to {forty:.3f} -- {((forty/one)-1)*100:.1f}% -- on the same")
    print("factory, same tooling, same people. Variety is paid for in repeats.")

    return {"best": best, "floor": floor, "conv": CONV, "M": M,
            "variety": variety, "fab_star": fab_star, "SUB": SUBSTR,
            "FIT": FIT, "INST": INST}

EXPERIMENT 6  |  PROGRESS FROM PICTURES -- estimating percent complete from a detector
==============================================================================

Model. A floor has N tracked objects. A share theta are genuinely complete.
A vision model reports 'complete' with sensitivity se on complete objects
and false-positive rate (1 - sp) on incomplete ones. The naive progress
estimate is the raw share reported complete. Rogan-Gladen inversion
recovers theta from the apparent rate:  theta_hat = (a + sp - 1)/(se + sp - 1).

Naive bias of the raw detector rate, by true progress:
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    se    sp      0.10      0.25      0.50      0.75      0.90
  0.95  0.95      +4.0      +2.5      +0.0      -2.5      -4.0
  0.90  0.90      +8.0      +5.0      +0.0      -5.0      -8.0
  0.85  0.95      +3.0      +0.0      -5.0     -10.0     -13.0
  0.95  0.85     +13.0     +10.0      +5.0      -0.0      -3.0
  0.80  0.80     +16.0     +10.0      +0.0     -10.0     -16.0
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
(cells are percentage points of bias in the raw rate)

The bias crosses zero exactly once, at the prevalence where false
positives and false negatives cancel:  theta* = (1-sp)/(2-se-sp)
  se=0.95 sp=0.95 -> theta* = 50.0%
  se=0.90 sp=0.90 -> theta* = 50.0%
  se=0.85 sp=0.95 -> theta* = 25.0%
  se=0.95 sp=0.85 -> theta* = 75.0%
  se=0.80 sp=0.80 -> theta* = 50.0%

(worst case over true progress, binomial variance propagated):
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    se    sp   Youden J    n needed  vs perfect
  1.00  1.00       1.00       2,401       1.00x
  0.95  0.95       0.90       2,965       1.23x
  0.90  0.90       0.80       3,752       1.56x
  0.85  0.95       0.80       3,752       1.56x

The cost of a weak detector is quadratic in Youden's J: a detector at

Correction needs se and sp to be KNOWN -- which means a labelled
sample from this site, this trade, this lighting. That labelled
sample is the carrier, and it is what does not transfer.

Three notes on method, for anyone minded to check it.

The break-even routine computes exact prefix sums of nb up to 100,000 and continues with an Euler–Maclaurin expansion beyond that, then bisects. This matters because several break-even values in this article exceed 106 and a naive loop would not terminate in reasonable time.

Experiment 3 simulates a single chain two hundred thousand times and then derives the k-chain distribution analytically as F(x)k, rather than simulating each k separately. This removes Monte-Carlo noise from the dependence on k, which is the quantity of interest.

Experiment 7 is a genuine rule engine, not a description of one. The building model is an explicit Python dictionary, each clause is a predicate function, and the pass, fail and refer verdicts in Figure 11 are what the functions returned when this document was built.
