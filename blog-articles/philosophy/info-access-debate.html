Knowledge, Copyright, and the Commons

Information Wants to Be Free… Or Should It?

The real fight is not between generosity and greed. It is between several public goods that can collide: access, sustainability, accuracy, creator livelihoods, and democratic control of knowledge.

The Misquoted Paradox

The famous line is usually shortened to "information wants to be free." Stewart Brand's actual 1984 point was sharper: information also "wants to be expensive" because valuable knowledge can change lives, markets, and institutions.1 Brand later described the line as a permanent paradox created by digital copying: distribution keeps getting cheaper while the value of timely, trustworthy information can remain very high.2

"On the one hand you have—the point you’re making Woz—is that information sort of wants to be expensive because it is so valuable—the right information in the right place just changes your life. On the other hand, information almost wants to be free because the costs of getting it out is getting lower and lower all of the time. So you have these two things fighting against each other."1

The question is therefore not whether all information should cost nothing. Weather alerts, vaccine trial data, court opinions, novels, trade secrets, personal medical records, source code, classroom materials, and investigative journalism do not raise the same moral, legal, or economic issues. The better question is: which kinds of information should be open by default, who pays for the institutions that make them reliable, and what rights should remain with creators and communities?

Thesis: publicly important knowledge should move toward openness, especially when public money, public health, education, law, or scientific reproducibility are involved. But openness works only when the system also pays for preservation, review, attribution, privacy, safety, and the labor of making knowledge usable.

The Old Bargain: Limited Monopolies for Public Progress

Modern copyright was not born as a natural property right in every possible reuse of information. The 1710 Statute of Anne described itself as an act "for the encouragement of learning" and granted authors or purchasers time-limited rights to print books.3 The U.S. Constitution uses similar public-purpose language, empowering Congress to grant authors and inventors exclusive rights "for limited Times" in order to promote progress.4

That bargain still matters. U.S. copyright protects original expression fixed in a tangible medium; it does not protect ideas, facts, procedures, systems, methods, concepts, principles, or discoveries.5 In other words, the law is already built around a balance: authors need enforceable rights, but society needs facts, ideas, learning, quotation, criticism, preservation, and new work.

International human-rights language reflects the same tension. Article 27 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights recognizes both the right to share in scientific advancement and the right of authors to protection of their moral and material interests.6 The United Nations Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights later framed access to the benefits of scientific progress as a live rights issue in a world shaped by fast-moving science and technology.7

Long Before the Web: Societies, Libraries, Copies

The modern access debate has older roots than computers. Scientific societies were already building institutions for trusted circulation centuries before the internet. In 1665, Royal Society secretary Henry Oldenburg began publishing Philosophical Transactions to circulate reports sent by correspondents across countries and fields; the journal later evolved from an editor-driven news periodical into a research journal and introduced systematic peer review in 1832.36 Scientific communication has always needed both speed and trust.

Public libraries turned a similar idea into civic infrastructure. The Boston Public Library was established in 1848 and describes itself as the first large free municipal library in the United States; its Copley Square building carries the motto "Free to All."37 The library model did not abolish authorship or publishing. It created a public layer of access on top of private publishing markets, funded by communities because reading and reference were treated as public goods.

Mechanical copying then made the old bargain harder to police. Photocopiers made individual articles portable in a way print subscription law had not anticipated. U.S. copyright law responded with Section 108 of the 1976 Copyright Act, which the Copyright Office summarizes as allowing libraries and archives, under limited conditions, to reproduce and distribute copyrighted works for preservation, replacement, and research.38 The point was not that copying became unrestricted; it was that scholarship and preservation required explicit exceptions.

Early networking culture added another historical layer: open technical documentation. RFC 1, published in April 1969, was already an informal network note about host software for the ARPA Network.39 The Internet Society's history argues that free and open access to protocol specifications was a key reason the internet grew quickly, because implementers could read, criticize, and build compatible systems without waiting for a closed standards vendor.40

By the early 1980s, the debate had also become a software question. Richard Stallman's 1983 GNU announcement promised a Unix-compatible system that would be given away for others to use, while the Free Software Foundation later clarified that "free software" was about user freedoms rather than simply zero price.41 That distinction still matters: open access to information is strongest when it includes rights to inspect, copy, modify, preserve, and redistribute, not merely the ability to read something once.

The Digital Turn: From Scarcity to Copies Everywhere

The internet did not invent the access debate, but it made the economics visible. In print, every extra book, journal issue, or database copy had physical costs. In digital networks, the next copy can be nearly free while the first reliable version may remain expensive to create, edit, check, host, preserve, and discover.

1971. Michael Hart started Project Gutenberg by typing the U.S. Declaration of Independence into a University of Illinois mainframe account; Project Gutenberg now describes itself as an online library of more than 75,000 free ebooks.8

1991. Paul Ginsparg founded arXiv as a preprint server; arXiv now hosts more than three million scholarly articles across eight subject areas and became an independent nonprofit organization in 2026.9 As of September 16, 2026, arXiv reported 3,170,041 submissions.10

1993. CERN made the World Wide Web source code available on a royalty-free basis, a decision CERN credits with helping the web flourish as an open system rather than a proprietary product.42

1995-1996. JSTOR was founded in 1995 after William G. Bowen, then president of the Mellon Foundation, proposed digitizing print journals to reduce library storage pressure and improve access; the Internet Archive was founded in 1996 to build an internet library for researchers, historians, and scholars.4344

1999 and 2012. Rice University's OpenStax traces its roots to Connexions, launched in 1999 as a community-built open educational resource project; OpenStax began in 2012 with peer-reviewed, openly licensed textbooks offered at no cost to students.45

2000. PubMed Central became available online as a free full-text archive at the U.S. National Library of Medicine; NLM says PMC began with two journals and now contains articles from thousands of journals.11

2001-2002. Creative Commons was founded in 2001 and released its first public licenses in 2002, giving creators standardized ways to keep copyright while granting public reuse rights.12

2002-2003. The Budapest Open Access Initiative offered the first widely used definition of open access; the Berlin Declaration soon called for open access to research outputs, raw data, metadata, source materials, and cultural heritage materials.1314

2008. Aaron Swartz published the Guerilla Open Access Manifesto, a deliberately confrontational call for civil disobedience against the enclosure of digitized scientific and cultural knowledge.46

This history matters because it shows that "free information" is not one movement. It includes public-domain digitization, preprints, open licenses, institutional repositories, funder mandates, open data, open-source software, open educational resources, and community publishing. Those projects share an intuition: the public value of knowledge rises when more people can inspect, reuse, teach, translate, preserve, and build on it.

What Broke in Scholarly Publishing

Scholarly journals began as a way to circulate discoveries, establish priority, and preserve the scientific record. The first scientific journals appeared in 1665, and the journal system eventually became the dominant channel for disseminating research.15 The strange modern twist is that much scholarly labor is donated or publicly funded while access is often sold back to universities, hospitals, firms, and individuals.

A large bibliometric study of 45 million Web of Science records found that, by 2013, the top five academic publishers accounted for more than half of papers in both natural and medical sciences and social sciences and humanities.15 RELX, Elsevier's parent company, reported a 2025 group adjusted operating margin of 34.8%; its Scientific, Technical & Medical segment reported GBP 2.714 billion in revenue and GBP 1.035 billion in adjusted operating profit in the same filing.16

Concentration Five publishers produced more than half of 2013 papers in the two broad fields studied by Lariviere, Haustein, and Mongeon.15
High APCs Nature lists a 2026 gold open-access APC of GBP 9,390 / USD 12,850 / EUR 10,850 for primary research in Nature.17

Open access can also reproduce inequity if it merely moves the paywall from reader to author. Article processing charges can be manageable for well-funded labs but exclusionary for independent scholars, early-career researchers, and institutions with weaker currencies or smaller grant budgets. And low-quality or deceptive journals can exploit author-pays incentives: a 2015 longitudinal study in BMC Medicine described the growth of publishers with questionable marketing and peer-review practices.18

That is why the best open-access argument is not "publishing costs nothing." It is that the costs should be transparent, proportionate, accountable, and aligned with scholarship rather than artificial scarcity.

The Ethical Split: Four Claims That All Have Teeth

The access debate gets crude when either side pretends the other has no point. The case for openness is strongest when information is publicly funded, necessary for public health or education, part of the law, or needed to verify scientific claims. The 2022 White House OSTP memorandum directed U.S. federal agencies to update public-access policies so federally funded research publications and supporting data become publicly accessible without embargo by the end of 2025.19 NIH's 2024 Public Access Policy, accelerated to July 1, 2025, requires covered author accepted manuscripts to be deposited in PubMed Central and made publicly available without embargo upon publication.20

The case for restriction is strongest when openness would expose private data, endanger people, erase creator income, encourage plagiarism, or remove the funding base for careful editorial work. Copyright, privacy law, security practice, and research ethics all exist because information can harm as well as liberate.

Claim What It Gets Right What Can Go Wrong
Public investment should create public benefit. Taxpayers, patients, teachers, and researchers benefit when publicly funded work is reusable and verifiable. "Publicly funded" does not automatically settle privacy, safety, Indigenous data sovereignty, or publication-cost questions.
Creators need enforceable rights. Books, journalism, music, software, and scholarship require labor, and many creators are not institutionally funded. Overbroad control can block preservation, education, research, disability access, and follow-on creativity.
Peer review and curation cost money. Reliable knowledge needs editors, infrastructure, indexing, archiving, corrections, and misconduct handling. Prestige and market power can turn necessary costs into rent extraction.
Access is an equity issue. UNESCO data show deep disparities in research investment: Sub-Saharan Africa spent 0.38% of GDP on R&D in 2023, while Europe and Northern America spent 2.55%.21 Access alone does not create research capacity; training, infrastructure, language access, grants, and local journals matter too.

Research4Life shows both the promise and the limits of partial access programs. WHO marked its 25th anniversary in 2026 and described it as a public-private partnership giving more than 12,000 institutions in over 120 countries access to more than 250,000 journals, books, and databases.22 That is important bridge-building, but it is still mediated access rather than a fully open knowledge system.

Shadow Libraries: Signal, Symptom, and Legal Risk

Shadow libraries are the uncomfortable evidence that demand for knowledge often exceeds legal supply. Sci-Hub, founded in 2011, became the best-known scholarly example. A peer-reviewed 2018 eLife study found that, as of March 2017, Sci-Hub's database contained 68.9% of 81.6 million scholarly articles registered with Crossref and 85.1% of articles published in toll-access journals.23

Library Genesis has a longer and more culturally specific history. Balazs Bodo's chapter in Shadow Libraries traces LibGen to Russian and post-Soviet practices of informal text sharing, academic scarcity, and the consolidation of earlier digital collections; the chapter describes LibGen as starting around 2008 and later absorbing material from Library.nu.24

Z-Library illustrates the legal side of the same conflict. In November 2022, the U.S. Department of Justice announced charges against two Russian nationals alleged to operate Z-Library, including criminal copyright infringement, wire fraud, and money laundering; DOJ also said the defendants were arrested in Argentina and that a network of domains had been seized.25

Aaron Swartz's case became the access debate's most visible American flashpoint. JSTOR says that, between late September 2010 and early January 2011, someone downloaded 4.8 million articles through MIT's network, that Swartz was later identified as the person responsible, and that JSTOR reached a civil settlement with him before telling federal prosecutors it had no further interest in the matter and did not want to press charges.47 MIT's later FAQ says Swartz faced 13 felony counts stemming from the downloads and died by suicide on January 11, 2013, before trial; the MIT review faulted the Institute's neutrality as a missed opportunity for leadership on open access, computer-crime law, and institutional responsibility.48

Shadow libraries are not a stable solution. They can violate copyright, expose users to operational and security risks, lack durable preservation guarantees, and route scholarly communication through adversarial infrastructure. But treating them only as piracy misses the diagnostic point: people use them because legal access is often fragmented, expensive, slow, geographically unequal, or locked behind institutional privilege.

Working Alternatives: What a Better Commons Looks Like

The strongest path forward is not one mechanism. It is a stack: public-access law, funder mandates, green repositories, no-fee publishing, open licenses, library consortia, preservation infrastructure, and quality standards.

Funder and Policy Mandates

Plan S launched in 2018 through cOAlition S and requires, from 2021, that publications from participating funders be published in open-access venues or made immediately available in repositories without embargo.26 UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on Open Science gives governments a broader framework that includes open scientific knowledge, infrastructure, capacity-building, incentives, and international cooperation.27

Green Open Access and Public Repositories

Repositories lower access barriers without requiring every journal to become open overnight. arXiv covers physics, mathematics, computer science, quantitative biology, quantitative finance, statistics, electrical engineering and systems science, and economics; submissions are moderated but not peer-reviewed by arXiv.9 PubMed Central preserves full-text biomedical and life-sciences literature at NIH/NLM, while PubMed itself contains more than 40 million citations and abstracts.1128

Diamond Open Access

Diamond open access removes fees for both readers and authors. UNESCO defines it as a model supported by shared infrastructures and open licensing, providing scholarly knowledge as a digital public good with no reader or author fees.29 DOAJ's public directory currently lists 23,385 open-access journals, including 14,438 journals without fees.30

Open Research Europe is one concrete platform in this direction. Established by the European Commission in 2021, ORE describes itself as an open-access publishing platform with open peer review and no cost for readers or authors; in 2026 it began a transition toward CERN-hosted, collectively governed infrastructure with broader European eligibility.31

Books, Not Just Articles

Monographs matter, especially in the humanities and social sciences. MIT Press's Direct to Open model uses collective library support to open scholarly books; MIT Press announced that it opened 80 new monographs in 2025 and 72 frontlist titles for 2026 through Direct to Open.32

Transformative Agreements

Germany's Projekt DEAL shows a negotiated route. The Elsevier-DEAL agreement registry lists a 2023-2028 publish-and-read agreement, and the Max Planck Digital Library says the agreement allows affiliated corresponding authors to publish open access in more than 2,500 Elsevier hybrid and fully open-access journals while restoring broad reading access.3334 These agreements can accelerate access, but critics worry they may preserve incumbent publisher power if they do not actually shift the system toward lower-cost, community-governed publishing.

Integrity Standards

Open does not mean unreviewed. DOAJ, COPE, OASPA, and WAME publish Principles of Transparency and Best Practice in Scholarly Publishing covering journal identity, peer review, fees, licensing, archiving, corrections, conflicts of interest, and research misconduct processes.35 Those boring-sounding standards are the plumbing of trust.

A Practical Test

  • Open by default for law, publicly funded research, public-health evidence, educational materials, and preservation of public-domain culture.
  • Paid and protected where creator livelihood, privacy, safety, trade secrecy, or community control make openness inappropriate.
  • Transparent costs for publishing, hosting, review, preservation, metadata, and correction.
  • Reusable rights through open licenses when reuse is the point, not merely free-to-read access.
  • Governance beyond platforms so public knowledge is not dependent on one company, one domain, or one political jurisdiction.

Where This Lands

Information does not literally want anything. People do. Students want textbooks they can afford. Researchers want literature they can inspect. Patients want access to the science their taxes and bodies helped produce. Authors want attribution and income. Editors want standards. Libraries want budgets that do not buckle under monopoly pricing. Societies want knowledge that survives beyond quarterly earnings, domain seizures, or institutional subscriptions.

The answer is not "everything free" or "everything owned." The answer is a knowledge commons with working institutions underneath it: rights where rights are needed, openness where exclusion harms the public, and enough funding to keep the record trustworthy. Brand's paradox remains useful because both halves are true. Information can be priceless and still need to be freely shared.
