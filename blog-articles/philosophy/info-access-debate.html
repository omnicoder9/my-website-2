Knowledge, Copyright, and the Commons

Information Wants to Be Free… Or Should It?

The real fight is not between generosity and greed. It is between several public goods that can collide: access, sustainability, accuracy, creator livelihoods, and democratic control of knowledge.

The Misquoted Paradox

The famous line is usually shortened to "information wants to be free." Stewart Brand's actual 1984 point was sharper: information also "wants to be expensive" because valuable knowledge can change lives, markets, and institutions.1 Brand later described the line as a permanent paradox created by digital copying: distribution keeps getting cheaper while the value of timely, trustworthy information can remain very high.2

"On the one hand you have—the point you’re making Woz—is that information sort of wants to be expensive because it is so valuable—the right information in the right place just changes your life. On the other hand, information almost wants to be free because the costs of getting it out is getting lower and lower all of the time. So you have these two things fighting against each other."1

The question is therefore not whether all information should cost nothing. Weather alerts, vaccine trial data, court opinions, novels, trade secrets, personal medical records, source code, classroom materials, and investigative journalism do not raise the same moral, legal, or economic issues. The better question is: which kinds of information should be open by default, who pays for the institutions that make them reliable, and what rights should remain with creators and communities?

Thesis: publicly important knowledge should move toward openness, especially when public money, public health, education, law, or scientific reproducibility are involved. But openness works only when the system also pays for preservation, review, attribution, privacy, safety, and the labor of making knowledge usable.

Four meanings of "free"

The word "free" carries several senses that get conflated in almost every conversation about open information, and much of the argument evaporates once they are held apart. The free software movement famously distinguishes "free as in speech" from "free as in beer": the first refers to user rights (inspect, modify, redistribute), the second to price.49 Two further senses matter here. "Free as in access" describes whether a person can reach the material at all, regardless of institutional affiliation, geography, or credential. "Free as in circulation" describes whether the information can travel—whether embargoes, export controls, non-disclosure obligations, or platform terms are artificially holding it back.

These meanings can come apart. A journal article can be zero-price but under all-rights-reserved copyright, blocking reuse in a translated textbook. A dataset can be openly licensed but locked behind an institutional login. An investigative report can be freely readable online yet legally cannot be quoted at length. Openness is a bundle of rights and affordances, and disagreements about "free information" are almost always disagreements about which strand of the bundle is at stake in a specific case.50

The Non-Rivalrous Nature of Information

The digital access debate rests on an economic fact that is older than the internet: information is non-rivalrous. If a physical object such as a laptop is transferred, only one party retains it. If a copy of a dataset, algorithm, or article is transferred, both parties can hold it simultaneously. The formal distinction between rival and non-rival goods was set out by Paul Samuelson in his 1954 paper "The Pure Theory of Public Expenditure," which introduced the modern definition of a public good—one whose consumption by one person does not reduce availability to any other.51

Information also tends toward being non-excludable once released. Copyright, patents, encryption, and paywalls attempt to reintroduce exclusion, but the underlying technology fights back: photocopiers, cassette tapes, personal computers, the web, torrent networks, and large-scale scraping have each in turn lowered the cost of duplication.52 The economists Michele Boldrin and David Levine argue in Against Intellectual Monopoly that this collision between digital abundance and legal scarcity is structural rather than incidental, and that the enforcement effort required to preserve the older model keeps growing.53

The consequence is a distinctive financing problem: how do you pay for something whose marginal cost of reproduction approaches zero, when the first copy—the research, the writing, the editing, the code review, the peer scrutiny, the archival preservation—can cost enormous sums? Society has, over the last two centuries, invented several answers, and every real-world open project mixes them.

Mechanism How It Finances First-Copy Costs Trade-offs
Copyright and patent Time-limited legal monopolies allow charging above marginal cost. Creates artificial scarcity; can block follow-on work; enforcement escalates online.
Subscription and advertising Bundling and attention markets recoup costs indirectly. Concentrates power in aggregators; distorts editorial incentives.
Public funding Government or foundation grants pay production up front; output can be released freely. Political dependency; uneven across nations and disciplines.
Cooperative or consortial finance Libraries, universities, or user communities pool contributions. Coordination costs; free-rider incentives if benefits are non-excludable.
Complementary services Give away the informational core; sell hosting, support, hardware, certification, or events. Requires an ecosystem large enough to sustain service demand.

Each mechanism is a bet about who should bear the first-copy cost and who should benefit from the near-zero marginal cost. Political and moral disagreements about openness are, in large part, disagreements about which bet is fairest for a given kind of information.54

The Old Bargain: Limited Monopolies for Public Progress

Modern copyright was not born as a natural property right in every possible reuse of information. The 1710 Statute of Anne described itself as an act "for the encouragement of learning" and granted authors or purchasers time-limited rights to print books.3 The U.S. Constitution uses similar public-purpose language, empowering Congress to grant authors and inventors exclusive rights "for limited Times" in order to promote progress.4

That bargain still matters. U.S. copyright protects original expression fixed in a tangible medium; it does not protect ideas, facts, procedures, systems, methods, concepts, principles, or discoveries.5 In other words, the law is already built around a balance: authors need enforceable rights, but society needs facts, ideas, learning, quotation, criticism, preservation, and new work.

International human-rights language reflects the same tension. Article 27 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights recognizes both the right to share in scientific advancement and the right of authors to protection of their moral and material interests.6 The United Nations Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights later framed access to the benefits of scientific progress as a live rights issue in a world shaped by fast-moving science and technology.7

Long Before the Web: Societies, Libraries, Copies

The modern access debate has older roots than computers. Scientific societies were already building institutions for trusted circulation centuries before the internet. In 1665, Royal Society secretary Henry Oldenburg began publishing Philosophical Transactions to circulate reports sent by correspondents across countries and fields; the journal later evolved from an editor-driven news periodical into a research journal and introduced systematic peer review in 1832.36 Scientific communication has always needed both speed and trust.

Public libraries turned a similar idea into civic infrastructure. The Boston Public Library was established in 1848 and describes itself as the first large free municipal library in the United States; its Copley Square building carries the motto "Free to All."37 The library model did not abolish authorship or publishing. It created a public layer of access on top of private publishing markets, funded by communities because reading and reference were treated as public goods.

Mechanical copying then made the old bargain harder to police. Photocopiers made individual articles portable in a way print subscription law had not anticipated. U.S. copyright law responded with Section 108 of the 1976 Copyright Act, which the Copyright Office summarizes as allowing libraries and archives, under limited conditions, to reproduce and distribute copyrighted works for preservation, replacement, and research.38 The point was not that copying became unrestricted; it was that scholarship and preservation required explicit exceptions.

Early networking culture added another historical layer: open technical documentation. RFC 1, published in April 1969, was already an informal network note about host software for the ARPA Network.39 The Internet Society's history argues that free and open access to protocol specifications was a key reason the internet grew quickly, because implementers could read, criticize, and build compatible systems without waiting for a closed standards vendor.40

By the early 1980s, the debate had also become a software question. Richard Stallman's 1983 GNU announcement promised a Unix-compatible system that would be given away for others to use, while the Free Software Foundation later clarified that "free software" was about user freedoms rather than simply zero price.41 That distinction still matters: open access to information is strongest when it includes rights to inspect, copy, modify, preserve, and redistribute, not merely the ability to read something once.

The Digital Turn: From Scarcity to Copies Everywhere

The internet did not invent the access debate, but it made the economics visible. In print, every extra book, journal issue, or database copy had physical costs. In digital networks, the next copy can be nearly free while the first reliable version may remain expensive to create, edit, check, host, preserve, and discover.

1971. Michael Hart started Project Gutenberg by typing the U.S. Declaration of Independence into a University of Illinois mainframe account; Project Gutenberg now describes itself as an online library of more than 75,000 free ebooks.8

1991. Paul Ginsparg founded arXiv as a preprint server; arXiv now hosts more than three million scholarly articles across eight subject areas and became an independent nonprofit organization in 2026.9 As of September 16, 2026, arXiv reported 3,170,041 submissions.10

1993. CERN made the World Wide Web source code available on a royalty-free basis, a decision CERN credits with helping the web flourish as an open system rather than a proprietary product.42

1995-1996. JSTOR was founded in 1995 after William G. Bowen, then president of the Mellon Foundation, proposed digitizing print journals to reduce library storage pressure and improve access; the Internet Archive was founded in 1996 to build an internet library for researchers, historians, and scholars.4344

1999 and 2012. Rice University's OpenStax traces its roots to Connexions, launched in 1999 as a community-built open educational resource project; OpenStax began in 2012 with peer-reviewed, openly licensed textbooks offered at no cost to students.45

2000. PubMed Central became available online as a free full-text archive at the U.S. National Library of Medicine; NLM says PMC began with two journals and now contains articles from thousands of journals.11

2001-2002. Creative Commons was founded in 2001 and released its first public licenses in 2002, giving creators standardized ways to keep copyright while granting public reuse rights.12

2002-2003. The Budapest Open Access Initiative offered the first widely used definition of open access; the Berlin Declaration soon called for open access to research outputs, raw data, metadata, source materials, and cultural heritage materials.1314

2008. Aaron Swartz published the Guerilla Open Access Manifesto, a deliberately confrontational call for civil disobedience against the enclosure of digitized scientific and cultural knowledge.46

This history matters because it shows that "free information" is not one movement. It includes public-domain digitization, preprints, open licenses, institutional repositories, funder mandates, open data, open-source software, open educational resources, and community publishing. Those projects share an intuition: the public value of knowledge rises when more people can inspect, reuse, teach, translate, preserve, and build on it.

Description, Norm, Institution

Debates over "free information" often collapse three distinct kinds of claim, and untangling them makes the disagreements easier to reason about.

The first is descriptive: a statement about how digital information actually behaves. "The marginal cost of copying a digital file is close to zero" and "encryption schemes can be broken" are empirical assertions about technology and economics.52

The second is normative: a statement about how the world ought to be arranged. "Publicly funded research should be publicly accessible" and "the law of the land should not be behind a paywall" are ethical claims that need argument, not measurement.55

The third is institutional: a specific rule, licence, or piece of infrastructure that operationalises a norm. The GNU General Public License, the Creative Commons licence suite, the NIH Public Access Policy, and cOAlition S's Plan S each translate a value commitment into enforceable text and organisational practice.562026

Why the distinction matters: "information wants to be free" is a descriptive-cum-philosophical slogan; it does not tell you how to licence a dataset, structure a grant contract, or fund a preservation archive. Institutions do that work, and choosing an institution requires the normative argument first.

Charlotte Hess and Elinor Ostrom made this point in their study of knowledge as a commons: information is a resource, but a functioning commons requires governance—shared rules about contribution, curation, dispute resolution, and long-term stewardship.57 Yochai Benkler's The Wealth of Networks argues in a similar spirit that networked information production is a genuine third mode of coordination alongside the state and the market, but that it depends on institutional scaffolding—licences, norms, platforms, funders—that is easy to underestimate.58

Free Software and Open Source: Two Kindred Traditions

Software is the domain where "openness" has been institutionalised most rigorously. It also contains the clearest example of two closely related movements that emphasise different values while sharing almost the same set of licences.

The free-software tradition

Richard Stallman announced GNU in September 1983, promising a Unix-compatible operating system that would be given away and could be shared freely.41 Stallman founded the Free Software Foundation in 1985 and, in 1989, released the first version of the GNU General Public License, whose signature feature is copyleft: any derivative work distributed to others must also be released under the same terms.56 The FSF frames free software as the guarantee of four freedoms—to run the program, to study and change it, to redistribute copies, and to distribute modified versions.49 The vocabulary is deliberately moral. "Free" refers to liberty, not price, and the argument is that proprietary software restricts users in ways that are ethically objectionable regardless of quality or cost.

The open-source tradition

In February 1998, following Netscape's announcement that it would release the Communicator source code, a group including Bruce Perens, Eric Raymond, Todd Anderson, Chris Peterson, and others coined the term "open source" at a strategy meeting in Palo Alto and shortly afterwards founded the Open Source Initiative.59 Perens adapted the Debian Free Software Guidelines into the Open Source Definition, which sets ten criteria a licence must satisfy—including free redistribution, availability of source code, permitted modifications and derived works, and no discrimination against persons or fields of endeavour.60 The framing is pragmatic: openness is defended less as a moral imperative and more as a superior method for producing reliable, evolvable software, echoing arguments Eric Raymond made in The Cathedral and the Bazaar.61

Copyleft versus permissive

The two traditions converge on almost all the same licences but disagree about which are ideal. Copyleft licences (GPL, LGPL, AGPL) require reciprocity; permissive licences (MIT, BSD, Apache 2.0) allow inclusion in proprietary products without a similar release obligation. Choice of licence is itself an institutional choice about how the commons should be defended: reciprocity locks openness into the downstream chain, whereas permissiveness prioritises adoption breadth even at the cost of some appropriation.62

Why software is a useful model

Software matters to the wider access debate because it is inherently compositional: libraries can be combined, forked, patched, and returned as improvements. That property maps naturally to a commons cycle of contribution, reuse, modification, and re-contribution. Scholarly literature, teaching materials, datasets, and civic records exhibit the same compositional structure to varying degrees, which is part of why licences originally designed for software have been adapted for text, data, and cultural works.63

What Broke in Scholarly Publishing

Scholarly journals began as a way to circulate discoveries, establish priority, and preserve the scientific record. The first scientific journals appeared in 1665, and the journal system eventually became the dominant channel for disseminating research.15 The strange modern twist is that much scholarly labor is donated or publicly funded while access is often sold back to universities, hospitals, firms, and individuals.

A large bibliometric study of 45 million Web of Science records found that, by 2013, the top five academic publishers accounted for more than half of papers in both natural and medical sciences and social sciences and humanities.15 RELX, Elsevier's parent company, reported a 2025 group adjusted operating margin of 34.8%; its Scientific, Technical & Medical segment reported GBP 2.714 billion in revenue and GBP 1.035 billion in adjusted operating profit in the same filing.16

Concentration Five publishers produced more than half of 2013 papers in the two broad fields studied by Lariviere, Haustein, and Mongeon.15
High APCs Nature lists a 2026 gold open-access APC of GBP 9,390 / USD 12,850 / EUR 10,850 for primary research in Nature.17

Open access can also reproduce inequity if it merely moves the paywall from reader to author. Article processing charges can be manageable for well-funded labs but exclusionary for independent scholars, early-career researchers, and institutions with weaker currencies or smaller grant budgets. And low-quality or deceptive journals can exploit author-pays incentives: a 2015 longitudinal study in BMC Medicine described the growth of publishers with questionable marketing and peer-review practices.18

That is why the best open-access argument is not "publishing costs nothing." It is that the costs should be transparent, proportionate, accountable, and aligned with scholarship rather than artificial scarcity.

The Ethical Split: Four Claims That All Have Teeth

The access debate gets crude when either side pretends the other has no point. Four normative claims all carry real weight in this argument, and mature policy has to hold them together rather than pick a favourite. Before any of them can be adjudicated in a specific case, each has to be given its strongest form.

The case for openness is strongest when information is publicly funded, necessary for public health or education, part of the law, or needed to verify scientific claims. The 2022 White House OSTP memorandum directed U.S. federal agencies to update public-access policies so federally funded research publications and supporting data become publicly accessible without embargo by the end of 2025.19 NIH's 2024 Public Access Policy, accelerated to July 1, 2025, requires covered author accepted manuscripts to be deposited in PubMed Central and made publicly available without embargo upon publication.20

The case for restriction is strongest when openness would expose private data, endanger people, erase creator income, encourage plagiarism, or remove the funding base for careful editorial work. Copyright, privacy law, security practice, biosecurity policy, and research ethics all exist because information can harm as well as liberate. Even the strongest advocates of open science accept that individual-level human-subjects data, weapons-relevant biology, and personally identifying records are not candidates for unrestricted release.91

Claim What It Gets Right What Can Go Wrong
Public investment should create public benefit. Taxpayers, patients, teachers, and researchers benefit when publicly funded work is reusable and verifiable. "Publicly funded" does not automatically settle privacy, safety, Indigenous data sovereignty, or publication-cost questions.
Creators need enforceable rights. Books, journalism, music, software, and scholarship require labor, and many creators are not institutionally funded. Overbroad control can block preservation, education, research, disability access, and follow-on creativity.
Peer review and curation cost money. Reliable knowledge needs editors, infrastructure, indexing, archiving, corrections, and misconduct handling. Prestige and market power can turn necessary costs into rent extraction.
Access is an equity issue. UNESCO data show deep disparities in research investment: Sub-Saharan Africa spent 0.38% of GDP on R&D in 2023, while Europe and Northern America spent 2.55%.21 Access alone does not create research capacity; training, infrastructure, language access, grants, and local journals matter too.

Public money, public benefit

The scale of publicly funded research turns this claim from an abstract principle into a large fiscal question. The National Science Foundation's National Center for Science and Engineering Statistics reported that U.S. federal obligations for research and development exceeded USD 195 billion in fiscal year 2023.77 The National Institutes of Health alone operates with a congressionally appropriated budget of over USD 47 billion in recent fiscal years, funding the biomedical literature that is the direct subject of the 2024 Public Access Policy.78 The European Union's Horizon Europe framework programme has a budget of approximately EUR 95.5 billion for 2021-2027 and requires immediate open access for peer-reviewed publications produced under its grants.79

The share of the resulting literature that is actually open has climbed but remains partial. Piwowar and colleagues' 2018 large-scale bibliometric study in PeerJ estimated that approximately 28% of scholarly articles catalogued in Crossref were available in some form of open access as of 2015, with the share continuing to rise year over year and higher rates among recently published papers.80 Federal and funder mandates are the largest lever available to shift that share upward without waiting for publishers to volunteer.

"Publicly funded" does not, however, settle every downstream question. Human-subjects research generates records covered by HIPAA in the United States and the GDPR in the European Union; those constraints persist regardless of the public origin of the grant.9293 Indigenous and traditional knowledge raises additional considerations that "open" alone cannot resolve. The Global Indigenous Data Alliance's CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance—collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, and ethics—were published in 2020 as a complement to the FAIR principles precisely because openness without governance can enable extraction rather than reciprocity.81

Creators need enforceable rights

The strongest form of this claim comes from creators whose work is not publicly funded and who cannot fall back on institutional salaries. The Authors Guild's 2018 income survey of U.S. book authors found that the median annual income from writing for full-time authors was USD 20,300, a decline of roughly 42% from 2009, with the income distribution highly concentrated in a small top decile.82 For freelance journalists, session musicians, and screenwriters, the shape is broadly similar: median incomes are low, distributions are highly skewed, and enforceable copyright is the mechanism through which sales, licensing, and royalty streams flow at all.

The international baseline for author rights is the Berne Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artistic Works, first concluded in 1886 and revised multiple times; it establishes minimum terms of protection and the principle of automatic copyright without formalities, and has more than 180 contracting parties administered by the World Intellectual Property Organization.83 Beyond economic rights, most Berne signatories recognise moral rights—attribution and integrity—which are independent of who owns the commercial rights and, in several jurisdictions, cannot be assigned or waived.83

The counter-point is that "creators need rights" is not the same as "rights should extend indefinitely." Copyright terms have been extended repeatedly in the twentieth century, most controversially by the U.S. Copyright Term Extension Act of 1998, which lengthened protection for most existing works to the life of the author plus seventy years and delayed the entry of works into the public domain by two decades.94 Fair use, first-sale doctrine, library privileges, and preservation exceptions exist because unlimited enforcement of creator rights would block the education, criticism, translation, and follow-on creativity that a healthy culture depends on.38 The claim has teeth; its edges do too.

Peer review and curation cost money

Reliable knowledge does not maintain itself. Editors, reviewers, indexers, copyeditors, permanent-identifier services, misconduct investigators, and preservation archives all sit behind the article a reader actually opens. A frequently overlooked component is the value of unpaid reviewer time: Aczel, Szaszi, and Holcombe estimated in Research Integrity and Peer Review that the monetary value of the time researchers donated to peer review in 2020 exceeded USD 1.5 billion for U.S.-based reviewers alone and reached several billion dollars across the major producer countries they studied.84 Peer review is not free; it is heavily subsidised by academic labour that publishers do not pay for.

Editorial integrity infrastructure is likewise real. The Committee on Publication Ethics maintains ten Core Practices covering allegations of misconduct, authorship disputes, complaints, conflicts of interest, corrections, data availability, ethical oversight, intellectual property, peer review processes, and post-publication discussion, and these practices structure how member journals handle disputed papers.85 The Retraction Watch Database, operated by the Center for Scientific Integrity, tracks tens of thousands of retracted papers and provides the raw material for meta-scientific work on research integrity.86 Long-term preservation is handled by dark archives such as CLOCKSS, a not-for-profit joint venture of academic publishers and research libraries that maintains a geographically distributed dark archive of the scholarly record.87

The failure mode of this claim is treating necessary costs as licence for arbitrary prices. Peer review, indexing, and preservation cost money; those costs are compatible with several revenue models, only some of which involve monopoly journal pricing. The RELX adjusted operating margins cited earlier16 are not a natural consequence of curation costs; they reflect market concentration among the top publishers documented by Larivière, Haustein, and Mongeon15 together with the prestige-driven inelasticity of library demand.

Access is an equity issue

Disparities in research investment are severe. UNESCO's 2026 data release shows Sub-Saharan Africa spending 0.38% of GDP on R&D in 2023, while Europe and Northern America spent 2.55%.21 That gap manifests not only as a research-output gap but as an infrastructure gap: fewer libraries can afford subscription bundles, fewer researchers can pay author-processing charges, and fewer institutions can staff the technical roles that support open science.

Subscription pricing pressure has been documented for decades. The Association of Research Libraries has tracked serial expenditures at its member libraries and reported cumulative inflation-adjusted increases in serial unit costs of several hundred percent between the mid-1980s and the 2010s, outpacing library budgets and forcing repeated cancellations.89 The "serials crisis" is the practical name for the pattern by which library budgets have failed to keep up with journal-bundle price growth, and it is the material precondition for the large-scale cancellations of "big deal" contracts that many university systems have negotiated over the past decade.95

Language and geography compound the pricing gap. Global bibliometric databases have historically over-represented English-language publication; scholarship published in national or regional languages can remain invisible to the citation measures used in hiring, promotion, and ranking. Juan Pablo Alperin has documented Latin America's SciELO- and Redalyc-centred publishing system as a large-scale alternative in which most journals are free to both readers and authors, are supported by public and university funding, and publish substantially in Spanish and Portuguese.90 SciELO now hosts scholarly content from journals across Latin America, the Caribbean, Iberia, and South Africa on a no-fee model.88

Partial-access programmes narrow but do not close the gap. Research4Life marked its 25th anniversary in 2026 and describes itself as a public-private partnership giving more than 12,000 institutions in over 120 countries access to more than 250,000 journals, books, and databases.22 That is important bridge-building, but it is still mediated access controlled by publisher participation rather than a fully open knowledge system, and it does nothing to remove APC barriers when researchers from those countries try to publish.

Where the four claims collide

The four claims are not additive. They point in different directions in specific cases, and honest policy has to name the conflicts rather than paper them over.

  • Openness vs. creator livelihood. A salaried academic author can accept a CC-BY licence with no personal income loss. A freelance journalist or novelist cannot. Uniform "open" mandates that ignore this distinction risk absorbing income from the creators with the least institutional cushion.82
  • Openness vs. privacy. Publicly funded biomedical research produces the very datasets whose subjects did not consent to have re-identified. Access controls on individual-level health data are not a violation of the public-benefit principle; they are its condition of possibility.9293
  • Curation costs vs. equity. Author-pays open access is compatible with the "peer review costs money" claim but often incompatible with the equity claim, because it moves the barrier from reader to author and disadvantages those without grant support.18
  • Equity vs. speed. Immediate open access under strict funder mandates can shift costs to author-pays models that entrench existing inequalities unless funders also subsidise diamond OA infrastructure.29

The plural conclusion is that no single instrument—subscription, APC, diamond OA, embargoed green OA, transformative agreement, or all-rights-reserved copyright—can simultaneously satisfy all four claims for all kinds of information. The design task is to match the instrument to the information: the law of the land needs the strongest openness, individual-level medical data needs the strongest access controls, publicly funded literature falls in between, and creative works occupy a third region with its own balance. The four claims are teeth on a single mouth; policy is the bite pattern.

Shadow Libraries: Signal, Symptom, and Legal Risk

Shadow libraries are the uncomfortable evidence that demand for knowledge often exceeds legal supply. Sci-Hub, founded in 2011, became the best-known scholarly example. A peer-reviewed 2018 eLife study found that, as of March 2017, Sci-Hub's database contained 68.9% of 81.6 million scholarly articles registered with Crossref and 85.1% of articles published in toll-access journals.23

Library Genesis has a longer and more culturally specific history. Balazs Bodo's chapter in Shadow Libraries traces LibGen to Russian and post-Soviet practices of informal text sharing, academic scarcity, and the consolidation of earlier digital collections; the chapter describes LibGen as starting around 2008 and later absorbing material from Library.nu.24

Z-Library illustrates the legal side of the same conflict. In November 2022, the U.S. Department of Justice announced charges against two Russian nationals alleged to operate Z-Library, including criminal copyright infringement, wire fraud, and money laundering; DOJ also said the defendants were arrested in Argentina and that a network of domains had been seized.25

Aaron Swartz's case became the access debate's most visible American flashpoint. JSTOR says that, between late September 2010 and early January 2011, someone downloaded 4.8 million articles through MIT's network, that Swartz was later identified as the person responsible, and that JSTOR reached a civil settlement with him before telling federal prosecutors it had no further interest in the matter and did not want to press charges.47 MIT's later FAQ says Swartz faced 13 felony counts stemming from the downloads and died by suicide on January 11, 2013, before trial; the MIT review faulted the Institute's neutrality as a missed opportunity for leadership on open access, computer-crime law, and institutional responsibility.48

Shadow libraries are not a stable solution. They can violate copyright, expose users to operational and security risks, lack durable preservation guarantees, and route scholarly communication through adversarial infrastructure. But treating them only as piracy misses the diagnostic point: people use them because legal access is often fragmented, expensive, slow, geographically unequal, or locked behind institutional privilege.

Working Alternatives: What a Better Commons Looks Like

The strongest path forward is not one mechanism. It is a stack: public-access law, funder mandates, green repositories, no-fee publishing, open licenses, library consortia, preservation infrastructure, and quality standards.

Funder and Policy Mandates

Plan S launched in 2018 through cOAlition S and requires, from 2021, that publications from participating funders be published in open-access venues or made immediately available in repositories without embargo.26 UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on Open Science gives governments a broader framework that includes open scientific knowledge, infrastructure, capacity-building, incentives, and international cooperation.27

Green Open Access and Public Repositories

Repositories lower access barriers without requiring every journal to become open overnight. arXiv covers physics, mathematics, computer science, quantitative biology, quantitative finance, statistics, electrical engineering and systems science, and economics; submissions are moderated but not peer-reviewed by arXiv.9 PubMed Central preserves full-text biomedical and life-sciences literature at NIH/NLM, while PubMed itself contains more than 40 million citations and abstracts.1128

Diamond Open Access

Diamond open access removes fees for both readers and authors. UNESCO defines it as a model supported by shared infrastructures and open licensing, providing scholarly knowledge as a digital public good with no reader or author fees.29 DOAJ's public directory currently lists 23,385 open-access journals, including 14,438 journals without fees.30

Open Research Europe is one concrete platform in this direction. Established by the European Commission in 2021, ORE describes itself as an open-access publishing platform with open peer review and no cost for readers or authors; in 2026 it began a transition toward CERN-hosted, collectively governed infrastructure with broader European eligibility.31

Books, Not Just Articles

Monographs matter, especially in the humanities and social sciences. MIT Press's Direct to Open model uses collective library support to open scholarly books; MIT Press announced that it opened 80 new monographs in 2025 and 72 frontlist titles for 2026 through Direct to Open.32

Transformative Agreements

Germany's Projekt DEAL shows a negotiated route. The Elsevier-DEAL agreement registry lists a 2023-2028 publish-and-read agreement, and the Max Planck Digital Library says the agreement allows affiliated corresponding authors to publish open access in more than 2,500 Elsevier hybrid and fully open-access journals while restoring broad reading access.3334 These agreements can accelerate access, but critics worry they may preserve incumbent publisher power if they do not actually shift the system toward lower-cost, community-governed publishing.

Integrity Standards

Open does not mean unreviewed. DOAJ, COPE, OASPA, and WAME publish Principles of Transparency and Best Practice in Scholarly Publishing covering journal identity, peer review, fees, licensing, archiving, corrections, conflicts of interest, and research misconduct processes.35 Those boring-sounding standards are the plumbing of trust.

A Practical Test

  • Open by default for law, publicly funded research, public-health evidence, educational materials, and preservation of public-domain culture.
  • Paid and protected where creator livelihood, privacy, safety, trade secrecy, or community control make openness inappropriate.
  • Transparent costs for publishing, hosting, review, preservation, metadata, and correction.
  • Reusable rights through open licenses when reuse is the point, not merely free-to-read access.
  • Governance beyond platforms so public knowledge is not dependent on one company, one domain, or one political jurisdiction.

The Wider Commons Family

Open access to scholarship is one member of a larger set of movements that share a common structure: individual contribution flows into a shared resource, which is then reused, modified, and recombined to produce further contributions. Each member specialises the general idea for a different kind of information.

Wikipedia The English Wikipedia contains over 7 million articles, and the family of Wikimedia projects together serves many billions of monthly page views across hundreds of languages.65
Open Educational Resources MIT OpenCourseWare has published materials from more than 2,500 MIT courses at no cost; OpenStax reports its openly licensed textbooks have saved students more than USD 3 billion since 2012.6645
Open Data The U.S. federal data catalogue at data.gov lists hundreds of thousands of publicly available datasets; the EU Open Data Portal aggregates open data from institutions across the Union.67

Open science and open notebooks

Open science extends the commons idea from finished publications to the research process itself: preregistration, open protocols, open lab notebooks, open peer review, open data, and open methods. UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on Open Science treats these as an integrated framework rather than a menu of separate reforms.27 The Center for Open Science's Registered Reports format, adopted by hundreds of journals, illustrates one concrete institutional change: peer review of the study design before results are known, in an attempt to reduce publication bias and questionable research practices.68

Open hardware

The Open Source Hardware Association maintains a definition and a certification programme for physical devices whose design files, schematics, and documentation are released under licences that permit study, modification, and redistribution.69 Open hardware demonstrates that even objects with real manufacturing costs can share informational designs on commons terms; what is licensed is not the finished device but the compositional information behind it.

The common structure

Across these movements, the same cycle repeats: individual contribution → shared resource → reuse → modification and recombination → further contribution. This is a very different pattern from the alternative arrangement of individual creation → exclusive ownership → restricted access → sale or licence.58 Neither pattern is inherently correct for all kinds of information, and mature systems mix them: proprietary drug pipelines coexist with public genomic databases; commercial studios coexist with public-domain film archives. The commons argument is not that exclusive rights should disappear, only that they are one tool among several and are frequently misapplied to information whose social value depends on being widely shared.

Paying for Free Knowledge

A common misreading of the open movements is that they oppose economic value in information. They do not. Almost all of them accept that the labour of producing knowledge—research, writing, editing, coding, curating, preserving—is expensive and must be paid for somehow. Their claim is narrower: the artificial scarcity of the resulting information is not the only way, and often not the best way, to fund that labour.

The distinction is between free information and free production. Free information does not imply free production; it implies that the point at which value is captured is shifted away from restricting access to the finished good.70 Several revenue models have proven viable in different sectors.

Model How Value Is Captured Illustrative Examples
Services and support The software or content is free; the vendor sells support contracts, training, integration, and certification. Red Hat, acquired by IBM in July 2019 for approximately USD 34 billion, is the archetypal case.71
Open core A permissively licensed core is complemented by proprietary enterprise features or a hosted service. MongoDB, Elastic, GitLab, and HashiCorp have all operated variants of this model, with ongoing licensing debates.72
Hosting The code is open; the operator sells convenience, uptime, and scale. Automattic operates the hosted WordPress.com service on top of the open-source WordPress codebase.73
Dual licensing The same code is offered under both a strong copyleft licence and a commercial licence. MySQL and Qt used this pattern for many years to combine community use with proprietary sales.62
Donations and membership Individual and institutional supporters fund a nonprofit steward that keeps the resource open. The Wikimedia Foundation reported total revenue of over USD 180 million in fiscal year 2023-24, the majority from small donations.74
Collective funding Libraries, funders, or governments pool payments to open works up front. MIT Press's Direct to Open and SCOAP3 in high-energy physics are working examples.3275
Public funding Grants, agency budgets, or infrastructure programmes cover production directly. arXiv, PubMed Central, and Open Research Europe rely on a mix of public and philanthropic support.91131

None of these models is a silver bullet. Open core faces recurring tension between openness and monetisation, prompting well-known relicensing episodes such as MongoDB's move to the Server Side Public Licence in 2018 and Elastic's move away from Apache 2.0 in 2021.72 Donation funding depends on continued institutional and individual trust. Public funding is politically vulnerable. Consortial models must solve free-rider problems. The honest answer is plural: knowledge commons require plural funding, and the design of those funding mechanisms is at least as important as licence choice.76

The strategic point is that "give it away" and "have a business model" are not opposites in this landscape. They are the two halves of every stable open project. Where a project has one without the other, either the commons erodes or the finances do.

Where This Lands

Information does not literally want anything. People do. Students want textbooks they can afford. Researchers want literature they can inspect. Patients want access to the science their taxes and bodies helped produce. Authors want attribution and income. Editors want standards. Libraries want budgets that do not buckle under monopoly pricing. Societies want knowledge that survives beyond quarterly earnings, domain seizures, or institutional subscriptions.

The answer is not "everything free" or "everything owned." The answer is a knowledge commons with working institutions underneath it: rights where rights are needed, openness where exclusion harms the public, and enough funding to keep the record trustworthy. Brand's paradox remains useful because both halves are true. Information can be priceless and still need to be freely shared.

The through-line across open access, open source, open data, open educational resources, and open science is not the abolition of ownership. It is the recognition that knowledge is cumulative: what one generation restricts, the next generation cannot build upon. The technologies that made copying nearly free also made restriction more expensive and more contestable. What remains is the harder political work of deciding which parts of the informational world should be governed as a commons, which parts should remain private, and how the institutions in between should be paid for. That is not a slogan. It is a set of choices we have to keep making, sector by sector, deliberately.
