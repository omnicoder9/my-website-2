IThe Question

Every inquiry has to be conducted by some means, and the inquiry into reason has a peculiar shape: the tribunal before which the matter is tried turns out to be the accused. We ask what reason is, and we must reason to an answer. We ask whether the standards of good thinking are binding on everyone or merely local customs dressed up as necessities — and any verdict we reach, on either side, will itself be offered as a piece of reasoning, expecting to be judged by the very standards in dispute. There is no neutral ground, no place to stand outside the practice of giving and weighing reasons from which the practice could be assessed. This is not a defect to be cleared away. It is the first and most stubborn fact about the subject, and much of what follows is an attempt to understand what kind of authority a faculty can have when it is the sole judge in its own case.

The word covers a surprising amount of ground. When we say that human beings are rational animals, we name a capacity. When we say that the icy road was a reason to drive slowly, we point to a consideration. When we work through a proof line by line, we perform an activity. And when we call a belief or a decision reasonable, we ascribe a property — conformity to some standard of how one ought to think or act. These are not four words that happen to share a spelling; they are four aspects of a single phenomenon, and the connections among them are exactly what a theory of reason must explain. Does the capacity reliably track the considerations? Is the activity governed by norms we discover, or norms we invent? Is calling something reasonable a report about the world, an expression of our commitments, or a bid for others' agreement?

Behind these questions sits the one this essay takes as its spine. Suppose two communities disagree, not about some matter of fact, but about what counts as a good inference or a sufficient reason. Is there anything that could make one of them right — some fact about validity or justification that holds independently of what anyone accepts — or is the standard of the reasonable, in the end, just the standard a community happens to enforce, so that to be reasonable is at bottom to think as one's fellows think? The crude version of the second view is easy to refute and few have held it. But it has sophisticated descendants, and the interesting question is whether any of them can be made to work without quietly presupposing the very thing they set out to deny.

The stakes are not merely academic. If the reasonable were simply the customary, then the reformer who tells a community that its settled ways of thinking are mistaken would be, by definition, unreasonable — and the possibility of criticising a consensus from within would collapse. If, on the other hand, rational norms floated entirely free of any human practice, graspable only by some infallible intellectual vision, it becomes obscure how creatures like us could ever come to know them, or how we could be so often and so systematically wrong. A satisfying account has to make room for both the objectivity that lets us correct one another and the fallibility that makes correction necessary. Getting the relationship right between reason and agreement is the work of the whole piece.

The plan is to earn an answer rather than assert one. We begin with the concept itself, teasing apart its senses and mapping the kinds of inference it licenses. We then follow the idea across its long history — through the Greek discovery that argument has a form, the medieval effort to fit reason and revelation into one frame, the early-modern quarrel over whether reason is a source of substantive knowledge or the servant of the passions, and Kant's decisive reframing of reason as a power that legislates to itself. We watch logic come of age in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and encounter its own limits; we sit in on the laboratory, where psychologists have measured how people actually reason and found the results unflattering; and we ask why a species should have the faculty at all. Only then, with the materials in hand, do we return to the question of authority and to the living philosophical debate about reasons, rationality, and the demands they place on us.

IIAn Anatomy of Reason

Confusion about reason is often confusion among its senses, so it is worth fixing them before anything else. Consider first the difference between reason as a faculty — a standing power of the mind — and a reason as an item that counts in favour of something. The faculty is a mass noun; reasons are countable. A great deal of recent philosophy argues that the second is the more fundamental: that we should explain what it is to be rational in terms of responding correctly to reasons, rather than the other way around.[1] On that approach a reason is simply a consideration that favours a response — the storm favours cancelling the flight, the evidence favours the verdict — and reasoning is the activity of moving among such considerations while rationality is the property of doing it well.[2] Whether the countable or the mass sense is basic is itself contested, but keeping them apart dissolves a surprising number of pseudo-problems.

Cutting across that distinction is another, at least as old as Aristotle: the difference between theoretical and practical reason. Theoretical reason is directed at truth; its characteristic output is belief, and it answers to how things are. Practical reason is directed at action; its characteristic output is choice, and it answers to what is to be done.[3] The two have different directions of fit — belief aims to match the world, action aims to make the world match the intention — and much turns on whether they are two faculties or one faculty facing two ways.[4] A further question haunts the practical side. On a minimal, broadly Humean picture, practical reason does nothing but figure out means to ends it does not itself supply; it is purely instrumental. On a more ambitious, broadly Kantian picture, reason can assess ends themselves and impose substantive constraints on what one may will. The distance between these two pictures is one of the deepest fault lines in the subject, and we will return to it more than once.

The vocabulary itself carries sediment worth sifting. The Greeks had logos, which meant at once speech, ratio, account, and the rational order of things, and nous, the mind's power to grasp first principles directly. Latin split the field into ratio, the discursive movement from premise to conclusion, and intellectus, the immediate apprehension of what a discursive argument must start from. German preserved a version of the same contrast in Kant's technical opposition between Verstand, the understanding that applies concepts, and Vernunft, the reason that seeks the unconditioned.[5] The recurring intuition is that reasoning has two moments — a step-by-step inferential labour and a non-inferential seeing of where the steps begin — and any complete theory owes an account of both. Where the starting points come from, and whether they are self-evident or merely where argument happens to run out, will prove central to the question of authority.

The forms of inference

If reasoning is moving in thought from some claims to others, the natural next question is what forms that movement can take. Three are standardly distinguished, and they behave very differently. Deduction is the movement whose conclusion is already contained in its premises: if the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false, which is why valid deductive inference is called truth-preserving. Its great virtue is security and its great limitation is that it is, in a precise sense, uninformative — it makes explicit what was implicitly granted, and no more. Induction, generalising from observed cases to unobserved ones or to a universal claim, is the reverse: it is ampliative, genuinely extending our knowledge beyond the evidence, and for exactly that reason it is not truth-preserving. True premises can yield a false inductive conclusion, and no finite body of evidence entails a universal law. Abduction, or inference to the best explanation, reasons from a body of data to the hypothesis that would best account for it; it is the engine of most scientific and everyday theorising, and it too is defeasible, since the best explanation we can presently frame may be false.

These distinctions matter for our theme because they locate the pressure points. Deduction looks like the home of necessity and the least plausible candidate for anything conventional — yet, as we will see, even the authority of a deductive rule turns out to be hard to ground without circularity. Induction wears its problem on its sleeve: since Hume, we have known that no non-circular argument establishes that the future will resemble the past, and every attempt to license the inference seems to help itself to the very principle in question.[6] The forms of inference are thus not a neutral inventory. They are the places where the question of reason's foundations first becomes acute.

One more preliminary. It is tempting to identify reasoning with logic — to suppose that to reason well just is to conform to the laws of valid inference. But the identification is too quick, and recognising why sets up a problem we will confront directly later on. Logic is a theory of what follows from what; it is not, on its face, a set of instructions about what to believe.[7] When your premises jointly entail a conclusion you find absurd, logic does not tell you to accept the absurdity; it tells you only that you cannot keep all the premises and reject the conclusion, leaving open which to abandon. Good reasoning involves managing one's beliefs over time under limits of memory and attention, deciding what is worth inferring and what to give up — and none of that is dictated by the entailment relations logic describes. The gap between formal validity and rational belief is real, and it is one more reason that reason resists reduction to any single formal core.

With the concept sorted and the forms of inference in view, the shape of the journey ahead can be seen at a glance. The idea of reason did not arrive whole; it was assembled, contested, and remade across more than two millennia and across fields that only much later learned to talk to one another. IIILogos and the Birth of Inference

The Greeks did not invent reasoning — every culture argues — but they made the decisive discovery that argument has a form, separable from its content, which can be studied and judged in its own right. The word at the centre of the story, logos, meant speech and account and ratio and the intelligible order of the world all at once, and its ambiguity is telling: to give a logos was to give a reasoned account, and to grasp the logos of things was to grasp why they are as they are. Already in Parmenides one finds reason set against the senses as the faculty that reaches truth, the deliverances of the eye and ear dismissed as the way of mere seeming; and in Heraclitus the logos is the common rational principle that governs all things, which most people fail to comprehend even as they live by it.

Plato gave this the first systematic shape. In the Republic the soul is divided into three parts, and the highest, the calculating or reasoning part, is fitted to rule because it alone apprehends what is truly real.[8] Reality itself is graded: the visible world of changing things is only an image of the intelligible world of Forms, and the mind ascends from opinion about images to knowledge of the Forms by way of a discipline that moves from hypotheses to first principles. At the summit stands the Form of the Good, which Plato compares to the sun — as the sun makes things visible and gives them growth, so the Good makes the Forms intelligible and gives them their being. It is the founding image of reason as illumination, a metaphor of light that will recur for two thousand years: to reason well is to see, by a light not one's own, what was always there to be seen.

With Aristotle the study of reasoning becomes a science. The treatises later collected as the Organon — the “instrument” of thought — contain the first formal logic in history: the theory of the syllogism, in which the validity of an inference is shown to depend on its structure alone, so that any argument of a given valid pattern is valid whatever its terms.[3] This is the birth of logical form as an object of study, and it is difficult to overstate its originality. Aristotle further distinguished demonstrative knowledge, which derives conclusions with necessity from premises already known, from the grasp of those premises themselves, which cannot be demonstrated on pain of regress and must instead be apprehended by nous, an intellectual insight into first principles. He divided the intellect into a theoretical exercise aimed at truth and a practical one aimed at action, and he insisted that practical wisdom — phronesis — is a distinct excellence, not reducible to theoretical knowledge, since knowing the good in general does not settle what to do here and now. Even his cryptic remarks on an “active intellect” that makes potential objects of thought actual would occupy commentators for a millennium.

The Stoics extended the reach of reason in two directions. Cosmologically, they identified the logos with a divine rational fire pervading and ordering the universe, of which the human mind is a fragment, so that to live rationally is to live in accordance with the rational structure of nature itself. Logically, Chrysippus and his school developed something Aristotle had not: a logic of whole propositions and their connectives, built on a handful of basic argument patterns from which the rest could be derived.[9] Where Aristotle had analysed inferences by the terms within propositions, the Stoics analysed inferences among propositions — if the first, then the second; but the first; therefore the second — and in doing so produced what is, in effect, the first propositional logic, a rediscovery of whose sophistication had to wait until the twentieth century.[10]

Against all this confidence stood the Skeptics, and it is with them that our central worry first appears in sharp form. The Pyrrhonist tradition pressed a simple, corrosive question: by what criterion do we judge a claim to be true? If we answer with some standard, we may be asked to justify the standard, which requires a further standard, and so without end; or we may justify it by appeal to what it was meant to underwrite, and argue in a circle; or we may simply stop and declare some standard self-evident, which is to assert without proof. Every attempt to ground our judgements, the Skeptics urged, terminates in one of these three unsatisfactory ways.[11] This is the ancient ancestor of a trilemma we will meet again in modern dress, and it frames the whole subsequent history: the more reason claimed to secure knowledge, the more urgent became the question of what secured reason.

IVFaith, Reason, and the Two Lights

The medieval centuries are often caricatured as an age in which reason bowed to authority, but the reality is stranger and more interesting: they were centuries of intense, technical labour over exactly how far unaided reason could reach and where it had to give way to revelation. The inherited distinction between ratio, the discursive movement of argument, and intellectus, the intuitive grasp of principles, structured the whole discussion, and the governing metaphor remained one of light. Augustine held that the mind sees eternal truths only by a divine illumination, a light in which the intelligible is disclosed much as Plato's sun disclosed the Forms, and he made the relation of belief and understanding programmatic: one believes in order to understand, faith opening the door through which reason then walks.[12] Anselm turned the same formula into a method — faith seeking understanding — and produced, in the ontological argument, a piece of pure a priori reasoning that purports to derive the existence of God from the concept of God alone, the boldest possible claim for what reason can accomplish unaided.[13]

The towering synthesis is Aquinas's. He drew a careful line between the truths that natural reason can establish on its own and those accessible only through revelation. That God exists and something of the divine nature, he argued, reason can reach — his famous five ways are meant as demonstrations available in principle to any competent thinker — while other doctrines, such as the Trinity or the Incarnation, exceed reason's grasp and can only be received on faith, though never against reason.[14] Reason thus retains a genuine autonomy within its own domain even as it serves a theology beyond it; the two are distinct sources that cannot, rightly understood, conflict. This settlement gave philosophical reason room to operate while subordinating it, and the tension in that arrangement — how autonomous can a handmaiden be? — would drive centuries of further argument.

None of this happened in a Latin vacuum. Greek philosophy reached the medieval West largely through the Islamic world, where it had been preserved, translated, and enormously developed. Avicenna built an elaborate theory of intellect culminating in a separate Active Intellect from which the human mind receives the forms it thinks; Averroes, the greatest of Aristotle's commentators, argued for the fundamental harmony of philosophy and religion as two roads to a single truth, and defended the pursuit of demonstrative reasoning as itself a religious obligation.[15] The most searching internal critique also came from within that tradition. Al-Ghazali, in his Incoherence of the Philosophers, mounted a devastating attack on the metaphysical pretensions of the Aristotelians, arguing that their proofs fell far short of demonstration; most strikingly, he denied that we ever perceive genuine necessary connection between cause and effect, holding that what we call causation is only the habitual conjunction of events ordained by God — an analysis that anticipates Hume's by some six hundred years.[16] In the Jewish tradition, Maimonides undertook a parallel reconciliation, guiding the philosophically perplexed between the claims of Aristotelian reason and those of scripture and insisting on the limits of what the human intellect can know of God.[17]

The transmission and contest of these ideas reshaped Latin thought, and not without friction: the reception of a thoroughgoing Aristotelian naturalism provoked ecclesiastical condemnations of propositions thought to subordinate faith to philosophy, and the spectre of a “double truth” — one for reason, another for faith — was raised and repudiated.[18] Out of these disputes came a sharper sense of reason's boundaries. The later medieval turn associated with Scotus and Ockham, with its emphasis on the divine will and its suspicion of necessary structures in the world that reason might read off a priori, widened the gap between what faith holds and what reason can prove, and in doing so cleared ground on which the early modern conception of reason — more autonomous, more anxious about its own foundations — would be built.

VReason Unbound: Early Modernity

The textbooks divide the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries into rationalists and empiricists, and like most textbook divisions this one is too clean to be quite true; but it captures a real disagreement about whether reason is a source of substantive knowledge of the world or only an instrument for organising what the senses supply. What unites the period, across that divide, is a new and self-conscious concern with method — with securing knowledge on foundations that reason could inspect and certify for itself, now that the scaffolding of inherited authority had begun to give way.

Descartes made the ambition explicit. Seeking an unshakable starting point, he doubted everything that could be doubted and found one thing that could not: that he was thinking, and so existing, even in the act of doubt. Upon this he proposed to rebuild knowledge by the “natural light” of reason, the faculty of clear and distinct perception, taking as true whatever presented itself with the same self-evidence as the cogito.[19] Reason, for Descartes, is not the servant of the senses but their judge; and famously he held it to be the most equally distributed thing in the world, since every rational being possesses the light entire and errs only by misusing it. Spinoza pushed the rationalist program to its limit, casting his entire ethics in the geometrical manner, deriving propositions about God, mind, and freedom from definitions and axioms as if they were theorems, and ranking knowledge into kinds — from confused sensory imagination, through the adequate common notions of reason, to a third and highest intuitive knowledge that grasps things through their first cause.[20]

Leibniz supplied the deepest metaphysical articulation and the most prophetic fantasy. He elevated to first principles the law of contradiction, governing the truths of reason that hold in every possible world, and the principle of sufficient reason, that nothing is so without a reason why it is so rather than otherwise; the first grounds necessity, the second grounds contingent fact.[21] And he dreamed of a universal characteristic and a calculus of reasoning that would render all disputes decidable: when disagreements arose, the parties would simply take up their pens and calculate. The dream was premature by centuries and, as we will see, provably unrealisable in full — but it names an aspiration, the reduction of reasoning to mechanical computation, that has shaped logic and cognitive science ever since. On the other side of the divide, Locke insisted that the materials of all knowledge come from experience, denied that any ideas or principles are innate, and assigned to reason the more modest office of discovering the connections among ideas the senses provide; he was careful, too, to mark the province of reason off from that of faith, holding that nothing should be believed on revelation that contradicts the plain deliverances of reason.[22]

Then came Hume, and with him the most powerful assault the pretensions of reason had yet sustained. Two of his conclusions changed everything. The first concerns induction. We believe, and cannot help believing, that the future will resemble the past — that bread will nourish tomorrow as it did today — yet Hume showed that no argument justifies this belief. A demonstrative argument cannot, since a change in the course of nature implies no contradiction; and a probable argument cannot, since every such argument already presupposes that the unobserved resembles the observed, which is the very point at issue.[6] Our reliance on induction, he concluded, rests not on reason but on custom, a habit of expectation instilled by repetition. The second concerns motivation and value. Reason alone, Hume argued, can never move us to act, since it discerns only relations of ideas and matters of fact, neither of which by itself supplies an end; it is, in his deliberately provocative phrase, and ought to be, the slave of the passions.[23] He noted, too, the curious slide by which writers pass from statements about what is to statements about what ought to be, as though the latter followed from the former, and demanded to know how the transition could be justified. In three strokes — induction ungrounded, reason inert, ought unmoored from is — Hume left the ambitious conception of reason in ruins, and set the problem that Kant awoke to solve.

VIKant's Tribunal

Kant said that Hume woke him from his dogmatic slumber, and the work that resulted reoriented the entire subject. His organising image is the one this essay has borrowed: reason must submit to a critique conducted by reason itself, instituting a tribunal that will secure its rightful claims and dismiss its groundless pretensions.[5] The court is self-constituted; there is no higher authority to appeal to, because any such appeal would itself be an exercise of reason. What Kant proposed to try was reason's ambition to know the world as it is in itself, independently of any experience — and his verdict was a subtle division of the estate.

His central move, the one he likened to Copernicus's, was to reverse the assumed relation between mind and world. Instead of supposing that our cognition must conform to objects, he proposed that objects, as we can ever know them, must conform to the constitution of our cognition. Space, time, and the categories — substance, causality, and the rest — are not features we read off an independent reality but forms the mind imposes in the very act of experiencing anything at all. This is why there can be substantive knowledge that is nonetheless prior to experience: the synthetic a priori is possible precisely because we are describing the conditions we ourselves bring to any possible experience. On this account causation, the very relation Hume had dissolved into custom, is restored as an a priori condition of objective experience — not a habit but a rule without which experience of an ordered world would be impossible.

Kant sharply distinguished two higher faculties. The understanding is the power of concepts and judgements, at home within experience; reason proper is the power that seeks the unconditioned, the complete explanation, the totality that experience never delivers. And here Kant located a permanent temptation. Reason cannot help pressing beyond the bounds of possible experience — asking whether the world had a beginning, whether the soul is simple, whether there is a necessary being — and when it does, it falls into characteristic error, generating contradictions in which equally valid-seeming arguments prove opposite conclusions. These antinomies are not blunders a cleverer thinker could avoid; they are structural, the natural and inevitable illusion of a faculty overreaching its jurisdiction. The ideas of reason retain a legitimate use, but a regulative one: they guide inquiry toward ever more complete explanation without ever describing objects we could encounter. In marking this limit, reason performs its highest act — the discovery, by reason, of what reason cannot do.

The practical philosophy completes the reframing and, in Kant's own estimation, redeems what the theoretical critique had denied. Here reason is not merely instrumental. Practical reason legislates the moral law to itself in the form of the categorical imperative, a demand that binds unconditionally and can be derived, Kant held, from the bare idea of a rational will — act only on a principle you could will to be universal.[24] The dignity of a rational being lies in this autonomy: in being subject only to laws it gives itself, rather than to any external authority or contingent desire. This yields a third answer to our recurring question, distinct from both the objectivist and the conventionalist. The authority of rational norms, for Kant, is neither a fact about a Platonic heaven that we somehow intuit nor a mere convention that a community enforces; it is the self-legislation of reason as such, valid for every rational being because it flows from the conditions of rational agency itself. Whether that ingenious middle path can bear the weight Kant placed on it — whether reason can generate binding content from its own form alone — remains one of the great open questions, and the constitutivist program we will meet later is its direct descendant. Kant himself gave the age its motto and reason its charge: dare to know, and to think for oneself.

VIIThe Birth of Modern Logic

The nineteenth century inherited Kant's picture of an active, self-legislating reason and pulled it in two opposite directions — toward history and toward form. Hegel radicalised the historical strand. For him reason is not a fixed faculty surveying a finished world but a dynamic, developing process; the fixed oppositions of the understanding — between subject and object, finite and infinite — are overcome by a higher speculative reason that grasps their unity, and this movement is not merely in our thinking but in reality itself, which unfolds dialectically through history.[25] His notorious dictum that what is rational is actual and what is actual is rational expresses the ambition at its most extreme: to identify the structure of reason with the structure of the real, so that history is the process by which reason comes to know itself.[26] Whatever one makes of the metaphysics, Hegel bequeathed a permanent alternative to the timeless conception — the thought that reason has a history, that its standards develop, and that to understand rationality one must understand its genesis. That thought, secularised, runs through much of what follows.

Mill pulled the other way but with a naturalising twist that would prove a useful foil. In his vast System of Logic he treated logic and inductive method as the general theory of evidence and proof, and he leaned toward regarding the laws of logic themselves as very general truths about how the mind works — the most deeply entrenched generalisations of experience.[27] This is psychologism: the assimilation of the laws of thought to laws of thinking, of logic to a branch of empirical psychology. It is an intuitive position, and it would be the target of the most consequential polemic in the philosophy of logic.

For the decisive event of the century was Frege's, and it was twofold. In 1879 he published a slim volume that, almost single-handedly, created modern logic: a formal language capable of expressing generality by means of quantifiers and variables, able to represent inferences involving relations and multiple generality that had defeated the syllogistic for two thousand years.[28] It was the first advance on Aristotle's logic of comparable magnitude, and it made possible everything that came after — the formalisation of mathematics, the theory of computation, the very idea of a rigorous artificial language. But Frege paired this technical revolution with a philosophical thesis he defended with unmatched clarity, and it bears directly on our theme. The laws of logic, he insisted, are not laws of thinking but laws of truth. They do not describe how anyone in fact reasons; they prescribe how one must reason to preserve truth, and they would hold whether or not any mind ever grasped them.[29] To confuse the psychological question of how we think with the logical question of what is valid is, he argued, to abandon the objectivity of logic altogether, for if logic merely reported our mental habits it could carry no normative force and could not be used to correct thinking at all. Frege placed the contents of thought — the thoughts themselves, as opposed to the private acts of thinking them — in a “third realm,” neither physical nor mental, objective and shared. Whatever the difficulties of that metaphysics, his anti-psychologism is the sharpest statement ever made of the claim that the standard of reason is not a matter of what anyone thinks or agrees to.

Husserl, who had begun as a mathematician, mounted a parallel and equally influential attack. In the prolegomena to his Logical Investigations he argued that psychologism, by grounding the necessary laws of logic in contingent facts about the mind, could not account for their necessity and collapsed into a self-refuting relativism: if the laws of logic were merely how human minds happen to work, then the law of non-contradiction would bind only beings constituted as we are, and the psychologistic thesis itself, being a claim that purports to be objectively true, would undercut its own standing.[30] The convergence of Frege and Husserl, from different traditions, established anti-psychologism as the dominant view and set the terms for the twentieth century's confrontation with the foundations of reason — a confrontation that would test whether the objectivity they defended could survive the discovery of logic's own limits.

VIIIFormal Reason and Its Limits

The early twentieth century inherited Frege's tools and set about building. The great project of logicism sought to derive all of mathematics from logic alone; Russell and Whitehead's monumental attempt, whatever its ultimate fate, embodied the conviction that rigorous formal reasoning could secure the most certain of the sciences on purely logical foundations. Wittgenstein, in his early work, gave this formalism a metaphysical reading of startling ambition: logic is the scaffolding of the world, the propositions of logic are tautologies that say nothing because they are true under every possibility, and the limits of what can be sensibly said coincide with the limits of logical form.[31] Reason, on this picture, has a perfectly definite structure, and philosophy's task is to display it and thereby dissolve the pseudo-problems that arise from misunderstanding it.

The logical positivists of the Vienna Circle drew a radical conclusion about the status of that structure, and here conventionalism enters in its most explicit form. If logical and mathematical truths say nothing about the world — if they are true by virtue of the meanings of their terms — then, Carnap argued, the choice of a logical framework is not a matter of getting reality right but of practical convenience. In his principle of tolerance he made the point programmatic: there are no morals in logic; everyone is free to construct their own logical language as they wish, and the only question that can sensibly be raised about a system of logic is not whether it is true but whether it is useful for some purpose.[32] On this view the standard of valid inference is, in the end, something adopted rather than discovered — a convention. It is the strongest and most carefully worked-out version of the thesis that the reasonable is a matter of framework choice, and it provoked the century's most important rebuttal.

Quine, Carnap's admiring student and relentless critic, argued that logic cannot after all be founded on convention, and the argument is beautifully simple. Suppose the truths of logic are made true by our adopting certain conventions. The conventions must be stated as general stipulations, and to get from the general stipulations to the specific logical truths they are supposed to underwrite, one must apply the conventions to particular cases — which is to say, one must already reason logically. Logic is thus presupposed by any attempt to derive it from convention, and the proposed foundation turns in a circle.[33] Quine pressed the attack further in his assault on the distinction between analytic truths, supposedly true by meaning alone, and synthetic truths answerable to experience; finding no principled account of analyticity, he concluded that no statement is immune from revision and none is confirmed in isolation.[34] Knowledge faces experience as a whole, a web of belief in which even the laws of logic lie near the centre — maximally protected, revised only under extreme pressure, but not in principle exempt.[35] This picture occupies an unstable and fertile middle ground: rational norms are neither mere conventions we could change at whim nor eternal truths grasped by pure insight, but the most deeply entrenched strands of our best total theory of the world.

Then came the discovery that formal reason has intrinsic limits, and it came from within logic itself. In 1931 Gödel proved that any consistent formal system rich enough to express arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove, and cannot establish its own consistency by its own means.[36] The dream of Leibniz and Hilbert — a complete mechanical procedure that would settle every mathematical question — was shown to be unattainable in principle. What this implies about human reason has been fiercely disputed. Lucas, and later Penrose, argued that the theorems reveal a gap between minds and machines: for any formal system proposed as a model of the mathematician's reasoning, the mathematician can see the truth of a statement the system cannot prove, and so the mind cannot be merely such a system.[37] The argument is seductive and, most logicians think, flawed: it assumes that we can know our own reasoning to be consistent and sound, which is precisely what Gödel's second theorem suggests a system cannot establish about itself, so the claimed insight may be question-begging.[38] Whatever the verdict, the episode dramatised a permanent lesson — that reason's reach outstrips any fixed formalisation of it, and that the relationship between the norms of good reasoning and any mechanical procedure for applying them is deep and unresolved.

Two further developments rounded out the formal understanding of rationality. Tarski gave a rigorous definition of truth for formalised languages, showing how a notion that had seemed hopelessly paradoxical could be handled with precision, and thereby put the central semantic concept of theoretical reason on a secure footing.[39] And in the theory of decision, rationality received its most influential formal model. Von Neumann and Morgenstern proved that an agent whose preferences satisfy a few structural conditions — transitivity, continuity, and the like — can be represented as maximising expected utility, as if assigning numerical values and probabilities and choosing to maximise their product.[40] Ramsey had already shown how degrees of belief could be measured through betting behaviour and argued that a person whose credences violate the probability axioms is vulnerable to a set of bets guaranteed to lose — a Dutch book — so that probabilistic coherence is a condition of practical rationality; Savage later unified subjective probability and utility into a single axiomatic theory of rational choice under uncertainty.[41][42] These results gave the twentieth century a precise, powerful, and enormously fruitful conception of what it is to be rational — and, as the laboratory would shortly reveal, a conception that human beings conspicuously fail to satisfy.

IXReason in the Laboratory

Once the formal theories had furnished precise standards of good reasoning — the laws of logic, the axioms of probability, the principle of maximising expected utility — a new question became possible, and it belonged not to philosophy but to experimental psychology. Do human beings actually reason as the standards require? For most of history the question could not even be posed, because there was no exact norm to test performance against. In the twentieth century the norm arrived, the laboratory took up the question, and the answer turned out to be sobering.

The early expectation was optimistic. Piaget's account of cognitive development culminated in a stage of formal operations at which the adolescent mind was supposed to reason in accordance with the structures of logic itself, handling hypotheticals and combinatorial possibilities as a natural terminus of maturation.[43] The picture was of reason as the achieved competence of the normal adult. Then came a deceptively simple experiment that has been run in hundreds of variations ever since. In Wason's selection task, subjects are shown four cards and asked which they must turn over to test a conditional rule of the form if P then Q; the logically correct answer requires checking the case that could falsify the rule, and the overwhelming majority of educated adults get it wrong, characteristically seeking to confirm rather than to falsify.[44] Performance is not merely imperfect; it is systematically and predictably mistaken, and it barely improves with instruction.[45]

Stranger still, success depends on content in a way that pure logic says it should not. The identical logical structure that defeats people when stated abstractly becomes easy when dressed in certain concrete materials — checking whether drinkers are of age, whether a permission has been earned. Two influential explanations competed. One held that we reason well when a problem engages a pragmatic reasoning schema, a set of rules keyed to recurrent practical situations such as permission and obligation, rather than the syntax of the material conditional.[46] The other, more radical, held that natural selection equipped us with a special-purpose module for detecting cheaters in social exchange, so that the content effect reflects not general logic at all but an evolved competence for policing reciprocity.[47] Either way, the lesson was disquieting for the classical picture: there seemed to be no content-general logical faculty doing the work, only a patchwork of context-bound competences.

The most influential program was Kahneman and Tversky's study of the heuristics people use under uncertainty and the biases those heuristics produce. Rather than computing probabilities, people rely on mental shortcuts — judging likelihood by resemblance, or by how easily instances come to mind, or by adjusting insufficiently from an initial anchor — and these shortcuts, efficient as they are, generate characteristic and reproducible errors.[48] The starkest is the conjunction fallacy: told about a person whose description fits the stereotype of a feminist, subjects rank it more probable that she is a feminist bank teller than that she is a bank teller, judging a conjunction more likely than one of its own conjuncts, in flat violation of the most elementary law of probability.[49] The accumulation of such findings provoked what came to be called the rationality wars — a sustained dispute over whether the experiments show human beings to be fundamentally irrational, or whether something is wrong with taking the textbook norms as the measure.

Two responses reshaped the debate rather than settling it. Long before, Simon had argued that real agents face limits of information, time, and computational capacity that the ideal theory ignores, and that rationality must be understood as bounded: we do not optimise but satisfice, searching until we find an option that is good enough.[50] Building on this, Gigerenzer mounted a direct challenge to the heuristics-and-biases interpretation, arguing that fast and frugal heuristics are not crippled approximations to logic but well-adapted tools that exploit the statistical structure of real environments, often outperforming more elaborate procedures; on this ecological view the alleged errors frequently dissolve once problems are posed in the natural frequency formats to which our minds are suited, and the fault lies less in the reasoner than in the content-blind norm used to grade it.[51] Rationality, on this account, is a matter of fit between mind and world, not conformity to a context-free calculus.[52] Careful study of individual differences complicated both camps: those who give the normative answers tend to score higher on measures of cognitive ability and reflective disposition, which suggests the standard responses are not arbitrary even if most people miss them.[53]

Out of these disputes crystallised the most durable framework in the field: the dual-process theory of reasoning. On this view the mind harbours two broadly distinct kinds of process. One is fast, automatic, effortless, and largely unconscious, delivering intuitive responses; the other is slow, effortful, capacity-limited, and consciously controlled, capable of following explicit rules and of monitoring and overriding the intuitive default.[54] Kahneman's later synthesis carried this architecture to a wide readership as the interplay of a fast, intuitive system and a slow, deliberate one, and argued that much of the trouble comes from the effortful system's readiness to endorse the intuitive system's proposals without adequate scrutiny.[55] Many of the classic errors can be read as cases where the intuitive system supplies a compelling but incorrect answer that the reflective system fails to catch or correct.

Beneath the dual-process consensus, deeper disagreements persist about what reasoning actually consists in. One tradition holds that we reason by constructing and manipulating mental models — small-scale representations of the possibilities a set of premises leaves open — and that error arises because we build too few of them.[56] A rival tradition holds that we possess something more like an internalised system of formal inference rules, a mental logic applied to the syntactic form of what we believe.[57] And a still more thoroughgoing reinterpretation, the so-called new paradigm, proposes that much everyday reasoning is not deductive at all but probabilistic: many apparent logical errors turn out to be rational once we recognise that people are reasoning about degrees of belief under uncertainty rather than about certainties, and that the Bayesian calculus, not classical logic, is the appropriate norm.[58] At its most ambitious this becomes a general theory of the brain itself as a hierarchical prediction engine that continually generates and updates probabilistic models of its world, minimising the discrepancy between expectation and input — a picture on which inference is not one activity among others but the basic operation of the nervous system.[59]

The cumulative effect of a century of research is to drive a wedge between two things the classical tradition had run together: how people actually reason and how they ought to. The descriptive facts are messy, content-bound, and shot through with systematic bias; the normative standard, whatever it is, is not simply read off from performance. This is precisely what makes the empirical findings relevant to our guiding question rather than a mere catalogue of human frailty. For if the standard of good reasoning is not fixed by how anyone in fact reasons, then it must be answerable to something else — and the question of what that something is returns us, better armed, to philosophy.

XWhy We Reason: The Social Turn

The laboratory left a puzzle. If individual human reasoning is so prone to bias — so quick to confirm, so blind to falsifying evidence, so easily led astray by resemblance and salience — why do we possess the faculty at all, and how has it served us so well? An influential answer inverts the traditional assumption about what reasoning is for. On the argumentative, or interactionist, theory, reason did not evolve to help the solitary thinker arrive at true beliefs; it evolved for a social function — to produce arguments that persuade others and to evaluate the arguments others offer.[60] This reframing turns the supposed defects into design features. Confirmation bias is exactly what one would expect of a device built to construct a case for a predetermined conclusion; it is a flaw only if reasoning is meant to be an impartial truth-tracker, and no flaw at all if it is meant to be an advocate. And it predicts, correctly, that reasoning performs far better in the right social setting: when people with differing views argue and must answer one another's objections, the group frequently reaches conclusions that no individual would have reached alone, because the evaluative side of reason is more demanding than the productive side.[61] On this account reason is at its best not in the isolated mind but in the exchange between minds — a conclusion whose bearing on the question of authority we will have to weigh carefully.

But the evolutionary story cuts deeper than a hypothesis about function, for it can be turned into an argument against the objectivity it seemed merely to describe. If our faculties of reasoning and evaluation were shaped by natural selection, and selection cares about reproductive success rather than truth, what entitles us to suppose that those faculties track anything beyond fitness? The most influential version of this worry, framed for the evaluative case, presents a dilemma. Either the truths our faculties are supposed to track are independent of our responses, or they are not. If they are independent, then since selection favoured the responses for their contribution to survival and not for their correspondence to any such independent order, it would be a staggering coincidence if the two lined up, and we are left with no reason to trust our judgements. If, to avoid this, we say instead that the truths are constituted by our responses, we have abandoned the independence that made them look objective in the first place.[62] The argument was devised against moral realism, but its structure generalises to any domain of putatively mind-independent normative fact, including facts about what reasons there are and what follows from what.

A mirror-image argument runs in the opposite theological direction. Where the naturalist takes evolution to undercut the pretensions of reason to objective insight, Plantinga argues that evolution undercuts naturalism itself. If our cognitive faculties are the product of unguided selection, he contends, the probability that they are reliable — aimed at truth rather than merely at adaptive behaviour — is low or at best inscrutable, since many combinations of false belief and useful action would have served survival equally well.[63] Anyone who accepts both naturalism and evolution therefore has a defeater for the reliability of their faculties, and so for all their beliefs, including their belief in naturalism; the position, he concludes, is self-undermining in a way that a theistic account of reason as aimed at truth by design is not.[64] The argument is contested at every step — critics deny that true belief and adaptive behaviour come apart so easily, and press the reflexive question of whether the argument does not equally impugn the reasoning used to reach it — but it sharpens the point that matters here. The genealogy of reason, whether told in Darwinian or in theological terms, cannot by itself settle reason's authority; a faculty's origin and its entitlement are different questions, and to run them together is to commit the genetic fallacy in one direction or the other. Whether reason, however it came to be, answers to anything beyond itself is a question that genealogy raises but cannot close — and it is time to take it up directly.

XIThe Web of Concepts

Reason does not stand alone. It is bound up with a family of neighbouring notions — truth, justification, knowledge, and inference — and much of what is puzzling about it comes into focus only when these relations are made explicit. Theoretical reason is answerable to truth in the sense that belief aims at truth: to believe something is, in part, to hold it as true, and a consideration is a reason for belief insofar as it bears on whether the belief is true. This is why one cannot, on reflection, believe a proposition because it would be pleasant to; the only considerations that can directly move a rational believer are those that speak to truth. Reason is, in this sense, the faculty by which we are responsive to truth-relevant considerations.

To have such considerations in sufficient measure is to be justified, and here an ancient problem returns in modern form. If a belief is justified by being supported by reasons, those reasons are presumably further beliefs, which must themselves be justified, whose justifying reasons must be justified in turn. The chain of justification, it seems, must either go on forever, or double back on itself, or stop somewhere. Each option names a family of epistemological theory. Foundationalists hold that the regress stops at basic beliefs that are justified but not by further beliefs — the given, the self-evident, the incorrigible. Coherentists reject the linear picture entirely, holding that justification is a property of whole systems of belief that hang together, so that the apparent circularity is virtuous rather than vicious. Infinitists, biting the hardest bullet, accept that the chain of reasons is genuinely endless and argue that this is no worse than other things we accept. The structure of the problem is exactly the one the ancient Skeptics posed, and it is worth seeing laid out plainly.

Cross-cutting this debate is the question of where justification is located. Internalists hold that what justifies a belief must be accessible to the believer — something one could cite on reflection — and that a belief formed on inadequate grounds is unjustified however reliably it was produced.[65] Externalists locate justification in facts that need not be available to the subject at all, most influentially in the reliability of the process that produced the belief: a belief is justified if it was formed in a way that tends to yield truths, whether or not the believer can vindicate that fact. The dispute matters for our theme because it bears directly on whether being reasonable is a matter of what one can defend to others — an inherently dialectical, potentially social notion — or a matter of standing in the right causal relation to the world, which one might satisfy in complete solitude and ignorance.

That justification, even when added to true belief, does not add up to knowledge was the burden of one of the most famous short papers in the history of philosophy. Gettier described cases in which a person has a justified true belief that is nonetheless intuitively not knowledge, because the justification and the truth are connected only by luck — the belief is reasonable, and it is true, but it is true for reasons other than those that make it reasonable.[66] The lesson for us is that reasonableness and knowledge come apart: one can do everything reason requires and still fail to know, which shows that being rational is not the same as being right, and that the value of reason cannot lie simply in guaranteeing truth. A parallel set of questions surrounds the a priori — the knowledge, if such there be, that reason yields independently of experience, from the apparent self-evidence of elementary logic and arithmetic to the harder cases of metaphysics. Whether there is genuine rational insight into necessary truth, or whether the appearance of such insight is an illusion to be explained away, is among the deepest open questions about what reason can do.

Beneath all of this lies the most basic act of all, inference itself, and it harbours a problem that will carry us into the final questions. What is it to infer a conclusion from premises? It is not merely to have the premises and then to have the conclusion; the two states might succeed one another by mere association. Inferring seems to require that one draw the conclusion because one takes the premises to support it — a condition of taking one's premises to bear on one's conclusion that is surprisingly hard to analyse without either circularity or regress.[67] And here Lewis Carroll, of all people, identified the deepest difficulty in a dialogue of a few pages. When the Tortoise challenges Achilles to compel him, by logic alone, to accept a conclusion that follows from premises he grants, Achilles keeps adding the needed inferential principle as a further premise — if the premises are true, the conclusion is true — only to find that the Tortoise can always grant the new premise and still ask why he must now accept the conclusion.[68] The moral is profound: a rule of inference cannot function as just another premise in the argument it governs, for then it would need a further rule to license its own application, without end. The authority of a basic inference rule cannot itself rest on a belief that the rule is valid; it must reside somewhere else — in a disposition, a practice, a way of going on that is not a further piece of reasoning. That result, that the ground of inference is not itself an inference, is the hinge on which the question of reason's authority finally turns.

XIIIs the Reasonable Merely Convention?

We can now confront the question this essay took as its spine, with the resources to state it carefully. It will not do to ask flatly whether the reasonable is conventional, because that runs together several very different claims. Let us begin by disposing of the crudest, and then map the terrain of the serious positions.

The crude thesis is that to be reasonable simply means to think as most people in one's community think — that the standard of good reasoning is nothing but majority opinion or established custom. This view is not so much false as self-defeating. Anyone who asserts it puts it forward as true, and expects us to accept it because the arguments for it are good — that is, because it is reasonable to accept — which presupposes a standard of reasonableness the arguments answer to, rather than one constituted by whatever the audience already believes. If the crude thesis were correct, it could give us no reason to believe it, since our reason to believe anything would just be that our neighbours already do. Moreover, the view makes the reform of a consensus incoherent: it entails that the lone dissenter who turns out to be right — the Galileo, the abolitionist — was by definition unreasonable at the time, which inverts the very judgement we want to make. Almost no serious thinker has held the crude thesis. The interesting question is whether its sophisticated descendants fare better. The map below locates the main contenders.

Start at the objective pole. The strongest statement that rational norms are wholly independent of us is the anti-psychologism of Frege and Husserl, now the settled view among philosophers of logic: the laws of logic are laws of truth, not descriptions of how anyone thinks, and to reduce them to psychology is to lose their normative force and collapse into self-refuting relativism.[69] This much is secure, and it rules out the crudest naturalism. But it leaves a crucial question open. Granting that logical validity is not a psychological fact, is it a conventional one — a matter of the rules we have adopted rather than the way we happen to think? Here the twentieth century's great debate, between Carnap and Quine, comes back into play. Carnap was surely right that a great deal about a system of representation is conventional: which symbols we use, which of many logics we adopt for a given purpose, where we draw various boundaries, are matters of expedience rather than correctness, and to ask whether classical logic is true as against intuitionistic logic can look like a category mistake. Yet Quine's regress argument shows that this concession cannot be extended to the validity of inference itself. Even after we have chosen a framework, the fact that a given conclusion follows within it is not made to follow by our choosing; to derive the consequences of our conventions we must already reason, so the correctness of that reasoning cannot itself be one more convention on pain of circularity. The choice of notation is conventional; what the notation then commits us to is not.

The deepest modern challenge to objectivity comes from a different direction — not from logic or convenience but from the nature of rule-following, and it must be handled with care because it is so often misread. Wittgenstein observed that no fact about my past — no image, no formula I once rehearsed, no finite stretch of my prior behaviour — seems to determine what counts as correctly continuing a rule into a new case. Whatever I have done so far is compatible with indefinitely many different continuations, each of which could be described as “going on in the same way.” It seems to follow that grasping a rule cannot consist in possessing an interpretation, since any interpretation can itself be variously interpreted; rather, there is a way of following a rule that is not an interpretation but is exhibited in what we do, in a practice or custom embedded in a shared form of life.[70] Kripke sharpened this into a sceptical paradox: there is, he argued, no fact of the matter that constitutes my meaning one thing rather than another by a rule, and hence no fact that makes any particular continuation correct; what we have instead is a practice in which the community licenses some continuations and corrects others.[71]

It is tempting to read this as vindicating conventionalism — as saying that correctness just is what the community agrees to, so that the reasonable reduces after all to communal consensus. That reading is a misunderstanding, and everything turns on rejecting it. The community-based response does not say that the majority's agreeing that a step is correct is what makes it correct, as though rational validity were settled by a show of hands; a community can be, and often is, collectively wrong. What the considerations show, on the more defensible reading, is something subtler and more interesting: that the very distinction between correct and incorrect — the possibility of there being a standard one can meet or fail to meet — gets its grip only against the background of a practice. Shared practice is not the content of the standard, dictating which answers count as right; it is the medium in which answerability to a standard becomes possible for finite creatures at all. On this reading the rule-following considerations do not abolish objectivity; they tell us where objectivity has to live — not in a mechanism inside the individual head, nor in a Platonic order surveyed from nowhere, but in the public, corrigible practice of making and assessing moves that others can check. That is a claim about the home of reason, not about what reason answers to.

Between the objective and conventional poles lies the most influential attempt at a middle path, and it deserves to be taken seriously as more than a compromise. Goodman, addressing precisely the problem of what justifies the rules of inference, offered a deceptively modest answer. We justify a rule of deductive or inductive inference, he observed, by bringing it into agreement with the particular inferences we are actually prepared to accept and reject: a rule is amended if it sanctions an inference we are unwilling to make, and an inference is rejected if it violates a rule we are unwilling to give up. Justification is the delicate mutual adjustment of rules and judgements to one another, and the rules are justified precisely insofar as that adjustment has been achieved.[72] Rawls named this method reflective equilibrium and made it the engine of moral theory, matching principles to considered judgements by two-way adjustment until the two cohere.[73] In its wide form the method broadens the inputs to include background theories and rival views, so that the equilibrium sought is not a mere tidying of first reactions but a considered accommodation open to substantial revision.[74] This is genuinely a third option: neither foundationalist appeal to self-evident axioms nor conventionalist appeal to arbitrary stipulation, but a coherentist process answerable to our actual rational practice. Its besetting worry is conservatism. If the judgements fed into the process are distorted — by bias, by ideology, by the very confirmation-seeking the psychologists documented — then equilibrium may do no more than launder existing prejudice into apparent principle, systematising error rather than correcting it. Whether the method has the resources to be genuinely self-critical, or whether it merely elevates the status quo, is the standing question about it.

Against every attempt to pull reason toward the conventional or subjective pole stands a single powerful line of argument, and it is the strongest card the objectivist holds. Nagel calls it the last word. Any argument that reason is merely local, merely ours, merely the custom of a community, must itself be advanced as correct — not merely as something we happen to say, but as something the other party ought to accept because it is true and well-supported. In making that claim the relativist steps outside the local and appeals to exactly the kind of non-local, binding reason he set out to deny; the attempt to subjectivise reason refutes itself, because the subjectivising is itself put forward as an objective result.[75] Certain thoughts, Nagel argues, cannot be got outside of and surveyed as mere psychological facts about oneself, because any attempt to survey them from without must employ them from within. When I try to think that modus ponens is merely a local convention, I have to reason about conventions, employing the very inferential powers whose objectivity is in question; the challenge presupposes what it challenges. This is the modern form of the ancient turning-of-the-tables against Protagorean relativism, and it is why reason has, in a certain sense, the last word: not because its every verdict is infallible, but because any appeal against a rational conclusion must itself be conducted before the court of reason. There is no other court.

None of this restores the myth of reason as an infallible faculty gazing on necessary truths. That myth deserves to die, and a sober naturalism about logic helps kill it. On the anti-exceptionalist view, logic is not a body of self-evident truths insulated from all revision but a theory like other theories, adopted and amended by weighing how well rival systems fit the data of validity, how simple and strong and fruitful they are; logic is in the business of getting something right, and our access to it is fallible, revisable, and abductive rather than a matter of gazing at self-evident axioms.[76] But notice that this deflation cuts against the Platonist and the conventionalist alike. It denies infallible insight — but it does not make logic conventional, because abductive theory choice is answerable to the facts of what genuinely follows from what, not to agreement. That logic is revisable in the light of how well it captures those facts is the opposite of the claim that it is settled by decision. Even the vexed question of exactly how the objective facts of logic translate into norms binding on a reasoner — the problem, met earlier, that validity is a relation among propositions while rationality is a matter of managing one's beliefs — is a question about the form of reason's authority, requiring careful bridge principles between the two, not evidence that the authority is a fiction.[77] One can grant Harman's point that logic does not directly legislate belief revision and still hold that what logic describes is objective; the norms of reasoning are answerable to the facts of implication even if they are not identical with them.[7]

We are now in a position to state a considered view. The blunt question — objective or conventional? — is too coarse because it runs together three things that come apart. There is, first, the content and choice of a system of representation: which notation, which of several logics, where to draw a boundary. Here the conventionalist is substantially right; much is a matter of expedience, and nothing in the world dictates our choice of symbols. There is, second, the fixation of rule-following: what makes it the case that we are following one rule rather than another. Here the Wittgensteinian is right that the anchor is practice, custom, the corrigible life of a community — but this locates the medium of objectivity rather than dissolving it, explaining how beings like us can be answerable to a standard at all. And there is, third, the validity and authority of basic inference: whether a conclusion really follows, whether contradiction is really to be shunned. Here the regress arguments of Quine and Carroll, together with the reflexive argument of Kant and Nagel, converge on a conclusion the conventionalist cannot accommodate: the authority of basic reason is not itself a further convention we have adopted or a consensus we have reached, because adopting conventions and reaching consensus already presuppose it. You cannot use reason to establish that reason is merely a local agreement, for the establishing would have to be more than a local agreement to establish anything at all.

On this account, consensus is not what makes a conclusion reasonable; it is, at most, defeasible evidence that a conclusion is reasonable. When everyone competent who examines a question converges on the same answer, that convergence is a sign — a fallible one — that we have collectively tracked something real; but the convergence answers to the something, and not the other way around, which is why we rightly treat a consensus as overturnable by better argument. To be reasonable, then, is to be answerable to truth and to those constitutive norms of thought without which there could be no thinking about anything — including no thinking about this very question — where that answerability is refined, exercised, and administered through the fallible, social, historically situated practice of giving and assessing reasons. The community is where reason lives. It is not what reason answers to. The following comparison sets the main positions, and the view defended here, side by side.

XIIIThe Contemporary Landscape

Contemporary philosophy of reason is plural, technical, and unusually alive, but its main currents can be charted. The most prominent development is the reasons-first program, which takes the normative notion of a reason — a consideration that counts in favour of a response — as fundamental and irreducible. On this approach one does not analyse reasons in terms of anything more basic, such as desire-satisfaction or rule-conformity; rather, facts about what there is reason to believe and do are among the ground-floor furniture of the normative world.[78] In its most ambitious form this yields a robust non-naturalism, on which there are irreducibly normative truths, knowable by reasoning, that are no less objective for being no part of the causal order studied by science.[79] Reason, on this view, is the faculty by which we apprehend and respond to these favouring relations, and its objectivity is of a piece with the objectivity of the reasons themselves.

Whether practical reason is so objective is the subject of a long and clarifying dispute. Williams argued that all genuine reasons for action are internal: a consideration is a reason for an agent only if it connects, by some sound deliberative route, to something already in that agent's motivations, so that there are no reasons wholly external to what an agent could come to care about.[80] Against this broadly Humean position, a neo-Kantian tradition holds that reason itself is a source of ends and constraints, that the authority of practical norms derives from the agent's own reflective endorsement of principles no rational will could coherently reject.[81] The disagreement is the modern continuation of the ancient quarrel over whether reason is merely the servant of the passions or can be their master, and nothing less than the objectivity of practical reason turns on it.

A further contemporary debate concerns the very status of rational requirements. Much of what we call irrationality is structural — holding contradictory beliefs, intending an end while rejecting the known means, failing to draw an obvious conclusion — and these seem to be requirements of coherence among one's own attitudes, bearing on reasoning as a process by which attitudes are adjusted.[82] Yet this raises a sharp question: why should one care about mere coherence, as opposed to responding correctly to the reasons there actually are? If what one ought to do is determined by the reasons, the demand to be coherent can seem at best derivative and at worst a fetish.[83] Sorting out the relation between structural rationality, which governs the tidiness of one's attitudes, and substantive correctness, which governs their fit with the reasons, is among the most active problems in the field, with some arguing that rationality ultimately just is responding correctly to the reasons one possesses.[84]

Not everyone has approached reason with such confidence, and no honest survey can omit the tradition of suspicion. Nietzsche cast a cold eye on the philosophers' faith in reason, treating their systems as unwitting expressions of temperament and will, and questioning whether our deepest confidence in logic and cause reflects the structure of the world or merely a grammar we cannot see past.[85] In the twentieth century the Frankfurt School gave the critique a historical and political edge: reason reduced to the calculation of efficient means, severed from any capacity to reason about ends, becomes a mere instrument of control, and the Enlightenment's triumphant rationality, in this reading, turns dialectically into a new form of domination and myth.[86] These are not arguments that reason is invalid — they are, after all, arguments, and thereby pay reason the tribute of using it — but they are salutary warnings against identifying reason with any one of its historical forms, above all with the instrumental calculation that a technological age is tempted to mistake for the whole.

The most systematic contemporary response to that danger, and the most sophisticated attempt to locate reason in the social, is Habermas's theory of communicative rationality. Reason, on this account, is realised not in the monologue of the solitary thinker but in communicative action oriented toward mutual understanding, in which speakers raise validity claims — to truth, to rightness, to sincerity — that they stand ready to redeem through argument. Rational acceptability is cashed out in terms of what all affected could agree to under idealised conditions of discourse, free from coercion and open to any relevant contribution.[87] It is essential to see why this is not the crude conventionalism dismissed earlier, and the point vindicates the whole line of argument of the previous section. The consensus that confers rational warrant is not any actual agreement, which may be the product of manipulation, ignorance, or unequal power; it is a counterfactual, idealised agreement, and the idealising conditions — the norms of sincere, uncoerced, inclusive argumentation — are presupposed by the practice of argument itself rather than chosen by any community. The appeal to what would be agreed under ideal conditions is thus a way of articulating answerability to reasons, not a reduction of reasons to agreement; even here, at the most thoroughly social conception of reason on offer, the reasonable resists collapse into the merely customary. And the tradition that reason and a reasoned faith belong together, far from disappearing, has been reaffirmed at the close of the modern period, in the insistence that human reason has a genuine capacity to reach truth and must be defended alike against a fideism that would demean it and a relativism that would despair of it.[88]

XIVThe Court From Which There Is No Appeal

We began with a predicament and can end with its resolution. Reason, it turns out, is not one thing but a layered family of them, and many disputes about its nature are really disputes about which layer is in view. There is reason as a capacity — the evolved, fallible, dual-process machinery the psychologists study, prone to bias and bounded by the limits of any finite mind. There is reason as a set of constitutive norms — the demands of consistency, coherence, and valid inference without which there could be no thought with any content at all. There is reason as a truth-directed ideal — the standard of answerability to how things are and to what there genuinely is reason to do, which our capacities approximate at best imperfectly. And there is reason as a social practice — the historically situated, self-correcting activity of offering and assessing considerations before others who can check them. Each of these is a part of what the one word names, and confusion follows whenever one is mistaken for the whole.

With the layers distinguished, the question that has run through this essay can be answered. Is the standard of the reasonable merely a matter of convention or consensus? No — but the correct denial is more careful than a flat assertion of Platonic objectivity. Much about our systems of representation is indeed conventional, and the fixation of what it is to follow a rule is indeed rooted in shared practice; to that extent the conventionalist and the communitarian have seen something real. But the authority of basic reason — the validity of inference, the constraint of non-contradiction, the answerability of belief to truth — is not itself a convention we have adopted or a consensus we have reached, for the plain reason that adopting conventions and reaching consensus already presuppose it. Consensus is evidence of the reasonable, sometimes very good evidence; it is not what makes anything reasonable. The reasonable is answerable to truth and to the constitutive conditions of thought, and that answerability is exercised, refined, and administered — never constituted — through the fallible communal court in which reasons are given and weighed.

A faculty that were merely the sum of our conventions could not have surprised us with its own limits; that reason can arraign itself is the surest sign it answers to more than our agreement.The self-critique of reason

This is the sense in which reason is the court from which there is no appeal, and the metaphor should be understood precisely. It does not mean that reason's verdicts are infallible; the history recounted here is in large part a history of reason correcting its own mistakes, and reason's most profound achievements have been discoveries of its own limits. Kant used reason to map the antinomies into which reason falls when it overreaches. Gödel used impeccable reasoning to prove that no formal system can capture all the truths of arithmetic or certify its own consistency. Lewis Carroll used a children's-book dialogue to show that the rules of inference cannot be justified as further premises without regress. In each case reason turned on itself and found a boundary. But that self-arraignment is not a scandal that discredits reason; it is the strongest possible evidence that reason answers to something beyond our decisions and our agreements. A faculty that were nothing but the sum of our conventions could not catch itself in contradiction, could not prove its own incompleteness, could not expose the regress at its own root — for there would be no independent standard against which such failures could show up as failures. Only a reason answerable to truth can discover that it has fallen short of it.

The predicament of the opening, then, was never a trap. That the tribunal before which reason is tried is also the accused does not make the tribunal unfit to judge; it means only that there is no higher bench, and there could not be, since any higher bench would have to reason too. The alternative to reason judging its own case is not some more impartial authority but the abandonment of judgement altogether — and even that abandonment, if it is argued for, is one more case brought before the court it hoped to escape. The work of reason is therefore endless and self-correcting by its very nature: the labour of a court that must hear every appeal, including the appeal against itself, and that has nowhere to send the ones it cannot yet answer except back into the same patient, communal, and unfinished process of asking what, on reflection, we have most reason to believe. There is no last word except the one reason keeps giving itself.
