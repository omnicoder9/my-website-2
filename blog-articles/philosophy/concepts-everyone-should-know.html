What Did the Shortcut Throw Away?

Thirty-one concepts that educated people get wrong, and the single thing each everyday version leaves out

Most famous misunderstandings are not ignorance. They are compressions: a precise idea is carried around in a shorter form, and the shorter form has quietly discarded one specific thing. This article catalogues thirty-one such concepts across reasoning, statistics, science, economics, law and language, shows who has been caught by each, and argues that the discarded thing is almost always one of six.

1The question

There is a particular kind of error that survives education. A student who has never heard of a p-value cannot misinterpret one; a professor who teaches them can, and surveys show that most do1. The economists attending their own profession's annual meeting were asked a textbook question about opportunity cost, the idea many of them would name as the discipline's central insight, and fewer chose the right answer than would have done so by guessing2. Paul Erdős, one of the most prolific mathematicians in history, refused to accept the answer to a three-door puzzle until he watched a computer simulation produce it3.

None of these people lacked intelligence or training. What they had was a version of the concept that was good enough for most purposes and wrong for the one in front of them. That is what this article is about: concepts that everyone should know, chosen because they are so often known in a shortened form that breaks at exactly the moment it matters.

The organising claim is simple enough to state in a sentence and, I think, useful enough to be worth the length that follows. When a precise concept is compressed into its everyday version, the compression almost always throws away one specific thing, and there are only about six kinds of thing it can throw away. It can lose the direction of a relation, the denominator a number is out of, the alternative a choice is weighed against, the level (individual or aggregate) at which a claim holds, the magnitude of an effect or rate, or the modality of a claim, meaning how strongly and on what grounds it is asserted. Each of those losses has a question that restores it. Learn the six questions and you have a portable repair kit for a surprising share of the mistakes in this catalogue, including several made by people far cleverer than most of us.

The catalogue that follows has thirty-one entries, set out as numbered plates in five families: reasoning and evidence, probability and statistics, the physical and living world, economics and decisions, and law and language. Each plate gives the everyday reading, the technical sense, the thing the shortcut discards, and a case on record, usually a moment in history or a documented error by a notable person or institution. Twelve of the statistical claims are not left to assertion: a small deterministic laboratory, written in plain Python and run when this page was built, computes them, and every number quoted from it below comes straight from its output, reproduced in full in the appendix.

2Six things a shortcut can drop

The device is borrowed from the information-retrieval technology of the 1950s. An edge-notched card carries a row of holes along its edge, one per category. To code a card you cut the hole open to the edge for every category that applies. To retrieve, you stack the deck, push a needle through one hole position and lift: the cards that are notched there fall out, and the rest stay on the needle. Each plate below carries such a card. Its six holes are the six things a compression can discard. The hole for what the shortcut throws away is notched deep in that dimension's colour; where a second loss is also at work, its hole is notched shallow.

The six dimensions are not a theory of cognition, and they make no claim to be the only possible way to carve the territory. They are a practical coding scheme, and the coding of each plate is a judgement that a reader is entitled to dispute. What matters is that the categories are distinct enough that a needle through any one hole pulls out a recognisable family, and that each comes with a question short enough to ask in the middle of a meeting.

Two remarks before the catalogue. First, many plates involve more than one loss, and the card records the primary and, where relevant, a secondary. Second, the coding turns out to be informative in itself. As the ledger at the end shows, each family of concepts has a characteristic missing question: statistical errors overwhelmingly lose the denominator or the level, errors about evidence lose modality, and errors in economics lose the alternative. Direction, meanwhile, is rarely the main thing dropped but is the commonest accomplice.

3Why the shortcuts form

This is not an article about cognitive biases, and it would be a mistake to treat every misconception below as a defect of the person holding it. Most of them are the predictable product of how ideas travel. Five mechanisms account for most of what follows, and naming them briefly makes the catalogue easier to read.

The everyday word came first. "Theory", "significant", "random", "energy", "model", "risk" and "fit" were ordinary English long before they were technical terms. When a discipline borrows a word and sharpens it, the old sense does not leave; it sits beside the new one and wins whenever context is thin. Many plates below are cases of a technical term being read in its prior, looser sense.

A hard question gets swapped for an easier one. Kahneman and Frederick described attribute substitution, in which a judgement about a difficult target attribute is answered by consulting a related, more accessible one4. Asked how probable a hypothesis is given the data, people answer how probable the data are given the hypothesis, because that is the number on the page. The swap is invisible to the person making it, which is why it survives expertise.

Intuitive theories predate instruction. Long before anyone studies physics, they have a working theory of motion, and it is largely medieval: a thrown object carries an impetus that gradually wears out5. Halloun and Hestenes found that such beliefs were widespread among incoming college physics students and that conventional instruction did little to dislodge them6. Instruction often adds a new layer on top of the old one rather than replacing it.

Compression happens in transmission. Ideas are passed on in slogans, and slogans are short because they drop qualifications. "Survival of the fittest", "correlation is not causation" and "all models are wrong" each began as a compressed form of a careful idea, and each now circulates without the careful idea attached.

Repetition and authority make the short form feel true. Statements encountered repeatedly are rated more plausible than new ones, independent of their accuracy7, and corrections often fail to fully undo a belief once it has been integrated into a person's account of events8. Experts are not immune; they face a separate hazard in that once you understand an idea it becomes difficult to simulate not understanding it, which degrades the explanations they give to others9.

None of these mechanisms requires carelessness. That is the useful point. If misconceptions were simple ignorance, the remedy would be more information. Because they are compressions, the remedy is to know what the compression discarded and to ask for it back.

4Reasoning and evidence

The first family concerns the vocabulary of knowing: what a theory is, what proof is, what it means for a claim to be testable, and how conditions, causes and simplicity enter an argument. Four of its six plates lose modality, the strength and status of a claim. The other two lose direction and the alternative.

A01

Theory

Everyday reading
A hunch or guess; something not yet proven.
Technical sense
A well-substantiated explanation of some aspect of nature that incorporates facts, laws, inferences and tested hypotheses.
Thrown away
Modality — How sure, and on what grounds?
On record
Cobb County textbook stickers, 2002–2005

In science a theory is the most developed form an explanation can take, not the least. The National Academies define it as a well-substantiated explanation that draws together facts, laws, inferences and tested hypotheses, and they stress that a theory does not graduate into a fact or law with more evidence; theories explain facts and laws10. The hypothesis is the tentative item, a proposed explanation still awaiting test. Stephen Jay Gould put the distinction plainly: evolution is both a fact (organisms have changed over time) and a theory (natural selection and its companions explain how)11.

The everyday reading inverts this ranking, and the inversion has had legal consequences. In 2002 a Georgia school district placed stickers in biology textbooks describing evolution as "a theory, not a fact". A federal district court ordered them removed in 2005, finding among other things that the wording traded on the colloquial sense of the word12. The shortcut drops modality: it reads a word that signals the strongest kind of scientific claim as if it signalled the weakest. The repair is to ask what grounds the claim rests on, not what it is called.

A02

Evidence and proof

Everyday reading
Science proves things; once proved, they are settled for ever.
Technical sense
Proof is deductive and belongs to mathematics and logic. Empirical science accumulates evidence of varying strength, and its conclusions remain open to revision. Law sets explicit standards of proof that differ by context.
Thrown away
Modality — How sure, and on what grounds?
On record
Piltdown Man, 1912–1953

Three different practices share the word. In mathematics a proof is a deductive argument whose conclusion cannot be false if its premises are true. In empirical science nothing is proved in that sense: hypotheses are tested, evidence accumulates, and even well-established conclusions are held provisionally because a new observation could overturn them13. In law, "proof" names a threshold that a fact-finder must be persuaded past, and the threshold differs; the United States Supreme Court held in 1970 that due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt for every element of a criminal charge, a higher bar than the civil balance of probabilities14.

The Piltdown remains, presented in 1912 as an early human ancestor, show what happens when evidence is treated as settled. They shaped the interpretation of human evolution for four decades before fluorine dating and anatomical study in 1953 exposed them as a deliberate forgery combining a modern human cranium with an orangutan jaw15. The error was not that scientists accepted evidence; it was that the evidence was treated as more conclusive than its kind allows. The shortcut drops modality, and the question that restores it is: proof of what kind, to what standard?

A03

Falsifiability

Everyday reading
A falsifiable claim is one that has been shown to be false.
Technical sense
A claim is falsifiable if some possible observation would contradict it. Falsifiability is a property of the claim's logical form, not of its truth.
Thrown away
Modality · direction — How sure, and on what grounds?
On record
Popper on natural selection, 1976–1978

Karl Popper's criterion separates claims that stick their necks out from claims that cannot lose. "All swans are white" is falsifiable because a single black swan would refute it; a theory that can accommodate any outcome whatever explains nothing13. Popper's motivating examples were theories he regarded as compatible with every conceivable human behaviour, which he contrasted with Einstein's prediction of light bending, a prediction that could have failed16. The criterion also encodes a direction: no number of white swans verifies a universal claim, but one black swan falsifies it.

The everyday mistake reads the modal "could be shown false" as the actual "has been shown false", which turns a compliment into an accusation. But the more instructive case is Popper's own. In his 1976 autobiography he described Darwinism as not a testable scientific theory but a "metaphysical research programme"17. Two years later, in a lecture published in Dialectica, he said he had changed his mind about the testability and logical status of natural selection18. Even the author of the criterion misjudged which claims it applied to. The shortcut drops modality, with a secondary loss of direction: ask whether the claim could fail, and which observation would make it fail.

A04

Necessary and sufficient

Everyday reading
The two words mean roughly the same thing: a condition that matters.
Technical sense
A is necessary for B if B cannot occur without A. A is sufficient for B if A guarantees B. 'If A then B' makes A sufficient and B necessary.
Thrown away
Direction — Which way does the arrow run?
On record
Bradford Hill's viewpoints, 1965 onward

A valid driving licence is necessary to drive legally on public roads in most jurisdictions, but it is not sufficient: you also need a vehicle, insurance and permission to use the road. Conversely, being born in a country may be sufficient for citizenship without being necessary, since naturalisation is another route. The two notions are converses of one conditional, and mixing them up amounts to reading the conditional backwards. Peter Wason's selection task showed how hard this is. Given four cards and a rule of the form "if a card has a vowel on one side it has an even number on the other", most people turn the wrong cards, and in the original studies only a small minority chose the logically correct pair19.

A consequential misuse concerns the nine "viewpoints" that Austin Bradford Hill proposed in 1965 for judging whether an association is causal. They are widely taught as criteria, as if each had to be satisfied. Hill himself wrote that none of them could bring indisputable evidence for or against causation and that none could be required as a sine qua non20: in the terms of this plate, none is necessary and none is sufficient. The shortcut drops direction. The question is: does this condition guarantee the outcome, or merely permit it?

A05

Correlation and causation

Everyday reading
Things that go together cause one another; or, as a slogan, correlation never tells you anything about causes.
Technical sense
Causation is a claim about a counterfactual: what would have happened to the same units had the cause been absent. Correlation is evidence about causation only once the alternative explanations (confounding, selection, reverse causation, chance) have been addressed.
Thrown away
Alternative · direction — Compared with what?
On record
Hormone therapy and heart disease, 1991–2002

The modern account of causation defines an effect as a difference between two potential outcomes for the same units, one with the treatment and one without, only one of which can ever be observed21,22. A correlation compares different units, and it reflects a causal effect only if those units are otherwise comparable. The shortcut throws away the alternative: the unobserved world against which the effect is defined.

The most expensive recent example is postmenopausal hormone therapy. Observational studies through the 1980s consistently found that women taking oestrogen had substantially lower rates of coronary heart disease, and a 1991 quantitative review put the reduction at roughly half23. The randomised Women's Health Initiative trial, stopped early in 2002, found the combined therapy it tested did not protect against heart disease and on its primary measure increased it24. Women who chose hormone therapy had differed from those who did not in ways that also affected their hearts. At the lighter end, a 2012 paper in the New England Journal of Medicine found a strong correlation between national chocolate consumption and Nobel prizes per capita, offered partly as an illustration of exactly this hazard25.

The slogan "correlation is not causation" is itself a compression: correlation is often the first evidence of causation. The useful question is not whether the variables move together but what else would make them do so.

A06

Occam's razor

Everyday reading
The simplest explanation is always the right one.
Technical sense
Among explanations that account for the evidence equally well, prefer the one that posits fewer entities or assumptions. It is a methodological preference, not a law about the world.
Thrown away
Modality · alternative — How sure, and on what grounds?
On record
The phrase Ockham never wrote

The razor is a rule for choosing among rivals that are otherwise equal. It does not say that the world is simple, and it gives no guidance when a more complex hypothesis explains more. The everyday version upgrades a heuristic into a truth claim, dropping modality, and drops the conditioning clause about equal explanatory power, which is a loss of the alternative: simpler than which competitor, at explaining what?

The razor's own history is a small lesson in how compressions form. The well-known formula "entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity" does not appear in the surviving works of William of Ockham. W. M. Thorburn traced it in 1918 and found its earliest appearance in a 1639 commentary by John Punch, more than three centuries after Ockham's death26. Ockham did state related principles of parsimony, but his use of them was narrower than the slogan suggests and was grounded in theological as well as logical considerations27. The name attached to the idea is itself a simplification that the evidence does not support.

5Probability and statistics

Statistics is where the largest number of educated misconceptions live, partly because statistical results are now everywhere and partly because the subject's central objects, conditional probabilities and sampling distributions, are exactly the kind of thing that does not compress well. This family is twelve plates long, and nine of them lose the denominator or the level: they forget what a number is out of, or assign to one case a property of many. Most of these plates come with an experiment from the laboratory, because statistical claims are the easiest kind to check by computation.

B01

Statistical significance

Everyday reading
The effect is large, important or worth acting on.
Technical sense
The data would be unusual under a specified null model, at a chosen threshold. It says nothing directly about the size or importance of the effect.
Thrown away
Magnitude — How big, and how fast?
On record
The aspirin trial, 1989

Ronald Fisher suggested the 5% level in 1925 as a convenient point for judging whether a deviation was worth a second look28. It was never meant as a measure of importance. A large enough study can declare a trivial effect "significant", and a small one can miss an important effect entirely. The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement put it directly: statistical significance is not equivalent to scientific, human or economic significance, and a p-value does not measure the size of an effect29.

The independence runs in both directions, and the instructive case runs the less familiar way. The Physicians' Health Study randomised more than 22,000 American doctors to aspirin or placebo; the aspirin group had 44% fewer heart attacks and the trial's aspirin arm was ended early30. Yet the correlation between taking aspirin and avoiding a heart attack was tiny, explaining about a tenth of one percent of the variance. Robert Rosenthal used this example to show that a statistic that looks negligible can describe an effect that matters a great deal31. The shortcut drops magnitude. Ask how big the effect is, in units someone cares about32.

B02

The p-value

Everyday reading
The probability that the null hypothesis is true, or that the result is a fluke.
Technical sense
The probability, computed assuming the null hypothesis and every other assumption of the model, of data at least as extreme as those observed.
Thrown away
Direction · modality — Which way does the arrow run?
On record
Methodology instructors, 2002

A p-value is a statement about the data given a hypothesis, not about the hypothesis given the data. The everyday reading flips the conditional, dropping direction, and treats a long-run frequency under assumptions as a statement of belief, dropping modality33. Haller and Krauss put six common misreadings to German university psychologists: all of the students, 89.7% of researchers who did not teach methods and 80% of those who did endorsed at least one1.

The difference is not pedantic, and the laboratory shows why. Experiment E1 takes a field in which 10% of tested hypotheses are true, studies have 80% power, and the threshold is 5%. Then 36.0% of the results that reach significance are false positives, more than seven times the 5% that the threshold seems to promise. A direct simulation of 4,000 two-group studies gives 505 significant results, of which 189, or 37.4%, come from true nulls. Lower the share of true hypotheses to 1% and the false share rises to 86.1%. This is the arithmetic behind John Ioannidis's argument that many published findings are false34, and behind Jacob Cohen's complaint that the test does not tell us what we want to know35. Ask: probability of what, given what?

B03

Confidence interval

Everyday reading
There is a 95% probability that the true value lies inside this particular interval.
Technical sense
The procedure that produced the interval captures the true value in 95% of repeated samples, if its assumptions hold. Any one computed interval either contains the value or it does not.
Thrown away
Level — Of one, or of many?
On record
Researchers and students, 2014

Jerzy Neyman, who developed the theory, was explicit that the confidence coefficient describes the long-run behaviour of the method, not the probability that a particular realised interval contains the parameter36. The everyday reading assigns a property of a procedure to a single output of it, a loss of level. It is the most widespread statistical misconception among people who use statistics. Hoekstra and colleagues gave six false statements about a confidence interval to 120 psychology researchers and 442 first-year students; both groups endorsed more than three on average, and the researchers barely outperformed students who had had no instruction in inference at all37.

Experiment E2 makes the distinction visible. It draws 10,000 samples of 20 from a population with a known mean and builds the standard 95% t-interval for each. 94.98% of the intervals contain the true mean. Of the first hundred, plotted below, 7 miss. Each of those hundred intervals either captured the value or did not; the 95% belongs to the column, not to any line in it.

B04

Base rate

Everyday reading
If a test is 90% accurate and you test positive, you are 90% likely to have the condition.
Technical sense
The probability of the condition given a positive result depends on how common the condition was before testing. When the base rate is low, most positives can be false.
Thrown away
Denominator · direction — Out of what?
On record
Sally Clark, 1999–2003

Base-rate neglect is the habit of judging a case by how well it matches a description while ignoring how common such cases are38. Physicians are not exempt. In 1978, staff and students at Harvard Medical School were asked about a test with a 5% false-positive rate for a disease with prevalence 1 in 1,000; the correct answer is about 2%, but fewer than one in five gave it and the commonest answer was 95%39. Experiment E4 confirms the arithmetic at 1.96%. Gigerenzer and colleagues later gave 160 gynaecologists a mammography problem with prevalence 1%, sensitivity 90% and false-positive rate 9%; only 21% chose the right answer40. The arithmetic, laid out below as 1,000 women, gives 9 true positives and 89 false ones, so a positive result means cancer 9.2% of the time.

The same error sent a woman to prison. In 1999 Sally Clark was convicted of murdering her two infant sons, partly on testimony that the chance of two cot deaths in such a family was 1 in 73 million, obtained by squaring a single-death estimate of 1 in 8,543 (E4: 1 in 72,982,849). The Royal Statistical Society wrote publicly that the squaring assumed independence without justification and that the figure had been misread as the probability of innocence41. The relevant comparison is between rival explanations for two deaths, and a double murder is also extremely rare; Ray Hill's later analysis found that double cot death was the more probable of the two42. Her convictions were quashed in 200343. This is the "prosecutor's fallacy"44; a California court had overturned a robbery conviction on similar grounds in 196845. Ask: out of how many, and how common was it beforehand?

B05

Conditional probability

Everyday reading
When some options are eliminated, the remaining ones become equally likely.
Technical sense
New information updates probabilities according to how likely it was to arise under each possibility, which depends on the process that produced it.
Thrown away
Denominator · direction — Out of what?
On record
The Monty Hall problem, 1975–1991

A game show contestant picks one of three doors; one hides a car. The host, who knows where the car is, opens a different door to reveal a goat and offers a switch. Steve Selvin posed this in a letter to The American Statistician in 197546. When Marilyn vos Savant answered it in Parade in 1990, saying that switching wins two-thirds of the time47, she received thousands of letters, many from people with doctorates, telling her she was wrong48. Paul Erdős was among those who would not accept it until shown a simulation3.

The shortcut treats the two closed doors as symmetric. They are not, because the host's choice was constrained: he could not open your door or the car's. Experiment E9 enumerates every case exactly, giving 1/3 for staying and 2/3 for switching, and a simulation of 100,000 games gives 66.38% for switching. The decisive check is to change the process: if the host opens a door at random and merely happens to reveal a goat, switching wins 50.12% of the time. The same observation carries different information depending on how it was generated. What the shortcut drops is the denominator, the set of ways the evidence could have arisen.

B06

Independence

Everyday reading
After a run of reds, black is 'due'; or, a hot streak means the next shot is more likely to go in.
Technical sense
Independent events do not influence each other's probabilities. The law of large numbers concerns proportions over many trials, not compensation on the next one.
Thrown away
Level · denominator — Of one, or of many?
On record
The hot hand, 1985–2018

Tversky and Kahneman called it belief in the law of small numbers: the expectation that a short sequence should look like the long-run distribution, so that a run must soon be balanced49. This takes a property of an aggregate, the convergence of proportions over many trials, and applies it to the next individual trial, a loss of level. The converse misperception is that genuine random sequences look too streaky. Experiment E11 computes the distribution of the longest run of identical outcomes in 200 fair coin tosses: the expected longest run is 7.98 and a run of six or more occurs 96.5% of the time. People inventing "random" sequences tend to avoid runs that long, which is why Schilling could use run length to tell real tosses from fabricated ones in the classroom50.

The case on record has a twist. In 1985 Gilovich, Vallone and Tversky studied basketball shooting and concluded that the "hot hand" was a misperception: players were no more likely to hit after a run of hits51. For thirty years this was a textbook example of people seeing patterns in randomness. In 2018 Miller and Sanjurjo showed that the method itself was biased. In a finite sequence, the proportion of successes immediately following a success is, on average, below the underlying rate, because of how such flips are selected for counting52. Experiment E12 computes it exactly: for sequences of four fair tosses, the expected proportion of heads after a head is 17/42, about 40.5%, not 50%. Corrected for this, the original data show evidence of a hot hand. The researchers who identified a misconception had made a denominator error while measuring it.

B07

Regression to the mean

Everyday reading
When extreme cases move back toward average, something must have caused the change.
Technical sense
When a measurement includes chance variation, cases selected for being extreme on one occasion will tend to be less extreme on the next, with no intervention at all.
Thrown away
Denominator · alternative — Out of what?
On record
Horace Secrist, 1933

Francis Galton named the effect in 1886 when he noticed that the children of very tall parents were, on average, tall but less so than their parents53. Any quantity measured with noise shows it, and selecting cases because they are extreme guarantees it. The trap is that the selection is forgotten (a loss of the denominator) and so is the comparison group that would have shown the same movement without treatment (a loss of the alternative)54.

Experiment E3 simulates 10,000 people whose test scores have a reliability of 0.5 and retests them with nothing in between. The bottom tenth on the first test improves by 0.92 standard deviations on the retest; the top tenth falls by 0.86. Kahneman describes Israeli flight instructors who had concluded that praise made pilots worse and criticism made them better, because good landings were followed by worse ones and bad landings by better ones regardless of what was said55.

The most notable scholarly case is Horace Secrist's 1933 book The Triumph of Mediocrity in Business, which documented that firms with unusually high or low profits tended to move toward the average and interpreted this as a deep economic law56. Harold Hotelling's review pointed out that the pattern was a statistical inevitability that would appear in any such data and proved nothing about business57. The question that would have saved several hundred pages is: compared with what group, selected how?

B08

Sampling bias

Everyday reading
A big enough sample is representative.
Technical sense
Representativeness depends on how the sample was selected and who responded, not on its size. A large biased sample is precisely and confidently wrong.
Thrown away
Denominator — Out of what?
On record
The Literary Digest poll, 1936

In 1936 the Literary Digest mailed some ten million straw ballots and received about 2.4 million back, an extraordinary sample by any standard. It predicted that Alf Landon would defeat Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt won in one of the largest landslides in American history. The usual explanation is that the mailing lists came from telephone directories and automobile registrations, over-sampling the affluent in the depths of the Depression. Peverill Squire's analysis of 1937 survey data concluded that the sampling frame was only part of the problem and that non-response was the larger part: Landon supporters were more likely to send their ballots back58.

The shortcut drops the denominator: it reads the count of returned ballots without asking out of what population, and through what filter, they came. A sample's size governs the precision of an estimate; its selection governs whether it is estimating the right thing. A larger sample from a biased process reduces the noise and leaves the bias exactly where it was.

B09

Survivorship bias

Everyday reading
Look at the successes to learn what works.
Technical sense
Cases that survived a filter differ systematically from those that did not; conclusions drawn only from survivors ignore the failures that would change them.
Thrown away
Denominator · alternative — Out of what?
On record
Abraham Wald and the bombers, 1943

The best-known story involves Abraham Wald, a statistician at Columbia's Statistical Research Group during the Second World War. Returning aircraft showed damage concentrated in some areas, and the naive reading was to armour those areas. Wald's point was that the sample consisted of aircraft that had survived, so damage in the places where returning planes were rarely hit was evidence that hits there were fatal. His actual memoranda were a technical method for estimating vulnerability from the damage on survivors59; Mangel and Samaniego's account notes that the popular retelling simplifies the work considerably60.

Finance has the same shape. Mutual-fund databases that drop closed funds overstate the average performance of the industry, because the funds that close are mostly those that did badly61,62. Experiment E5 builds a market of 1,000 funds with identical return distributions, so that no fund has any skill, and closes any fund that trails the market by five points for two years running. After ten years, 346 survive. Their average annual return is 8.32%, against 2.65% for all funds including the dead, a gap of 5.67 percentage points produced entirely by the filter.

B10

Mean and median

Everyday reading
Both are 'the average' and describe the typical case.
Technical sense
The mean is the total divided by the count, and it describes the aggregate. The median is the middle value, and it describes the typical individual. In skewed data they can be far apart.
Thrown away
Level — Of one, or of many?
On record
American household wealth, 2022

The mean answers a question about the whole: if the total were shared equally, what would each get? The median answers a question about a person: what does the one in the middle have? Treating them as interchangeable assigns an aggregate property to the typical individual, a loss of level. Darrell Huff's 1954 book devoted a chapter to how the choice of average can be used to mislead63.

The gap is large wherever a quantity is skewed. In the Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances, median family net worth was $192,900 and mean net worth was $1,063,70064, a ratio of 5.51. Experiment E6 draws 20,001 values from a log-normal distribution with a median near 50,000: the mean comes out at 81,870, and 69.2% of the population lies below it. Add a single billionaire to a room of fifty people and the room's mean wealth jumps from 57,377 to 19,664,095 while the median does not move.

B11

Simpson's paradox

Everyday reading
If a pattern holds in every group, it holds overall; if it holds overall, it holds in the groups.
Technical sense
An association in aggregated data can reverse within every subgroup when the subgroups differ in size and baseline. Which level answers the question depends on the causal structure.
Thrown away
Level · denominator — Of one, or of many?
On record
Berkeley graduate admissions, 1973

Edward Simpson described the phenomenon formally in 195165, but its most famous instance is the 1973 graduate admissions data from the University of California, Berkeley, analysed by Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell. Pooled across the university, men were admitted at a markedly higher rate than women. Department by department, the gap disappeared and in several departments reversed66. Experiment E8 reproduces the six largest departments: pooled, 44.5% of men and 30.3% of women were admitted, yet women had the higher rate in 4 of the six. The explanation was where people applied: 92.8% of women's applications went to the four departments that admitted fewer than 40% of applicants, against 48.5% of men's.

The paradox is a loss of level, since a fact about the aggregate is read as a fact about its parts, combined with a loss of the denominator, the differing group sizes that weight the average. It is not resolved by a rule that always prefers the disaggregated data. Judea Pearl's analysis shows that whether to condition on the subgroup depends on whether it is a confounder or a consequence of the variable of interest22. The data alone cannot settle that; a causal account of how the groups came to differ can.

B12

Exponential growth

Everyday reading
Growth that is very fast.
Technical sense
Growth whose rate is proportional to the current size, so that the quantity doubles in a fixed time. It can be slow for a long time and then overwhelming.
Thrown away
Magnitude — How big, and how fast?
On record
Misjudged pandemics and loans

Exponential growth is defined by its shape, not its speed. A quantity growing at 1% a year is growing exponentially; it doubles every 69.66 years (E7). What makes such growth hard to judge is that the early stretch looks linear and the later stretch looks impossible. The physicist Albert Bartlett spent decades lecturing on what he called our inability to understand the exponential function67. Experimentally, people asked to extrapolate an exponential series underestimate it drastically68; in household finance, the same bias predicts how people borrow and save69; and early in the coronavirus pandemic, correcting people's underestimates of coronavirus growth increased their support for social distancing70.

E7 makes three versions concrete. A pond whose lily cover doubles daily and is full on day 30 is only 1% covered on day 23.36, a week before the end. A series growing at 25% per period, extrapolated with a straight line through its first five points, is underestimated 4.44-fold ten periods later. And a dollar compounded at 7% for 30 years becomes 7.61, against 3.10 under simple interest. The shortcut drops magnitude, specifically the magnitude of the rate as a proportion of the current level. Ask how long the quantity takes to double.

6The physical and living world

Science contributes some of the most widely circulated misconceptions, usually because a technical term was named with an everyday word and then popularised in a slogan. This family has six plates. Its losses are mixed, which is itself informative: errors about the natural world are not one kind of error.

C01

Evolution

Everyday reading
Organisms adapt during their lives because they need to, and pass the adaptation on.
Technical sense
Heritable variation in a population changes in frequency across generations through natural selection, genetic drift and other mechanisms. Individuals do not evolve; populations do.
Thrown away
Level · direction — Of one, or of many?
On record
Lysenko and Soviet agriculture, 1930s–1960s

The everyday version is a theory of individual striving: the giraffe stretches its neck and its offspring inherit the longer neck. That was close to Jean-Baptiste Lamarck's view in 180971, and it remains the most common misconception about evolution, along with the idea that evolution has a goal72. It assigns to an individual a change that only a population can undergo (a loss of level), and it reverses the causal arrow by making need the cause of variation rather than variation the material on which selection acts (a loss of direction).

Its most destructive historical form was Trofim Lysenko's agrobiology in the Soviet Union. Lysenko rejected Mendelian genetics in favour of a doctrine that environmental conditioning could produce heritable change in crops, and with Stalin's backing he dominated Soviet biology from the late 1930s. Geneticists were dismissed and some, including Nikolai Vavilov, died in prison. Soyfer's account describes the damage to Soviet agriculture and science, which persisted until Lysenko's fall from power in the mid-1960s73. A misconception about level, institutionalised by a state, set a scientific field back by a generation.

C02

Natural selection and fitness

Everyday reading
Survival of the strongest, fastest or most aggressive.
Technical sense
Differential reproductive success of heritable variants in a particular environment. Fitness means fit to that environment, measured in offspring, not strength.
Thrown away
Denominator — Out of what?
On record
'Survival of the fittest', 1864–1869

The phrase is not Darwin's. Herbert Spencer coined it in 1864 in his Principles of Biology74, and Darwin adopted it in the fifth edition of the Origin in 1869, adding it to the title of his chapter on natural selection75. Darwin's "fit" carried its older sense of suitability, as a key fits a lock. The modern sense has drifted toward physical fitness, and from there it is a short step to reading the phrase as a law that the strong prevail.

Fitness in biology is always relative to an environment and is counted in descendants72. A trait that is advantageous in one setting may be neutral or harmful in another, and an organism that is large, strong and aggressive but leaves fewer offspring is less fit in the technical sense. What the shortcut drops is the denominator: fit relative to what environment, compared with which alternative variants? The compressed slogan also invited an old objection, that "the fittest survive" is circular if fitness is defined as surviving. That was one reason Popper initially doubted that the theory was testable17. The circularity disappears once fitness is understood as a propensity in a specified environment that can be estimated independently of who happened to survive.

C03

Entropy

Everyday reading
Disorder, mess, the tendency of things to fall apart.
Technical sense
A state function measuring the number of microscopic arrangements consistent with a macroscopic description, or equivalently how widely energy is dispersed among available states.
Thrown away
Level — Of one, or of many?
On record
Von Neumann's naming advice to Shannon, 1940s

Rudolf Clausius coined the word in 1865 for a quantity that thermodynamics needed: one that, in an isolated system, never decreases76. Boltzmann's statistical interpretation tied it to the count of microstates compatible with a given macrostate. The everyday gloss "disorder" is a visual impression at the human scale, and it misleads in both directions. A shuffled deck of cards is "disordered" but its thermodynamic entropy is unchanged by the shuffle. Oil and water separate spontaneously into a more "ordered" arrangement, and many systems crystallise as entropy increases. Frank Lambert called disorder a "cracked crutch" for teaching entropy77, and Daniel Styer showed with worked examples why the visual picture is unreliable78.

The loss is one of level: entropy is a property of a macroscopic description of a system and is defined by counting at the microscopic level, whereas "disorder" judges the appearance of a single arrangement. The concept's reputation for obscurity is old. Claude Shannon recalled that John von Neumann advised him to call his measure of information "entropy" partly because nobody really knew what entropy was, which would give him the advantage in any debate79. The anecdote is often told as a joke; it is also a fair description of how the word has been used since.

C04

Relativity

Everyday reading
Everything is relative; there is no objective truth, only points of view.
Technical sense
Measurements of time intervals and lengths depend on the observer's frame of reference, in a precisely specified way that keeps the laws of physics and the speed of light the same in every inertial frame.
Thrown away
Denominator · modality — Out of what?
On record
Einstein on the name, 1921

Einstein's 1905 paper begins from two postulates: that the laws of physics take the same form in every inertial frame, and that the speed of light in vacuum is the same for every inertial observer80. Everything else follows from insisting on those invariances. Durations and lengths do differ between frames, but they transform according to exact rules, and combinations such as the spacetime interval are the same for everyone. The theory is, in that sense, an account of what does not depend on the observer.

The popular reading drops the frame-specific denominator (relative to which frame, by what transformation?) and inflates a statement about measurement into a statement about truth, a loss of modality. The misunderstanding was apparent within Einstein's lifetime. In 1921 he corresponded with Eberhard Zschimmer, an engineer who had written that the "relativisation" of space and time seemed to conflict with absolute knowledge; the editors of Einstein's collected papers note that the alternative name "invariant theory" had been suggested by Felix Klein in 191081. Had that name stuck, a century of loose talk might have taken a different shape.

C05

Model

Everyday reading
A model is a faithful description of reality; or, since 'all models are wrong', none can be trusted.
Technical sense
A deliberately simplified representation built for a purpose under stated assumptions. Its reliability depends on whether the assumptions hold for the use to which it is put.
Thrown away
Modality — How sure, and on what grounds?
On record
Kelvin's age of the Earth, 1862–1907

George Box's line that all models are wrong appeared in 197682, and its familiar continuation, that some are useful, in 197983. The point was not cynicism but calibration: a model's conclusions inherit the strength of its assumptions, so the right question is never whether a model is true but whether its simplifications matter for the question being asked. Both everyday readings lose modality, one by treating model output as fact, the other by treating all model output as worthless.

William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, offers the classic case. In 1862 he calculated the age of the Earth by treating it as a solid body cooling by conduction from an initially molten state, arriving at a range of 20 to 400 million years that he later narrowed toward the lower end84. Geologists who needed far more time were outgunned by the authority of the physics. In 1895 his former assistant John Perry showed that if the interior conducted heat more effectively than the surface rocks, as convection in a partly fluid interior would allow, the same data permitted ages of billions of years85. England, Molnar and Richter later argued that Perry had identified the real flaw, which was not the arithmetic but the assumption of a uniformly rigid Earth86. Radiometric dating now gives about 4.5 billion years87. Kelvin's error was a confident reading of a model whose assumptions he had not tested.

C06

Accuracy and precision

Everyday reading
Two words for the same thing: being exact.
Technical sense
Accuracy is closeness to the true value. Precision is closeness of repeated measurements to one another. A measurement can be precise and badly inaccurate.
Thrown away
Magnitude — How big, and how fast?
On record
The Hubble Space Telescope mirror, 1990

The international vocabulary of metrology separates the two cleanly. Accuracy describes how close a measured value is to the true value; precision describes how close repeated measurements are to each other, regardless of where they cluster88. A rifle that puts every shot in a tight group two inches left of the bullseye is precise and inaccurate. The shortcut collapses two magnitudes into one, so that a small spread is mistaken for a small error.

The Hubble Space Telescope's primary mirror is the most expensive illustration. It was polished with extraordinary precision, and when the telescope reached orbit in 1990 its images were blurred by spherical aberration because the mirror's shape was wrong at the edge by about two micrometres. The investigation found that the instrument used to guide the polishing, a reflective null corrector, had been assembled with a lens out of position by 1.3 millimetres, so the mirror was ground very exactly to the wrong figure. Conflicting results from other test instruments had been discounted89. Corrective optics installed by astronauts in 1993 restored the telescope's vision. The question that restores the lost magnitude is: consistent with each other, or close to the truth?

7Economics and decisions

Economic reasoning has a characteristic structure: the value of a choice is defined by what else you could have done. It is therefore unsurprising that three of the four plates in this family lose the alternative. The fourth, risk against uncertainty, loses modality, and it is the one with the most expensive history.

D01

Opportunity cost

Everyday reading
The money you spend on something.
Technical sense
The value of the best alternative given up by making a choice. It includes forgone opportunities, not only cash outlays.
Thrown away
Alternative — Compared with what?
On record
Economists at the 2005 ASSA meeting

James Buchanan stressed that cost in this sense is subjective and prospective: it is the value of the option you do not take, as seen by the person choosing, at the moment of choice90. The everyday reading counts the price paid and drops the alternative entirely. If a free afternoon could have been spent earning money, the afternoon was not free.

Ferraro and Taylor tested the profession on its own central concept. At the 2005 meetings of the Allied Social Science Associations they asked nearly two hundred economists a question adapted from an introductory textbook: you have a free ticket to a concert by Eric Clapton; Bob Dylan is playing the same night, tickets cost $40, and you would pay up to $50 to see him. What is the opportunity cost of seeing Clapton? The answer is $10, the net value of the forgone Dylan evening. Only 21.6% of respondents chose it, fewer than the 25% that random guessing among four options would have produced2. Even people who teach the idea are prone to drop the comparison it depends on. Ask: compared with the best thing I could have done instead?

D02

Sunk cost

Everyday reading
We have already spent so much that we cannot stop now.
Technical sense
A cost already incurred and unrecoverable. Because it is the same whichever option is chosen next, it should not by itself affect a forward-looking decision.
Thrown away
Alternative · direction — Compared with what?
On record
The 'Concorde fallacy', 1976

A sunk cost is identical across every option still open, so it cannot help to choose between them. The error is to compare the future against the past rather than against the other possible futures, a loss of the alternative combined with a reversal of the decision's direction in time. Arkes and Blumer showed the effect experimentally in 1985: theatre-goers randomly given discounts on season tickets attended fewer performances than those who paid full price, and people asked about a half-finished project were more willing to complete it the more had already been spent91.

The name "Concorde fallacy" was given in 1976 by Richard Dawkins and Tamsin Carlisle in a paper on animal behaviour, criticising the argument that a parent that has invested more in offspring should be less willing to desert them; the analogy was to the Anglo-French supersonic airliner, whose development continued long after its commercial prospects had dimmed92. There is a fair caveat. A past investment can carry information about the project, or bear on reputation and commitments, and those are forward-looking reasons. What the concept rules out is the past expenditure as a reason in itself.

D03

Comparative advantage

Everyday reading
Being better at producing something than someone else.
Technical sense
Being able to produce something at a lower opportunity cost than someone else. Trade can benefit both sides even when one is absolutely better at everything.
Thrown away
Alternative · denominator — Compared with what?
On record
Samuelson's answer to Ulam

David Ricardo's 1817 example used England and Portugal, wine and cloth, measured in the labour of men for a year93. Portugal needs fewer workers for both goods, so it has an absolute advantage in each. But a unit of cloth costs Portugal more wine than it costs England, and a unit of wine costs Portugal less cloth. Samuelson's answer was comparative advantage: logically true, he said, and yet not obvious, as shown by the thousands of intelligent people who have never grasped it or believed it after it was explained to them94. D04

Risk and uncertainty

Everyday reading
The same thing: not knowing what will happen.
Technical sense
Risk describes outcomes whose probabilities can be estimated reliably. Uncertainty describes situations in which there is no reliable basis for assigning probabilities at all.
Thrown away
Modality — How sure, and on what grounds?
On record
A bank's 25-sigma days, August 2007

Frank Knight drew the distinction in 1921: risk is measurable, uncertainty is not, and it is uncertainty in his sense that allows entrepreneurial profit96. John Maynard Keynes made the same point about long-run expectations, observing that for many questions there is no scientific basis for any calculable probability; in such cases, he wrote, "we simply do not know"97. Daniel Ellsberg's experiments showed that people treat the two differently, preferring a bet on known odds to one on unknown odds even when they cannot say why the unknown should be worse98.

The everyday merger drops modality: it treats a probability that has been assumed as if it had been measured. In August 2007 the chief financial officer of a major investment bank said publicly that its funds had seen moves of 25 standard deviations several days in a row. Dowd and colleagues worked out that under the normal distribution implied by such a statement, a single 25-sigma day would be expected far less than once in the lifetime of the universe99. The statement was not evidence of extraordinary bad luck. It was evidence that a situation of uncertainty had been modelled as one of risk, with a distribution whose tails were simply wrong.

8Law and language

The last family is short and mixed. One plate is a legal doctrine that is commonly cited from a case overruled decades ago; one is a term from Aristotelian logic whose everyday use has drifted to mean almost the opposite; one is a word whose "misuse" is older than most of the people who complain about it. Language plates are a useful corrective to the rest of the catalogue, because they show that not every departure from a technical sense is an error.

E01

Free speech

Everyday reading
I have a legal right to say anything anywhere, and the limit is 'shouting fire in a crowded theatre'.
Technical sense
In the United States, the First Amendment restrains government action, not private parties, and modern doctrine protects advocacy unless it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it. Free speech as a broader moral principle is a separate question.
Thrown away
Direction · modality — Which way does the arrow run?
On record
Schenck v. United States, 1919

Two compressions coexist. The first concerns direction: the constitutional guarantee runs against the state. In 2019 the Supreme Court reaffirmed that a private operator of public-access television channels was not a state actor and so was not bound by the First Amendment in deciding what to air100. Whether private platforms and employers ought to respect a principle of free expression is a real question, argued since at least John Stuart Mill101, but it is a moral and political question, not a constitutional one.

The second concerns status, a loss of modality. The most quoted limit on speech, Justice Holmes's remark that free speech would not protect a man "falsely shouting fire in a theatre and causing a panic", comes from Schenck v. United States in 1919102. It was a passing illustration, not the holding, and the case upheld the conviction of socialists for distributing leaflets opposing the draft. Its "clear and present danger" test was effectively replaced in 1969 by Brandenburg v. Ohio, which protects advocacy unless it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and is likely to do so103. Citing the theatre line as the governing law of free speech quotes an illustration from a case whose doctrine was abandoned half a century ago.

E02

Begging the question

Everyday reading
Raising the question; prompting one to ask.
Technical sense
Assuming, in the premises of an argument, the conclusion the argument is supposed to establish.
Thrown away
Direction — Which way does the arrow run?
On record
Aristotle's petitio principii

The phrase is a translation, by way of the medieval Latin petitio principii, of a fallacy Aristotle discusses in the Prior Analytics: asking the audience to grant at the outset the very thing to be shown104. "This book is reliable because it says so, and it would not say so if it were not reliable" begs the question. The logical sense names an argument whose arrow loops back on itself, a defect of direction: the conclusion is used to support the premises that are supposed to support it.

In everyday English the phrase is now used far more often to mean "raises the question", as in "the result begs the question of what happens next". Usage guides record decades of complaint about this and also record that the newer sense is entrenched105. This plate is included less as a correction than as a warning about ambiguity. In a logic seminar or a legal brief, the phrase still means circular reasoning, and a reader who hears only the newer sense will miss an accusation.

E03

Literally, and the problem of usage

Everyday reading
Using 'literally' for emphasis is always an error; so is 'decimate' for heavy destruction and 'disinterested' for uninterested.
Technical sense
Words mean what communities of speakers use them to mean, and many 'misuses' are centuries old. The useful distinction is between senses that cause ambiguity in context and those that do not.
Thrown away
Modality — How sure, and on what grounds?
On record
Dickens, Twain and Fitzgerald

"Literally" in its intensive sense, where it marks emphasis rather than a claim of literal truth, has been used by major writers since at least the nineteenth century; Merriam-Webster's usage note cites examples from Dickens, Mark Twain and F. Scott Fitzgerald106. The intensive use changes the word's modality, from a guarantee of factuality to a marker of strong feeling, and that is precisely why it irritates: it borrows the force of the literal sense while abandoning its content.

Other favourite complaints have similar histories. "Decimate" originally named the Roman military punishment of executing one soldier in ten, described by Polybius107; its broader sense of severe destruction is long established. "Disinterested" has carried both the senses "impartial" and "uninterested" since the seventeenth century, with the impartial sense now usual in careful writing105. The lesson of this plate is not that anything goes. It is that the confident claim "that word doesn't mean that" is itself frequently a misconception. The better question is whether, in this sentence, the reader could take it two ways.

Three things stand out. The first is that the families have signatures. Of the twelve statistical plates, nine lose the denominator or the level; of the six plates on reasoning and evidence, four lose modality; and of the four economic plates, three lose the alternative. That is not an artefact of the categories, which were drawn up for the whole catalogue before the plates were coded. It reflects the fact that each discipline's central objects are vulnerable in a particular place. Statistical objects are always "out of" something; claims about evidence are always held with some strength; economic values are always relative to a forgone option.

The second is that direction is the commonest accomplice. It is the primary loss on only four plates, but it appears as a secondary loss on six, more than any other dimension, so it is involved in ten of the 31 in all. Reading a conditional backwards is rarely the whole story of an error, but it is very often part of it, because once the denominator or the alternative has been dropped, there is nothing left to stop the arrow being reversed.

The third is that magnitude is the rarest primary loss, at three plates. That seems surprising until one notices that magnitude errors tend to be noticed: a number that is badly wrong usually collides with experience. The more dangerous compressions are those that leave the number intact and remove the thing that tells you what the number means.

10Six questions to carry

The practical value of the scheme lies in its questions, which are short enough to ask aloud and pointed enough to expose the most common compressions. They work best when asked of a sentence rather than a topic, and best of all when asked of a sentence you yourself have just said.

  1. Which way does the arrow run? Before accepting a conditional claim, state it in the other direction and check whether it is a different claim. "Most significant results are true" and "most true hypotheses give significant results" are different claims. So are "this test catches 90% of cancers" and "90% of positives are cancer".
  2. Out of what? Ask for the denominator and for how the cases reached you. Survivors, respondents, admitted applicants and extreme scorers were selected; the base rate was there before the evidence arrived.
  3. Compared with what? Ask for the alternative. A cost is the best thing given up; a cause is a difference between a world with it and a world without it; a result that looks good may look ordinary against the right comparison.
  4. Of one, or of many? Ask whether the property belongs to an individual, a population or a procedure. Coverage belongs to a procedure, evolution to a population, an average to an aggregate.
  5. How big, and how fast? Ask for the size of the effect and the shape of the growth. Detectable is not large; consistent is not correct; steady percentage growth is not steady growth.
  6. How sure, and on what grounds? Ask what kind of claim it is. A theory is not a guess, evidence is not proof, a heuristic is not a law, and a probability that has been estimated is not the same as one that is merely assumed.

The catalogue is a reminder that these questions are not only for others. Some of the errors above were made by people at the top of their fields: a mathematician of Erdős's stature, a physicist of Kelvin's, a philosopher of Popper's, statisticians trying to measure the very fallacy they were committing. What protected the rest of us, in each case, was not intelligence but the habit of someone asking one of these six questions at the right moment. Kelvin was answered by a former assistant who asked what his model assumed. Secrist was answered by a reviewer who asked what the comparison should have been. The hot-hand researchers were answered, thirty years on, by two economists who asked how the sample had been selected.

A concept is known well, in the end, when you know not only what it says but what its everyday version leaves out. That is a modest standard, and it is one that most of us, most of the time, do not meet.

def t_crit_975(df):
    # Student t 0.975 quantile by bisection on a numerically integrated cdf.
    def pdf(t):
        return math.exp(math.lgamma((df + 1) / 2) - math.lgamma(df / 2)) / math.sqrt(df * math.pi) * (1 + t * t / df) ** (-(df + 1) / 2)
    def cdf(t):
        n = 4000; h = t / n; s = pdf(0) + pdf(t)
        for i in range(1, n):
            s += (4 if i % 2 else 2) * pdf(i * h)
        return 0.5 + s * h / 3
    lo, hi = 1.0, 4.0
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if cdf(mid) < 0.975: lo = mid
        else: hi = mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2

        say(f"  {c}: 1 cloth costs {L[c]['cloth']/L[c]['wine']:.4f} wine; 1 wine costs {L[c]['wine']/L[c]['cloth']:.4f} cloth")

# ------------------------------------------------- E11 longest run
def e11():
    section("E11", "How long is the longest run in 200 fair coin flips?")
    n = 200
    # P(longest run of identical outcomes < k) via DP on current run length
    def p_less(k):
        # sequences where no run of identical symbols reaches k; first symbol fixed by symmetry
        dp = [0.0] * (k + 1); dp[1] = 1.0
        for _ in range(n - 1):
            nd = [0.0] * (k + 1)
            for L in range(1, k):
                if dp[L] == 0: continue
                nd[L + 1] += dp[L] * 0.5
                nd[1] += dp[L] * 0.5
            nd[k] = 0.0
            dp = nd
        return sum(dp[1:k])
    dist = []; prev = 0.0; exp = 0.0
    for k in range(1, 22):
        cdf = p_less(k + 1)
        pk = cdf - prev; prev = cdf
        exp += k * pk
        dist.append([k, r(pk, 5)])
    p_ge6 = 1 - p_less(6)
    mode = max(dist, key=lambda x: x[1])[0]
    say(f"  expected longest run of heads-or-tails: {exp:.3f}; most likely value {mode}; P(longest >= 6) = {p_ge6*100:.1f}%")
    OUT["E11"] = {"n": n, "expected": r(exp, 3), "mode": mode, "p_ge6": r(p_ge6), "dist": dist}

# ------------------------------------------------- E12 hot hand selection bias
def e12():
    section("E12", "The hot-hand selection bias (Miller & Sanjurjo)")
    rows = []
    for n in [3, 4, 5, 10, 20]:
        tot = Fraction(0); cnt = 0
        for seq in product([0, 1], repeat=n):
            after = [seq[i + 1] for i in range(n - 1) if seq[i] == 1]
            if after:
                tot += Fraction(sum(after), len(after)); cnt += 1
        e = tot / cnt
        rows.append({"n": n, "expected": str(e) if n <= 5 else None, "value": r(float(e))})
        say(f"  n={n:2d}: expected proportion of heads immediately after a head, averaged over sequences = {float(e):.4f}" + (f"  (= {e})" if n <= 5 else ""))
    OUT["E12"] = {"rows": rows}
