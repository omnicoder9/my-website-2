A stylometric result is a number attached to a claim about a person. The number is real; the attachment is an inference, and it is the weakest part of the apparatus. Between the text and the writer stand a topic, a genre, an audience, a medium, an editor, a decade, a schooling and a professional convention, each of which moves the same counts that the writer moves, and several of which move them further. This article sets out what a writing style is, how the field has learned to measure one, and how the measurement is defended against everything else in the room.

1What a writing style is

A writing style is a pattern of selection. Language offers a speaker or writer more than one way to accomplish nearly any communicative task: the same proposition can be a clause or a nominalisation, the same relation can be because or since or a colon, the same list can take a serial comma or not. Where the alternatives are close enough in meaning that the choice is not forced by what is being said, the choice is available to be made habitually, and a habit of choosing is what a style is 1. The classical stylistic traditions differ in emphasis rather than in substance: style as deviation from a norm, style as choice among equivalents, style as recurrence of a pattern. All three presuppose the same underlying object, an inventory of options and a distribution over them 2.

The separation of style from content is never clean, and it is worth being precise about why. Two realisations of "the same" proposition are rarely exact paraphrases: a passive with the agent dropped genuinely says less than the corresponding active. What the choice model requires is weaker, only that the alternatives be close enough in communicative effect that the selection is underdetermined by the message, and that weaker condition is satisfied constantly, which is why style is measurable at all 1 2.

A fourth tradition treats style as foregrounding: patterning of the message for its own sake, which Jakobson placed at the centre of poetic language 3. For stylometry it matters chiefly as a warning. Foregrounded style is the part of a style a writer controls, notices and can be asked about, and therefore the part most easily imitated and the least useful as evidence.

The distinction matters because it tells you where a style is not to be found. Elements of a text that are wholly determined by what is being communicated carry no stylistic information at all, and elements wholly determined by the language carry none either: every English writer puts the before the noun. The stylistically informative part of a text is the residue after obligatory grammar and communicative necessity have been subtracted, which is why the field's most durable features are the ones the writer has least reason to think about 4.

Linguistics gives the pattern of selection several names according to what governs it. Register is variation associated with a situation of use: the systematic difference between a statute, a recipe and a text message 5 6. Genre is variation associated with a conventional text type and its expected moves, the thing a research article shares with other research articles because readers of research articles expect it 7. Dialect and sociolect are variation associated with a place and a group 8. Idiolect is the residue: the version of the language a single person has, arising from that person's particular history of exposure and use 9 10.

Stylometry is the measurement of these patterns, and authorship analysis is its best-known application, but the two are not the same. A stylometric measurement is agnostic about which source of variation it has measured; it returns a distance, and the interpretation of that distance is a separate act. This is the point on which almost every dispute in the field turns, and the reason the useful question about a stylometric result is not "how large is the difference?" but "what else could have made it that size?"''' 11.

Style as a description and style as evidence

Two research programmes with different standards share the same instruments. Literary and corpus stylistics describe how a text works, and a measurement there is a route to interpretation: a claim about how a distribution of pronouns supports a reading is answerable to the text, and being wrong costs an argument 12 13. Forensic and attributional stylometry assigns a text to a person, and being wrong costs something else. The same feature set that makes a persuasive figure in a literary study may be nowhere near sufficient as evidence, and the field has repeatedly had to relearn that the standards do not transfer 14.

The relevant standards for evidence are not internal to linguistics. Daubert asks whether a technique has been tested, peer reviewed, has a known error rate, has standards controlling its operation and enjoys general acceptance 15; the National Research Council and later the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology applied that framework to feature-comparison methods generally and found most of them short of it, particularly on the question of a measured error rate under realistic conditions 16 17. Authorship analysis has been admitted, excluded and admitted again in various jurisdictions, and the recurring judicial complaint is not that the counting is wrong but that the step from counting to conclusion is undocumented 18 19.

Where the term comes from

The word is Wincenty Lutoslawski's. He introduced stylometry in print in 1897 for a method of dating Plato's dialogues by counting stylistic features, and set out its principles the following year 20 21; the 1890 date attached to the coinage throughout the secondary literature does not survive checking 22. The practice is far older than the word. Leon Battista Alberti compared vowel and consonant proportions in Latin poets and orators in 1466, in a treatise on ciphers, and a modern audit of his counts finds the direction of his claim defensible but his samples far too small to establish it 23. Augustus De Morgan proposed in an 1851 letter, published only posthumously, that disputed Pauline epistles might be settled by average word length 24; Mendenhall turned the suggestion into a procedure a generation later 25.

That history earns a paragraph here for a reason beyond priority. Every one of these was a single-feature proposal, and every one was met with the observation that the feature moves with something other than the author. Eddy's reply to Mendenhall, published within weeks, argued that the characteristic curve is governed principally by the language rather than by the writer 26. The objection has never been retired. It has been reformulated for each new feature set in turn, and the methodological history of the field is largely the history of learning to answer it in advance rather than after publication; Morton's confident single-marker attributions of the 1970s are the best-known modern casualty 27.

2Style as a statistical pattern

No single feature identifies a writer. This is not a limitation of present methods but a property of the object: the features available for measurement are frequencies of things that every competent user of the language also produces, and the differences between people are differences of rate. A writer who never used whilst and one who used it in one sentence per thousand are distinguishable only across enough sentences for the difference between zero and one per thousand to be visible above sampling noise. Style is therefore a distributional object, and stylometry is applied statistics on very small samples of very sparse data 28.

The sparseness is structural rather than incidental. Word frequencies in any natural text follow a steeply skewed distribution in which a handful of items account for most tokens and the great majority of types occur once or twice 29 28. A feature set built from frequent items is therefore small, stable and individually weak; a feature set built from rare items is large, individually strong and almost entirely unestimable from the quantity of text an actual case supplies. Every methodological choice in stylometry is a position on that trade-off 30.

Three consequences follow, and most of the field's methodology is a response to them.

Identification depends on combinations

Because individual features are weak, they are used in bulk. Mosteller and Wallace's study of the disputed Federalist papers is the founding demonstration: no one marker settled the question, but a Bayesian combination of about thirty carefully screened function words gave odds strongly favouring Madison over Hamilton on all twelve disputed papers 31 32. Burrows's Delta generalised the move by abandoning the hunt for discriminating markers altogether: take the most frequent words in a reference corpus, standardise each to a z-score across the corpus, and rank candidates by mean absolute difference 33. Delta works because standardisation gives each feature an equal vote, so that a pattern spread thinly across a hundred weak features can outweigh a single strong coincidence 34.

The choice of how to combine features is not neutral. Evert and colleagues decomposed Delta into feature selection, feature scaling and distance, and showed that the improvement claimed for the cosine variant comes specifically from normalising the feature vector to unit length, which suppresses the influence of a handful of extreme features that would otherwise dominate a Manhattan distance 35. Variants proliferate because they keep working slightly better on slightly different corpora 36 37 38 39; the honest summary is that the metric is a modelling assumption about how deviations should be added up, and it is chosen empirically.

Features operate at several linguistic levels

A stylistic signal is available at every level of description at which a language offers alternatives. Below the word there are character sequences, which capture spelling, morphology and punctuation habit at once and need no linguistic processing 40. At the word level there are the closed-class items whose frequencies are largely independent of subject matter 4, and the open-class items whose frequencies are not. At the sentence level there are part-of-speech sequences 41, grammatical rewrite rules 42, and dependency relations 43. Above the sentence there are discourse markers, cohesion devices and paragraph rhythm 44. Outside the language proper there are the traces of the writing situation itself: formatting, greeting conventions, emoticon use, the shape of a signature block 45.

Levels differ in the data they need and in what they are vulnerable to. Character n-grams are the most robust performers in comparative evaluations and require the least text, but they absorb topic through stems and content words, which is why the affix-and-punctuation subset of them generalises across topics better than the full set 46 47. Syntactic features are more expensive, need a parser whose errors are then indistinguishable from style, and were shown by van Halteren and colleagues to be genuinely informative but less so than lexical features 48 49.

Above the sentence the options are cohesive rather than grammatical: how reference is maintained across clauses, how much is left implicit, how relations are signalled or not signalled 50. These are the features a literary stylistician reaches for first and an attribution study reaches for last, because they are the most interpretable and the most expensive to count reliably at scale 51.

What makes a feature usable

Five properties, and no feature has all five. A usable feature must discriminate: between-author variance must exceed within-author variance for that feature, on the material at hand. It must be stable within an author across the range of material the case involves. It must be ubiquitous, occurring often enough that a short text yields an estimate at all, which is the real reason function words dominate the field: they are the only linguistically interesting items frequent enough to estimate from a page 4. It must be independent enough of the rest of the set that combining features adds information rather than recounting it, a condition the standard sets fail badly, since the rates of the, of and a move together with nominal style. And it must be resistant: not trivially controllable by a writer who wishes to conceal, and not removable by an editor 52 14.

These pull against one another in something close to a conservation law. Features frequent enough to be stable in short texts are the ones most constrained by grammar, and therefore the least individual; features distinctive enough to identify a person are rare enough that their absence from a sample means nothing. Rare-event methods such as n-gram tracing accept the trade explicitly, buying very high precision at the cost of a recall so low that most comparisons return no evidence at all 53 54.

How the claims are evaluated

The field's self-assessment literature is unusually candid. Successive surveys report the same structural problems: incomparable evaluations, corpora chosen for availability, methods reported on a single dataset with no error bars 55 56 57 58 59 60. Comparative studies in which one team re-implements the competing methods and runs them on shared data are rarer and far more informative, and they tend to find that the choice of features matters more than the choice of classifier, and that several celebrated methods become indistinguishable once the corpus is held fixed 61 62 63.

One corpus has absorbed so much of this effort that agreement on it now demonstrates very little. The disputed Federalist papers have been re-analysed with letter-pair frequencies fed to neural networks, with genetic feature search, with principal components and with every Delta variant in circulation, all reaching the same conclusion Mosteller and Wallace reached 64 65. A method that agrees with them has cleared a low bar on material selected over sixty years for being tractable. It is also worth reporting which frequency stratum a method draws on, since Burrows found usable authorial signal well below the most frequent words, in bands that Delta as normally configured never reaches 66.

Reported accuracy also depends on the number of candidates, the length of the samples and the volume of training text per candidate, none of which is standardised across studies. Where these are varied deliberately the effect is large: attribution that looks solved at novel length degrades sharply at paragraph length 67, and a method that separates two candidates cleanly can fail at fifty 68. High accuracy on one well-chosen corpus — a disputed Oz volume separated by principal components, an intertextual distance validated on a controlled English collection — shows that the signal exists in that material 69 70 71. It does not supply an error rate for anything else.

Conscious and unconscious, stable and situational

Style is partly deliberate. Writers imitate, parody, revise toward a target and follow style guides; a pastiche is a deliberate attempt to move measured features toward another writer's values, and it succeeds often enough to matter 72. Deliberate concealment succeeds more reliably still: asked simply to write differently or to imitate a specific author, subjects in the Brennan and Greenstadt experiments drove several standard attribution methods down to or below chance 52, and automated obfuscation has since been developed as a research programme in its own right 73 74. What is deliberate is also detectable, in that the effort of concealment leaves its own signature 75 76.

Style is also partly outside awareness. The frequency with which a person writes of rather than a genitive, or begins sentences with a conjunction, is not usually a monitored decision, which is exactly why those features are the useful ones 77 78. And style is only conditionally stable. It changes over a career, measurably and in a direction that can be used to date works 79 80; it responds to the influence of what an author has read 81; it varies between the same person's speech and writing, and between one platform and another 82 83. Stability is thus a quantity to be estimated for a given writer over a given interval, not a property to be assumed.

3The instruments: eighteen feature families

What follows is a catalogue of the feature families in routine use, each recorded as a plate with a fixed field set: what it counts, at which level, how much text it needs before its estimate is stable, what non-authorial source moves it hardest, and how it fails when it fails. The estimates of required text are order-of-magnitude figures drawn from the sources cited on each plate, not measurements of my own.

Each plate also carries a reading on the confound ladder introduced in the next section, shown as a core sample: how much of the surrounding situation has to be held constant before the feature can be read as evidence about a person rather than about a situation. A plate at the surface needs nothing held constant; a plate at the bottom of the core cannot be read as authorial on its own at all.

Two patterns run through the catalogue. The first is that depth on the ladder is inversely related to convenience: the families needing the least text and the least linguistic processing are the ones most contaminated by subject matter, while the families that survive the most controls are either expensive to compute or too rare to appear in a short document at all. The second is that the single family which reads as authorial with nothing held constant is also the only one whose evidential weight cannot be quantified, because it depends on a population frequency that no corpus reports. That is not a gap in the catalogue. It is the shape of the problem.

4Where the variation comes from

Every stylometric measurement is a sum of effects, only one of which is the writer. The list below is not a taxonomy of nuisances to be dismissed; several of these sources are larger than the authorial effect, and the whole methodology of the field is organised around holding them constant.

Twelve sources

Topic. The strongest and most obvious. Content-word frequencies are almost wholly determined by subject matter, and topic leaks into supposedly topic-neutral feature sets through stems, collocations and the content-bearing parts of character n-grams. Cross-topic attribution is measurably harder than same-topic attribution, and the gap is large enough that same-topic evaluations systematically overstate what a method can do 116 84. Explicit masking of topic-bearing material is one of the few interventions that reliably narrows the gap 85 117.

Topic contamination is measurable rather than merely arguable, which is what makes it tractable. Designs that hold the author fixed while varying the topic, or that deliberately arrange for topic and author to disagree in the test set, quantify how much of a reported accuracy was topic all along 118 119.

Genre and register. A person's email, lecture notes and published articles differ from each other on the standard feature sets by more than two people's articles differ from one another. Biber's multidimensional analyses established the scale of register variation in English on independent grounds decades before it became an attribution problem 44 120, and the cross-domain condition remains the setting where methods degrade most 103 121 122.

Audience and formality. Formality is not a single dial but it behaves like one for many features: pronoun rates, contraction rates, nominalisation, hedging and connective choice all shift together with the intended reader 6 99.

Medium. The channel imposes its own constraints: message length limits, threading conventions, keyboard affordances, autocorrection. Attribution accuracy drops when known and questioned documents come from different platforms even when the writer and topic are held fixed 103 83.

Editing. Published prose is a collaboration. Copy editors normalise punctuation, split long sentences, impose a house style and remove exactly the idiosyncrasies with the highest evidential value. Translation is the extreme case: a translated text carries a measurable signature of its translator, sometimes strongly enough to swamp the original author 123.

Time period. Both the language and the writer move. An author's measured style drifts across a career at a rate that supports relative dating of undated works 79 80, which is the same statement as: known documents separated from a questioned document by twenty years are a degraded reference sample.

Education, community and dialect. Systematic variation by region, social network and schooling is the subject matter of sociolinguistics, and it means that similarity between two texts may reflect shared background rather than shared authorship 8 124. In the authorship setting this is a base-rate problem: the informativeness of a shared feature depends on how common it is in the relevant population, which is exactly the quantity nobody has measured 100.

Professional and institutional convention. Legal drafting, clinical notes and standards documents constrain their writers so tightly that the institution is easier to identify than the individual. This is the sharpest form of the problem, because the constraint is deliberate and the resulting texts are numerous.

Collaboration and derivation. Co-authorship, boilerplate, quotation and revision of a shared source create textual similarity with no authorial content whatever; the stylometric signal of a document family is stronger than the signal of a drafter, as the experiment in section 7 shows. Disentangling collaborators is a distinct problem with its own methods 125 126.

Where collaboration is sequential rather than simultaneous, sliding a classifier along the text in overlapping windows can locate the seam without naming anyone, which is a narrower question than attribution and a considerably more tractable one 127.

Formulaic language. Much of any text is prefabricated: collocations, fixed phrases and institutional formulae selected as wholes rather than assembled word by word 128. Shared formulaic material inflates similarity between texts that share a domain, and it is the main reason word-sequence methods return matches between documents with no author in common 97 96.

Deliberate variation. Imitation, pastiche, pseudonymity and obfuscation, discussed above 72 52.

Sampling. Text length is a source of variation in its own right, because several standard measures are functions of length rather than of style; short samples both degrade estimates and bias them 67 91.

Machine assistance. Recent and rapidly growing. Text that has been drafted, rewritten or polished by a language model carries that model's distributional preferences; large models can imitate a target style well enough to move stylometric measures toward it 129 130, and paraphrasing a text through a model is an effective attack on detectors that rely on distributional signatures 131 132.

The sources interact

Listing them separately is a convenience. In real material they multiply rather than add: an author's topic range is a function of their profession, their register range is a function of their topic, and the period fixes both the available orthographic norms and the editorial regime the text passed through. Two consequences follow for practice. The first is that controlling one source rarely controls another, so a study that matches genre and reports cross-topic robustness has established less than the sum of the two claims. The second is that the residual after all controls is not the author but the author-in-that-situation, which is precisely the position the resource-constraint account of linguistic identity arrives at from the theoretical side 102 133. A stylometric result is therefore best read as a statement about a person writing under stated conditions, and its transportability to other conditions is an empirical question with, usually, a discouraging answer 121 103.

Is the author effect a register effect?

A stronger claim is available, and it deserves stating because it would reorganise the ladder rather than adjust it. Grieve argues that stylometry works because authors write in subtly different registers, not in subtly different dialects: running a multidimensional register analysis and a stylometric analysis in parallel on two newspaper columnists recovers not merely two successful classifications but the same underlying dimensions of variation 134. On that account the authorial signal is not a stratum lying beneath register but a fine-grained instance of it, which would explain both why function words work at all and why matching the situation is the single intervention that most improves accuracy.

The systemic-functional account is compatible with this and supplies a mechanism: if an idiolect is an aggregate of habitual choices within the option networks a language provides, individual and situational variation are the same kind of thing observed at different grains 135 44. The practical consequence is identical either way, which is why the ladder survives the disagreement. Whether register is the author's medium or the author's confound, it has to be held constant before the measurement means anything about a person.

The confound ladder

These sources can be ordered by how routinely they must be controlled before a feature reads as authorial. The ordering used on the plates runs from the surface downward: topic first, because it is the largest and the easiest to vary accidentally; then genre, audience and formality; then medium and editing; then period; then community, dialect and education; with the individual at the bottom. A plate's rung is the shallowest depth at which the feature can be read as evidence about a person, given that everything above it has been held constant.

The rung is an assignment from the cited literature, not a measurement, and the rule that keeps it from being arbitrary is stated here: a plate's rung counts how many sources, taken in that order from the surface, have to be held constant before published comparisons show the feature discriminating authors better than it discriminates those sources. A feature nobody has tested against a given source is placed conservatively, below it. Note what the ladder does and does not measure: it is about false authorial readings, not about availability. A feature an editor deletes fails by going missing, which costs recall and not validity, and it is recorded on the plate rather than in the rung.

The distribution is the point. One family of eighteen reads as authorial without controls, and that one is the least quantifiable of the set: individual spelling and error habits, whose evidential weight depends on a population base rate that has never been established 100 93. Nine of eighteen cannot be read as authorial until at least the genre and the medium have been matched, and four carry no authorial reading in isolation at any depth. The centre of mass sits at the rung where topic and genre have both been controlled, which is a fair summary of what a competent analysis actually does before it reports anything.

5From counts to a claim

Between a table of frequencies and a statement about a person sit four decisions, each of which changes the answer and none of which is fixed by the data. They are worth separating, because published work routinely reports the first and leaves the other three implicit.

Scaling: what counts as a large difference

Raw relative frequencies cannot be compared across features, because the varies by whole percentage points between texts while whilst varies by hundredths of one. Some transformation must equalise them before they can be summed, and the transformation is a claim about what an equally important difference looks like. Burrows's z-scoring says that a difference matters in proportion to how unusual it is within the reference corpus, which makes every feature an equal voter and makes the reference corpus part of the measurement 33 34. Change the reference corpus and the same two texts move apart or together without either text changing. Vector-length normalisation goes further, suppressing the few features whose deviations are extreme, and Evert and colleagues showed that this suppression, rather than any property of the cosine, is what makes cosine Delta the better performer 35 36 38. Experiment 7 in the next section is a small instance of the general result: on a fixed feature matrix, the choice among three standard distances moves a quarter of the nearest-neighbour assignments.

Distance, classifier, likelihood ratio

Three families of method turn scaled features into an answer, and they answer different questions. A distance method ranks candidates by similarity and returns the closest; it is cheap, interpretable, and says nothing about whether the closest candidate is close enough 33 39. A classifier learns a decision boundary from labelled samples of each candidate; it is more accurate where training text is plentiful, and it inherits every regularity in the training material, which in practice means it can learn topic and report it as authorship 58 57 109. A likelihood ratio asks how much more probable the observed similarity is if the two texts share an author than if they do not, which is the form forensic science and the courts actually require 136 137.

The third framing is the most demanding because it makes the missing quantity explicit. The denominator, how probable this degree of similarity is between texts by different people, cannot be assumed; it has to be estimated from a reference population resembling the case, and assembling that population is usually the hardest part of a real analysis 14 100. Distance and classifier methods do not remove the requirement. They conceal it.

Thresholds and the verification problem

Closed-set attribution needs no threshold: it names the nearest of a fixed list, and it will name someone even when the true author is absent. Verification does need one, and a threshold is only as good as the distribution of different-author distances it was set against 138. Techniques exist that avoid setting one directly. Unmasking measures how quickly the separability of two texts collapses as its strongest features are removed, on the reasoning that same-author differences rest on a few surface features while different-author differences are distributed across many 139. But this replaces one calibration problem with another, and shared-task evaluation is what has made the difficulty comparable across systems rather than a matter of assertion 122 56.

Verification has accordingly become the field's central task rather than a variant of it, and the approaches that do well are the ones that model the difference between same-author and different-author distances explicitly instead of assuming a cut-off, whether by intrinsic profiling, cross-genre calibration or a learned similarity function with an attention mechanism that can be inspected afterwards 140 141 142. None of this dissolves the hardest version. Koppel and colleagues call it the fundamental problem: two short documents, no candidate list, no reference population, and a question that still has to be answered yes or no 143.

What the evaluation metric hides

Accuracy on a balanced closed set is the field's default number and it is close to uninformative on its own. It depends on the number of candidates, on how much training text each has, on whether the corpus happens to be sampled so that authors differ in topic, and on how long the samples are. Experiment 4 in the next section is a compact demonstration: a headline drafter-recovery figure decomposes, once the register confound is separated out, into perfect performance on the documents whose answer the register gave away and a materially lower figure on the documents that actually posed the question. Benchmarks built specifically to remove such shortcuts report lower and more honest numbers 144 63, and the underlying hazard, a metric that rewards a model for exploiting a regularity the analyst did not intend to test, is neither new nor confined to linguistics 145 93.

6The stylome

The term names a hypothesis. Van Halteren and colleagues proposed that each writer possesses a stylome, a collection of measurable characteristics sufficient to distinguish that writer from others, and tested it on a corpus of student authors rather than the established authors on whom earlier work had relied 48. The result was affirmative and heavily qualified: the authors were separable with high confidence, but the number of features required was an order of magnitude larger than had been assumed, and syntactic features contributed less than lexical ones. The qualification is the useful part. A stylome, on this evidence, is not a compact identifier but a diffuse one.

The hypothesis was tested by the same group in two further designs, once with syntactically annotated data and once with a controlled experiment in which students wrote to a common brief, expressly to remove topic as an explanation for the separability 42 146. Same task, same topic, same register, different people is the closest the field has come to isolating an authorial effect, and it is why the stylome claim carries weight in spite of its qualifications.

Why the fingerprint analogy fails

The analogy to a fingerprint is made constantly and is wrong in three specific ways, each of which corresponds to a property a biometric identifier must have.

Three properties a fingerprint has and a stylome does not

Uniqueness with a known base rate. Fingerprint comparison rests, at least in principle, on population statistics for ridge features. For linguistic features there is no comparable database: the frequency of a given spelling or construction in the relevant population of possible authors is essentially never known, so the likelihood ratio a comparison should deliver cannot be computed from first principles 100 136. Score-based likelihood-ratio frameworks address the form of the inference, but they need a reference population to calibrate against, and assembling one for a given case is the hard part 137.

Permanence. Ridge patterns are fixed in utero. Style drifts across a career 79 80, moves with the situation 44, and can be changed at will 52.

Invariance to acquisition conditions. A print is the same print whether lifted from glass or from paper. A text's measured style depends on its genre, its length, its medium and whether an editor saw it, and those dependencies are frequently larger than the between-author differences the measurement is supposed to detect 103 67.

What survives the analogy's collapse is weaker and still useful. There is good evidence that people differ systematically and measurably in their linguistic habits; that the differences are stable enough over comparable material to support inference; and that the inference is reliable in proportion to how much of the situation is held constant. That is a description of a scientific instrument with a restricted operating range, not of an identifier.

Individuality without uniqueness

The theoretical literature has largely stopped asserting uniqueness and started asking what would make individuality expectable. The systemic-functional account holds that an idiolect is the aggregate of a person's habitual choices within the option networks the language provides, so that individuality is a consequence of a unique history of use rather than an axiom 135 133. The resource-constraint account goes further, treating linguistic identity as a property of the interaction between a person and a situation rather than of the person alone, which makes situational variability a prediction of the theory rather than an embarrassment to it 102 14. Corpus work on individual usage supports the general claim while showing how much data is needed to see it 147 148.

Usage-based theories of grammar reach the same place from a different direction. If grammatical knowledge is assembled from a person's accumulated experience of language rather than instantiated from a template shared across speakers, then individual differences in grammatical knowledge are expected rather than anomalous 149 150, and they should surface as systematically different preferences among competing constructions. Careful historical work on a single alternation finds exactly that, and finds it stable enough within writers to be used 151.

How much of a stylome is visible at a time

If a stylome is diffuse, the operative question is not whether it exists but how much of it a given document exposes. The answer is unfavourable. Van Halteren and colleagues needed a number of traits an order of magnitude larger than the field had assumed to separate ordinary writers 48, and the traits available in a document scale with its length in the same square-root fashion that vocabulary does, so a short text does not contain a small clean sample of a person's habits but a heavily truncated one 67 28. Experiment 4 in the next section puts a blunt number on the consequence: at a few hundred words per sample, a text's own continuation is frequently not its nearest neighbour. Any claim about a stylome that does not state the length of the material it was estimated from has left out the term that dominates the result.

The verification problem

Closed-set attribution — choose which of these five people wrote it — flatters a method, because the answer is guaranteed to be present and errors are relative. The realistic problems are open-set attribution, where the true author may be absent, and verification, where two texts must be judged same- or different-author with no candidate list at all 68 138. Verification is harder in a way that matters for the stylome hypothesis: it requires a threshold, and a threshold requires knowing the distribution of distances between different-author pairs in the relevant population, which is the base-rate problem again. The unmasking technique sidesteps it by measuring how fast the distinction between two texts degrades as its strongest features are removed, on the reasoning that same-author differences collapse quickly and different-author differences do not 139. Shared-task evaluations have made the difficulty measurable rather than anecdotal 152 122.

Profiling: the other thing a stylome would license

If measurable habits index a person, they should also index that person's attributes, and a substantial literature infers gender, age, education and native language from text 153 154 155 124. The results are real and the interpretation is delicate. Reported accuracies rest on the correlation between an attribute and the topics, genres and situations typical of people who have it, so a gender classifier trained on blogs may be in substantial part a topic classifier, and the systemic-functional analyses that motivate the feature choices do not by themselves establish otherwise 156. Profiling is the same measurement as attribution with a weaker ground truth and a stronger temptation, and its errors fall unevenly on writers whose language does not resemble the training population 157.

What the case studies establish

An individual attribution is not evidence about the method unless the answer is independently known. A few are. The Rowling case is the cleanest, because the question was put before the answer was public and the analysis was documented as a protocol rather than as a conclusion 158. Studies with external philological ground truth do the same work at greater scale: the tests of Caesar's disputed continuations, and of the twelfth-century collaboration between Hildegard of Bingen and her secretary, are informative because the historical record constrains what a correct answer would look like 159 125. Where no external ground truth exists, as in the analysis of the Jack the Ripper correspondence, a stylometric result can establish relations among documents without establishing who wrote them 160 — which is, on this article's argument, the honest limit of what the instruments deliver.

7Seven measurements

The experiments below were run at build time with the Python standard library and no network access, on text already present on the machine: the free-software licence collection, the JDK command manuals, and docstrings from the Python standard library. Three registers, three drafting bodies, and a known ground truth for both. The full transcript is reproduced at the end of this section, and every number quoted here comes from it.

Vocabulary richness measures sample size

The type-token ratio and its relatives are the oldest stylistic measures and the least usable. Measured on a single body's output in a single register, the type-token ratio falls by 76.5% between a 1,000-token sample and a 24,000-token sample of the same corpus, and the hapax proportion falls by 43.7%, while nothing about the writing has changed. Yule's K, which was designed to be independent of sample size, moves by 3.5% over the same range and is the only one of the three that is comparable across unequal samples 90 91. Vocabulary growth on the same corpus follows Heaps's law closely, with an exponent of 0.5038 and an R² of 0.9832: doubling the sample multiplies the vocabulary by 1.418, not by two 161 92 28.

The practical consequence is a rule the field adopted long ago and which is still violated: samples must be equalised before richness measures are compared, and if they cannot be equalised, the measure should not be used 162.

What the nearest neighbour actually recovers

Across all 25 documents, Burrows's Delta on the hundred most frequent words assigns every document a nearest neighbour from its own register: 100.0% correct. It also assigns 96.0% of documents a nearest neighbour from the same drafting body, which looks like an authorship result. It is not. For 12 of the 25 documents the register determines the drafter outright — every manual is Oracle's, every docstring is CPython's — so a correct register guess scores a correct drafter guess for free. On the 13 documents where register and drafter can disagree, the drafter is recovered 12/13, or 92.3%; the headline figure is that number diluted with 12 free passes.

The mean distances make the ordering explicit. Pairs from the same document family average 0.3769; pairs from the same drafter in different families average 0.7066; pairs from the same register by different drafters average 0.7164; pairs from different registers average 1.1231. The second and third figures are the ones to look at. Once you leave the document family, being written by the same organisation is worth almost nothing next to being written for the same purpose — the two means differ by about a per cent, while crossing a register costs more than half as much again.

A second test on the same data measures stability rather than similarity. Each document long enough to survive it was split in half, and each half was matched against every other half. A half's own other half — same author, same register, same topic, same document, written continuously — is its nearest neighbour only 15/40 times, 37.5%. This is the sampling problem in its rawest form: at these lengths, the instrument frequently cannot recognise a text as a continuation of itself 67.

A register effect need not be large to be fatal — but it must be uneven

The simulation sweeps the size of a register effect against a fixed author effect in a closed-set task with 5 candidates and 4 known texts each. Two conditions: all known and questioned texts in the same register, and each text drawing its register at random from three. In the matched condition accuracy never falls below 100.0% anywhere in the sweep, however large the register effect becomes, because an offset shared by every candidate cancels out of the comparison. In the mixed condition accuracy falls from 100.0% to 35.6% against a 20.0% chance floor.

This is the precise statement of why cross-domain attribution is the hard case and same-domain evaluation is misleading 121 144. A large confound that is common to the whole comparison is harmless. A modest confound that varies between candidates is not. It also explains why the standard advice — match the genre of your known documents to the questioned document — is not fussiness but the main thing keeping the method usable 11 14.

Perfect separation costs nothing to find

With two candidate authors and 5 texts each, the probability that a single feature carrying no authorial information at all nonetheless separates the two groups perfectly is 0.007937. Search 87 such features and the chance of finding at least one perfect separator is even money; search five hundred and it is 98.1%. Modern feature sets contain thousands.

This is why a marker discovered by searching a feature space is not evidence until it has been validated on data that took no part in the search, and why the field's older literature — in which a striking discriminator is found and then reported — is largely uninterpretable 11. The statistical machinery for this failure is well documented outside linguistics 163 164 145, and the cusum controversy of the early 1990s is the field's own worst case: a technique accepted in British courts before it had been tested, and dismantled by several independent replications within four years 165 166 89.

The distance is part of the answer

Holding the corpus and the feature matrix fixed and varying only the distance function changes the nearest neighbour for 24.0% of documents between Manhattan Delta and cosine Delta, 12.0% between Manhattan and Euclidean, and 20.0% between cosine and Euclidean. On these documents all three recover the register perfectly and they disagree about the drafter. The metric is not a detail of implementation; it is a hypothesis about how stylistic deviations combine, and it should be reported and justified like one 35 34.

None of these seven results is new. They are reproductions, on small and slightly unusual data, of things the literature already knows: that richness measures track length 91, that domain dominates 116, that short samples fail 67, that search inflates significance 11, and that Delta variants disagree 35. That they reproduce so easily, on twenty-five documents and two hundred lines of standard-library Python, is the point worth taking away.

From that, a short list of requirements that a characterisation of writing style has to meet before it supports a claim about a person. None of them is novel; all of them are routinely skipped.

Six requirements

State the population. A distance is meaningless without the set of alternatives it was measured against. Open-set and verification framings force the question that closed-set framings hide 68 138.

Match the situation. Known and questioned documents should agree in genre, medium, formality and approximate date, and where they cannot, the mismatch is a stated limitation with a measured cost, not a footnote 121 83.

Report the sample sizes and the length dependence. With the length at which the chosen method has been shown to work, and the evidence for that figure 67.

Fix the feature set and the metric in advance. Or else validate on held-out data and say so, because a feature space searched for a discriminator will produce one 11.

Give an error rate under conditions resembling the case. This is the requirement Daubert actually imposes and the one that authorship analysis has most often failed 15 17 93. Shared tasks exist for exactly this purpose 152 122.

Express the conclusion as a strength of evidence, not an identification. The likelihood-ratio framing makes the missing quantity — the base rate in the relevant population — visible instead of letting it be assumed 136 137 14.

Protocols meeting most of these conditions exist and have been published with validation data attached 167 14. What is missing is not method but obligation. Nothing compels an analyst to adopt one, the incentives in scholarship and in litigation both run the other way, and the gap between what the field knows about its own reliability and what gets asserted in a report remains the discipline's central professional problem 168 169.

There is also a prior question the requirements do not reach. Craig asked it early and it has never been answered comfortably: if you can tell two authors apart, have you learned anything about them 170? A Delta score is not an account of a style. It is a statement that two distributions differ, with no commitment as to how. Characterising a style in the older sense — saying what a writer does, in terms a reader could recognise and an editor could apply — is a separate and largely unautomated task, and the interpretable feature sets that would support it are the ones with the weakest discriminating power 99 51.

Where the field is going

Two developments are changing what these requirements mean in practice. The first is representation learning. Models trained on very large collections of documents with author labels produce embeddings that place same-author texts together, and they outperform frequency methods on the benchmarks 110 112 113; fine-tuned per-author language models, scored by perplexity, do better still, and their success on content words rather than function words is a direct challenge to the field's oldest working assumption 86. The cost is interpretability and the risk is the old one in a new place: a learned representation can encode topic and register just as thoroughly as a hand-built feature set, and has to be tested for it explicitly 111 63.

The second is machine-generated and machine-edited text. Detection of model-generated text is a stylometric problem with an adversary 171, and the results so far are discouraging in a familiar way: detectors that work in-distribution degrade under paraphrase 131 132, carry demographic bias against non-native writers 157, and are being supplemented by watermarking schemes that place the signal in the generator rather than trying to recover it from the text 172 173. Meanwhile the ordinary case is no longer a text with one author but a text with a writer, a model and an editor in some unknown proportion 174.

That last change deserves stating plainly, because it alters what the object of measurement is. For a century the field's implicit model was one text, one writer, one style, with editing treated as noise. The realistic model now is a pipeline: a person drafts, a model rewrites, a person revises, a platform normalises. Each stage leaves traces at different linguistic levels, and the levels are exactly the ones the plates in section 3 separate. Whether that is an opportunity or the end of authorship attribution as ordinarily understood is not yet decidable from the published evidence, and the honest position is that the instruments were calibrated on a text-production process that is no longer the common one 171 129.

The right conclusion is not that stylometry does not work. It works, within a range that a century of results has mapped fairly precisely: enough text, matched situations, a stated population, a validated feature set, and a conclusion reported as a strength of evidence. Nearly every documented failure of the method — the cusum affair, the winnowed Shakespeare claimants, the disputed attributions that later collapsed — happened outside that range and was presented as though it were inside it 87 175 176. The question in the subtitle is not rhetorical. It is the whole method: before a number is allowed to be about a person, everything else that could have moved it has to be accounted for, and the accounting is the work.
