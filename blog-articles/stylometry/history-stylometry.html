The question

one instruction, repeated for a hundred and seventy years

On 18 August 1851 Augustus De Morgan wrote to a country clergyman with an experiment he had no intention of performing himself. Run your eye over the Greek of the Pauline epistles, he suggested, then over the Epistle to the Hebrews, and pay no attention at all to the meaning; then ask whether the second does not deal in longer words than the first. 1 That instruction — pay no attention at all to the meaning — is the founding move of the discipline later called stylometry, and it is stranger than it looks. It asks a reader to become a bad reader on purpose, to discard everything a text is for, in order to reach something the writer could not help.

Historians of the field have taken that date as its birthday. 2,3 The date is convenient rather than correct. Leon Battista Alberti had compared the proportion of vowels in Latin poets and orators four centuries earlier, in a treatise on ciphers, and had thought to mention the size of the page he counted. 4,5 Al-Kindi had tabulated letter frequencies six centuries before that. 6,7 What De Morgan added was not counting. It was the proposal that a count could settle a dispute about a person.

Everything since is a sequence of answers to a single question, asked over and over of different features. This article is organised around it.

What did they count, and why did they believe the author could not help it?

Put formally, a stylometric feature has to satisfy four conditions before it can carry any weight, and a fifth that most of the field's failures come from leaving unstated. It must be countable, so that two analysts tallying the same text agree. It must be stable within one writer, so that two samples of the same hand look alike. It must vary between writers, so that two hands look different. It must be beyond deliberate control, or a forger simply matches it. And it must be insensitive to everything else — to subject, genre, date, editor, compositor, translator, and the writer's own age — because every one of those also moves counts, and usually moves them further than authorship does.

The first three conditions are ordinary measurement problems and the field has been good at them. The fourth is the interesting one, because it is not a fact about text at all. It is a claim about a writer's attention. And the fifth is where nearly every method in this history has actually broken.

The control ladder

Read the history through the fourth condition and it acquires a direction. Each generation, finding its predecessor's feature contaminated, reached for a feature further beneath the writer's notice. Word length gave way to function words, function words to character sequences, character sequences to learned vectors with no human description, and learned vectors to a watermark that no writer produced at all. The article scores every method on that descent, which it calls the control ladder.

The ladder is not a quality scale. A feature low on it is not better evidence; it is harder to fake and harder to explain, and those two properties arrive together and never separate. That trade is the substance of the last seventy years of the subject and of most of its courtroom trouble.

The second axis: what a decision costs

The ladder alone would make the history look like progress. It is not, because every feature also has a price, and the price is text. Rates estimated from a sample carry sampling error, and the amount of known-authorship text needed to resolve a difference is set by the size of the difference relative to the rate. That quantity — words of known text per decision — is the second axis of every plate below, and the two together are the article's gauge.

The gauge on every plate. The horizontal axis is the control ladder, C5 at the left to C0 at the right, warm to cold. The burden figures are not decoration. Empirical work puts the minimum viable sample for a standard method at roughly 2,500 words of Latin prose and 5,000 of a modern novel, 8 and the disputed texts that motivated the whole enterprise — the Epistle to the Hebrews, a contested confession, a single anonymous letter — are frequently an order of magnitude shorter. 9,10 A method that needs more text than the question supplies is not a method; it is a wish.

02

Before the count

attribution as philology

Attribution is older than measurement, and the pre-quantitative methods were not primitive versions of the later ones. They were a different kind of argument. Lorenzo Valla did not weigh the Donation of Constantine against a distribution; he showed that its Latin used words and named institutions that did not exist in the fourth century, and that was the end of it. 11 Richard Bentley dated the Epistles of Phalaris six hundred years too late by the same move, on coins, dialects and towns that had not been founded. 12 An anachronism is a proof by contradiction: one instance suffices, and no second instance strengthens it.

That is why these arguments sit at the warm end of the ladder. The evidence is content the writer chose, on purpose, in full view. It works because a forger cannot know which of his own words are recent — a limitation of knowledge, not of attention. And it fails precisely where the later history begins: when the candidates are contemporaries. Two writers of one generation share their anachronisms.

The tradition these plates stand for is long, technically sophisticated, and unusually self-aware about its own evidence. Alexandrian editors marked Homeric lines they judged spurious with a marginal obelus, and the grounds they gave were internal: a usage that did not occur elsewhere, a scene that contradicted another, a metrical habit out of place. Friedrich August Wolf gathered those observations in 1795 into the argument that the poems as transmitted were not the work of a single man at a single time, and did it without a single count.13 In 1753 the physician Jean Astruc noticed that Genesis alternates between two names for God and used that alternation to separate the text into parallel documents.14 That is an argument from the distribution of a feature, made a century before De Morgan, by someone who tabulated occurrences without ever computing a rate.

Richard Bentley is the limiting case in both directions. His 1699 dissertation on the letters attributed to Phalaris demolished them on grounds that are still the model of the form — the dialect is wrong for the date, the cities named were not yet founded, the coinage referred to did not yet exist — and it is one of the great pieces of reasoning in the history of scholarship. His 1732 edition of Paradise Lost, which emended hundreds of lines on the theory that a dishonest amanuensis had corrupted a blind poet's dictation, applied the same confidence to a text that needed none of it, and destroyed his reputation for judgement.15 The technique had no way to tell the two cases apart. Nothing in the method said when to stop.

The bridge between philology and counting was built in Shakespeare studies, and it was built out of verse. Edmond Malone removed the three parts of Henry VI from the canon in 1787 on the frequency of rhyme and of end-stopped lines; 16 James Spedding divided Henry VIII between Shakespeare and Fletcher in 1850 by counting feminine endings, 17 a year before De Morgan's letter and with no knowledge of it. The New Shakspere Society, founded by Furnivall in 1873, made this the programme of a whole scholarly institution and published tables of verse ratios in its Transactions. 18,19

Harold Love's survey sets out the taxonomy this article inherits: external evidence, meaning documentary testimony about who wrote a thing, and internal evidence, meaning anything the text itself is taken to disclose.20 Counting lives entirely inside the second category, and it entered that category with a specific promise — that it could supply internal evidence which did not depend on the analyst's taste. The promise was worth making because the pre-quantitative internal argument had no defence against a sufficiently determined reader. Every Baconian who found a cipher in Shakespeare was doing internal evidence.

Alberti is the plate that should unsettle the standard chronology most, and he is not an isolated eccentric: he was the period's most systematic student of the relation between form and rule, and cryptography was for him one application of that interest among several.21 His vowel-frequency comparison of Latin poets and orators sits four centuries before De Morgan's letter and is a real empirical claim about a distributional difference between two bodies of text. What he did not have was any way to ask how much text the claim required. A modern audit of the counts finds the assertion defensible in direction but establishes that demonstrating it at conventional power needs somewhere between forty and a hundred and twenty pages of seven hundred letters.4 Alberti was working from a fraction of that. He was right, and he could not have known he was.

These are counts, and they are compared as ratios, and they are also almost entirely uncalibrated. Nobody asks how large a difference in the feminine ending rate two samples of one poet would show by chance. The question would not have seemed well posed: sampling theory existed, but the idea that a play is a sample from an author would not become natural for another sixty years. 22 The verse tests were also, from the start, used to do two jobs at once — to separate hands and to date plays — without any way to establish which of the two a given ratio was reporting. That ambiguity has never entirely left the subject.

03

The hand count

when the feature became a distribution

Thomas Corwin Mendenhall was a physicist, and the analogy he reached for was spectroscopy. De Morgan had suggested a mean; Mendenhall proposed the whole distribution, a ‘word-spectrum’ or characteristic curve plotting how often words of each length occur. 23 He took the first thousand words of Oliver Twist and counted. The curves were strikingly stable within an author across blocks of text, and strikingly different between authors, and that pair of observations is the empirical foundation of everything that followed.

Fourteen years later a wealthy Bostonian paid him to settle the Bacon–Shakespeare question with it. Two women operating a counting machine tallied some four hundred thousand words of the plays and two hundred thousand of Bacon. 2,24 The result did not support the sponsor's theory. It matched Marlowe. Mendenhall published it anyway, which says something creditable about him and nothing whatever about the method.

Mendenhall was not working alone, and the 1880s produced a cluster of people who independently decided that the sentence, not the word, was the unit worth counting. L. A. Sherman at Nebraska measured average sentence length across four centuries of English prose and found a long, steady decline from the Elizabethans onward, which he treated as a fact about the language rather than about individuals.25 That framing is exactly Eddy's objection stated as a research programme: if the quantity is principally a property of period and genre, then whatever remains for the author is a residual, and nobody in the 1880s had a way to isolate a residual.

The English-language literature ran ahead of its own statistics for another thirty years. Louise Pound argued in 1896 that the first fragment of the Romaunt of the Rose was Chaucer's on the basis of rhyme practice and dialect forms, tabulating occurrences and comparing rates against the accepted canon.26 In Russia, Nikolai Morozov proposed in 1915 that every author has a ‘linguistic spectrum’ — the profile of function-word and particle rates, which he explicitly likened to spectral lines, and which he claimed identified a writer the way an emission spectrum identifies an element.27 The idea is Mosteller and Wallace's, forty-eight years early, and it went nowhere, partly because Morozov used it to argue that most of classical literature was a medieval forgery. A method's first application shapes its reception more than its logic does.

The objection arrived almost immediately. Within weeks of the 1887 paper, Henry T. Eddy wrote to Science to point out that the characteristic curve is controlled principally by the language a composition is written in, that word length may not be adequate to characterise a writer, and that several enumerations applied together would afford tests that no single one could. 28 By 1904 the criticism was fully developed: works agreeing in form of composition, though by different authors, yield curves in closer agreement than works of divergent form by one author. 29 Mendenhall replied in the same volume. 30 The dispute was never resolved by measurement, because the measurement that would resolve it was not available. It is available now.

The experiment builds two authors that differ by 0.16 letters of mean word length — a realistic authorial gap — and then puts one of them into a second genre. Word length separates the two authors reliably: 86.2% at a thousand tokens and 99.8% at five thousand. The confound is larger than the signal by an order of magnitude. In chi-square distance between true profiles, one author across two genres sits 12.9 times farther from himself than two authors sit from each other in one genre, and a nearest-profile rule assigns his second-genre writing to the other author 100% of the time.

This is worth being precise about, because it is the pattern that recurs for the next century. Mendenhall's curve is real. It is stable. It does differ between authors. It is simply not mostly about the author, and no amount of extra counting fixes that, because more text sharpens the estimate of a quantity that is measuring the wrong thing. Eddy's proposed remedy — use several counts at once — helps only if the counts are confounded in different directions, and genre moves nearly all surface counts the same way.

A second line ran through Plato and never touched the first. Lewis Campbell, in an Oxford edition of 1867 that went unread for two decades, ordered the dialogues by their rare words. 31 Wilhelm Dittenberger, knowing nothing of Campbell, did the same thing in 1881 with frequent particles and connective formulae. 32 Arthur Frederking followed, and already in 1882 was insisting that a single criterion licenses no chronological conclusion. 33 It was Wincenty Lutoslawski who joined the Scottish and German lines, coined a name for the result, and stated it as a law.

The name matters for the record, because the citation usually given is wrong. Principes de stylométrie is dated 1890 in a great many secondary sources; it was delivered in June 1897 and printed in 1898. 34 The term's first public appearance is the short 1897 note ‘On stylometry’ in the Classical Review, which Lutoslawski himself called the first public explanation of the method. 19,35 A year earlier he had presented the same work in Paris and called it simply the statistics of style. 36

Lutoslawski's law of stylistic affinity holds that of two works of the same author and the same size, that one is nearer in time to a third which shares with it the greater number of stylemes — provided their differing importance is taken into account, and provided enough of them have been observed. 37 Both provisos are doing enormous work. The importance weights are supplied by the analyst; the number sufficient is not specified. Cox and Brandwood would later put the Plato chronology on a proper inferential footing using clausula rhythms, 38 and the modern consensus ordering owes more to that line than to the five hundred stylemes. 39

Constantin Ritter completed the German side of the Plato work in 1888, testing the chronology on several hundred separate stylistic criteria at once — particles, formulae of assent, avoidance of hiatus — and treating agreement across independent criteria as the thing that carried the argument.40 That is the same logic Juola would formalise as a protocol a hundred and twenty-seven years later, and it is the strongest idea the nineteenth century contributed: no single count is trustworthy, and convergence between counts that could have disagreed is what evidence looks like.

George Udny Yule brought the discipline something it had never had: the concept of a sampling distribution. His 1939 paper on sentence length asks not whether two texts differ but whether the observed difference exceeds what random sampling would produce, 22 and his 1944 book on De Imitatione Christi makes the point structurally by treating an author's vocabulary as a population from which a work is drawn. 41

The casualty was vocabulary richness. Every measure then in use — the ratio of types to tokens above all — is a function of how much text you happen to have. Yule constructed a characteristic, later called K, defined so as not to be.

The instrument Yule needed had been available since 1900, when Pearson published the chi-square criterion for asking whether an observed distribution of counts could plausibly have arisen from a hypothesised one.42 It is the first general-purpose test that takes a table of frequencies as its input, and it is what converts a characteristic curve from a picture into a claim with a probability attached. Mendenhall had the curves and not the test; by the time the test was ordinary equipment, the curves had been abandoned. The discipline's habit of acquiring the right tool one generation after the question that needed it is one of the more consistent patterns in this history.

One unchanging generating process, sampled at seven lengths, gives a type–token ratio that falls from 0.754 to 0.298, a drop of 60.5% caused entirely by the length of the sample. Yule's K moves 7.2% across the same hundredfold span. The consequence is not subtle. Given an author who is genuinely 15% richer in the tail than a rival, but who survives in a 20,000-word text while the rival survives in 5,000, the type–token ratio names the richer author correctly in 0% of pairs and K in 100%.

Later work qualified this: K is not perfectly constant either, and no lexical-richness measure is entirely free of length effects. 43,44 The deeper reason is Zipf's. Word frequencies in any natural language follow a steep rank–frequency law, 45,46 with consequences that Mandelbrot, and then a long line of quantitative linguists, made precise. 47–49 The practical corollary for attribution is unforgiving: most of the vocabulary is too rare to estimate from any realistic sample, and the part that is estimable is dominated by exactly the words that carry no subject matter. That is not a limitation the field worked around. It is the reason function words won.

Sentence length, meanwhile, refused to die. W. C. Wake spent the 1950s establishing sentence-length distributions for Greek authors with the care Yule had brought to nouns, and concluded that the distributions were stable enough within an author to be diagnostic and different enough between authors to discriminate — a finding that Morton then took much further than Wake's own data supported.50 C. B. Williams, who had come to the problem from entomological sampling, wrote the summary of this whole line of work in 1970: word length, sentence length and vocabulary richness are genuine numerical properties of texts, they are all heavily contaminated by genre and by sample size, and the contamination is not a nuisance term to be minimised but the dominant effect to be modelled.51 That is the correct conclusion, reached seventy years late, and it arrived just as the field's attention moved elsewhere.

04

The statistical turn

a verdict becomes a posterior

Frederick Mosteller and David Wallace did not set out to solve the Federalist problem. They set out to see whether Bayesian methods could survive a large-scale data analysis, and picked a problem where the answer was independently believed to be known. 52,53 The result is the paper the field has never stopped citing, and the reason is not the verdict — historians had already made the case for Madison on documentary grounds 54 — but the protocol.

Three decisions in it were permanent. The first is the choice of feature. Because the topic written about heavily influences the rate at which a word is used, the filler words of the language — articles, prepositions, conjunctions, an, of, upon — have fairly stable rates where meaningful words like war and legislature do not. 52 The second is the model: word counts are not Poisson, and the negative binomial fits them because rates vary between documents even within one author. The third is the output. Not a verdict but log odds, with the prior stated and a sensitivity analysis over it.

The Bayesian machinery was not improvised. I. J. Good had spent the post-war decade working out how to estimate probabilities for events that occur rarely or not at all in a sample — the problem that dominates any analysis where the informative words are the uncommon ones — and Mosteller and Wallace's treatment of low-count markers descends directly from that work.55 They also inherited a live warning. Lindley had shown in 1957 that a Bayesian analysis and a classical significance test can be made to disagree arbitrarily on the same data, with the significance test rejecting a hypothesis the posterior overwhelmingly favours, and that the disagreement grows with sample size.56 For a discipline whose samples were about to grow by four orders of magnitude, that was the relevant caution, and the fact that the authors chose to present both analyses side by side rather than pick one is the reason the book still reads as honest. The second edition, twenty years on, reprints the original with the classical treatment expanded rather than quietly retired.57

It is worth seeing what a rare word buys, because the intuition runs the wrong way.

At the rates involved, separating two authors on the alone, at 95% confidence and 80% power, needs 31,235 words of each. On to it needs 390,085. On upon — seventeen times rarer than the — it needs 3,001, because the two candidates differ in it by a factor of fourteen rather than by a tenth. One rare marker is worth 5.45 times the four commonest words of English put together. The Monte Carlo check returns empirical power 0.801 against a nominal 0.800.

That is the arithmetic behind Mosteller and Wallace's marker set, and behind the discipline's long attachment to a handful of function words. It also shows why the method is fragile. Evidence concentrated in a few rare features is evidence that a single contaminated feature can move.

L4bayesian log-odds — see the lab

With twenty function words on 2,000-word documents, the median absolute log odds is 3.12 — about 1,300 to 1 — and the misclassification rate is 3.33%. Evidence accumulates in proportion to length, from 0.63 in log odds at 250 words to 13.83 at 8,000. Then one contextual word is admitted to the set by mistake, its rate driven by subject rather than by author. It shifts the log odds by 1.50 on average and flips 4.25% of verdicts to the wrong author, against a baseline error of 3.33%. Mosteller and Wallace's rule — admit only words whose rate the subject cannot move — is variance control, not literary taste.

Alvar Ellegård's study of the Junius letters, published the year before, went the other way: not thirty carefully chosen words but 458 expressions, each scored by a distinctiveness ratio against a control corpus of one and a half million words of contemporary English. 58,59 The control corpus is the innovation. Without it, a marker's rate in a suspect's writing means nothing, because there is no way to tell an authorial preference from a period commonplace. Ellegård's weakness is the mirror image of Mosteller and Wallace's strength: his markers were selected after reading the questioned text, which turns a large bag of weak evidence into a large bag of circular evidence. The modern statistical literature has returned to the same data with sparse high-dimensional methods and reached compatible conclusions. 60

The method's first decade of imitators shows how much of its discipline was personal rather than procedural. C. S. Brinegar applied word-length and function-word tests to ten letters signed ‘Quintus Curtius Snodgrass’ that had been attributed to Mark Twain, and concluded on distributional grounds that Twain did not write them — a clean negative result, of the kind attribution literature rarely publishes.61 C.-E. Särndal, writing in an applied statistics journal four years later, set out what a disputed-authorship decision actually requires: a stated loss function, a prior over candidates, and an explicit account of what the reference population is supposed to represent.62 By 1983 M. W. A. Smith was surveying a field that had accumulated a dozen incompatible techniques and no agreed way to compare them, and observing that most published applications validated a method on the very texts that motivated it.63 That criticism would be made again, in almost identical terms, in 1998, in 2007 and in 2013.

One strand went in a different direction entirely and asked what a word-frequency distribution implies about the words a writer never used. Sichel fitted a compound Poisson family to word-frequency data that handled the enormous mass of once-occurring words better than Zipf's law did;64 Efron and Thisted then used exactly that machinery to estimate how many words Shakespeare knew but did not happen to write down, getting a figure of roughly thirty-five thousand unused types.65 When a poem attributed to Shakespeare surfaced in 1985, they turned the estimate into a test — the new poem should contain rare and unused words at the rate the model predicts — and reported that it did, while being unusually explicit that the result was consistent with authorship rather than evidence for it.66 That distinction is the one most attribution papers fail to draw.

The cusum affair

Andrew Q. Morton's cumulative-sum technique is the discipline's cautionary tale, and it is worth telling properly because the failure was specific. 67–69 The method plots two cumulative sums for a text — sentence length, and the count of two- and three-letter words per sentence — rescales the second to the first's range, and looks for the place where the tracks separate. A separation is read as a second hand. No threshold is specified. The chart is the evidence.

It was accepted in British and Irish courts from 1990 and used in a series of criminal cases turning on disputed confessions and statements. 70 Between 1992 and 1994 five independent studies found it unreliable: Canter on confessions, 71 de Haan and Schils, 72 Hardcastle, 73,74 Hilton and Holmes, 75 and Sanford and colleagues on the underlying assumptions. 76 Holmes and Tweedie's review collected the evidence. 77 Attempts to put weighted cusums on a proper inferential footing showed what the original lacked. 78 It also failed a live demonstration on television. The technique is now cited as the reason the field built shared evaluation.

What was wrong with it is not that the two series are uninformative. It is that cumulative sums of two different quantities wander apart whether or not anything changed, and no gap size was ever attached to a false-positive rate.

L7the cusum chart — see the lab

Four thousand simulated texts with one author throughout, and four thousand with a genuine second hand for the last third, give a divergence statistic with an AUC of 0.651 — a real signal, and a weak one. The median one-author text separates by 0.267 of the chart's height; the median two-author text by 0.349. A gap of a quarter of the chart appears in 56.2% of texts with exactly one author. A threshold set to catch 80% of real insertions flags 65.5% of innocent texts.

The lesson generalises past cusum. A statistic that separates populations on average is not thereby a statistic that can decide a case, and a picture with no stated error rate cannot be cross-examined. That is the standard the 2009 US National Research Council report would later apply to feature-comparison forensics generally, 79 and that later reviews sharpened. 80

Forensic authorship analysis did not stop; it professionalised. Its theoretical basis is idiolect — the claim that each speaker's language is individual and leaves traces in any sample. 10,81 Its practical problem is the one Ellegård solved for Junius and courts routinely leave unsolved: the reference population. Without it, a rare phrase shared between a suspect and a ransom note has no calculable weight, and the prosecutor's fallacy — reading the probability of the evidence given the hypothesis as the probability of the hypothesis given the evidence — has nothing pushing against it. 82,83 Evaluative reporting guidance now asks for a likelihood ratio and a stated population; 84 the extent to which testimony meets that standard varies a great deal. 85,86

The professionalisation had a legal driver. Daubert in 1993 made federal trial judges responsible for assessing whether proffered scientific testimony rested on a testable method with a known error rate and general acceptance in its field,87 which is a standard cusum could not have survived and which stylometric testimony has had to be built to meet ever since. Carole Chaski argued through the 1990s and 2000s that most forensic authorship work failed the error-rate requirement outright, because the techniques had never been validated against a corpus of known writers under realistic conditions.88 The best-known American case ran the other way: the linguistic analysis that helped identify Theodore Kaczynski from the Unabomber manifesto was qualitative, based on distinctive phrasing and idiosyncratic usage, and was used to support a search warrant rather than offered as a statistical identification at trial.89 Donald Foster, whose attribution of Primary Colors to Joe Klein was the field's great public success, published a book-length account of his methods in 2000 and was subsequently obliged to retract a high-profile attribution;90 that sequence, celebrity followed by a retraction, is a recurring shape.

What the field has instead of a single method is a standard of practice. The reference handbook sets out the range of forensic linguistic evidence and the conditions under which each kind is admissible,91 and the working consensus it describes is narrow: authorship analysis is most defensible as verification between a small closed set of candidates, with a corpus of comparison writing from each, and it should report a strength of evidence rather than an identification.92 That is Mosteller and Wallace's position, reached again from the other end.

05

The machine count

the clerical bottleneck disappears

Roberto Busa began the Index Thomisticus in 1949 and finished in 1980: a concordance of roughly eleven million words of Aquinas, lemmatised, produced first on punched cards and then on tape. 93,94 Nothing in it is an attribution method. Its significance is that it removed the thing that had actually limited the field, which was never arithmetic but clerical labour — Mendenhall's two women and their counting machine, Yule's index cards, Ellegård's 458 hand-tallied expressions.

The Brown Corpus of 1967 added the other missing piece: a balanced million-word reference for written American English, sampled by genre with a stated design. 95 Ellegård had built his control corpus by hand for one problem. Now there was a standing one. The cost is a habit the field still has: the corpus becomes the population by default, and normal comes to mean whatever is in the corpus.

It is worth recording what the field looked like from inside at the moment the machines arrived. A 1996 survey of stylometry for a general scholarly readership could still present the subject as a set of competing statistics in search of a criterion, with no way to rank them.96 A decade later, Juola's monograph-length review had a field with hundreds of published feature sets, several dozen classifiers, and the beginnings of a shared evaluation culture — and its central complaint was that almost nothing had been compared under common conditions.97 Grieve then ran that comparison: thirty-nine different quantitative techniques, one corpus, one protocol, and a ranking at the end. Function-word and character-level measures came out on top, most of the elaborate composite measures did not beat them, and combining techniques beat any single one.98 The result is a good example of why evaluation infrastructure matters more than any individual idea in it: it retired more methods in one paper than a decade of argument had.

John Burrows made the decisive methodological move, and made it twice. In 1987 he applied principal component analysis to the frequencies of the very commonest words in Jane Austen and showed that the axes recovered characters, narrators and authors without any theory of style at all. 99 In 2002 he proposed Delta: take the most frequent words, convert each document's relative frequencies into z-scores against the corpus, and compare documents by the mean absolute difference of those z-scores. 100

Delta is not a probability. It is a distance, and it produces a ranking of candidates, and the ranking is only as good as the candidate set. It nonetheless became the field's default baseline, was tested and extended immediately, 101 given a geometric and probabilistic reading, 102 and reimplemented in tooling that made it standard practice 103 — and is still, in 2026, the thing a new method is expected to beat. 104,105

The experiment puts twenty authors, three known texts each, against 1,200-word test documents, and sweeps the number of most-frequent words used from five to a thousand under three different distances. Accuracy climbs from 28.3% on five features to 95.0% on five hundred — a factor of 3.4 — while the three distance functions never differ by more than 8.3 points at any feature count. The lever is the size of the feature set, not the metric laid over it. That is also the conclusion of the most careful re-examination of Delta in the literature, which found its performance to rest on properties of vector normalisation rather than on the intuition Burrows offered for it. 106

What the z-scoring does is redistribute the vote. Five features out of a thousand are 0.5% of the set; they supply 3.7% of an un-normalised L1 distance — 7.4 times their share — and 0.4% of a Delta distance, which is their share to a rounding error. Scaling is the step that stops probability mass from standing in for evidence.

The 1990s and 2000s then produced a feature explosion. Rudman counted more than a thousand proposed style markers and observed, correctly, that the field had no way of choosing among them. 107,108 Holmes surveyed the same ground with more patience. 109,110 What eventually disciplined it was not a theory but an evaluation regime: shared corpora, sealed test sets, and from 2011 an annual competition with containerised submissions, which has now taken more than 1,100 systems through its platform. 111,112 The cusum affair is the reason that infrastructure exists.

The literary application that has absorbed the most effort is the Shakespeare canon, and it is the best place to see what changes when a method becomes routine. Craig and Kinney's volume applies function-word and lexical-word tests to the disputed and collaborative plays with a discipline the earlier Shakespeare literature conspicuously lacked: a defined comparison set of contemporary dramatists, explicit sample sizes, and results reported as classification rates rather than verdicts.113 MacDonald Jackson's work on Arden of Faversham and A Lover's Complaint combined rare-word and collocation evidence with the same kind of controls.114 The New Oxford Shakespeare then took the unusual step of publishing its attribution reasoning as a separate companion volume, so that the evidence for each disputed ascription in the edition could be inspected and disputed independently of the text.115 Whatever one concludes about the individual attributions, the practice of shipping the argument with the edition is the right one, and it is a direct descendant of Mosteller and Wallace publishing their negative results alongside their positive ones.

06

The learned count

the feature stops being chosen by a scholar

The move below the word was made early and took twenty years to be believed. Kjell classified authors on letter-pair frequencies with a neural network in 1994; 116 Keselj and colleagues generalised to character n-gram profiles in 2003; 117 Stamatatos, who had been working on automatic style categorisation since 2000, 118 established the robustness of character n-grams as the field's most reliable single feature type. 119,120

The reason is mechanical rather than linguistic, and the lab makes it visible.

L6below the word — see the lab

With twenty authors and one 20,000-word reference text each, character 3-grams beat the top three hundred words by 22.6 points at 250 tokens of questioned text, by 15.3 at 500, and by nothing at all at 2,500, where both saturate. Two things are happening. A 3-gram profile takes one observation per character rather than one per word, so a 250-token sample is effectively a 1,500-observation sample; and a morphological habit shared across hundreds of distinct word types is a single dense feature at the sub-lexical level and hundreds of separate, mostly-zero features at the lexical one.

That is the whole case for going below the word, and it is a case about sample size, not about the soul of style. It comes with a bill. A character 4-gram is not a thing a writer has any representation of, which is what makes it hard to fake, and is also what makes an error impossible to explain to a jury. 121

The machine-learning turn also produced, for the first time, a literature about its own failure modes. Koppel, Schler and Argamon's survey states the problem structure that organises everything after it: closed-set attribution with plenty of training text is essentially solved, and everything hard in the field — open sets, very short texts, one known candidate and no negative examples, cross-genre and cross-topic transfer — is hard for reasons that adding classifiers does not address.122 Their unmasking technique is the most original response to that: instead of asking how well a classifier separates two texts, it asks how fast the separation degrades when the most useful features are removed one by one, on the theory that a genuine same-author pair collapses quickly and a different-author pair does not.123 It is a method whose output is a curve rather than a score, and it answers a question the scores cannot.

Two other lines follow from the same problem. The impostors method handles the one-candidate case by generating a crowd of plausible alternative authors from a background corpus and asking how often the questioned text prefers the candidate over a randomly chosen impostor,124 which converts an unanswerable absolute question into a relative one; later work pushed toward verification schemes that avoid picking an explicit threshold at all.125 Meanwhile the same feature machinery was pointed away from identity: Writeprints assembled thousands of lexical, syntactic, structural and content features for identity-level identification in online forums,126 and the blog corpus work showed that age and sex are predictable from function-word and part-of-speech rates at accuracies well above chance.127 That is the fourth condition doing what it always does: a feature outside the writer's control does not care which fact about the writer it leaks. By the time the field was surveyed again in 2017 the applications on offer included profiling, deception detection and forensic identification, and the technical core was the same for all of them.128

Two 2012 papers changed the stakes. Narayanan and colleagues showed that authorship identification degrades gracefully with the size of the candidate set, running 1,188 features against 100,000 blog authors and getting useful accuracy where the field had assumed the problem was hopeless. 129 Anonymity on the open internet, it turned out, is not a matter of removing a name.

Brennan, Afroz and Greenstadt supplied the counter-move in the same year, and it is the single most important negative result in the subject. Existing stylometry assumes that authors have not tried to disguise their style. When ordinary subjects — not writers, unfamiliar with stylometry, spending little time on it — were asked to obfuscate, recognition fell to the level of random guessing; when asked to imitate a target, they succeeded up to 67% of the time. 130,131 Later work automated the attack and made it transferable. 132,133 Author obfuscation has been a shared-task category ever since.

L8adversarial editing — see the lab

The lab prices the attack. Delta over the hundred commonest words, twenty candidates, 5,000-word texts, and an editor who touches nothing but function words, pushing each rate toward the corpus mean. At a 1% edit rate the true author is still ranked first 98.3% of the time. At 3%, 93.3%. At 5% it is 45.0%, and the mean rank of the true author has moved from 1.00 to 2.62. Five per cent of tokens in a 5,000-word text is about 250 substitutions, none of them visible to a reader.

The obfuscation literature grew up alongside the attribution literature and is best read as its error bar. The same group that built the imitation corpus also showed that deliberate stylistic deception is itself detectable — hoaxes and imitations leave characteristic traces, because writing unlike yourself is a task with its own signature.134 That result is genuinely two-edged. It means an adversary who obfuscates naively can be caught, and it means the arms race has the structure every arms race has, where each side's countermeasure becomes the other side's feature.

This is the fourth condition failing in the open. ‘Involuntary’ was never a claim about physiology. It was a claim about attention, and attention is exactly what a motivated writer can redirect. The features survive because nobody is watching them and stop surviving the moment somebody is. Every subsequent move down the ladder is, in part, an attempt to get below the level at which watching is possible.

The last human-designed feature set gave way around 2019. Instead of choosing features and fitting a classifier, train a representation directly on millions of author-labelled documents so that two texts by one writer land close together. 135,136 These representations verify authorship on texts far shorter than Delta can handle and transfer across platforms. They also learn topic when nobody stops them, which is why content-controlled training became a stated requirement rather than a refinement: a model that keys on subject matter scores beautifully on a benchmark and fails the moment the subject changes. 137,138 Large language models can now be prompted to perform the task directly, with accuracy that is respectable and explanations that are not evidence. 139

The case literature since 2013 is where the modern methods are actually judged. Juola's analysis of the Galbraith–Rowling attribution is the cleanest example, and he published it as a protocol rather than a result: state the candidate set in advance, use several independent measures, report where they disagree, and treat convergence across unrelated feature spaces as the evidence.140 The Elena Ferrante investigations put a dozen research groups on the same question with different methods and published the disagreements alongside the agreements,141 with Savoy's contribution notable for reporting the strength of the evidence for Domenico Starnone rather than announcing an identification.142 Cafiero and Camps addressed the long-running claim that Corneille ghost-wrote Molière and found the reverse of what the claim predicts, using features at several linguistic levels;143 they later turned the same apparatus on the QAnon posts and reported a small set of suspects with the caveats attached.144 The most striking positive result of the period is the attribution of a previously anonymous Latin text to Apuleius by verification methods that were developed for modern English and applied to second-century Latin without retuning.145 Related work has extended the same machinery to detecting style change within a single text.146

Alongside the cases, a corrective literature. Kestemont asked the question the field had avoided for fifty years — why function words in particular, and what linguistic theory, if any, justifies the choice — and found the usual justification to be post hoc: they work, they are topic-independent, and the theory was fitted afterward.147 Eder catalogued the ways corpus construction silently determines results: unbalanced sample lengths, editorial normalisation, modernised spelling, a single author's texts drawn from one edition and a rival's from another, each of which can manufacture or destroy a signal.148 The statistical apparatus for doing this properly was by then standard equipment in linguistics generally,149 which makes the persistence of the errors a matter of practice rather than of available theory.

07

The synthetic count

when the writer is a sampler

Everything above assumes a person on the other end of the text, with habits they did not choose. Generated text breaks that assumption at the root, and the field's response has been to run the same playbook one rung lower.

The features now are not linguistic at all. GLTR visualised per-token rank under a language model; 150 DetectGPT observed that generated passages sit near a local maximum of the model's log-probability surface, so that small perturbations lower the probability more sharply for machine text than for human text; 151 Fast-DetectGPT replaced the perturbation with a conditional-probability curvature that is far cheaper; 152 Binoculars compares two closely related models to normalise away the prompt's own difficulty. 153 All of these are stylometry in the strict sense — a countable property of the text, stable within a class, differing between classes — on a feature with no human description whatever.

They are also, all of them, up against three walls.

The evaluation infrastructure caught up quickly, and its findings are more sober than the product announcements. The 2024 shared task on machine-generated text detection ran human and machine texts through detectors under controlled conditions and reported what the leaderboards usually hide: performance is strongly dependent on the generator, on the domain, and on whether the machine text has been touched by a human afterward, and systems that top the table on one generator's output can fall to near chance on another's.154 A detector is not a measuring instrument for a property of text. It is a classifier trained against a specific set of generators, and it degrades the way classifiers degrade when the distribution moves.

L9machine-text detection — see the lab

The first wall is length, and it is the friendly one. A likelihood ratio on per-sentence surprisal reaches AUC 0.904 at ten sentences and 1.000 at fifty. The second is paraphrase. A rewriter that restores human-like variance takes the same detector at 100 sentences from 1.000 to 0.742 — roughly what three sentences of un-paraphrased text would have given it. That matches the published attacks: recursive paraphrasing drops one watermark detector from 97% to 15% at a 1% false-positive rate, 155 and paraphrase evades detectors generally unless the deployer retains a retrieval index of everything it has generated. 156

The third wall is arithmetic and cannot be climbed at all. At a 1% false-positive rate and 95% sensitivity — an operating point better than most deployed detectors achieve 157,158 — precision is 49.0% when one document in a hundred is machine-written and 83.3% when one in twenty is. In a cohort of thirty innocent documents, the probability that at least one is flagged is 26.0%; at two hundred, 86.6%. OpenAI withdrew its own classifier in July 2023 on the stated grounds of low accuracy. 159 Detectors have been documented to flag non-native English writers disproportionately, for a reason that follows directly from the feature: a writer who adheres closely to standard constructions produces low-surprisal text, which is the whole signal. 160

The honest response to a natural feature that will not hold is to stop looking for one. Watermarking biases the generator's own sampling under a secret key, so that the detector tests a hypothesis about the key rather than about the prose. 161,162 The false-positive rate is then exact and does not depend on the text; schemes exist with formal undetectability guarantees, 163 and at least one has been deployed and evaluated at production scale. 164 PAN's 2026 campaign runs watermark insertion and attack as a shared task in its own right, alongside detection in mixed and obfuscated authorship. 111

It is worth naming what has happened here. For a hundred and seventy years the discipline looked for a feature the writer could not help. It has now arrived at a feature the writer never produced: a signal installed by the party that owns the sampler, detectable only by that party, absent entirely if the weights are open or the text is rewritten. The ladder's bottom rung turns out not to be a very deep property of authorship. It is a property of infrastructure, and it belongs to whoever runs it. Regulation has moved the same way, requiring machine-readable marking of synthetic content rather than requiring anyone to detect it after the fact. 165,166

The adjacent question — which model wrote this — is ordinary closed-set attribution again, with the candidates being systems rather than people, and it works about as well as closed-set attribution ever does. 167,168 Models can be told apart by grammatical and rhetorical profile at the population level, 169,170 and they are conspicuously bad at recognising their own output. 171 The framing that has emerged distinguishes attribution from verification and argues the two are different problems that the literature has been running together. 172

The lab

nine experiments, and what they are not

Every number quoted above comes either from a cited source or from the program transcribed below. It is a single Python file, standard library only, seeded, and it runs in about ninety seconds. The full transcript follows verbatim.

A warning about what it is. Each experiment measures the behaviour of an estimator on synthetic text whose generating parameters are known by construction. No experiment here is evidence about any real author, and none is offered as such. That is deliberate: the questions this article asks — how much text does this decision need, what happens to it when genre shifts, what does one contaminated feature cost, how many edits defeat it — are questions about method, and on real corpora they cannot be answered because the truth is what is in dispute. Where a synthetic model makes an assumption that matters, the code states it and the prose repeats it: authorial rate offsets whose spread grows with word rank, because the rate of the commonest words varies least between writers; a morphological habit shared across word types, because that is why sub-lexical features work; a topic perturbation on the lexical tail, because that is the confound the whole history is about.

Where published empirical work bears on the same quantity, it is cited next to the lab result, and the two are kept distinct.

Two experiments were rebuilt after their first version produced results that were true of the simulation and misleading about the world. The first version of the characteristic-curves experiment separated its two authors by half a letter of mean word length, which is far larger than any real pair of English prose writers, and it made the method look better than it is; the rebuilt version uses 0.16 letters, which is the order of difference actually observed between authors within a genre. The first version of the Delta experiment asserted that z-scoring suppresses the commonest words, and the measurement did not support the assertion, so the claim in the text is now the decomposition the experiment actually produces: the top five of a thousand features take 3.7% of a raw distance and 0.4% of a Delta distance. Both rebuilds are recorded here rather than in a footnote because the first versions would have supported more confident sentences, and that is the direction in which this literature has historically erred.

Three things are visible in the field that are not visible in the narrative.

The first is that the descent is real but not monotonic. The oldest plate in the article, Alberti counting vowels in 1466, sits at C1 — lower than everything for the next four hundred and fifty years. Letter counting came first because it was easy, not because anyone had reasoned their way beneath the word; the ladder was climbed back down deliberately only after the C3 and C4 features had failed in public.

The second is the trade. Burden falls sharply as the ladder descends: the C3 and C4 methods want 104 to 105 words of known text per decision, while the C0 and C1 methods want 102 to 103. That is the reason the field moved, and it is a real gain. The same descent is a monotonic loss of explicability. Every method that can decide a case from three hundred words is a method whose evidence cannot be shown to the person it is being used against.

The third is the discontinuity at the bottom. Nineteen of the twenty plates count something a writer produced. The twentieth counts something installed in the sampler. It sits at the bottom of the ladder and at the lowest burden in the table, and it is not on the same scale as the rest at all — which the ledger records rather than hides.

The fourth thing is visible only as an absence. There is no column in the ledger for accuracy, because the accuracies reported in this literature are not comparable across rows — each is a property of a particular corpus, candidate set and text length, and the differences between those setups swamp the differences between methods. That is the single most reliable finding of the evaluation studies, and it is the reason the two axes here are cost and control rather than performance.

10

What the ladder shows

The history of stylometry is usually told as a story of increasing power: hand counts to punched cards to machine learning, each stage doing more with less. That story is true and it is not the interesting one. The interesting one is that a single unexamined premise has been passed down intact through every stage, and that the whole sequence of methodological changes has been a series of attempts to keep it true.

The premise is that somewhere in a text there is a quantity the writer does not govern. De Morgan put it in word length. Mendenhall put it in the shape of the whole length distribution, and Eddy pointed out within weeks that the shape belongs mostly to the language. Yule moved it to the rate of repetition and had to invent a sampling theory to do it. Mosteller and Wallace moved it to the filler words, and their central methodological rule — admit only words the subject cannot move — is a confession that the premise had already failed once for content words. Morton claimed the quantity was visible on a chart and it was not. Burrows moved it into a few hundred standardised frequencies. Stamatatos and Koppel moved it below the word. The neural representations moved it somewhere nobody can point to. And watermarking abandons the premise entirely: if no quantity in the text is beyond the writer's reach, install one that was never the writer's to begin with.

The experiments in this article say the same thing three times from three directions. Word length is stable, differs between authors, and is dominated by genre by a factor of thirteen. Function-word evidence is concentrated in a few rare markers and one contaminated feature moves 4.25% of verdicts. Delta over a hundred function words survives a 3% edit rate and collapses at 5%. In each case the feature is real. In each case the thing it is mostly measuring is not the author.

There is a structural reason the fourth condition kept failing quietly rather than loudly, and it is not specific to stylometry. An attribution study has a large number of undeclared choices in it — which words to count, how to segment, which candidates to admit, what counts as a comparable sample — and the analyst typically makes those choices while looking at the data, in pursuit of a hypothesis they already find plausible. That is enough to generate a confident result from noise without anyone doing anything they would recognise as improper.173 The base-rate argument compounds it: when a field tests many weakly-motivated hypotheses on small samples with flexible analysis, most of what it publishes as positive will be wrong,174 and a field where the interesting cases are by definition the contested ones is exactly such a field. The cusum affair looks in retrospect like a scandal about one man. It was closer to an ordinary outcome of a method with no pre-registered protocol, no error rate, and a courtroom willing to accept it.

What follows for practice

Three consequences seem to survive the whole history.

The reference population is the method. Ellegård understood this in 1962 and built a control corpus of one and a half million words for one attribution. 58 Everything that has gone wrong in court since has gone wrong at the same point: a shared rare phrase carries no weight until you know how often it is shared by people who are not the suspect, and a detector's 1% false-positive rate says nothing about a particular accusation until you know the base rate in the pile it was applied to. The most important number in the lab's final experiment is not the AUC. It is that precision at a 1% false-positive rate and 95% sensitivity is 49.0% when one document in a hundred is machine-written.

Report a likelihood ratio and a population, not a verdict. Mosteller and Wallace did this in 1963, including a sensitivity analysis over their prior. 52 Delta does not, and cannot, because a distance ranking has no probability attached; that is not a deficiency of Burrows's paper but a property of the object. 100,102 A ranking over a candidate set is a useful thing to compute and a dangerous thing to report, because the nearest candidate is nearest whether or not the true author is in the set. 175,176

Expect an adversary, and price the attack. The assumption that writers are not trying to hide was reasonable when the questions were about dead poets and is not reasonable about anything else. Obfuscation took untrained subjects to chance in 2012, 130 paraphrase takes machine-text detectors most of the way back to the coin-flip, 156 and the lab puts the cost of defeating a standard method at around 250 substitutions in five thousand words. A method's accuracy on a cooperative corpus is an upper bound with no lower bound attached.

The question, once more

De Morgan's instruction was to pay no attention to the meaning. A hundred and seventy-five years of work have shown what that costs. Attention is the only thing that makes a feature involuntary; the moment attention arrives, the feature is gone. Each generation has responded by moving to a feature the writer cannot attend to, which has meant moving to a feature nobody can interpret, which has meant that the discipline's evidence has become steadily more reliable and steadily less answerable.

The last rung is the honest end of that road. A watermark is a feature with no interpretive content at all and an exactly known error rate — everything a court could want, available only from the party that ran the model, and absent the moment anyone runs a different one. It is the correct answer to the question the field has been asking since 1851, and it works by changing the subject: not what did the writer fail to conceal, but what did the infrastructure record. Whether that counts as stylometry is a matter of definition. Whether it is what anyone was looking for is not.

Method. Twenty plates, each on a fixed six-field set and an assay chip giving control rung and burden. Nine seeded, standard-library-only experiments, executed at build time with their transcript embedded verbatim; every quantity in the prose and figures comes either from that transcript or from a cited source, and the text says which. All figures are generated programmatically as SVG. Figure numbers follow document order. References are Vancouver and were validated in both directions at build time.

Limits. The lab measures estimators on synthetic text with known generating parameters. It is not evidence about any real author, and its absolute accuracies are properties of the synthetic corpus, not of English. Burden figures on the plates are stated to the nearest decade; where they are declared rather than measured, the plate says so.
