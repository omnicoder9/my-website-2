Software Assurance

Evidence, argument, and the audit of trust

Software security is what you do. Software assurance is what you can show — to a regulator, a customer, a certification body, or a court — about what you did, on which artefact, on which date, and with what independence. This is a working account of that discipline: where it came from, what its frameworks actually require, which evidence is worth what, and the specific ways in which certificates come apart in the hands of people who mistake them for security.

01

The claim beneath the claim

CNSSI 4009 · NASA-STD-8739.8

The definition the United States government has used for two decades turns on its first four words. Software assurance, in the federal glossary, is the level of confidence that software behaves as intended and carries no vulnerabilities — deliberate or accidental — introduced at any point in its lifecycle1. Not the state of. Not the fact that. The level of confidence: a claim about the observer's epistemic position, not about the code.

That framing is not an accident of drafting. The same glossary carries a second, older definition inherited from NASA which drops confidence entirely and casts assurance as the planned, systematic activities that keep lifecycle processes and products conformant to requirements, standards and procedures1,2. Between those two entries sits the whole tension of the field. One defines assurance as a property of belief; the other as a property of process. In practice, the industry has settled on a bargain that satisfies neither definition cleanly: we perform systematic activities, we retain artefacts proving we performed them, and we treat the artefacts as a proxy for the confidence we cannot measure directly.

It is worth separating three things that get collapsed in conversation and never should be collapsed in an audit.

Security

A property of the running system. Largely unobservable. You never know it; you only ever fail to disprove it.

Verification

Activities that produce signals about that property. Reviews, scanners, fuzzers, proofs. Each has a coverage envelope, and each is silent outside it.

Assurance

A structured, evidenced argument that the signals justify a stated level of confidence, addressed to a specific audience with a specific decision to make.

The third of these is a communication problem as much as an engineering one. An assurance artefact exists because somebody who cannot inspect your code has to decide whether to run it on their network, put it in a patient, or fly it. Two practitioners have noted that the definition's two clauses have very different maturity. Establishing that software does what it is supposed to do is ordinary functional verification: a discipline decades old, well understood, and practised by every competent engineering team. Establishing that it contains no vulnerabilities, including ones placed there deliberately, is the part nobody has solved3.

Figure 1 states the structural fact that most compliance programmes discover the hard way. The layers are strictly dependent. An audit report is a statement about records; records are a statement about verification activity; verification activity is a statement about engineering practice. If the bottom layer is thin, every layer above it is a well-formed description of a thin thing. This is why we passed the audit and we are secure are different sentences that happen to be true or false independently.

02

Two lineages, still arguing

1970 → 2027

Software assurance has two ancestries that were never reconciled, and nearly every present-day dispute — should we certify the product or the producer? — is a re-run of their disagreement.

The evaluation lineage

The first begins with the 1970 Defense Science Board task force chaired by Willis Ware, whose report is remembered for stating the need for computer security but which mattered more for a subtler observation: that evaluating a system to determine whether it provided adequate security for a particular application was extraordinarily difficult4,5. The problem was posed as an evaluation problem from the very first document.

Two years later, the Air Force Electronic Systems Division commissioned James P. Anderson's planning study, which introduced the reference monitor: an abstraction that mediates every access of a subject to an object, and which must be tamper-proof, always invoked, and small enough to be subjected to analysis complete enough to be assured6,7. That last requirement is the birth of software assurance as an engineering constraint. Anderson did not merely say the mechanism must be correct; he said it must be small enough that its correctness can be demonstrated. Assurability became a design goal.

The Trusted Computer System Evaluation Criteria — the Orange Book — turned that into a graded scheme. Issued in interim form in 1983 and as DoD 5200.28-STD in December 1985, it defined hierarchical divisions D through A, with assurance requirements escalating from documented testing to verified design8. It anchored a whole shelf of colour-coded companion volumes, the Rainbow Series, which NIST now hosts explicitly as historical artefacts9. The Orange Book's structural insight survives everything that replaced it: security policy, accountability and assurance are separate axes, and you can have a strong policy with weak assurance that it is enforced.

Its weakness also survives. Product evaluation is slow, expensive, tied to a fixed configuration, and stale on arrival in a market that ships weekly. The European ITSEC and Canadian CTCPEC schemes fragmented the problem further, and the Common Criteria was the harmonisation effort — now maintained as CC:2022 and adopted as the five-part ISO/IEC 15408 series, with the evaluation methodology as ISO/IEC 1804510,11. Common Criteria gave the world its enduring vocabulary: the Target of Evaluation, the Protection Profile as an implementation-independent statement of need, the Security Target as the vendor's specific claim, and the Evaluation Assurance Levels EAL1 through EAL711.

The 2014 correction

The Common Criteria community made a diagnosis worth remembering. Evaluation Assurance Levels turned out to be widely misread as a security score, when they are a measure of evaluation depth. The 2014 revision of the Common Criteria Recognition Arrangement shifted emphasis away from EALs toward collaborative Protection Profiles written by international technical communities for specific product categories, and limited mutual recognition of bespoke Security Target evaluations to the equivalent of EAL212. In other words: the scheme concluded that a comparable, technology-specific claim beats an incomparable, deep-sounding number.

The safety lineage, which got there first

Running in parallel, civil aviation built the most disciplined assurance regime in existence. DO-178C, published in December 2011 as the revision of DO-178B13, assigns each software item a Design Assurance Level derived from the system safety assessment — Level A for failure conditions classified as catastrophic, down to Level E for no effect — and then specifies the number of objectives that must be satisfied at each level, and which of them must be satisfied with independence14,15. The counts are concrete: on the order of 71 objectives at Level A, 69 at B, 62 at C and 26 at D16,17. The guidance spans planning, development, verification, configuration management, quality assurance and certification liaison, with bidirectional traceability across the lifecycle as a first-class requirement17.

Two features of this regime are worth stealing wholesale. First, rigour is indexed to consequence rather than applied uniformly. Second, the deliverable is a defined set of lifecycle data, agreed in advance in the plans, which the certification authority reviews at staged points. There is no ambiguity at the end about what evidence was owed.

The producer lineage

The second ancestry starts inside industry rather than defence, and it evaluates the organisation. Its origin moment is Microsoft's January 2002 Trustworthy Computing initiative, which became mandatory internal policy as the Security Development Lifecycle in 200418,19. The 2004 ACSAC paper describing it reads as a template for everything since: threat models during design, static analysis during implementation, a focused security push with code review and penetration testing, and — crucially for assurance — a Final Security Review conducted by a team independent of the product group before release20. The independent gate is the assurance artefact; the rest is engineering.

By 2008–09 this had been abstracted into maturity models. OpenSAMM, led by Pravir Chandra, and the Building Security In Maturity Model, from Gary McGraw, Sammy Migues and Brian Chess, share a common ancestor and forked over exactly the question that divides the field: whether to be prescriptive (here is what you should do) or descriptive (here is what comparable firms are observed to do)21,22. The first BSIMM study interviewed nine firms; BSIMM16, published in January 2026, reports on 111 organisations across 128 activities in 12 practices and four domains23. Longitudinal analysis of that dataset has tracked the dispersal of security work out of central teams and into product teams as DevSecOps took hold24.

The collision

The lineages collided in December 2020. The compromise of the SolarWinds Orion build pipeline, distributed to roughly 18,000 customers through a signed, legitimately-issued update25, invalidated the premise underneath both traditions at once. Product evaluation assumes the evaluated artefact is the shipped artefact. Producer certification assumes the producer's process is the thing that determines what ships. A build system compromise breaks both assumptions simultaneously, because the code that was reviewed and the binary that was delivered were different objects and nothing in the assurance chain was designed to notice.

Everything in federal software policy since is downstream of that realisation.

03

The shape of an argument

ISO/IEC 15026-2 · GSN

Before surveying the frameworks, it is worth being precise about the object they are all trying to produce, because most organisations produce a degraded version of it without realising a better form exists.

The rigorous form is the assurance case. ISO/IEC 15026-2 fixes a minimum structure for it. There is a claim at the top, about some property of the system. There is reasoning — laid out, not implied — that works down through subordinate claims. At the bottom there is evidence. And running alongside all of it, the standard insists on something most compliance documents omit entirely: the assumptions the reasoning depends on, stated explicitly26. The standard is domain-neutral by design — safety cases, security cases and reliability cases are the same construct with different top claims26.

The usual graphical rendering is the Goal Structuring Notation, introduced by Tim Kelly at the University of York, which represents goals (claims), strategies (the inference between a goal and its subgoals), solutions (evidence), and the context, assumptions and justifications that bound them27. The Claims-Argument-Evidence framework is a close cousin, and both align with the Object Management Group's Structured Assurance Case Metamodel; the 2022 edition of ISO/IEC 15026-2 uses GSN directly28,29. All of them descend from Toulmin's model of argument: a claim supported by evidence, connected by a warrant that can itself be challenged28.

The reason to care about this in a commercial setting, where nobody will ever ask you for a formal assurance case, is that the assurance case makes explicit three things a control checklist structurally hides.

  1. The context in which the claim is meant to hold. Secure is not a property; resistant to a remote unauthenticated adversary with no physical access, given the deployment assumptions in section 2 is. Almost every disputed breach post-mortem turns out to be a disagreement about context that nobody wrote down.
  2. The warrant. A checklist asserts that a control exists. An argument asserts that the control, given this evidence, makes the claim above it more likely to be true. The second is attackable, which is the point. Research on assurance-case quality has focused precisely on separating the safety argument from a confidence argument about why the first argument should be believed27.
  3. The assumptions. Evidence rests on assumptions — that the compiler is honest, that the build host was clean, that the test corpus resembles production traffic. Written down, these become a threat list. Unwritten, they become the incident report.

Empirical work on real assurance cases has found that arguments routinely need to cover process characteristics as well as product ones — who did the work, with what independence, under what configuration control — in order to be convincing to a regulator30. That is precisely the evidence that modern audit regimes demand, arrived at from a different direction.

04

Frameworks: describing the work

SP 800-218 · SAMM · BSIMM

Frameworks in this space divide cleanly by what they are for. A framework tells you what outcomes to achieve. A maturity model tells you how far along you are. A certification scheme tells you what a third party will check. Confusing the three is the most common structural error in compliance programmes, and it is expensive: teams buy a maturity assessment and present it to a customer who wanted an attestation.

The Secure Software Development Framework

NIST SP 800-218 is now the centre of gravity in Western software assurance, largely because policy made it so. Published as version 1.1 in February 2022, it deliberately provides outcome-based practices that can be integrated into any lifecycle model rather than prescribing a methodology31,32. It organises those outcomes into four groups — Prepare the Organisation, Protect the Software, Produce Well-Secured Software, Respond to Vulnerabilities — each decomposed into practices, tasks, notional implementation examples and references to established bodies of work31.

Two properties make the SSDF unusually well-suited to being an assurance backbone. First, because it names outcomes rather than tools, an attestation against it is a claim you must be able to evidence in your own terms — which is harder than buying a product but survives tooling changes. Second, it was written from the outset with acquisition in mind: the framework provides a common vocabulary so that purchasers can express requirements to suppliers31. That is the function of an assurance standard, as distinct from a security standard.

The framework is mid-revision. Executive Order 14306 directed NIST to publish a preliminary update, and the initial public draft of SP 800-218r1 — SSDF version 1.2 — appeared on 17 December 2025 with a comment period that closed on 30 January 202633,34. The draft's centre of gravity has shifted toward delivery and toward measurable improvement: secure and reliable deployment of patches and updates, testing that reflects default and common configurations, and organisational measurement of the practices themselves33. NIST also published SP 800-218A in July 2024, an SSDF community profile that adds practices and tasks specific to generative AI and dual-use foundation model development across the lifecycle35.

Maturity models: prescriptive and descriptive

OWASP SAMM and BSIMM answer different questions and are frequently bought as substitutes. SAMM is prescriptive and open: defined maturity levels across streams, with an improvement roadmap, and organisations are explicitly not expected to reach the same level everywhere21. BSIMM is descriptive and proprietary: it records what participating organisations actually do, so its value is comparative benchmarking rather than a target state21,23. The useful combination is obvious once stated — SAMM to plan, BSIMM to calibrate against peers — and neither produces anything a customer's procurement team can accept as assurance, because neither involves an independent examiner attesting to anything.

Sector frameworks

Sectors with a safety heritage did this earlier and, in one case, better. IEC 62443-4-1, published in 2018, specifies secure product development lifecycle requirements for industrial automation and control systems, spanning security requirements definition, secure design, secure implementation with coding guidelines, verification and validation, defect and patch management, and product end-of-life36. Two of its choices are worth borrowing regardless of sector. It layers a maturity-level framework over its practices, so that an assessor judges how deeply a practice is embedded rather than whether a document exists; and it specifies the required level of independence of testers from developers as a normative table rather than a suggestion36,37. The pattern generalises: where a sector already had a safety lifecycle standard, security assurance was grafted onto it — in medical devices, onto the existing quality-system and premarket-review apparatus38 — and where it did not, the SSDF filled the gap.

05

Regimes: pricing the work

FedRAMP · SOC 2 · CMMC · CE

A certification regime is a market instrument. It converts an unobservable property into a tradeable token, and its design determines what that token is worth. The two variables that matter are shown in Figure 5: how independent the examiner is, and whether they examined the artefact or the organisation.

SOC 2 — the private-sector default

SOC 2 is an attestation engagement performed by a CPA firm against the AICPA's Trust Services Criteria, defined in TSP section 100 — the 2017 criteria with revised points of focus issued in 202239,40. Five categories exist: Security, Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality and Privacy. Only Security — the common criteria — is mandatory in every engagement; the rest are elected according to what the service organisation commits to40. The engagements are performed under the attestation standards, AT-C sections 105 and 20541.

The distinction that carries all the weight is Type I versus Type II. A Type I report addresses the suitability of design of controls at a point in time. A Type II report addresses operating effectiveness across an observation window, typically three to twelve months42. This is the single most important structural feature of commercial software assurance, and the reason Type II is the only version worth asking for: it is the only common report that makes a claim about a period rather than a moment.

Two properties are routinely misunderstood by buyers. The 2022 update revised the points of focus, not the criteria, and the points of focus are considerations rather than requirements — a service organisation is not obliged to meet every one40,39. And crucially, the organisation designs its own controls; the criteria state objectives, not implementations40. A SOC 2 Type II report therefore tells you that an auditor tested controls the company chose, against criteria the company scoped, and found them operating. It does not tell you the controls were adequate to the threat.

FedRAMP — and the most interesting redesign in the field

FedRAMP is the US government's programme for authorising cloud services, built on NIST SP 800-53 control baselines. It is currently undergoing the deepest rearchitecture in its history, mandated by the FedRAMP Authorization Act and OMB Memorandum M-24-15, under the banner FedRAMP 20x43. The direction of travel is the most consequential development in this article, because it is a working attempt to solve the problems every other regime is still describing.

The old model produced narrative documents: System Security Plans, Security Assessment Reports, Control Implementation Summaries — richly formatted templates requiring considerable manual effort to maintain43. The new model replaces them with a Certification Package built around minimum mandatory information in a JSON schema, comprising a Certification Package Overview, a Security Decision Record consolidating rules, controls and Key Security Indicators, and a Secure Configuration Guide43. Key Security Indicators are the substantive innovation: a reduced set of machine-checkable assertions about a running system, published as a formal standard alongside a Minimum Assessment Scope standard that constrains how narrowly a provider may draw its boundary44.

The parameter ceiling

Buried in a June 2026 FedRAMP public notice is the sharpest critique of prescriptive compliance any regulator has published about its own scheme. FedRAMP is removing the vast majority of FedRAMP-assigned control parameter values from the Rev5 baselines. The stated reason: a mandated minimum also functions as a maximum. The programme's own worked example is a parameter demanding an action at least every thirty days — which providers then treat as the target, even where the commercial service already performs that action daily43. Providers must now assign their own parameter values, per the process in SP 800-53B, and defend them43,45. This is a regulator concluding that prescription had inverted its own intent.

The transition has dates. FedRAMP launched the Consolidated Rules for 2026 in late June 202646. New Rev5 certifications close on 11 June 2027; existing Rev5 authorisations are expected to terminate no later than 2029, with the programme's published expectation pointing at the end of 202843,46. Providers holding current Rev5 certifications must adopt the new approach at their first independent assessment completed after 1 January 202743. Meanwhile the programme has been explicit that it stopped providing updated technical guidance and in-depth package reviews for Rev5 after March 202547.

CMMC — assurance as a condition of contract

The Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification programme illustrates what happens when attestation becomes a precondition for revenue. The programme structure was codified in 32 CFR part 170 effective December 2024; the acquisition rule at 48 CFR was published on 10 September 2025 and took effect on 10 November 2025, inserting the DFARS 252.204-7021 clause into solicitations48,49. A four-phase rollout follows, each phase a year apart: Phase 1 requires Level 1 and Level 2 self-assessment; Phase 2, from 10 November 2026, requires third-party Level 2 certification for most contractors handling controlled unclassified information50,49.

The adoption data is the useful part. As of the October 2025 community town hall, 431 organisations had achieved a final Level 2 certification — approximately 0.5% of the roughly 80,000 companies the Department of Defense estimates will need it50. Assurance at scale is not a documentation problem. It is a capacity problem: assessor supply, remediation lead time, and the simple fact that most suppliers discover their evidence does not exist only when someone asks to see it.

The European turn: assurance as product law

The EU Cyber Resilience Act — Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 — does something none of the American regimes do: it makes software assurance a condition of market access for essentially all products with digital elements, enforced through conformity assessment, an EU declaration of conformity and CE marking51,52. It entered into force on 10 December 2024. Chapter IV on notification of conformity assessment bodies applied from 11 June 2026; the Article 14 reporting obligations apply from 11 September 2026; the main obligations apply from 11 December 202751,53.

The reporting regime deserves attention because it is an assurance obligation with a clock on it. On becoming aware of an actively exploited vulnerability or a severe incident affecting product security, a manufacturer must submit an initial report within 24 hours, further information within 72 hours where available, a final vulnerability report no later than 14 days after a security update or workaround, and a final incident report one month after initial notification — routed through a single reporting platform being built by ENISA for exchange with national CSIRTs54. Penalties for non-compliance with the essential requirements reach €15 million or 2.5% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher52. Microenterprises and small enterprises may not be fined for missing the 24-hour deadline, and open-source stewards are shielded from CRA infringement penalties51.

Statutory assurance in medical devices

The United States has one clean example of assurance written directly into statute. Section 3305 of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 added section 524B to the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, effective 29 March 2023, obliging sponsors of cyber devices to submit cybersecurity information — including a software bill of materials covering commercial, open-source and off-the-shelf components — in premarket submissions38. FDA finalised implementing guidance on 27 June 2025, adding a dedicated section addressing 524B, and has since superseded it with a further revision38,55,56. The mechanism is worth noting: no new criminal offence was created, but non-compliance renders a device adulterated or misbranded, which routes cybersecurity failures into pre-existing enforcement machinery.

06

The evidence layer

SBOM · provenance · SLSA · VEX

Everything above depends on artefacts that outlive the process that produced them. Since 2021 this layer has consolidated faster than any other part of the discipline, driven by the recognition that the audit question is not did you scan? but can you prove that this binary is the result of that scan?

Inventory: the software bill of materials

NTIA published The Minimum Elements For a Software Bill of Materials in July 2021, establishing the baseline: data fields, automation support, and practices and processes57. CISA published a substantially expanded draft in August 2025, for comment through 3 October58,59. The new draft raises expectations to match tooling maturity, adding fields including component hash, licence, tool name and generation context — whether the SBOM was produced before, during or after the build — and clarifying existing elements such as SBOM author and software producer59,57. As of mid-2026 the 2025 draft remains a public-comment document and does not create new legal requirements, which means the 2021 baseline is still the safe floor to build to.

Generation context matters more than it sounds. An SBOM produced by scanning source before the build describes intent; one produced by instrumenting the build describes what was actually linked. The xz-utils backdoor lived precisely in that gap, in a modified build-to-host.m4 present in the release tarball but absent from the Git repository60.

Provenance: SLSA and in-toto

An SBOM says what is inside. Provenance says where it came from. The SLSA framework — now at specification v1.1 under the Open Source Security Foundation — defines a Build track whose levels describe increasing trustworthiness and completeness of a package artefact's provenance61. A SLSA provenance attestation is carried in the in-toto attestation format: builder identity, source repository and commit, build entry point and parameters, invocation identifier, and the digest of the output artefact62.

The level distinctions are narrow and precise, which is their virtue. Level 1 requires that provenance exist and be distributed; it makes no claim about the trustworthiness of the build platform, so an attacker who controls that platform can produce fake provenance62. Level 2 requires the provenance to be generated and signed by a hosted build platform rather than the project. Level 3 requires the platform to isolate tenants such that the provenance is unforgeable even by a compromised build62. Notably, reproducible and hermetic builds were removed from SLSA 1.0 as requirements, on the grounds that they proved too hard to implement in practice — a candid concession that the industry's most elegant integrity mechanism remains mostly aspirational63.

Signing closed the last gap. Sigstore's contribution was to eliminate long-lived key management by binding ephemeral signing certificates to OIDC identities and recording them in a transparency log, which made artefact signing tractable for open-source projects that had never managed keys at all64. The audit consequence is significant: signature verification becomes checkable against a public append-only log rather than against a key whose custody nobody can evidence.

The honest caveat, from practitioners at a 2024 supply-chain summit: self-attestation to SLSA levels is typically provided by the producer, and self-attestation can be misleading or fabricated — an open problem, not a solved one63.

Aftercare: VEX, CSAF and exploitation signals

SBOMs created a problem they did not solve. A component list matched against a vulnerability database produces an enormous number of findings that are irrelevant because the vulnerable code path is never reached. The Vulnerability Exploitability eXchange is the structured assertion that answers this: a supplier states whether a specific vulnerability actually affects a specific product, and can affirmatively assert not affected with a justification65,66. VEX is implemented as a profile of the OASIS Common Security Advisory Framework version 2.0, the standard for machine-readable advisories65,67.

CISA's stated strategy ties these together explicitly: machine-readable advisories via CSAF, impact clarification via VEX, and prioritisation via Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorization with the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue as an input68. The KEV catalogue, launched in 2021 under Binding Operational Directive 22-01, lists vulnerabilities with confirmed evidence of active exploitation — not proof-of-concept code, not theoretical exploitability — and binds federal civilian agencies to remediation deadlines69. For an assurance programme, KEV membership is the cleanest available external signal for justifying prioritisation decisions to an auditor: it is a third-party fact, not an internal judgement.

07

When evidence becomes a data structure

OSCAL · KSI · continuous ATO

The defining shift of the last five years is not any single control. It is the move from assurance-as-document to assurance-as-queryable-state.

NIST's Open Security Controls Assessment Language provides machine-readable representations of control catalogues, baselines and profiles, system security plans, assessment plans, assessment results, and plans of action and milestones — expressed in XML, JSON and YAML, with lossless translation between them70. It is organised in three layers: control, implementation and assessment71. The assessment results model carries observations, risks and findings, and observations can contain human- or machine-generated evidence of compliance or non-compliance72. NIST states the purpose plainly: setting the foundation for security automation, with particular focus on continuous authorisation to operate and continuous monitoring70.

The design decision worth understanding is that OSCAL sits at a deliberately high level of abstraction, allowing implementers to collect evidence from a wide variety of tools without prescribing any specific tool output72. This is the same choice the SSDF made about practices, applied to evidence, and it is correct for the same reason: the tooling changes faster than the standard can.

FedRAMP 20x is the first regime to bet its architecture on this. Where the old model asked a provider to describe, in prose, how a control was implemented, Key Security Indicators ask for machine-verifiable assertions about the running service — and the provider is expected to innovate on presentation while the underlying data stays semi-structured and machine-readable43. The consequence for engineering organisations is direct: the system that produces your compliance evidence stops being a document repository and becomes a pipeline with the same reliability requirements as production.

A design brief for the evidence pipeline

If assurance evidence is going to be queried rather than assembled, it needs the properties any other production data stream needs. Timestamped at generation, not at collection. Attributable to an identity, not a shared service account. Bound to a digest, so the artefact examined is provably the artefact shipped. Immutable once written, or at minimum append-only with a verifiable history. Retained beyond the longest audit window you will face, which for a twelve-month SOC 2 Type II period plus fieldwork is closer to eighteen months than twelve. Evidence that fails any one of these is evidence you will be re-creating by hand at exactly the wrong moment.

08

What the evidence actually proves

soundness · completeness · cost

Assurance programmes routinely present tool output as though the tools were oracles. They are not, and the research literature is unusually clear about their envelopes. A serious assurance argument states what each technique can and cannot see, because the auditor will not, and the adversary already knows.

Static analysis

The most useful study for assurance purposes examined C/C++ static analysis tools against a dataset of actual code changes that introduced exploitable vulnerabilities, rather than the synthetic benchmarks most evaluations use. A single tool produced warnings in the vulnerable functions of 52% of vulnerability-contributing commits. At least 76% of the warnings in those vulnerable functions were irrelevant to the actual vulnerability, and 22% of the vulnerability- contributing commits went undetected entirely because of limitations in the tools' rules73. Read that as an assurance claim and it says: this technique halves your blindness on one class of defect and tells you almost nothing about where.

The false-positive burden is not an inconvenience; it is a soundness artefact. Static analysers over-approximate program behaviour to preserve soundness, producing warnings along infeasible paths and abstracting away pointer aliasing, external libraries and conditional compilation. In large codebases the consequences are severe: studies report over 95% false-alarm rates for null pointer dereference detection by state-of-the-art open-source analysers on projects at the scale of the Linux kernel74. Systematic reviews conclude that no single tool achieves complete vulnerability detection across cases, and recommend combination rather than selection75,76. Warning types themselves differ systematically in their disposition to be false positives77.

None of this is an argument against static analysis. It is an argument against writing static analysis performed, zero findings in an assurance case without stating the tool's rule coverage, the language subset analysed, and the triage disposition of suppressed findings. Notably, when developers are studied in the field, the majority configure tools to detect more vulnerabilities rather than fewer warnings — they prioritise reducing false negatives over reducing false positives, which is the correct instinct and the opposite of what a metrics dashboard rewards74.

Formal verification, and what it costs

At the far end sits machine-checked proof. The seL4 microkernel is the reference achievement: the first formal, machine-checked proof of functional correctness of a complete general-purpose operating system kernel, from an abstract specification down to its C implementation78. The subsequent comprehensive verification added proofs of integrity, confidentiality and information-flow non-interference, and worst-case execution time analysis79.

The economics are the point. The verification cost roughly 12 person-years of non-recurring engineering for about 8,500 source lines of code, an estimated $350 per line, and the correctness proof itself runs to around 200,000 lines of proof script80,79. Verification effort was found to scale with roughly the square of specification size, which is the strongest available argument for keeping trusted components small80. Against that, the seL4 project reports no functional-correctness defects in verified code since the proof completed in 2009 — more than fifteen years of deployment81. And the proofs exceed what the highest Common Criteria level demands: EAL7 requires formal models of the security policy, functional specification and design with formal correspondence between them, but only an informal mapping from design to implementation, whereas seL4 proves down to the implementation and further to the binary82.

The floor nobody can prove past

There is a hard epistemic limit under all of this, and it is worth stating in an article about evidence. Ken Thompson's 1984 Turing Award lecture demonstrated that a compiler can be made to insert a backdoor into a program it compiles, and to insert the backdoor-insertion logic into any compiler it compiles — such that the malicious behaviour is invisible in every line of source code anyone can inspect83. His conclusion was that you cannot trust code you did not totally create yourself. Diverse double-compiling offers a practical counter for the specific compiler case by cross-checking builds under independent toolchains84, but the general lesson holds: at some point every assurance argument terminates in an assumption about a tool, a machine, or a person that the argument itself cannot verify. The professional obligation is to know where that point is and write it down, not to pretend it does not exist.

09

How assurance fails

post-mortems

The failure modes have a common structure: the assurance artefact remained accurate while the thing it described drifted away from it.

Failure by scope: the SolarWinds statement

The SEC's October 2023 complaint against SolarWinds and its Chief Information Security Officer was the first time the Commission charged a CISO individually in connection with cybersecurity, and the first time it alleged scienter-based securities fraud in connection with a breach85,86. In July 2024 the court dismissed most of the case, rejecting the application of the internal accounting controls provision to general cybersecurity controls and finding the post-incident disclosure claims impermissibly reliant on hindsight86,85. What survived was narrow and instructive: the claim that the company's public Security Statement — a marketing artefact describing its internal controls — was materially false86,85. The SEC ultimately stipulated to dismiss the entire action with prejudice in November 202587.

Whatever one concludes about the enforcement theory, the engineering lesson is durable. The document that created legal exposure was not the SOC 2 report or the FedRAMP package. It was the security page on the website, written by people who were not the people responsible for the controls it described. Assurance statements leak out of the compliance function and into marketing, sales questionnaires and RFP responses, where nobody applies the evidentiary discipline that the formal artefacts receive.

Failure by trust model: xz-utils

The xz-utils backdoor, CVE-2024-3094, is the clean counterexample to process-based assurance. An actor operating as Jia Tan contributed to the project over roughly two years, using sock-puppet accounts to pressure the overworked volunteer maintainer into accepting co-maintainer help, and eventually controlled releases88,60. The malicious payload was introduced not through reviewable source but through a modified build-to-host.m4 in the GitHub-hosted release tarball, absent from the Git repository and therefore untraceable through source control, alongside obfuscated binary test files; it injected code during the build on x86-64 Linux with glibc and GCC, via dpkg or rpm60. Versions 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 shipped it, and it carried a CVSS score of 10.089. It was found because a Microsoft engineer benchmarking PostgreSQL noticed SSH logins taking longer than expected.

Every process control an assurance framework would ask about was nominally satisfied. Code was reviewed. Commits were attributed. The maintainer was legitimate. The failure was in the trust model itself — the assumption that a long-tenured contributor is a verified one, and that the release artefact matches the repository. SLSA Level 3 provenance and reproducible builds address exactly this, which is why their difficulty is not an academic concern.

Failure by decay

The least dramatic failure mode is the most common. Evidence has a half-life. A penetration test is a statement about a build that no longer exists. A threat model describes an architecture three refactors ago. A SOC 2 Type II window closes and the ninety days before the next one begins are unobserved by construction. An SBOM generated at release describes a container that has since been rebuilt on a new base image. Nothing was falsified; the artefacts simply stopped corresponding to anything.

This is the deepest argument for the machine-readable turn. Not efficiency — freshness. An assertion that can be re-evaluated on demand cannot decay silently.

Failure by policy churn

Assurance regimes are political objects, and 2025–26 demonstrated how quickly the ground moves. Executive Order 14028 in May 2021 directed NIST to produce secure software development guidance and directed OMB to require agencies to use only software from providers attesting to those practices90,91. OMB M-22-18 in September 2022 implemented that requirement, and CISA developed a Common Form for collecting the attestations, finalised in March 2024 after 110 sets of public comments91,92. Executive Order 14144 in January 2025 pushed toward more rigorous third-party risk management90.

Executive Order 14306, on 6 June 2025, rescinded large portions of that structure — including the requirement that contractors submit technical data and attestation artefacts — while retaining the direction to NIST to establish an industry consortium at the National Cybersecurity Center of Excellence and to publish a preliminary SSDF update by 1 December 202534,93,94. The accompanying fact sheet characterised the attestations as imposing unproven and burdensome software accounting processes that prioritized compliance checklists over genuine security investments94. Then on 23 January 2026, OMB Memorandum M-26-05 rescinded M-22-18 and M-23-16 outright, replacing the government-wide mandate with a risk-based approach in which agencies maintain complete software and hardware inventories and develop assurance policies matched to their own risk determinations; use of CISA's Common Form became optional, and agencies adopting cloud service terms are told to specify that producers must provide an SBOM of the runtime production environment on request91.

The practical reading for a software producer is not that the obligations vanished. It is that they de-standardised. A single government-wide form has been replaced by heterogeneous, contract- specific demands from individual agencies — which is more work, not less, and which rewards organisations whose evidence is structured enough to answer arbitrary questions rather than one particular form.

10

Building a programme that survives contact

practice

What follows is opinionated, and derived from the failure modes above rather than from any single framework.

Start from the claim, not the control list

Write the top-level claim you need to be able to defend, with its context and assumptions, in the form Section 3 describes. Our multi-tenant SaaS resists a remote unauthenticated adversary and a malicious tenant, given the deployment assumptions in our architecture document. Then decompose it. Controls should appear as evidence supporting subclaims, not as the organising structure. A programme organised around a control catalogue produces a complete catalogue and an undefended claim.

Make evidence a build artefact

Every verification activity should emit a durable, timestamped, digest-bound artefact automatically, into the same storage with the same retention as any other release artefact. If a human has to remember to screenshot something, that evidence does not exist. The test: pick a random release from nine months ago and reconstruct — without asking anyone — what was scanned, what was found, who dispositioned each finding, and what shipped.

Design the boundary before someone else does

Scope is where assurance quietly collapses. FedRAMP publishes a Minimum Assessment Scope standard precisely because providers were drawing boundaries narrowly enough to exclude the interesting parts44. Decide deliberately what is inside, document why, and be willing to defend the exclusions. An honest narrow scope is a legitimate artefact. A scope drawn to flatter the result is the thing that turns a certificate into a liability.

Separate the finding from the decision

The most audit-fragile part of most programmes is triage. Every suppressed or deferred finding needs a recorded decision, a decision-maker, a justification and a review date. VEX exists to make the not affected case a structured artefact with a justification taxonomy rather than a ticket comment66. Adopt that discipline internally even where you do not publish VEX externally: the pattern of judgements, not the individual judgement, is what an assessor is sampling.

Instrument what a regulator would ask

A small set of measures does most of the work, and each should be derivable from the evidence pipeline rather than reported by hand.

Treat open-source dependencies as suppliers you cannot audit

You will not perform due diligence on 1,400 transitive dependencies. What is tractable is automated signal. OpenSSF Scorecard runs a set of automated checks against a project's repository configuration, CI setup, dependency management and contribution practices, scoring each from 0 to 10, with results published as a public dataset95,96,97. It measures practices, not the absence of defects — but for a supplier you cannot interview, an observation that a project requires code review and has branch protection is a real, if thin, assurance signal. Complementary initiatives fund the projects that carry the most weight: Alpha-Omega directs engineering effort at critical projects, with automated analysis across a broader set98.

11

Where this is going

2026 →

Four movements are visible from here, and they pull in different directions.

Assurance is becoming continuous or it is becoming decorative. FedRAMP's Key Security Indicators, OSCAL's assessment layer, and the CRA's rolling reporting obligations all push in the same direction: away from a periodic examination of documents toward a standing, machine-checkable assertion about a running system43,70,54. Point-in-time artefacts will not disappear, but their credibility relative to continuous evidence is falling, and the organisations that treated compliance as a quarterly project will find that the quarterly project no longer produces an acceptable output.

The regulatory centre of gravity has moved to Brussels. With the American federal mandate rescinded and replaced by agency-level risk determination91, the CRA is now the most consequential software assurance instrument in the world by reach — it applies to products, not to government suppliers, and it is enforced through market access with turnover-scaled penalties52. The practical question for the next eighteen months is how much of the essential requirements the harmonised standards actually pin down, because that is where the difference between a genuine assurance regime and a documentation exercise will be settled.

Generative AI is arriving on both sides of the ledger. As a subject, NIST has already published SP 800-218A to extend the SSDF to generative AI and dual-use foundation model development35, and every existing certification scheme now faces the question of how to evaluate a system whose behaviour is not specified by its source. As an instrument, AI-assisted analysis is being applied to the false-positive problem that limits static analysis at scale74. But the deeper issue is provenance: when a substantial fraction of committed code is machine-generated, the assurance chain in Figure 6 needs a link that does not currently exist — an attestation about how a change was produced, not merely who committed it.

The capacity problem is unsolved. The CMMC figure — 431 Level 2 certifications against an estimated 80,000 organisations needing them50 — is the single most sobering data point in this article. Assurance regimes are priced as though assessment supply were elastic. It is not. Every scheme that scales assurance by adding required audits eventually runs into a market that cannot staff them, at which point either the deadlines slip, the depth of assessment falls, or self-attestation quietly returns. All three have already happened at least once.

The thing worth holding onto

Software assurance is the discipline of making a defensible statement about something you cannot fully observe, to someone who cannot inspect it, in a form that survives the passage of time and the scrutiny of an adversary who reads it too. That is a genuinely hard epistemic problem, and it does not go away when you buy a compliance platform.

The measure of a good assurance programme is not whether it produces a certificate. It is whether, on the day something goes wrong, the organisation can say precisely what it knew, when it knew it, what it checked, what it deliberately did not check, and why those were reasonable decisions given what it understood at the time. Anderson asked in 1972 for mechanisms small enough that their correctness could be demonstrated6. Fifty-four years later, the question has scaled but not changed: what, exactly, are you prepared to demonstrate?
