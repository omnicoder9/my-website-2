00The question

Robot kinematics is a small subject with an unusually rigid shape. It has one object of study, the map from joint coordinates to the pose of a body in space, and everything in a kinematics syllabus is a fact about that map or a device for handling one of its properties. That is why the syllabus is so stable: coordinate frames, rotation representations, forward kinematics, inverse kinematics, the Jacobian. Change the textbook and the chapter headings barely move [1,2,3,4,5,6,7], and the handbook chapter that surveys the field states the same division [8,9].

What the chapter headings hide is that the subject is not a sequence of techniques but a sequence of concessions. The map is smooth and single-valued going forward and neither of those things going backward. The space it lands in is a Lie group and not a vector space. The group of rotations inside it is a compact three-manifold that no three coordinates can cover. None of these are difficulties in the sense that a better algorithm would resolve them; they are theorems. Every construct in the syllabus is a response to one of them, and every response works by moving the difficulty somewhere the engineer would rather have it.

This article reads the subject that way. It takes the seventeen standard constructs, one per leaf of the usual taxonomy, and asks the same six questions of each: what does it buy, what defect does it not remove, where does that defect end up, how does it show itself, what test detects it, and what is the evidence. The thesis question in the title is meant literally. Nothing in kinematics deletes a defect. The interesting content of the subject is the bookkeeping of where each one goes.

Two things make this more than a rhetorical frame. The first is that the defects have a geometry: each one lives on a set, and that set has a codimension. A defect at codimension one lives on a surface you can be near or far from, detect, and steer around; the singular configurations that dominate the literature are of this kind. A defect at codimension zero has positive measure. It is not a place. It is a standing charge on every configuration, and no trajectory planner avoids it. The small gauge on every plate below records that codimension, because it turns out to sort the subject in a way the conventional grouping does not.

The second is that the claims are measured rather than asserted. Nine deterministic experiments, written against the Python standard library with no numerical dependencies, run at build time; their console output is reproduced verbatim later in the article, and every number quoted anywhere here comes from that run. Where an experiment contradicted the claim it was written to support, the claim was changed. Two were.

Each construct below gets one plate. The six fields are the same on every plate, so they can be read down a column as well as across. The gauge on the right renders the defect's locus at its actual codimension inside a stylised chart of the construct's own domain: a hatched field when the failure set has positive measure, a curve when it is a hypersurface, a point when it is codimension two, and a hollow marker when there is no locus at all and the defect is a standing cost rather than a place.

INaming a place

Coordinate frames and homogeneous transformations. Before anything moves, you have to be able to say where it is.

The first two constructs are the ones least often treated as substantive. A frame is a choice of axes; a homogeneous transform is a matrix that packages a rotation and a translation. Both are introduced in the first pages of every text and neither is usually revisited, because both are correct and neither has an interesting failure mode in the sense that a singularity is interesting.

They are here because they set the pattern the rest of the article follows. In both cases the construct is exactly right and the defect it leaves behind is not in the mathematics at all. It has been moved into a convention that the numbers do not carry: which frame a triple belongs to, and which operations a four-by-four matrix may legally take part in. Nothing in the representation records either. A wrong answer and a right answer are numerically indistinguishable, and the only available remedy is a discipline enforced outside the arithmetic.

That is a genuinely different kind of problem from a rank deficiency, and it is worth naming before the more famous material arrives, because the same shape recurs at every stage. The construct works. The residue is a convention. The convention is invisible in the data, and so it fails silently.

K01

Coordinate frames

Robot position and orientation → Coordinate frames
codim0
BUYS
A vocabulary in which position and orientation are sayable at all.
DEFECT
There is no privileged frame. Every numerical quantity in the system is a statement relative to a choice, and the choice is not recoverable from the numbers.
PARKED AT
In the metadata. The defect leaves the arithmetic and becomes a labelling discipline that no equation enforces.
SHOWS UP AS
Sign flips, axis permutations, quantities that are individually correct and jointly meaningless; the classic failure is a correct number in the wrong frame.
DETECTOR
Exact, if frames are carried as types rather than conventions: a transform graph rejects a composition whose parent and child do not match.
EVIDENCE
[10,11,12,13]

A frame is three orthonormal directions and an origin, and the reason it comes first in every treatment is that without one the words position and orientation do not denote. This is not pedantry. A robot system holds simultaneously a world frame, a base frame, one frame per link, a tool frame, one per sensor, and usually one per object it has perceived, and the numbers attached to all of them look identical: three floats and four more, or a matrix. Nothing in the representation records which of the dozen frames a given triple belongs to.

The standards do what they can. ISO 8373 fixes the vocabulary and ISO 9787 fixes the coordinate systems and motion nomenclature for industrial robots, so that base, mechanical interface and tool frames mean the same thing across vendors [10,11]. In the ROS ecosystem the same job is done by convention rather than standard: REP 103 fixes units and axis orientation, x forward, y left, z up, and REP 105 fixes the semantics of the mobile-platform frame chain [12,14]. The tf library then does the only thing that actually helps, which is to make the frame a first-class object, maintain the transform tree with timestamps, and refuse compositions that do not typecheck [13,15,16]. The model description formats do the corresponding job for the static structure: a URDF or SDFormat file names every link and joint frame explicitly, so that the frame graph is data rather than an assumption [17,18].

That is why this defect scores an exact detector at codimension zero, an unusual combination. The failure is not rare and not a configuration; it is available on every line of code that handles a pose. But it is completely mechanically detectable once frames are carried in the type rather than in the variable name. The cost of not doing so is well documented outside robotics as well: the Mars Climate Orbiter was lost to a quantity that was numerically correct in the units it was computed in [19]. The defect never disappears. It is moved out of the mathematics and into a discipline, and the discipline is either enforced by a tool or it is not enforced.

K02

Homogeneous transformations

Robot position and orientation → Homogeneous transformations
codim0
BUYS
Rotation and translation compose by a single matrix multiplication, associatively, with inversion in closed form.
DEFECT
SE(3) is a group and a manifold, not a vector space. Sixteen numbers carry six degrees of freedom under ten constraints, and no arithmetic operation other than group multiplication preserves them.
PARKED AT
In the operations you are not allowed to perform. The representation is exact; the closure is what is lost.
SHOWS UP AS
Averaged poses that are not rigid; linearly interpolated poses that shear; accumulated products that slowly acquire scale.
DETECTOR
Exact: the bottom row and the orthonormality residual of the upper-left block are both checkable in constant time.
EVIDENCE
[20,21,22,23]

The homogeneous transform is the best-value trick in the subject. Embedding a rotation and a translation in one four-by-four matrix makes composition of rigid motions into matrix multiplication, which is associative for free, and makes the inverse available without solving anything. Chains of links become products; the whole of forward kinematics is then a single line [1,24].

What the embedding does not do is make SE(3) linear. The set of valid transforms is a six-dimensional submanifold of a sixteen-dimensional matrix space, cut out by ten constraints. Every operation that is not group multiplication or inversion leaves the manifold. Adding two poses is meaningless; scaling one is meaningless; averaging a set of them componentwise produces a matrix that is not a rigid motion, and interpolating between two of them linearly produces intermediate states that shear and shrink [23,25]. The group is also non-commutative, so the order of a composition is load-bearing in a way that the notation makes easy to lose track of.

The alternative formulations do not remove this. The product-of-exponentials form writes the chain as a product of matrix exponentials of screw coordinates, which is coordinate-free and needs no link frames at all, but it is a statement about the same group and inherits the same closure [3,20,22,26]. Screw theory, which supplies the underlying geometry, is older than robotics and says the same thing: a general rigid displacement is a rotation about an axis together with a translation along it, and that structure is not additive [27,28,29,30,31]. The classical treatments develop the whole of displacement geometry on that basis, and they remain the reference for the algebraic structure that the matrix notation compresses [32,33,34,35].

The defect is therefore parked in a rule rather than in a region. There is no configuration at which the homogeneous transform misbehaves; there is a class of operations that must never be applied to it, and the representation itself gives no warning when one is. The detector is trivial and exact, which is precisely why the failure survives: a check that is cheap and never run is not a check.

IINaming a rotation

Rotation matrices, Euler angles, quaternions. Three answers to one question, and a topological theorem that says none of them is clean.

Orientation is where kinematics stops being bookkeeping. The set of orientations is SO(3), a compact three-dimensional Lie group, and it is not homeomorphic to any open subset of three-dimensional Euclidean space. That single fact generates the entire structure of this group of plates.

The consequence is a trilemma with no fourth option. Use nine numbers and accept redundancy that arithmetic erodes. Use three and accept a singular set where the parameterisation degenerates. Use four and accept that every orientation has two names. Each of the three standard representations picks one of these, and the choice is not a matter of taste or of era: it is a choice about which of three unavoidable costs the rest of the system will pay.

What makes the group worth measuring rather than merely stating is that the costs are not equal in size, and that the intuitive ranking is wrong in at least one place. The singular set of a three-angle chart is famous and occupies a vanishing fraction of the group; the double cover of the quaternions is treated as a footnote and applies to half of all orientation pairs. Two of the three experiments behind this group were written to check exactly that asymmetry, and both confirmed it.

There is a further point that only appears once the numbers are in front of you. The test that is easiest to run on an orientation, whether the representation is still a valid member of the group, is not the test that predicts accuracy. Four integrators can pass it identically and disagree about where the robot is pointing by three orders of magnitude. That is the sharpest instance in the article of a detector that is exact, cheap, and aimed at the wrong quantity.

K03

Rotation matrices

Robot position and orientation → Rotation matrices
codim—
BUYS
A global, singularity-free, unique representation of orientation with composition by multiplication and inversion by transposition.
DEFECT
Nine numbers for three degrees of freedom, held together by six constraints that floating-point arithmetic erodes. The redundancy is not optional; it is the price of having no singular set.
PARKED AT
In a recurring projection cost, and in the choice of which projection.
SHOWS UP AS
Slow loss of orthonormality under accumulated products, and, less visibly, a biased attitude error introduced by the wrong repair.
DETECTOR
Exact for the stated defect: the Frobenius residual of the transpose product measures departure from the group directly.
EVIDENCE
[36,37,38,39]

The rotation matrix is the only common representation with no singularity anywhere. That is what the gauge on this plate records: the defect has no locus. SO(3) is covered globally, every rotation has exactly one matrix, composition is multiplication and the inverse is the transpose. What it costs is redundancy, six constraints on nine numbers, and the constraints are not preserved by arithmetic [36,40].

In a long integration the loss is measurable. Experiment L2 propagates a constant body rate for 60,000 explicit Euler steps and the raw matrix accumulates an orthonormality residual of 1.603e-01, which is no longer a rotation in any useful sense. Every standard repair drives that residual to machine precision and they become indistinguishable on the test.

They are not indistinguishable on the thing that matters. Repairing with Gram-Schmidt leaves an attitude error of 0.295 degrees after sixty seconds; repairing by projection onto the nearest rotation, computed with the Newton iteration for the orthogonal polar factor, leaves 0.002744 degrees, 108 times better, on the same trajectory with the same step size [37,41]. Gram-Schmidt is a valid orthogonalisation and not the nearest one: it privileges the first column and re-injects an asymmetric error at every step. Markley's singular-value formulation of Wahba's problem is the same observation from the attitude-determination side [38].

The matrix is also the oldest of the three representations and the one the others are defined against. Euler's theorem, that any orientation is a rotation about some axis, is the reason a three-parameter description is even conceivable; Rodrigues gave the composition formula and Cayley the rational parameterisation [42,43,44]. Converting a matrix back to a quaternion is not trivial to do stably, which is why Shepperd's branch-selecting algorithm and Bar-Itzhack's eigenvector method are still the standard references [45,46,47]; the exponential-map parameterisation used in optimisation has its own careful treatment [48]. All of this apparatus exists because the matrix is the common currency and everything else has to be exchanged into it.

So the honest reading of this plate is a warning about detectors rather than about matrices. The available test is exact, cheap and answers a different question from the one being asked. Passing an orthonormality check certifies membership of the group and says nothing about accuracy. The only construction that removes the error rather than repairing it is one that never leaves the group in the first place, which is what Lie-group integrators do [39,49,50,51,52].

K04

Euler angles

Robot position and orientation → Euler angles
codim1
BUYS
Three numbers for three degrees of freedom, readable by humans, differentiable, and directly meaningful for machines whose axes are physically stacked.
DEFECT
No three-parameter chart covers SO(3). Some set of orientations always exists at which the parameterisation degenerates and the map from angular velocity to angle rates becomes unbounded.
PARKED AT
On a codimension-one surface in the angle domain, and in the band around it where the rates are merely large rather than infinite.
SHOWS UP AS
Gimbal lock: two axes align, one degree of freedom disappears from the parameters, and commanded rates diverge as the singular set is approached.
DETECTOR
Exact: the determinant of the rate map is a single trigonometric factor, and its reciprocal is the amplification.
EVIDENCE
[36,40,53,54]

The topological fact is old and unambiguous. SO(3) is a compact three-manifold that is not homeomorphic to any open subset of R^3, so no set of three coordinates covers it without singularity; Stuelpnagel states it, and the same obstruction is what makes the Hopf fibration interesting [36,54,55,56,57]. Any three-angle convention is a chart, and a chart is by definition partial. The twelve standard conventions differ in where the singularity is, not in whether there is one [40,53,58].

Experiment L1 measures the price on Haar-uniform rotations rather than asserting it. For the ZYX convention the matrix relating angle rates to body angular velocity has determinant cos(pitch), and across 60,000 uniform samples 0.957 per cent of all orientations require a rate amplification above ten, 0.0067 per cent above a hundred, with a worst observed value of 170. Orientations within five degrees of the singular pitch account for 0.365 per cent of the group, against the analytic 0.381 per cent, which is the sanity check on the sampler.

The interesting half of the experiment is what happens with two charts. The ZYZ convention is singular where ZYX is not. Across the same sample, the number of orientations at which both charts exceed an amplification of ten is 0, and the worst case of the better of the two is 1.8473. A two-chart atlas therefore bounds the amplification at a small constant everywhere on SO(3), which is exactly what an atlas is for and exactly what a single chart provably cannot do.

The defect has not been removed by that construction. It has been moved to the seam: something must now decide which chart is in use, that decision is discontinuous, and the switching logic is a new component with its own failure modes. This is the general shape of the trade in this article. Charts are cheap and partial; atlases are complete and have seams; and there is no third option, because the obstruction is topological rather than computational.

K05

Quaternions

Robot position and orientation → Quaternions
codim0
BUYS
Four numbers, one constraint, no coordinate singularity, cheap composition, well-conditioned interpolation, and a natural geodesic on the sphere.
DEFECT
The unit quaternions are a double cover of SO(3). Every orientation has two names, q and -q, and the ambiguity is global rather than exceptional.
PARKED AT
Everywhere, as a sign convention that must be applied by hand; or, if canonicalised away, on a codimension-one cut where the representation jumps.
SHOWS UP AS
Interpolation that takes the long way round; unwinding, in which a controller drives the body through a full extra turn to reach an orientation it is already at; sign flips between filter updates.
DETECTOR
Exact: the sign of the inner product of the two quaternions decides it in four multiplications.
EVIDENCE
[59,60,61,62]

Quaternions win on nearly every axis. Four parameters with one constraint is the minimum redundancy that avoids a coordinate singularity; composition is sixteen multiplications; renormalisation is a division; and the sphere carries an obvious geodesic, which is what makes spherical linear interpolation the default in animation and attitude work [59,60,63,64,65]. In L2 the quaternion integrator finishes 430 times more accurate than the Gram-Schmidt matrix integrator at identical step size.

The double cover is the price, and it is not a corner case. Experiment L3 draws 100,000 pairs of Haar-uniform orientations and finds that 50.456 per cent of them have a negative quaternion inner product, so an interpolation that does not check the sign takes the long path. The mean excess rotation on those pairs is 107.1 degrees and the worst is 348.6. The check is trivial. Half of all unprepared pairs need it.

Canonicalising the representation, forcing the scalar part non-negative so that each orientation has one name, does not resolve this; it converts a global two-to-one ambiguity into a discontinuity on the set where the scalar part vanishes. L3 samples 40,000 geodesics of a half turn and finds that 50.420 per cent of them cross that cut, at which point the representation jumps by the maximum possible distance in quaternion space while the physical orientation moves not at all.

Bhat and Bernstein showed that this is not an artefact of the choice of coordinates but a consequence of the topology: no continuous state feedback on the unit quaternion can globally asymptotically stabilise a rigid body without either unwinding or a discontinuity, because SO(3) is not contractible [61,66]. The accepted resolution is a hybrid controller with hysteresis on the sign, which is once again an atlas with a seam and an explicit switching rule [62].

IIIBuilding the chain

Joint variables, link transformations, Denavit-Hartenberg parameters, end-effector pose. Composing local facts into a global one.

Forward kinematics is the part of the subject that works. Given the joint variables and a model of the links, the pose of the tool is a product of transforms, computable in microseconds, exact up to the accuracy of the model, single-valued and smooth. There is no ambiguity to resolve and no iteration to converge.

The four constructs in this group are the ingredients of that computation, and the defects they carry are all defects of the model rather than of the map. The joint variables live on a torus that the coordinate vector flattens. The link transforms compose multiplicatively, so error is amplified by downstream reach rather than added. The Denavit-Hartenberg parameterisation is minimal and canonical and discontinuous in the geometry precisely where arms are designed to be. And the pose at the end has no unit-free magnitude, so the error that every downstream algorithm minimises is measured in a unit somebody chose.

The pattern here is a trade of fidelity for compactness that is made once, at modelling time, and then inherited by everything else. It is also the group in which the gap between what a robot does and what a robot is described as doing is widest. Experiment L4 is the clearest case: a hundredth of a degree of manufacturing error moves a standard model parameter by hundreds of metres while the machine itself barely moves at all.

K06

Joint variables

Forward kinematics → Joint variables
codim1
BUYS
A coordinate on exactly the freedoms the machine actually has, in the units the actuators and encoders speak.
DEFECT
The configuration space of a revolute chain is a torus, not a Euclidean space. Angles wrap, and joint limits then cut the torus into a region whose shape is not a box in any global coordinate.
PARKED AT
On the branch cut, one per revolute joint, where the chosen coordinate jumps by a full turn.
SHOWS UP AS
Interpolation that unwinds a joint the long way; distance metrics in joint space that report large differences between neighbouring configurations; limit checks that pass on the wrong branch.
DETECTOR
Exact: the wrap test is a comparison, and the shortest angular difference has a closed form.
EVIDENCE
[2,3,67,68]

A revolute joint's variable is an element of the circle, not of the real line. An n-joint revolute arm therefore has configuration space T^n, and the habit of writing q as a vector in R^n is a chart on that torus with 2 pi periodicity discarded. This is a smaller defect than the ones on either side of it, but it is the same species: a global object handled by a local coordinate [67,68].

The consequences are ordinary and constant. Any planner that interpolates linearly in the chart may drive a joint the long way around a cut it does not know is there. Any cost function that uses Euclidean distance in joint space is measuring the chart, not the torus, and reports a large distance between two configurations that are physically adjacent. Any comparison against joint limits is a statement about the chosen representative, so a controller that receives an angle outside the representative range must decide whether that is a violation or a wrap [2,3].

Joint limits then do something more interesting than truncation. They make the reachable subset of the torus a region with boundary, and it is that region rather than the torus that governs the inverse problem. Experiment L5 makes the size of the effect concrete: with limits applied, the mean number of legal postures reaching a given pose falls from eight to 4.96, and only 19.4 per cent of targets retain all eight. The topology decides how many answers exist; the limits decide how many are usable; and neither is visible in the coordinate vector itself.

Because the cut is a single codimension-one set per joint and the wrap test is exact, this is one of the tractable defects. It is listed here because it is almost always handled implicitly, by a convention about ranges that lives in comments rather than in code, and because it is the reason joint-space interpolation and task-space interpolation disagree in ways that surprise people who have not looked at the topology.

K07

Link transformations

Forward kinematics → Link transformations
codim0
BUYS
Locality. Each joint's effect is one transform, and the chain is their product, so the model is built and debugged one link at a time.
DEFECT
Errors compose multiplicatively and are amplified by every link downstream. An angular error at the base is a position error at the tool proportional to the remaining reach, so the model's accuracy is not the accuracy of its parts.
PARKED AT
In the calibration budget, where it becomes a parameter-identification problem rather than a modelling one.
SHOWS UP AS
A robot with excellent repeatability and poor absolute accuracy; tool-point error that grows as the arm extends; residuals that will not go below a floor.
DETECTOR
Proxy: identified parameters explain measured poses, but the true parameter error is never observed, only the residual of a particular measurement set.
EVIDENCE
[69,70,71,72]

The chain rule of kinematics is the reason the subject is tractable. Writing the pose of link i relative to link i-1 as a single transform makes the full pose a product, makes derivatives telescope, and makes the recursive algorithms of dynamics possible [24,73]. The same structure is what makes errors uncooperative.

Because the composition is multiplicative, a small orientation error introduced at joint i propagates to the tool as a displacement whose magnitude scales with the distance from joint i to the tool. A milliradian at the shoulder of a one-metre arm is a millimetre at the tool; the same milliradian at the wrist is a few hundredths. Sensitivity is therefore structural, not statistical: the same manufacturing tolerance costs different amounts depending on where in the chain it sits [70,74].

This is why calibration exists as a discipline with its own taxonomy, and why the taxonomy is organised by which level of the model is being identified rather than by algorithm [69,71,75]. It is also why the detector on this plate is a proxy and not exact. Identification fits parameters that minimise the residual against a set of measured poses. What is verified is that the model explains those measurements; the true parameter error is unobservable, and a model with compensating errors in two links can fit a pose set well and extrapolate badly. ISO 9283 formalises the distinction the whole field turns on, between pose repeatability, which is a statement about variance, and pose accuracy, which is a statement about the model [72].

The defect sits at codimension zero because it is present in every configuration. There is no posture at which link transforms stop composing multiplicatively. What changes across the workspace is only the size of the lever arm, and the calibration problem is the exercise of buying that error down with measurements rather than removing it.

K08

Denavit-Hartenberg parameters

Forward kinematics → Denavit-Hartenberg parameters
codim1
BUYS
Four parameters per joint instead of six, by fixing frames to the common normal between consecutive axes. A canonical, minimal, comparable description.
DEFECT
The common normal is undefined when consecutive axes are parallel, so the parameters are a discontinuous function of the physical geometry precisely where arms are usually designed to be.
PARKED AT
On the parallel-axis set, and in the ill-conditioned neighbourhood around it where the parameters are defined but enormous.
SHOWS UP AS
Identification that will not converge; parameters that swing by metres between two nominally identical robots; a model that is minimal and useless for calibration.
DETECTOR
Exact: the sine of the twist angle, or the conditioning of the identification Jacobian, both signal it before it bites.
EVIDENCE
[76,77,78,79]

The Denavit-Hartenberg convention is the oldest device in the modern subject and one of the most successful. By placing each link frame on the common normal between successive joint axes it reduces the six parameters of a general relative pose to four, and it does so canonically, so that two engineers describing the same arm produce the same table [24,76,80].

The reduction has a precondition. The common normal between two lines is unique when the lines are skew and undefined when they are parallel. Real arms are built with parallel shoulder and elbow axes on purpose, so the degenerate case is not exotic; it is the nominal design. What happens near it is worse than a removable singularity. Experiment L4 takes two nominally parallel axes offset by half a metre and tilts one by delta. The foot of the common normal slides along the axis as a cotangent: at delta of one milliradian, about 0.057 of a degree, the DH offset is 250 metres on a half-metre link. Between one hundredth of a radian and one thousandth the parameter moves 225 metres while the hardware moves 0.516 of a degree.

The pose of the arm is a continuous function of its geometry. Its DH description is not. That gap is a property of the convention, and it is fatal for calibration, because identification requires the map from parameters to pose to be well conditioned. Hayati's correction adds a small rotation parameter for the parallel case; Mooring analysed the misalignment directly; Everett and colleagues set out the identifiability conditions; and the complete-and-parametrically-continuous models were built specifically so that the parameterisation varies continuously with the geometry [77,78,81,82,83].[84]

Two other responses are worth naming. Khalil and Kleinfinger's modified convention rearranges the frame assignment so that tree-structured and closed chains are describable, which fixes a different limitation of the original [1,79]. The product-of-exponentials formulation avoids link frames altogether and is continuous in the geometry, which is the cleanest available answer to this particular defect [3,22,26]. Both reallocate rather than delete: one gives up minimality, the other gives up the canonical table that made DH useful for communication in the first place.

K09

End-effector pose

Forward kinematics → End-effector pose
codim0
BUYS
A single object naming the whole task-space state, so that a goal is one quantity and an error is one difference.
DEFECT
SE(3) admits no bi-invariant Riemannian metric. There is no frame-independent, unit-free way to say how large a pose error is, so any scalar combining millimetres and radians encodes an arbitrary choice of length scale.
PARKED AT
In a weighting matrix, usually undeclared, often a default, always a stipulation rather than a measurement.
SHOWS UP AS
Solvers and planners that rank two candidate poses differently when the model is re-expressed in different units or the tool frame is moved.
DETECTOR
None. There is no test that identifies the right weighting, because there is no fact of the matter that a weighting could get right.
EVIDENCE
[23,25,85,86]

Almost every algorithm downstream of this plate needs a scalar error on SE(3). Inverse-kinematics solvers minimise one, planners use one as a cost, controllers regulate one, and specifications quote one. The trouble is that the group does not supply it.

The result is old and clean. A bi-invariant metric on SE(3), one that is unchanged under both a change of world frame and a change of body frame, does not exist; the rotation part is compact and admits one, the translation part is not, and the semidirect product admits none. Loncaric proved the corresponding statement for stiffness and compliance; Park, Zefran, Kumar and Croke, and Lin and Burdick set out what this means for mechanism design, for motion generation, and for the very idea of a frame-invariant kinematic metric [23,25,85,86,87].

In practice the gap is closed by a stipulation. A characteristic length is chosen and used to convert radians into millimetres, either explicitly, as Angeles and Lopez-Cajun propose, or silently, by writing the model in metres and adding the two norms [88,89,90]. The stipulation is legitimate and unavoidable. What is not legitimate is forgetting it, because the choice is then smuggled into every downstream comparison. Experiment L7 shows what that costs when the same reasoning is applied to the Jacobian rather than to the error, and the answer is that roughly a quarter of configuration pairs change places.

This is one of two plates in the article with no detector at all. That is a stronger statement than saying the detector is expensive or approximate. There is nothing to detect: no experiment distinguishes a correct weighting from an incorrect one, because the weighting is a convention, and a convention is answerable to the task rather than to the geometry. The right response is to declare the length scale as a specification and to check that conclusions are stable under changing it, which is what ISO 9283 does implicitly by fixing test poses and reporting position and orientation separately [72,91].

IVRunning it backwards

Solving for joint configurations, multiple solutions, singularities, reachability. The map has no inverse, so we build something that acts like one.

Everything in this group follows from one asymmetry. The forward map is a function and the backward relation is not. It is not merely that the inverse is hard to compute; it is that there is no inverse to compute, because the preimage of a pose is generically a finite set of points and occasionally empty.

The four plates take the four ways that shows up. There is the question of how to find any solution at all, which for general geometries reduces to a degree-sixteen polynomial and in practice to a numerical method with a seed. There is the question of what to do with several, which turns out to have no continuous global answer and, for a class of manipulators nobody expected, no local test either. There is the set where the map stops being locally invertible, which is a hypersurface and the one genuinely well-behaved defect in the article. And there is the question of what the map's image is at all, which has voids and a boundary and a much smaller subset in which orientation is also free.

Two of these four are commonly conflated and should not be. Singularities are where the differential loses rank; multiplicity is about the branch structure of the global map. The standard intuition, that solution branches are separated by singular surfaces so that a singularity-free path preserves posture, is a theorem for some arms and false for others. That distinction is fifty years old in singularity theory and about thirty in robotics, and it is still absent from most working practice.

Forward kinematics is a product of matrices. Inverse kinematics is the preimage of a point under a nonlinear map between two six-manifolds, and the asymmetry between those two sentences is the whole of this group of plates.

The tractable case is a design choice rather than a mathematical one. Pieper showed that when three consecutive joint axes intersect at a point, position and orientation decouple and the problem admits a closed-form solution; that is why the spherical wrist exists on essentially every industrial arm [24,92]. The general case resisted for decades. Lee and Liang, and then Raghavan and Roth, reduced the general 6R to a univariate polynomial of degree sixteen, which is tight: Manseur and Doty exhibited a manipulator with sixteen real solutions [93,96,97]. Efficient and numerically stable versions followed [94,98], and continuation and numerical algebraic geometry now supply certified counts for arbitrary geometries [99,100,101].

Experiment L5 works with a spherical-wrist arm where the eight branches are derivable in closed form. Across 4,000 targets generated as forward images of random in-limit postures, all eight branches are recovered exactly and the generating posture is among them every time, which is the correctness gate on the derivation. The same experiment then runs a multi-start damped-Newton solver on the same targets with sixty-four random restarts, and it finds 6.88 distinct branches on average, 86 per cent of the eight that provably exist, recovering all of them on 20 of the 25 targets tried.

That gap is the defect made visible. A numerical solver reports a lower bound on the solution set, never the set. This is not a criticism of numerical methods, which are what everyone uses: IKFast generates analytic solvers by symbolic preprocessing where the geometry allows, TRAC-IK runs a Newton method and a nonlinear program concurrently, and Levenberg-Marquardt formulations handle unreachable targets gracefully [95,102,103,104]. It is a statement about what such a solver can be asked. It answers whether a solution exists near a seed. It does not answer how many there are, and it cannot be made to.

The solver families are worth separating, because they fail differently. Algebraic elimination gives the complete set for geometries that admit it, and the subproblem decomposition of Paden and Kahan does the same for chains built from recognisable screw motions [20,105,106]. Closed-loop differential schemes drive the forward residual to zero with a feedback law and inherit the branch of their seed [107,108]. Generalised algebraic formulations and heuristic solvers such as FABRIK trade completeness for speed or for graceful behaviour on unreachable targets [109,110,111]. All three return an answer; only the first returns the answer set.

The standard mental model of inverse kinematics is a set of labelled branches, shoulder left or right, elbow up or down, wrist flipped or not, separated by singular surfaces. It is a good model for the arms it was built on and it is not true in general.

Experiment L5 quantifies the benign part. On a spherical-wrist arm with realistic limits the number of legal postures reaching a given pose is distributed across two, four, six and eight, averaging 4.96; 19.4 per cent of targets keep all eight and 18.1 per cent are down to two or fewer. The freedom this plate credits is real, and it is smaller than the branch count suggests, because limits remove most of it exactly where the workspace is most useful [116,117].

The non-benign part is a result from singularity theory. Whitney classified the stable singularities of maps between equidimensional spaces into folds, which are codimension one, and cusps, which are codimension two [112,118,119]. A manipulator whose singular set contains a cusp can move from one posture to another along a path that stays entirely in the non-singular region. Such manipulators are called cuspidal, and they are not pathological: El Omri and Wenger gave a recognition condition for 3R positioning arms, Wenger classified them and mapped the uniqueness domains, Innocenti and Parenti-Castelli demonstrated singularity-free posture change, and computer-algebra methods have since been used to classify the connected components of the non-singular configuration space exactly [113,114,115,120,121,122,123].

The consequence for practice is specific. The rule that posture is preserved along any path avoiding singularities is false, and it is a rule that path planners, teach-pendant workflows and offline programming tools rely on. The defect sits at codimension two, which is the reason it is so easy to miss: a cusp is not something one encounters by accident while sampling, and a robot can run for years without meeting one. It scores no detector because the offline decision procedure is a property of the design, not of the motion, and no local measurement taken during a trajectory reveals that a posture change is under way until it has happened [124,125].

A singularity is a rank deficiency of the differential of the forward map. That definition is worth keeping in front of the more operational ones, because it says immediately what kind of set the singularities form. Rank deficiency is one determinantal condition, so generically the singular set is a hypersurface in configuration space: codimension one, measure zero, with a well-defined notion of distance to it [125,128].

The classifications in the literature are classifications of the same object by different criteria. Gosselin and Angeles distinguish singularities by which of the two Jacobians degenerates, which matters for closed chains; Zlatanov, Fenton and Benhabib give a unifying framework covering the cases that the input-output distinction misses; Kieffer analyses the bifurcation behaviour at isolated singularities [126,127,129]. For a serial arm the practical taxonomy is coarser and geometric: boundary singularities where the arm is fully extended or folded, and interior singularities where axes align, of which the wrist singularity of a spherical wrist is the one every operator has met [1,2].

The image of the singular set is the boundary of the reachable workspace. That is why this plate and the reachability plate are two views of one fact: the frontier of what the arm can reach is exactly where it can no longer move in every direction [130,131]. It is also why the boundary is not a defect that engineering removes. It is the shape of the machine.

The detector is exact and inexpensive, which distinguishes this plate sharply from the two above it. The smallest singular value of the Jacobian is zero on the singular set and decreases continuously as it is approached, so distance to singularity is measurable rather than inferred [132,133]. The complication is not detection but interpretation, and that is the subject of the last group of plates: the scalar that measures the distance is not scale-invariant, and the decision it is used for is a decision about joint rates that have units the singular value does not.

Workspace analysis is the oldest quantitative part of the subject and it has a well-developed theory: Kumar and Waldron characterised the workspace boundary as the image of the singular set, Gupta and Roth studied how design parameters determine shape and the existence of holes, and the classification of workspace types by link ratio and offset is standard [130,131,134,137,138].

Experiment L6 makes both halves of the defect visible on a planar three-link arm with links of 0.6, 0.25 and 0.15 metres. Because the first link is longer than the other two together, an unreachable disc of radius 0.20 metres is forced by the link ratios alone, before any joint limit applies; it accounts for 4.0 per cent of the maximum-reach disc. Adding realistic limits brings the total unreachable share to 13.8 per cent. That void is not a control problem and no planner removes it. It is a consequence of the arithmetic of the link lengths.

The second half is larger and much less often drawn. Of the grid cells the tip can reach at all, only 4.6 per cent can be reached in every one of eight sampled headings. The reachable workspace and the dexterous workspace differ by more than an order of magnitude on this arm, and it is the position-only figure, the one quoted on datasheets as a reach radius, that describes the larger set. A task specified by position alone can be sited anywhere in the reachable set; a task that also fixes the tool orientation cannot.

This is the practical content of capability and reachability maps, which discretise the workspace and record achievable orientations per cell in order to place the robot or the task correctly [135,136,139]. The detector is a proxy for a specific reason: those maps are sampled. An exact description of the reachable set of a general six-axis arm is a semialgebraic set that nobody computes, so what is available is a discretisation whose resolution is a modelling choice and whose gaps can be either real voids or sampling artefacts [100,140].

VDifferentiating

The Jacobian, joint velocities, end-effector velocity, singular configurations. Linearising the map, and inheriting everything the map hides.

Differentiating the forward map produces the Jacobian, and with it the whole of velocity-level control: resolved rate, operational-space control, force-torque duality, redundancy resolution, singularity handling. It is the most practically loaded object in the subject and the linearisation is genuinely a simplification, because at a fixed configuration everything becomes linear algebra.

What the linearisation does not do is discard the defects of the map it linearises. It inherits every one of them and adds two of its own. The added ones are specific and both sit at codimension zero. The Jacobian's rows do not share units, so every scalar summary built from it carries an undeclared length scale; and the pseudoinverse, which is the obvious way to use it, is not integrable, so a closed task path does not produce a closed joint path.

The inherited one is the singularity, and this group is where it is finally priced. K12 establishes that the singular set exists and is measurable. K17 asks what happens when a controller has to run near it anyway, and the answer is the article's thesis in its most literal available form: the component of commanded motion along the unservable direction has to go somewhere, and damping chooses only whether it appears as an unbounded joint rate or as a bounded lag off the path.

It is worth noticing that three of the four plates in this group score exact detectors and sit at codimension zero. That combination, a defect you can measure precisely and cannot avoid at all, is the characteristic signature of differential kinematics, and it is why so much of the practice in this area consists of choosing and declaring parameters rather than of solving problems.

The geometric Jacobian is the most used object in the subject. It maps joint rates to the end-effector twist, transposes to map wrenches to joint torques, and its pseudoinverse is the basis of resolved-rate control [2,144,145]. Its singular values describe how well the arm transmits motion in each direction, and a family of scalar indices summarises that: Yoshikawa's manipulability, the product of the singular values; the condition number; the smallest singular value [88,141,146,147].

The problem is dimensional. For an arm with revolute joints the top three rows carry units of length and the bottom three do not, so the matrix is not a linear map between normed spaces in any canonical sense. Doty and colleagues set this out carefully and Schwartz and colleagues developed the general treatment of noncommensurate systems; Merlet's paper on parallel robots is the sharpest statement of the practical consequences [91,142,148].

Experiment L7 measures it directly. It samples six hundred configurations of a six-axis arm, computes each index in metres, then re-expresses the same robot in millimetres and recomputes. Over 179,700 configuration pairs, 22.91 per cent are ranked differently by the condition number and 19.41 per cent by the smallest singular value. The product of the singular values disagrees on 0.00 per cent, because scaling the length unit multiplies it by exactly the same factor of ten to the ninth at every configuration, which changes the value and not the order.

That contrast is the useful result. Unit covariance is not a technicality; it is the test of whether an index is measuring the robot or the spelling. An index that changes its ranking under a change of unit has a free parameter in it, and the responses in the literature are all ways of fixing that parameter honestly: a characteristic length, an explicitly declared weighting, or a geometrically defined measure derived from a Riemannian metric on the group rather than from the matrix entries [88,89,143,149]. The defect sits at codimension zero because it is present at every configuration; there is no posture at which the Jacobian's rows agree about units.

Resolved-rate control is the standard way to use a redundant arm: take the commanded task velocity, apply the pseudoinverse of the Jacobian, add a null-space term for whatever secondary criterion is in play, and integrate [144,154,155]. Klein and Huang's review noted the property that makes it awkward, which is that the resulting joint path is not a function of the task path [150].

Experiment L8 measures it on a planar three-link arm tracking a closed circle with closed-loop resolved rate. The tool returns to its starting point every cycle; the arm does not. Over eight loops of a hundred-millimetre circle the joint configuration drifts 17.73 degrees from where it started, monotonically, with no sign of returning.

The signature identifies the cause. If drift were an integration artefact it would scale with path length, and refining the step would reduce it. It does not. Running four nested loops through the same start posture with enclosed areas in the ratio 2.00, 4.00 and 8.01 gives drifts in the ratio 1.84, 3.35 and 6.14, against perimeter ratios of 1.41, 2.00 and 2.83. Drift tracks area. That is the signature of a holonomy: the quantity accumulated around a closed loop is an integral of a curvature over the enclosed region, and it is a property of the pointwise resolution rule, not of the numerics.

Shamir and Yomdin gave the mathematical characterisation of when a redundancy resolution is repeatable, and Baillieul's extended Jacobian and the general class of augmented-task methods are the standard answers: add enough integrable constraints that the joint path becomes a function of the task path [151,152,153,156]. L8 confirms this empirically. Adding a null-space term that pulls toward a fixed preferred posture leaves the drift at 2.59 degrees after eight loops, 6.8 times smaller than the plain pseudoinverse and, more importantly, bounded rather than accumulating. The freedom the null space provided has been spent on making the solution repeatable, which is the trade this plate records.

The wider literature on redundancy resolution is largely a catalogue of what to spend the null space on: obstacle avoidance, joint-limit avoidance by gradient projection, reduced-gradient formulations, saturation schemes that respect hard limits, and hierarchical quadratic programs that order several tasks by priority [157,158,159,160,161,162]. Each of these is a way of making the pointwise choice depend on something integrable. None of them recovers the freedom; they allocate it.

End-effector velocity

BUYS
A six-vector describing the instantaneous motion of the whole body, in the coordinates a task is naturally specified in.
DEFECT

PARKED AT
In the reference-point convention, and in the integrator that reconstructs pose from rate.

The twist is the right object and its subtlety is entirely in the linear part. The angular velocity of a rigid body is a property of the body: every point shares it. The linear velocity is not. What a twist's linear component records is the velocity of the body-fixed point that happens to coincide with the reference point at that instant, so moving the reference changes it by the cross product of the angular velocity with the displacement. This is the content of the classical screw axis and of the spatial and body velocity distinction [20,27,29,73].

In practice the reference point is the tool centre point, and the tool centre point is a configurable quantity that changes when a gripper is fitted. A velocity limit expressed as a Cartesian speed is therefore a statement about a point that the integrator can move. Safety standards handle this by specifying the point explicitly, which is the only available remedy: ISO/TS 15066 defines speed and separation monitoring against the relevant body points rather than against an unqualified Cartesian rate [164,165].

The second half of the defect is the one that produces silent numerical error. A twist is an element of the Lie algebra and a pose is an element of the group, so reconstructing a pose from a rate is an exponential map and not a sum. Integrating by addition leaves the group at first order, which is exactly what L2 measures: the raw explicit-Euler update accumulates an orthonormality residual of 1.603e-01 in one minute, and the exact exponential update accumulates none [39,51,166]. The corresponding statement in control is that proportional-derivative feedback on SE(3) has to be formulated on the group to be frame-independent, which Bullo and Murray set out [163,167].

Both halves are exactly detectable and both are conventions rather than computations, which is why they persist. Nothing in a twist's four or six numbers records where it was taken or how it is meant to be integrated.

Nothing directly. This plate records what differential control does when it meets the obstruction named at K12, and what the standard remedy actually achieves.

Damped least squares is the standard treatment and it is a good one. Replacing the pseudoinverse with a Tikhonov-regularised inverse bounds the joint rate near a singularity, at the price of no longer solving the problem exactly; Nakamura and Hanafusa, and independently Wampler, introduced it, and the variants since have concerned how the damping factor is chosen [168,169,170,171,172,173].

Experiment L9 runs the worst case: a planar two-link arm commanded straight outward along the direction it cannot serve as it straightens, approaching within a fraction of a micron of full extension. The smallest singular value reached on the path is 0.00337. Undamped, the peak joint rate is 13.3 radians per second, which is beyond what most industrial arms will accept. With a damping factor of one hundredth the peak rate falls to 2.19 radians per second, a factor of 6, and the peak tracking error rises from 0.016 to 0.57 millimetres.

The full sweep says something the two-point comparison does not. Across seven damping values the peak rate falls by a factor of 29 while the peak error rises by a factor of 655, and the product of the two, which would be constant if damping merely relocated a conserved quantity, rises monotonically from 0.21 to 4.66. Damping is a conversion and the conversion is lossy. Past the knee, additional damping buys a decreasing amount of rate reduction for an increasing amount of error, which is the quantitative case for the variable-damping and singular-value-filtering schemes that adapt the factor to the measured proximity rather than fixing it [168,170,171,173].

What no value of the factor does is serve the singular direction. The component of the commanded motion along it is not achievable at any joint rate, so it must appear somewhere, and the choice is only whether it appears as an unbounded rate command or as a bounded lag. That is the thesis of this article in its most literal form, measured on a two-link arm.

VIThe lab

Everything numeric in this article comes from one script. It is written against the Python standard library only, with no array or linear-algebra package: the eigenvalue routine, the singular values, the damped least squares and the closed-form inverse kinematics are all a few hundred lines of plain Python in the build directory. That constraint is deliberate. It keeps every step auditable, and it makes the results independent of what a numerical library does behind a call.

The rotation samplers use Shoemake's subgroup algorithm, which draws uniformly with respect to Haar measure on SO(3) rather than uniformly in any set of angles; getting that wrong would flatter every chart-related result in L1 and L3 [174]. Nothing here is intended as a substitute for the production libraries, which solve much harder versions of the same problems: KDL, Pinocchio, RBDL, Drake and MoveIt are the reference implementations, and their treatment of frames, model description and singularity handling is the practical form of everything on these plates [175,176,177,178,179].

Seeds are fixed, so the run is reproducible. Two experiments carry explicit correctness gates that abort the build if they fail: the inverse-kinematics derivation in L5 must recover the generating posture on every one of four thousand targets, and L1's sampler is checked against the analytic measure of the band around the Euler singularity.

Two experiments were rebuilt after their first run, and the rebuilds are disclosed here rather than hidden. L8 originally compared two loops of different radii whose centres also differed, which confounded the area effect it was meant to isolate; it now runs four nested loops through a common start posture. L9's first version ran along a path that never came close enough to the boundary to load the singular direction, so the undamped peak rate was unremarkable and the conclusion was not supported; the path now approaches full extension along the singular direction itself. A third claim was withdrawn rather than rebuilt: an earlier draft asserted that damping conserves the product of peak rate and peak error, and the measurement in L9 shows that it does not.

VIIThe ledger

Seventeen plates, three quantities each: where the defect lives, how big that set is, and what can detect it. Sorting by the first of those produces the result this article was written to test.

Ten of the seventeen defects sit at codimension zero. They have positive measure. They are not configurations, they are not avoidable by planning, and there is no sense in which a trajectory can be routed around them. Five sit at codimension one, on hypersurfaces that can be approached, measured and avoided. One sits at codimension two. One has no locus at all.

The five at codimension one are the ones the subject is famous for. Gimbal lock, the parallel-axis degeneracy of the Denavit-Hartenberg convention, the branch cut in a joint angle, and the two singularity plates are precisely the material that kinematics courses spend their time on, and they are precisely the defects that occupy a measure-zero set. They are teachable because they are localisable. A student can be shown the surface and told to stay away from it.

The ten at codimension zero are mostly not taught as defects at all. Frame relativity, the non-linearity of SE(3), the double cover of the quaternions, multiplicative error composition, the absence of a bi-invariant metric, the set-valuedness of the inverse, workspace voids and the dexterity gap, the Jacobian's mixed units, the non-integrability of the pseudoinverse and the reference-point dependence of a twist. Each is presented in the standard treatment as a property, a convention or a technique, and each is a standing charge that every configuration pays.

The detector column adds a second, sharper cut. Thirteen of the seventeen have an exact test, which sounds encouraging until it is read alongside the codimension. Seven of those thirteen exact detectors sit on codimension-zero defects, which means the test tells you the charge is being levied and offers no way to stop paying it. The exactness is real and it buys visibility, not avoidance.

Two plates have no detector, and they are the two where practice substitutes a stipulation for a fact. There is no correct weighting between millimetres and radians in a pose error, because SE(3) supplies none; and there is no online test for whether a motion is about to change posture, because on a cuspidal manipulator that change need not cross anything measurable. In both cases the honest engineering move is the same: declare the convention as a specification and check that conclusions survive changing it.

VIIIWhat the ledger says

The reason to read kinematics this way is not that the standard presentation is wrong. Every construct on these plates does what the textbooks say it does, and the field's choices are, on inspection, well made. It is that the standard presentation organises the material by what each construct is for, and that organisation systematically hides which difficulties were solved and which were relocated.

The relocations follow a pattern. A defect is moved out of the arithmetic and into a convention, which is how frames, reference points and length scales are handled. Or it is moved out of a single chart and onto a seam between charts, which is how Euler angles and quaternion sign conventions are handled. Or it is moved out of the kinematics and into a selection criterion, which is how the multiplicity of inverse solutions and the redundancy of a null space are handled. Or it is converted into a different currency at a lossy rate, which is what damping does to a singularity. In no case is anything deleted, and in every case the new home is somewhere less visible than the old one.

That is the practical import. A convention is invisible in the numbers, so it fails silently. A seam is a piece of switching logic that nobody writes tests for. A selection criterion is buried in a solver's seed. A lossy conversion has a knee that nobody plots. The measurements in this article are all of the same kind: they take the thing that was moved somewhere less visible and put a number on it, and the numbers are not small. 50 per cent of quaternion pairs need the sign check. 23 per cent of configuration pairs change places when the length unit changes. 5 per cent of a reachable workspace is reachable in every orientation. A hundredth of a degree of axis misalignment moves a Denavit-Hartenberg parameter by 225 metres.

If there is a single recommendation in this, it is to treat the codimension of a defect as a design variable rather than a curiosity. A defect at codimension one can be handled by avoidance, and the engineering question is how to measure the distance to it. A defect at codimension zero cannot, and the engineering question is entirely different: what is the standing cost, is it declared anywhere, and does anything in the system notice when it is paid. The subject spends most of its teaching effort on the first kind and most of its field failures on the second.
