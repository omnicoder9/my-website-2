Almost every security control a user ever sees — the firewall, the scanner, the incident-response retainer — is a response to a decision that was already made, badly, months earlier by someone writing code. Secure software engineering is the discipline of making that earlier decision well. It is not a phase, a tool, or a team. It is the claim that the security properties of a system are determined by its requirements and its design, and that everything downstream is either evidence that those properties hold or damage control for the fact that they do not.

The distinction matters because the two halves of the field have very different economics. Detection is a recurring cost that scales with the size of your estate and the ingenuity of your adversaries. Prevention is a one-off cost that scales with the number of times you make the same design decision. A parameterised query written once in a framework's data-access layer removes an entire weakness class from every application built on it. A web application firewall configured to catch SQL injection removes none of them; it merely raises the price of exploitation, forever, at your expense.

This article is about how that claim became a discipline: where its principles came from, what its standards actually say, which of its practices survive contact with a release schedule, and which of its most-repeated justifications turn out, on inspection, to rest on very little. It is written for engineers who have to do the work, and it assumes you would rather be told what the evidence supports than what the vendor deck says.

§1 The proactive premise

Secure software engineering (SSE) is the practice of building security into a system from its earliest phases: eliciting security requirements alongside functional ones, modelling how the design could be abused before it exists, choosing languages and frameworks whose defaults are safe, and writing code against standards that encode what the organisation has already learned. Its defining commitment is that the cheapest defect is the one that never gets written, and the second cheapest is the one a compiler or a linter refuses to accept.

The word engineering is doing real work in that phrase. Ross Anderson's definition of security engineering — building systems that remain dependable in the face of malice, error and mischance — puts malice on the same footing as the other two, which is what separates it from reliability engineering.1 A reliability engineer asks what happens when a component fails; a security engineer asks what happens when a component is made to fail by someone who has read the source, has a budget, and gets to choose the timing.

SSE is routinely confused with four adjacent things. The confusion is not pedantic: teams that conflate them consistently buy the wrong tools, hire for the wrong skills, and are surprised by the wrong incidents.

Read in that order, the disciplines form a chain in which each link is expensive precisely to the extent that the previous one failed. Software assurance regimes exist because purchasers cannot inspect the engineering directly; the NIST Secure Software Development Framework was written, in part, so that acquirers could express what they wanted from suppliers in a shared vocabulary.2 DevSecOps exists because the engineering practices are worthless if they cannot run inside a fifteen-minute pipeline.3

What the practice looks like in the working day is narrower than the rhetoric suggests. It is a data-flow diagram redrawn on a whiteboard when someone proposes a new integration. It is a rule in the shared linter that fails the build when string concatenation reaches a query API. It is an authorisation helper that takes a subject, an action and an object, and cannot be called without all three. It is a code review comment that says what happens if this identifier belongs to another tenant? before the feature ships rather than after a researcher asks the same question in a report.

§2 How the field learned this, painfully

The idea that security must be designed in rather than added on is older than the personal computer, and it was correct the first time it was stated. In February 1970 a Defense Science Board task force chaired by Willis Ware delivered the report that founded the field, concluding that security in resource-sharing computer systems could not be achieved by procedural controls around a machine but had to be a property of the machine's design.4 Two years later James Anderson's study for the US Air Force gave the idea its enduring mechanism: the reference monitor, an abstraction that must mediate every access, be tamper-proof, and be small enough to be verified.5 Every modern policy engine, every service mesh authorisation filter, is a reference monitor that has forgotten its own name.

In 1974 Paul Karger and Roger Schell performed a penetration analysis of Multics — then the most security-conscious operating system in existence — and found vulnerabilities that no amount of testing would have removed, because they were consequences of design decisions.6 Revisiting the work three decades later, the same authors observed that the industry had spent the intervening years rediscovering, at enormous cost, problems that had been documented and understood in 1974.7 It is a chastening paper to read in a room full of people planning a greenfield architecture.

Saltzer and Schroeder published eight design principles in 1975. Not one of them has been retired. What has changed is only the machinery through which each is expressed.

The synthesis arrived in 1975, when Jerome Saltzer and Michael Schroeder published a survey of information protection in the Proceedings of the IEEE whose section on design principles became the most durable single page in the literature.8 The 1980s formalised government assurance in the Trusted Computer System Evaluation Criteria — the Orange Book — which made assurance a purchasable rating and, in doing so, made it something suppliers optimised for rather than something users benefited from.9

Then the field's attention moved, because the threat did. The Morris worm of November 1988 propagated through a buffer overflow in the fingerd daemon and a debug feature in sendmail, disabled a substantial fraction of the machines on the ARPANET, and produced both an analysis that is still taught10 and a government report recommending coordinated incident response.11 The CERT Coordination Center was established at Carnegie Mellon within weeks.12 For the next decade the discipline was reactive by necessity: Aleph One's 1996 Phrack article turned stack smashing from folklore into a teachable technique,13 and by 1999 the industry had a shared namespace for talking about individual flaws in the form of CVE.14

The turn back towards construction happened at the end of the 1990s inside Microsoft. In April 1999 Loren Kohnfelder and Praerit Garg circulated an internal paper, The threats to our products, that proposed a structured procedure for finding threats during design rather than attacks during testing; the mnemonic was STRIDE.15 Adam Shostack has argued that this was the inflection point at which threat modelling became an engineering practice rather than an art,16 and Kohnfelder's own retrospective is candid about how much of the original method was too heavy for the systems it was aimed at.17

Three years later a series of worms against Windows produced Bill Gates's Trustworthy Computing memorandum, which subordinated features to security as a matter of stated corporate priority,18 and, more consequentially, a company-wide security push in which development stopped while engineers were trained and existing code was reviewed. The repeatable process distilled from that experience became the Security Development Lifecycle, mandated across Microsoft products from 2004 and described publicly by Steve Lipner in the same year.19,20 It remains the archetype of the genre.21

In parallel, the practice went public. OWASP was founded in 2001 as an open community for application security material,22 Gary McGraw and John Viega published Building Secure Software the same year,23 and McGraw's Software Security gave the field its slogan and its seven touchpoints — code review, architectural risk analysis, penetration testing, risk-based security testing, abuse cases, security requirements and security operations.24,25 In 2009 McGraw, Brian Chess and Sammy Migues did something more unusual: instead of prescribing practices they went and measured what real firms actually did, producing the first Building Security In Maturity Model from observations of nine organisations.26

The last decade added two things the earlier eras lacked. The first was a series of incidents that made the abstract argument concrete to people who sign cheques: Heartbleed in 2014 demonstrated that a large fraction of the internet's confidentiality rested on a library maintained by a handful of unpaid people;27 the 2017 Equifax breach exposed data on roughly 147 million people through an Apache Struts vulnerability that had been patched months earlier;28,29,30 SolarWinds in 2020 showed that a build system is a more efficient target than any of the products it builds;31 and Log4Shell in 2021 showed that a single logging call in a ubiquitous library could be an unauthenticated remote code execution primitive across most of the Java ecosystem.32

The second was law. Executive Order 14028 in May 2021 made secure development practices and software bills of materials a condition of selling software to the United States federal government.33 CISA and its international partners published Shifting the balance of cybersecurity risk in 2023, whose central argument is that the burden of security should sit with manufacturers rather than customers.34 And the European Union's Cyber Resilience Act, in force since December 2024, will make demonstrable security engineering a condition of placing a product with digital elements on the European market.35,36 The discipline that began as a research programme is finishing as a regulatory one.

§3 The principles that did not age

Saltzer and Schroeder's eight principles are quoted so often that they have become wallpaper. They deserve better, because each of them is a falsifiable claim about design, and each is violated routinely by systems whose architects could recite all eight.8

Three of them repay particular attention because they are the ones most often honoured in the breach.

Complete mediation says that every access to every object must be checked. The characteristic modern failure is to mediate at the wrong layer: an API gateway authenticates the caller and a route table decides which handler runs, and everyone assumes that authorisation has therefore happened. It has not. Authentication establishes who is calling; authorisation is a statement about a specific object, and it can only be made where the object is known. This is precisely why broken access control has remained the top category in the OWASP Top 10 across the 2021 and 2025 editions, and why object-level authorisation failures dominate API breaches.37 A checked route is not a checked record.

Fail-safe defaults says that the default is denial and access is granted by explicit permission. Its modern form is the argument that security features should not be a paid upgrade — a position CISA's Secure by Design pledge makes explicit by asking manufacturers to ship multi-factor authentication, logging and single sign-on without an additional charge.38,34 The economic reading is sharper than the technical one: when a vendor sells security as an add-on, the vendor's revenue depends on the insecurity of the default configuration.

Psychological acceptability is the principle the industry ignored for thirty years and then rediscovered under the name usable security. Whitten and Tygar's demonstration in 1999 that competent users could not correctly operate PGP 5.0 established that a mechanism nobody can use correctly provides no security at all.39 The developer-facing version of the argument came later and matters more for this article: Green and Smith's observation that cryptographic APIs are routinely designed as if their users were cryptographers,40 and Acar and colleagues' finding that the information source a developer consults measurably changes the security of the code they write — Stack Overflow producing functional but less secure results than official documentation.41 If your safe API is harder to call than the unsafe one, you have not built a control; you have built a trap with a warning sign.

§4 What actually goes wrong

Design principles are only useful if they are pointed at the defects that actually occur. Two public datasets carry most of the weight here, and they are frequently confused. The OWASP Top 10 is an awareness document organised by risk category; the CWE Top 25 is a ranking drawn from MITRE's Common Weakness Enumeration, a dictionary of individual weakness types,42 scored by a formula combining prevalence and severity. Neither is a test plan, and both are misused as one.

The 2025 edition of the Top 10 was the eighth, drawing on data covering more than 2.8 million applications and roughly 175,000 CVE records mapped to weaknesses, with eight categories selected from the data and two promoted by a practitioner survey.37 Its authors made a deliberate methodological choice to prefer root causes over symptoms — hence cryptographic failures rather than sensitive data exposure — which is exactly the choice a secure-engineering audience needs, because you cannot design against a symptom.

Four movements in that chart are worth reading closely.

Broken access control stays first, now carrying forty CWEs and explicitly covering the object- and function-level authorisation failures that dominate API testing. On average 3.73 per cent of tested applications contained at least one weakness in the category.37 This is the category no scanner reliably finds, because whether a given user should be able to read a given record is a statement about business intent that exists nowhere in the code.

Security misconfiguration climbs from fifth to second, which the Top 10 authors attribute to the steadily increasing share of application behaviour that is determined by configuration rather than code.37 Infrastructure-as-code did not create this problem, but it did make it possible to deploy the same misconfiguration to a thousand environments in eleven seconds.

Software supply chain failures enter at third, expanding the older vulnerable and outdated components category to cover the whole ecosystem of dependencies, build systems and distribution infrastructure. It is the category with the fewest observations in the contributed data and the highest average exploitability and impact scores derived from CVEs.37,43 That combination — rare in testing, catastrophic in effect — is the signature of a risk that testing is structurally bad at finding.

Insecure design slips from fourth to sixth, and the authors read this as genuine industry improvement in threat modelling and secure design rather than a reduction in importance.37,44 It is the only movement on the chart that can plausibly be attributed to the discipline this article describes actually working.

Alongside it, the 2025 CWE Top 25 was compiled from the weaknesses behind 39,080 CVE records published between June 2024 and June 2025.45 Cross-site scripting held first place for a second year and SQL injection moved up to second — two weakness classes for which complete, well-understood, essentially free preventions have existed for over two decades. Missing authorisation rose five places to fourth. Three buffer-overflow variants entered the list. CISA frames the list explicitly as an input to Secure by Design practice rather than as a scanning checklist: the point of knowing that cross-site scripting is still first is not to scan harder for it but to ask why any templating engine still permits it.45

A note on reading these lists correctly

Prevalence data is a measurement of what the industry can currently test for at scale, not of what is actually most dangerous. The Top 10 authors say so directly, which is why two of the ten categories are chosen by survey rather than by data.37 Business-logic abuse, design flaws and authorisation errors are systematically under-represented in any dataset built from automated testing, for the simple reason that a scanner has no model of what the application is supposed to do. Treat the lists as a floor, never as a scope.

§5 Threat modelling: asking how it could be abused

Threat modelling is the design-phase activity that gives secure software engineering its proactive character. It is also the activity most often performed badly, because it is usually introduced as a document to be produced rather than a conversation to be had. The Threat Modeling Manifesto, published in 2020 by a group including several of the practice's originators, deliberately declines to specify a method and instead states values — a culture of finding and fixing design issues over checkbox compliance, people and collaboration over processes and tools — along with four questions that any method must answer.46

  1. 1 What are we working on?
  2. 2 What can go wrong?
  3. 3 What are we going to do about it?
  4. 4 Did we do a good enough job?

The first question is answered with a model of the system, and the model that has survived best is the data-flow diagram: external entities, processes, data stores, the flows between them, and — critically — the trust boundaries those flows cross.47 The diagram is not documentation. It exists to make boundaries visible, because a trust boundary is the only place a threat can be defined precisely: it is where data or control passes from a principal you trust one amount to a principal you trust a different amount.

The second question is answered by systematic enumeration, and STRIDE remains the most usable scaffold because it is a list of properties rather than a list of attacks. Spoofing negates authenticity, tampering negates integrity, repudiation negates non-repudiation, information disclosure negates confidentiality, denial of service negates availability, and elevation of privilege negates authorisation.15,47 Attack lists go stale; properties do not.

Two things about that table are worth stating plainly. First, every entry in the right-hand column is a testable requirement, not an aspiration — which is what makes a threat model an input to engineering rather than an artefact of governance. Second, the exercise took one crossing. A model of a realistic service has dozens, and this is the honest difficulty with STRIDE: applied exhaustively it produces a combinatorial explosion, and Kohnfelder himself has acknowledged that the original method was a major undertaking for large systems.17 Shostack's account of running the practice at Microsoft scale is the most useful corrective: the methods that survived were the ones that could be taught to non-specialists and completed inside a normal design meeting.49 The practical answer is to scope by boundary risk rather than by completeness: model the crossings where the trust delta is largest, re-model on material design change, and accept that a shallow model of the whole system plus a deep model of three crossings beats a deep model of everything that never gets finished.

Other methods exist and are worth knowing. PASTA is a seven-stage, risk-centric process that begins from business objectives and is better suited to organisations that must justify security spend in risk terms.50 LINDDUN is the privacy counterpart, enumerating linkability, identifiability, non-repudiation, detectability, disclosure, unawareness and non-compliance, and it is the right tool when the design question is about personal data rather than compromise.51 Attack trees, introduced by Bruce Schneier in 1999, remain the clearest way to reason about a specific high-value target by decomposing a goal into the ways it can be achieved.52 For adversary behaviour rather than design flaws, MITRE ATT&CK catalogues observed techniques and CAPEC catalogues attack patterns at the level a designer can consume.53,54

The fourth manifesto question — did we do a good enough job? — is the one most programmes skip, and it is the one that determines whether threat modelling becomes a habit or a ritual. The minimum honest answer is a check that each identified threat has either a linked mitigation with a test, or an explicitly accepted risk with a named owner and a review date.

§6 Requirements that can be verified

A security requirement that cannot be tested is a preference. The perennial failure mode is the non-functional requirement that says the system shall be secure, or its slightly more sophisticated cousin, the system shall comply with OWASP. Neither can be passed or failed, which means neither can be scheduled, estimated, or argued about in a planning meeting — and anything that cannot be argued about in a planning meeting will lose to something that can.

The OWASP Application Security Verification Standard exists to solve exactly this. First published in 2008 and substantially rewritten for version 5.0.0, released in May 2025, it provides roughly 350 requirements across 17 chapters, each written to be verifiable, with identifiers in a chapter.section.requirement form so that a finding, a test and a piece of evidence can all point at the same string.55,56 The 5.0 release restructured chapters around how applications are now built and tested, pruned requirements that had become untestable or obsolete, and shipped a mapping from 4.0.3 identifiers because the renumbering broke every runbook that referenced the old ones.57

The mechanical practice that makes this work is turning selected requirements into three things at once: a story in the backlog, a test in the suite, and a line in the release evidence. A requirement such as verify that the application does not use unsupported, insecure or deprecated client-side technologies is a lint rule. A requirement about output encoding is a set of unit tests over the templating layer. A requirement about authorisation is an integration test matrix in which every role attempts every operation on objects it does and does not own — the single most valuable test artefact most teams do not have. Where source-level verification is impossible, the Web Security Testing Guide supplies the procedures.58 For mobile, the equivalent pairing is MASVS with its testing guide.59

The other half of requirements work is the abuse case: the user story's evil twin, written from the attacker's point of view, and one of McGraw's original touchpoints.25 Where a user story says as a customer I can reset my password by email, the abuse case says as someone with access to a customer's inbox for ninety seconds I can permanently take over their account, and the acceptance criteria that follow — token single use, short expiry, session invalidation on reset, notification to the old address — are requirements a developer can implement and a tester can check. Abuse cases are cheap, they need no tooling, and they convert security from an objection raised late into a specification agreed early.

§7 Secure defaults and the shape of a safe API

The most leveraged work in secure software engineering is not writing secure code. It is making insecure code awkward to write. Every weakness class that has been genuinely eliminated at scale was eliminated the same way: someone changed the platform so that the safe construction became the default construction, and the unsafe one became an explicit, visible, reviewable departure. OWASP's Proactive Controls organise the same idea as a developer-facing list of things to build rather than weaknesses to avoid,60 and Kohnfelder's book-length treatment — written by one of STRIDE's two authors — is the best single account of the design reasoning behind them.61

Injection: solved in principle since the 1990s

SQL injection persists at second place in the CWE Top 25 not because the fix is hard but because the unsafe API is still the convenient one.45 The fix is to separate code from data at the protocol level, so that user input can never be parsed as syntax.

unsafe string-built query
cur.execute(
    "SELECT id, total FROM orders "
    "WHERE customer_id = '" + customer_id + "' "
    "AND status = '" + status + "'"
)

The database receives one string and must decide what is syntax. A customer_id of ' OR '1'='1 changes the meaning of the statement.

safe parameterised query
cur.execute(
    "SELECT id, total FROM orders "
    "WHERE customer_id = %s AND status = %s",
    (customer_id, status),
)

The statement is parsed once; the values arrive out of band and can never be promoted to syntax. Identical in cost, and usually faster.

The engineering move is not to write the right version. It is to make the wrong version impossible to merge. A repository-wide lint rule that fails on any call to an execute API whose first argument is not a string literal takes an afternoon to write and removes the weakness class from every future commit. Where the query genuinely must be dynamic — a sortable column name, say — the value is validated against an allow-list of known identifiers, never escaped.62

Cross-site scripting: context is the whole problem

The reason cross-site scripting has held first place in the CWE Top 25 for two consecutive years, three decades after it was first described, is that the correct encoding depends on where the value lands.45 A value safe in HTML text is dangerous in an attribute, and a value safe in an attribute is catastrophic inside a <script> block or a href. Hand encoding therefore fails, because it requires every developer to reason correctly about context on every insertion, forever.

The answer is a templating engine that is context-aware and escapes by default — Go's html/template, Angular's default interpolation, React's JSX text nodes — combined with a Content Security Policy that removes the value of an injection even when one occurs.63,62 The residual risk then concentrates in a small number of explicitly unsafe escape hatches: dangerouslySetInnerHTML, v-html, template.HTML. Those names are themselves a security control. Every one of them is greppable, and a rule that requires a reviewer's approval on any diff touching them converts a diffuse weakness class into a bounded review queue.

Authorisation: the API that cannot be called wrongly

Broken access control is the top OWASP category and the one automation is worst at finding.37 The engineering response is not more scanning; it is an API shape that makes the omission of a check a compile-time or test-time failure rather than a silent one.

pattern deny-by-default object authorisation
class Forbidden(Exception): ...

def authorize(subject, action, resource):
    # Central mediation point. No matching policy means denial.
    for rule in POLICY.rules_for(action):
        if rule.matches(subject, resource):
            return rule.decision
    raise Forbidden(f"no policy grants {action} on {type(resource).__name__}")

def get_order(subject, order_id):
    order = repo.load(order_id)          # 1. load the object
    authorize(subject, "order:read", order)  # 2. authorise the object, not the route
    return support_projection(order)     # 3. return only what this caller needs

Three properties of that shape do the work. The check takes the resource, so it cannot be satisfied by a role claim alone — this is complete mediation at the object level, and it is what stops the identifier-walking attack that scanners call BOLA. The absence of a matching rule raises rather than returns, so a policy gap is loud. And the return value is a projection rather than the domain object, so the confidentiality decision is made once in a named function that can be unit tested, rather than implicitly by whatever the serialiser happens to emit.

The corresponding test is the one to insist on: a matrix that, for every role and every operation, asserts the expected outcome against both an owned and an unowned object. It is tedious to write once and it catches the single most common class of production security defect thereafter.

Cryptography and secrets: pick the boring option

Cryptographic failures sit fourth in the 2025 Top 10, and the overwhelming majority of them are misuse rather than broken primitives.37 The engineering rule follows directly from the usable-security literature: developers should not be composing primitives.40 Use a misuse-resistant library that offers one authenticated-encryption function with no mode selection and no nonce parameter; store passwords with a memory-hard function such as Argon2 or scrypt at parameters chosen against current guidance rather than folklore;48 and keep key material out of the repository entirely, because a secret in git history is a secret that has been published. Pre-commit secret scanning is one of the few controls that is unambiguously worth blocking on, since the cost of a false positive is seconds and the cost of a false negative is a credential rotation across an entire estate.

The test for a secure default

A default is secure when a competent engineer under deadline pressure, who has not read the security guidance, produces safe code by taking the shortest path. If the safe path requires knowing that the safe path exists, it is documentation, not a control. The corollary is uncomfortable: most security guidance is a confession that the platform is wrong.

§8 Memory safety, and what it proves

The clearest natural experiment in the discipline's history has been running inside Android since 2019, and it settles an argument that had been conducted in anecdote for thirty years.

Memory-safety defects — out-of-bounds access, use after free, double free — have historically accounted for around seventy per cent of serious vulnerabilities in large C and C++ codebases, and the pattern has been consistent enough across vendors that both the NSA and a CISA-led international group have published guidance urging migration to memory-safe languages.64,65 In February 2024 the US Office of the National Cyber Director went further, framing the choice of programming language as a matter of national security.66

Android's approach was not a rewrite. Rust became a platform language in Android 12, and from Android 13 the majority of new code was written in a memory-safe language — Rust, Java or Kotlin — while existing C and C++ was left largely alone and maintained with targeted fixes.67 Rust was the load-bearing choice because its ownership and borrowing rules move memory errors from runtime to compile time without a garbage collector, which is what made it viable in a systems context where the incumbent language was C++.68 The reasoning was that vulnerability density is highest in new code and decays as code ages, so changing the language of new code produces a disproportionate effect without the cost and regression risk of rewriting.

The results are unusually clean. The share of Android vulnerabilities attributable to memory safety fell from 76 per cent in 2019 to 35 per cent in 2022 — the first year they were not the majority — with the annual count dropping from 223 to 85 over the same period.67 By 2024 the share was 24 per cent and the count below fifty.69 In 2025 it fell below 20 per cent for the first time, and Google reported a roughly thousandfold reduction in memory-safety vulnerability density in Rust code compared with the platform's C and C++, alongside secondary findings that Rust changes required fewer review revisions and were rolled back less often.70

Three lessons generalise beyond Android, and only three.

First, prevention compounds while patching does not. A vulnerability found and fixed removes one defect. A weakness class eliminated at the language level removes every future instance of it, including the ones nobody would have found.

Second, you do not have to rewrite. The strategy that worked was to stop adding to the problem, which is available to every organisation with a legacy codebase and no appetite for a multi-year rewrite. The corollary is that the argument we cannot migrate, our codebase is too large is a non-sequitur: nobody is asking you to migrate the codebase, only the next module.

Third, memory safety is a floor, not a ceiling. Android's own near-miss — a buffer overflow inside an unsafe block in a Rust AVIF parser, caught before public release and mitigated at runtime by the allocator anyway — is a reminder that memory-safe languages remove a class, not a category.70 Nothing in Rust prevents broken access control, and broken access control is still the number one risk. The Android numbers are evidence for eliminating weakness classes at the platform level; they are not evidence that any particular language is a security strategy.

§9 Verification: what each technique can actually see

Verification is where secure software engineering meets its budget. The tooling market is large, noisy, and organised around the implicit claim that coverage is additive: buy one of each and the gaps close. They do not. Each technique is defined as much by its blind spot as by its coverage, and the blind spots overlap in exactly the place that matters most.

Static analysis reads code without running it and is the only technique that can run before the code works. Its strength is data-flow reachability — tracing untrusted input to a dangerous sink — which makes it genuinely good at injection and at hardcoded secrets. Its weakness is the false-positive rate and the resulting human cost. The literature here is unusually honest: Bessey and colleagues' account of commercialising a static analyser argues that developer trust is the binding constraint and that a tool which cries wolf is abandoned regardless of its true-positive rate;71 Johnson and colleagues found the same in interviews, with false positives and poor presentation cited as the dominant reasons developers stop using analysers;72 and Google's account of running static analysis at scale concludes that the deciding factors are workflow integration and a ruthless bar on which checks are allowed to block, not analytical sophistication.73 The engineering consequence is a policy, not a purchase: a small set of high-confidence rules blocks the build, everything else is advisory, and any rule that exceeds an agreed false-positive rate is disabled rather than tolerated.

Software composition analysis answers a different question — what did we include? — and is close to a solved problem technically and unsolved organisationally. It finds known vulnerabilities in declared dependencies, which is the entire category of Equifax-style failures, and it finds nothing else.29 Its practical difficulty is prioritisation: the number of open advisories against a mature dependency tree exceeds any team's remediation capacity, so triage must be risk-based. CVSS gives severity but says nothing about likelihood;74 the Exploit Prediction Scoring System supplies a data-driven probability of exploitation;75 and CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue supplies ground truth about what is actually being used against real targets.76 A defensible policy patches everything in KEV on a fixed clock, uses EPSS to order the rest, and treats raw CVSS as the tiebreaker it is.

Dynamic and interactive testing exercise the running system. DAST sees what the application actually exposes, including the configuration and the deployment, which is why it catches misconfiguration that no source-level tool can. IAST instruments the runtime during functional tests and gets better precision at the cost of requiring good test coverage to reach anything. Both are limited by the same thing: they can only test what they can reach, and the authenticated, multi-step, state-dependent parts of an application are where the interesting defects live.

Fuzzing is the technique with the best evidence base and the least adoption outside systems programming. Its original demonstration in 1990 — feeding random input to UNIX utilities and crashing between a quarter and a third of them — remains one of the most embarrassing results in the literature.77 Modern coverage-guided fuzzers evolved into continuous infrastructure: OSS-Fuzz has run against hundreds of open-source projects since 2016,78 and AFL++ consolidated a decade of research into a widely used implementation.79 If a codebase has a parser — and almost every codebase has a parser somewhere — fuzzing it is the highest-yield verification investment available.

Human review is the column that is strong where the automated columns are weak. No tool has a model of what the application is supposed to do, which is why business-logic abuse and authorisation errors are found by people or not at all. This is not an argument for more review in general; it is an argument for aiming the scarce review capacity at the boundary crossings the threat model identified, rather than spreading it evenly across diffs.

The metric that quietly corrupts programmes

Counting findings makes verification look productive and makes prevention look like it does nothing. A team that eliminates a weakness class at the platform level will show a declining finding count, which under a findings-based metric is indistinguishable from a team that stopped scanning. If a programme measures only what it detects, it will systematically underinvest in the only activity that scales. Measure escaped defects, mean time to remediate, and the proportion of services on the hardened platform; leave finding counts to the tool dashboards.

§10 The supply chain is part of your design

Modern application code is mostly not written by the team that ships it. That is not a lament; it is the economic basis of the industry. But it means that a threat model which stops at the application's own boundaries is modelling a small minority of the attack surface, and it explains why software supply chain failures entered the 2025 Top 10 at third place with the highest average exploitability and impact scores in the dataset.37,43

Four of those incidents deserve to be understood in detail, because each broke a different assumption.

Equifax (2017) broke the assumption that knowing about a vulnerability is sufficient. The Apache Struts flaw was disclosed and patched in March 2017; the intrusion began in May and continued undetected for seventy-six days, exposing data on roughly 147 million people.30,28,29 The failure was inventory: the organisation could not reliably enumerate where the vulnerable component ran. Every SBOM mandate since is downstream of that fact.

SolarWinds (2020) broke the assumption that reviewed source produces trustworthy binaries. The attacker inserted code into the build process, so the repository and the shipped artefact disagreed, and every downstream verification of the vendor's signature succeeded.31 This is the incident that made build-system integrity a first-class concern.

Log4Shell (2021) broke the assumption that a logging library is not security-relevant. A feature permitting JNDI lookups in logged strings turned any code path that logged attacker-controlled data into unauthenticated remote code execution across most of the Java ecosystem.32 The lesson is about transitive trust: nobody chose Log4j for its lookup feature, and most affected organisations did not know they had it.

xz-utils (2024) broke the assumption that the threat is technical. An actor spent roughly two years building a reputation as a helpful contributor, was granted co-maintainership of a compression library used by essentially every Linux distribution, and inserted a backdoor into the release tarballs that targeted SSH authentication.80 It was found by accident, by an engineer investigating a performance anomaly. There is no scanner for this. The controls that address it are social and procedural: multi-party review of releases, reproducible builds that reveal a mismatch between source and artefact, and adequate funding of critical maintainers.

The response has consolidated around three mechanisms, and it is worth being precise about which problem each solves, because they are routinely sold as interchangeable.

Provenance is the newer and more interesting layer. The in-toto framework defines how to attest to each step in a supply chain,83 and SLSA organises the resulting requirements into tracks and levels; the build track runs from L0, where nothing is claimed, to L3, where a hardened and isolated build platform generates provenance the build itself cannot forge.84,87

§11 The standards landscape, sorted

The documents in this field appear to overlap and mostly do not. They answer three different questions — what should our organisation do, what must this product withstand, and how do we prove either to a third party — and treating a document written for one question as an answer to another is the commonest way a security programme comes to produce paperwork instead of safety.

The NIST Secure Software Development Framework (SP 800-218) is the closest thing the field has to a common vocabulary. It organises practices into four groups — Prepare the Organization, Protect the Software, Produce Well-Secured Software, and Respond to Vulnerabilities — each decomposed into tasks with notional implementation examples and references into other standards.2,91 Crucially it is outcome-based rather than prescriptive: it tells you what must be true, not which tool to buy. Version 1.1 remains current as of 2026, and its practical weight comes from being the framework against which US federal software producers attest.91,33

§12 Measuring a programme without lying to yourself

Two instruments dominate, and the difference between them is epistemological rather than practical. OWASP SAMM is prescriptive: five business functions — governance, design, implementation, verification and operations — containing fifteen security practices, each with three maturity levels and defined activities, intended to be used as an improvement roadmap scoped to what an organisation actually needs.92 BSIMM is descriptive: it records what participating organisations were observed to do. The current edition, BSIMM16, reflects observations across more than a hundred organisations and describes 128 activities grouped into twelve practices under four domains — governance, intelligence, secure software development lifecycle touchpoints, and deployment.26,101

The distinction matters because the two answer different questions and are frequently misused as each other. SAMM will tell you what to do next. BSIMM will tell you what your peers are doing, which is not the same as what you should do — a point its own authors make, since an activity being common is evidence of feasibility, not of effectiveness. A programme that treats a BSIMM score as a target has confused a thermometer with a thermostat.

Beneath the models, the metrics that survive contact with reality are few. Escaped defects — security defects found after release, by anyone — is the only outcome measure that cannot be gamed by scanning less. Mean time to remediate, segmented by severity and measured from availability of a fix rather than from discovery, tells you whether the organisation can act on what it knows. Coverage of the hardened path — what proportion of services use the sanctioned authentication library, the context-aware templating engine, the attested build — is the leading indicator that prevention is working, and it is the number that should go up when finding counts go down. And threat model currency: the proportion of services whose model has been revisited since their last material design change. Everything else is instrumentation.

The organisational pattern that consistently correlates with these numbers improving is the security champion: an engineer embedded in a delivery team with allocated time, a route to the security function, and enough authority to stop a design.102 It works for an unglamorous reason. Security teams are outnumbered by developers by roughly two orders of magnitude in most organisations, so any model that routes every decision through the security team is a queueing system with an unbounded backlog. Championing distributes the routine decisions and reserves the specialists for the hard ones. DevOps research points the same way: the practices that predict high delivery performance are those that push decisions towards the people doing the work.103

§13 When engineering practice becomes law

For most of its history secure software engineering was optional, and its economics were governed by what Ross Anderson identified in 2001 as a misalignment of incentives: the party best placed to prevent a harm is frequently not the party that bears it.104 Regulation is the standard remedy for that class of market failure, and it has arrived.

Executive Order 14028 (May 2021) used federal purchasing power rather than statute, requiring software sold to the US government to be developed in conformance with secure development practices and accompanied by a bill of materials; NIST's SSDF is the framework against which producers attest.33,2 CISA's Secure by Design programme, beginning with the 2023 joint guidance and extended by a voluntary pledge launched in May 2024 with 68 initial signatories and seven concrete goals, applies reputational pressure instead: signatories publish progress reports against goals including increased multi-factor authentication, reduction of entire vulnerability classes, and improved customer ability to gather evidence of intrusions.34,38,105,106 The pledge remains voluntary and non-binding, which is both its limitation and the reason several hundred manufacturers signed it.

The EU Cyber Resilience Act is the significant departure, because it is statutory and it attaches to market access. Adopted in October 2024 and in force since 10 December 2024, it imposes essential cybersecurity requirements on products with digital elements, conformity assessment obligations, CE marking, and a duty of care across a defined support period.35,36 Its timetable is staged: reporting obligations for actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents apply from 11 September 2026, with an early warning due within 24 hours and a full notification within 72; the main obligations apply from 11 December 2027.36,107

The engineering consequence is specific rather than general. A 24-hour early-warning obligation is not a policy document; it is an on-call rotation, a triage rubric that can classify actively exploited under time pressure, and a communications path that works at 3 a.m. on a Sunday. A duty of care across a support period is a commitment to keep a build pipeline capable of producing and shipping a patch for a version you stopped selling three years ago. Both are engineering capabilities that take longer to build than the compliance documents that describe them — which is the argument for starting from the capability rather than from the clause.

§14 The economics of shifting left, honestly

Almost every business case for secure software engineering rests on a single claim: that fixing a defect in production costs somewhere between ten and a hundred times what it costs to fix in requirements. The figure appears in vendor material, in conference talks, and in a great many internal slide decks. It deserves more scepticism than it gets.

Laurent Bossavit traced the citation chains behind several of software engineering's best-travelled numbers, including the exponential defect-cost curve, and found that they generally terminate in secondary references rather than in data.108 The curve is most often attributed to Barry Boehm's Software Engineering Economics,109 where, on Bossavit's reading, the illustrative diagram is presented as an expression of judgement rather than as a fitted result — and subsequent authors reproduced the graph as though it were empirical. The strongest version of the claim, that the ratio is a hundred to one or a thousand to one, has never been established with anything resembling the rigour its ubiquity implies.

What survives the scrutiny

The direction is well supported and the multiplier is not. Later empirical work on defect reduction supports the general finding that finding defects earlier is cheaper, without supporting any particular exponential.110 Use the direction; abandon the number. A business case that cites a fabricated ratio is a business case that collapses the moment a competent finance function checks the reference.

There is, in any case, a stronger argument available that does not depend on a defect-cost curve at all. It rests on three observations.

First, some costs are not proportional to defect count. A design flaw that requires re-architecting an authorisation model after launch costs what it costs regardless of how many findings it generates, and the cost is dominated by coordination, migration and customer disruption rather than by engineering time. This is the category that threat modelling addresses and that no amount of late testing recovers.

Second, prevention is a fixed cost against an unbounded liability. A safe templating engine is paid for once. The stream of cross-site scripting findings it prevents is unbounded in time, and each of those findings would have carried triage, remediation, regression testing and release costs. This is the compounding effect visible in the Android data.69,70

Third, and increasingly, the cost is no longer discretionary. Under the Cyber Resilience Act, a product that cannot demonstrate conformity cannot be placed on the European market.35 Under federal procurement rules following EO 14028, a producer that cannot attest to secure development practices cannot sell.33 Where regulation binds, the question stops being what security engineering saves and becomes what it costs to be permitted to trade.

Anderson's economic framing remains the most honest summary of the situation: information security fails not principally because the technical problems are unsolved, but because the party who would have to bear the cost of the solution is often not the party who suffers when it is absent.104 Everything in the previous section is an attempt to move that cost back onto the party who can act on it.

§15 Machine-generated code and the same old defects

Assistants that generate code are now a routine part of development, and BSIMM16 records organisations extending governance, review and policy activities to cover AI-generated code rather than treating it as a separate discipline.101 That instinct is right, and the evidence supports it: what has changed is throughput, not the nature of the defects.

The first systematic study, by Pearce and colleagues in 2022, evaluated GitHub Copilot's output across scenarios drawn from high-risk CWEs and found that a substantial proportion of generated programs contained weaknesses — the assistant reproducing the distribution of the code it was trained on, insecure examples included.111 Perry and colleagues then ran the more important experiment, a controlled user study, and found that participants with access to an AI assistant wrote less secure code than those without, while being more confident that their code was secure.112 The confidence gap is the finding that matters for engineering practice, because it predicts reduced scrutiny at exactly the point where scrutiny is most needed.

Three implications follow, and none of them requires a new discipline.

The verification burden shifts rather than disappearing. If generated code carries a defect distribution similar to human code but arrives faster, the constraint moves to review and testing capacity. Automated checks that block on high-confidence rules become more valuable, not less, because they are the only part of the pipeline that scales with generation throughput.73

Secure defaults become the highest-leverage intervention available, for a reason specific to how these systems work. An assistant working in a codebase where every database call goes through a parameterised helper will produce parameterised calls, because it completes the pattern in front of it. The platform is now shaping machine output as well as human output, and the return on getting it right has roughly doubled.

The systems being built are themselves new attack surface. NIST published SP 800-218A in July 2024 as an SSDF community profile adding practices specific to generative AI and dual-use foundation model development,113 the AI Risk Management Framework supplies the governance wrapper,114 and OWASP maintains a Top 10 for large language model applications covering prompt injection, insecure output handling, and the agentic risks that arise when a model can invoke tools.115 The important observation for a secure-engineering audience is that prompt injection is a trust-boundary problem in a very familiar shape: untrusted data reaching an interpreter that cannot distinguish instruction from content. It is the 1998 injection problem with a probabilistic parser, which is why it is harder rather than easier — there is no parameterised query for natural language.

§16 A reference pipeline

Everything above resolves, in practice, into a set of decisions about what runs where and what is permitted to stop a release. The second half of that is the part organisations get wrong: a pipeline that blocks on everything is a pipeline engineers learn to route around, and an advisory finding with no owner is a finding that does not exist.

Four rules make the difference between a pipeline that improves security and one that merely generates evidence of effort.

Latency determines adoption. Checks that run on a pull request must complete inside the time a developer is willing to wait, which is minutes. Incremental analysis on changed files, dependency scanning limited to changed manifests, and full scans relegated to a nightly job is not a compromise; it is the only configuration in which the fast checks stay trusted.

Blocking conditions are agreed in advance and written down. The list should be short enough to memorise and stable enough that nobody negotiates it during an incident. A committed credential, a failed signature verification, a new critical finding in changed code, a failing abuse-case test. Everything else is a ticket with an owner and a date.

Exceptions expire. Every security programme accumulates exceptions; the functional difference between programmes is whether exceptions have end dates that are enforced by the pipeline rather than by goodwill. An exception without an expiry is a policy change made by whoever was on call.

The evidence is a by-product, not a project. The SBOM, the provenance attestation, the test results, the threat-model reference and the record of accepted risk should be emitted by the build that produced the artefact.84,86 Evidence assembled afterwards for an audit is both expensive and, in a meaningful sense, false: it describes what someone reconstructed rather than what happened.

§17 What this discipline cannot do

It is worth being clear about the boundaries of the claim, because overselling it is how security programmes lose credibility with the engineers they depend on.

Secure software engineering does not eliminate vulnerabilities; it changes their distribution. It moves defects out of the classes that automation and language design can address, and concentrates what remains in the classes that require judgement about intent — authorisation, business logic, design. That is a substantial improvement and it is not the same as safety. The Android data show a weakness class collapsing, not a vulnerability count reaching zero.70

It does not address the parts of the problem that are not technical. The xz-utils backdoor was a social engineering operation against a maintainer's goodwill and exhaustion, and no coding standard reaches it.80 Neither does it address the funding structure that leaves critical infrastructure dependent on unpaid maintainers, which Heartbleed made visible in 2014 and which has improved only in parts since.27

And it does not survive an organisation that does not want it. Every practice in this article can be performed as theatre: a threat model produced after the design is frozen, an ASVS mapping written to satisfy an auditor, a pipeline gate whose blocking conditions have been quietly emptied. The difference between the two versions is not visible in any artefact either produces. It is visible only in whether, when an engineer says this design lets a support agent read any customer's address, the schedule moves.

What the discipline does offer is unusually well established for a field this young. The design principles have held for fifty years.8 The claim that eliminating weakness classes at the platform level beats finding instances of them has moved from argument to measurement.69 The standards are mature enough to be boring, which is the correct state for a standard.2,55 And the question that started the whole enterprise — how could this be abused? — still costs nothing to ask, still has to be asked by a person, and is still, by a wide margin, the highest-yield thirty seconds available in a design review.
