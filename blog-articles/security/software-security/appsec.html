01
Definition

A discipline defined by what it doubts

Application security is not the practice of writing secure code. It is the practice of refusing to believe that the code is secure until something has tried to prove otherwise. That distinction — between the engineer who builds and the security engineer who tests, measures and defends what was built — is the organising fact of the entire field, and it explains almost everything about how AppSec teams are staffed, tooled and judged.

Secure software engineering is a property of the development craft: parameterised queries instead of string concatenation, framework-supplied authorisation instead of hand-rolled checks, a memory-safe language where the workload allows one. AppSec is the specialised function that runs alongside that craft and applies adversarial pressure to it. In practice this means operating automated scanners across source and running systems, orchestrating manual penetration tests, triaging inbound bug bounty reports, and maintaining the vocabulary and metrics that let an organisation say something falsifiable about its own risk. The function is simultaneously proactive — reviewing code and designs before release — and reactive, finding and confirming flaws that already exist.

The staffing model has converged across the industry on a hub-and-spoke shape. A small central team of security engineers owns the tooling, the standards and the hard judgement calls; a distributed network of security champions, embedded in product teams and usually developers by trade, owns the local context that no central team can hold. Neither half works without the other. Central teams without champions produce findings that nobody owns; champions without a central team produce inconsistent standards and no leverage.

The middle lane is uncomfortable by design. An AppSec team that produces no findings is either working on trivial software or is not working. A team that produces nothing but findings, with no route to remediation, has converted itself into an expensive alarm. The craft of running the function well is almost entirely about the space between those two failure modes, and this article is organised around it: where the discipline came from, the vocabulary it uses to describe defects, the standards that define “good enough”, the tools that generate the raw material, the human work that automation cannot do, and the programme mechanics that turn all of it into shipped fixes.

Why now Exploitation of software vulnerabilities has become the single most common way attackers get in.

Verizon's 2025 report put exploitation of vulnerabilities at 20% of breaches as an initial access vector, a 34% year-on-year rise that placed it just below credential abuse [1, 2]. Edge devices and VPNs accounted for 22% of that exploitation activity, up almost eightfold from 3% the previous year, with a median 32 days to full remediation [3, 4]. In the 2026 edition, vulnerability exploitation displaced stolen credentials from the top spot for the first time in the report's nineteen-year history, the median time to patch slipped to 43 days, and the share of CISA known-exploited vulnerabilities fully remediated by surveyed organisations fell from 38% to 26% [5].

Those numbers describe a discipline losing ground on throughput while gaining ground on visibility. The tooling is better than it has ever been. The queue behind it is longer than it has ever been. Understanding why requires going back to the point where finding software flaws became a repeatable activity rather than an accident.

02
History

From a worm to a profession

The field has a reasonably precise origin: November 1988, when a self-propagating program written by a graduate student exploited a stack buffer overflow in the BSD fingerd daemon, among other vectors, and disabled a substantial fraction of the machines then connected to the internet. The Morris worm made two things obvious that had been arguable before. Memory corruption in ordinary utility code was exploitable, not merely a crash; and a single defect in widely deployed software was a systemic problem rather than a local one. Decades later, the US Office of the National Cyber Director would still open its argument for memory-safe languages by pointing at the same class of bug and observing that it had vexed the digital ecosystem for over thirty-five years [6].

What the worm did not supply was method. That arrived in November 1996, when “Smashing the Stack for Fun and Profit” appeared as file 14 of Phrack 49 [7]. The article did something the security literature had not done: it explained stack layout, return-address overwriting and shellcode construction as a general, reproducible technique, with worked examples, on the assumption that the reader would go and try it [8]. Exploitation stopped being folklore. Within two years the same treatment had been given to web-tier defects — SQL injection in particular — and by the turn of the century a generation of practitioners existed who could reliably find flaws that developers could not see.

The institutional responses came in three waves. The first was vocabulary. MITRE's CVE list, launched in 1999, gave the industry stable identifiers for individual vulnerabilities; CWE, which followed in the mid-2000s, gave it names for the underlying weakness classes. Without both, no two scanners could agree that they had found the same thing, and no organisation could measure its own trend line.

The second wave was process, and it came from an unlikely place. In January 2002, following a run of worm outbreaks against its own products, Bill Gates circulated the Trustworthy Computing memo; the Windows division halted feature development and diverted its developers into security training, threat modelling, code review and penetration testing [9, 10]. Steve Lipner and Michael Howard formalised what came out of that push as the Security Development Lifecycle, which added security activities and deliverables to each phase of Microsoft's development process and required an independent final security review before release [11]. The SDL became mandatory for Microsoft products in 2004 and was then published, which mattered more than the internal adoption: Cisco, Adobe and others adapted it, and the idea that a company should have a named, auditable secure development process moved from novel to expected [12, 13, 14].

The third wave was community infrastructure. OWASP, founded in 2001, produced the artefacts that most practitioners now treat as the field's shared reference material — the Top 10 risk list, the Application Security Verification Standard, the Web Security Testing Guide, the Software Assurance Maturity Model — on an open-source basis that no vendor controls. The resulting split is durable: vendors supply tools, OWASP supplies the definitions the tools are measured against, and standards bodies and regulators supply the obligation to use both.

Two later events reshaped the discipline's centre of gravity from code the organisation wrote to code it merely imported. In 2017, attackers exploited an unpatched Apache Struts flaw (CVE-2017-5638) in a consumer dispute portal at Equifax; the patch had been available since 7 March, an internal instruction to apply it went out on 9 March, a scan on 15 March failed to find the vulnerable system, and the intrusion ran undetected from May until 29 July [15, 16, 17]. The eventual figure was roughly 145 million US consumers [18]. Then in December 2021, CVE-2021-44228 in Apache Log4j 2 — a logging library present, transitively, in an enormous share of Java deployments — turned a single string in a log message into remote code execution, and eight national cyber agencies published joint mitigation guidance within a fortnight [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Neither incident involved an exotic vulnerability. Both involved an organisation that could not answer, quickly, the question of where a given library was running.

The lesson of Equifax and Log4Shell was not that dependencies are dangerous. It was that an inventory you cannot query in an afternoon is not an inventory.

That is why the current era of AppSec is characterised less by new detection techniques than by asset intelligence: software bills of materials, build provenance, reachability analysis, and the increasingly regulatory pressure to keep all three current. The rest of this article follows that arc.

03
Vocabulary

CWE, CVE, CVSS, EPSS, KEV: five words that mean different things

An AppSec programme is a measurement system, and measurement systems fail when their units are confused. Five identifiers do most of the work, and practitioners who conflate them make predictable errors of prioritisation.

IdentifierAnswersCardinalityCommon misuse
CWE What kind of mistake is this? ~968 weakness types in the published dictionary Treated as a severity signal. It is a taxonomy, not a ranking.
CVE Which product, which versions, which specific flaw? One per disclosed instance; tens of thousands per year Assumed to exist. Most defects found in first-party code never get one.
CVSS How severe is it if exploited? 0.0–10.0, four metric groups in v4.0 Used as a work queue. Base score alone omits threat and environment.
EPSS How likely is exploitation in the next 30 days? Probability, recalculated daily Read as a severity score. High probability, low impact is common.
KEV Has exploitation actually been observed? Binary membership of a curated catalogue Used as complete coverage. Inclusion always lags the first attack.

CVSS version 4.0, released on 1 November 2023, restructured scoring into Base, Threat, Environmental and Supplemental metric groups precisely because so many organisations were running their remediation programmes off the Base score alone [24]. The OWASP Top 10 team noted a practical consequence of the redesign: the v4.0 algorithm no longer exposes separable exploitability and impact sub-scores, which is why the 2025 list still derives its exploit and impact weightings from v2 and v3 data across roughly 175,000 CVE records [25].

EPSS exists to answer the question CVSS cannot: of the enormous population of scored vulnerabilities, which ones are attackers actually going to use? CISA's original directive establishing the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue made the underlying case bluntly, observing that fewer than 4% of all CVEs have ever been publicly exploited, while among those that are, 42% are used on the day of disclosure and 75% within 28 days [26, 27]. Severity alone therefore mis-sorts the queue by an enormous margin.

Calibration Probabilistic scoring is useful and imperfect, and should be treated as one input rather than an oracle.

Independent analysis of EPSS against subsequent KEV additions found that only 19.9% of CVEs later confirmed as exploited held an EPSS score above 0.5 beforehand, just 8.3% ever exceeded 0.9, and more than 22% had no EPSS score at all prior to exploitation [28, 29]. A programme that fixes only what EPSS flags will miss a meaningful share of what is actually used against it.

The federal directive layer has itself moved in the direction of blended judgement. BOD 22-01 established the KEV catalogue in November 2021 with fixed remediation deadlines per flaw [26]. On 10 June 2026, CISA revoked both BOD 22-01 and the older BOD 19-02 and replaced them with BOD 26-04, which assigns each vulnerability one of four remediation windows based on four variables: whether the asset is publicly exposed, whether the flaw is in KEV, whether the exploit can be automated, and the technical impact an attacker gains after exploitation [30, 31, 32, 33]. The shift from a single deadline to a risk-tiered window is the same shift that mature private programmes made years earlier, and it is worth noting the direction of travel: the regulator is converging on the practitioner's model, not the other way around.

Everything in this section describes the vulnerability. None of it describes the application. Whether the vulnerable function is reachable from untrusted input, whether the affected service is internet-facing, and what an attacker gets after exploiting it are facts only the owning organisation holds. That is the gap the programme mechanics later in this article exist to close.

04
Risk taxonomies

The Top 10 and the Top 25 are not competing lists

Two annual publications dominate how AppSec risk gets communicated to non-specialists, and they are frequently mistaken for rivals. They measure different populations with different methods and are best used together.

OWASP Top 10:2025

The eighth installment of the Top 10 was announced at Global AppSec in Washington DC in November 2025 and finalised in January 2026 [34]. It is derived from data on over 2.8 million applications contributed by thirteen named organisations plus anonymous donors, with 589 CWEs analysed and 248 mapped into the ten categories [25]. The methodology is deliberately hybrid: eight categories are ranked from contributed testing data, and two are promoted by a practitioner survey, on the reasoning that testing data is inherently retrospective and cannot capture risks the industry has not yet built automated tests for [25].

Four changes matter operationally:

  • Broken Access Control holds first place, now carrying 40 mapped CWEs, with an average of 3.73% of tested applications exhibiting at least one of them. Server-Side Request Forgery, a standalone category in 2021, has been folded into it [25, 35].
  • Security Misconfiguration climbs from fifth to second at 3.00% of applications across 16 CWEs, which OWASP attributes to the steadily growing share of application behaviour that is determined by configuration rather than code [25].
  • Software Supply Chain Failures enters at third, expanding the old “Vulnerable and Outdated Components” category to cover dependencies, build systems and distribution infrastructure. It carries only 5 CWEs and the fewest occurrences in the data — but the highest average exploit and impact scores of any category, and it was voted a top concern in the community survey [25, 36].
  • Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions is new at tenth, with 24 CWEs covering improper error handling, logic errors and fail-open behaviour [25, 37].

Injection falls to fifth. It remains the category with the greatest number of associated CVEs across its 38 CWEs, spanning the frequency-and-impact spectrum from cross-site scripting to SQL injection [25]. A category moving down the list is a statement about relative prevalence, not about whether a given team can stop testing for it.

CWE Top 25

The 2025 CWE Top 25, published by CISA with MITRE and HSSEDI in December 2025, works from a different base: 39,080 CVE records disclosed between June 2024 and June 2025, scored by prevalence and severity of impact [38, 39]. Cross-site scripting (CWE-79) held first place for a second consecutive year, SQL injection (CWE-89) moved up to second, and cross-site request forgery to third [40, 39]. The notable movement was in authorisation: Missing Authorization (CWE-862) rose five positions to fourth, alongside gains for Incorrect Authorization and Missing Authentication for Critical Functions. Six weaknesses were new entries, including three buffer overflow variants, Improper Access Control (CWE-284), Authorization Bypass Through User-Controlled Key, and Allocation of Resources Without Limits or Throttling [40, 39].

How to use both Different questions, different lists.

The OWASP Top 10 answers “what should our awareness training, our threat models and our board reporting be organised around?” — it is a risk taxonomy over applications, and OWASP publishes it as a standard awareness document rather than a testing specification [41]. The CWE Top 25 answers “which specific weakness classes should our static analysis rules, our secure coding standard and our procurement language name explicitly?” — it is a weakness ranking over disclosed vulnerabilities. OWASP itself addresses the obvious question of why it does not simply publish ten CWEs: not all CWEs exist in all languages and frameworks, and common vulnerability types are spread across multiple CWE identifiers that different testers choose differently [25].

05
Standards

Verification standards: turning “secure” into a checklist you can fail

A risk list tells you what to worry about. A verification standard tells you what to test and what constitutes a pass. The difference is the difference between awareness and assurance, and it is where AppSec stops being advisory.

OWASP ASVS 5.0

The Application Security Verification Standard, launched in 2008, is the field's main open specification of testable security requirements for web applications and services. Version 5.0.0 was released on stage at Global AppSec EU in Barcelona in May 2025 — the first major release in six years, following 4.0 in 2019 and the 4.0.3 patch in 2021 [42, 43]. It expanded coverage to roughly 350 requirements across 17 chapters, up from 286 across 14, simplified Level 1 for easier adoption, added dedicated chapters for web frontend security and self-contained tokens, updated cryptography guidance to include post-quantum considerations, and did a pruning pass on requirements judged redundant or untestable [43, 44, 45].

Two structural details matter for anyone operating it. First, requirement identifiers are versioned: the canonical form is v<version>-<chapter>.<section>.<requirement>, so v5.0.0-1.2.5 unambiguously names one requirement in one release, which is what makes ASVS usable as contract language and as a report schema [42]. Second, the three levels are a scoping device, not a maturity ladder: L1 is what any application should meet, L2 is the default for applications handling sensitive data, and L3 is for systems where the consequences of compromise are severe. Choosing L3 for a marketing site is a misuse of the standard, not rigour.

ASVS 5.0 also deliberately declines to cover AI-specific requirements, on the position that the subject warrants its own standard; OWASP's Artificial Intelligence Security Verification Standard is the separate effort [45]. Practically, a large fraction of L1 and L2 requirements can be checked by automation — static analysis, dependency scanning and configuration review — which is what makes ASVS viable as a continuous CI gate rather than an annual audit exercise [45, 46].

The testing guides

ASVS states what to verify; the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide states how. WSTG v4.2 is organised into eleven testing categories containing hundreds of individually identified test cases, each describing the procedure, the evidence to collect and how to interpret the result; version 5.0 is in development [47, 48]. A well-specified engagement declares an ASVS level for scope and cites WSTG identifiers for method, so that findings reference both the requirement that failed and the technique that demonstrated it [49].

Around those sit the engagement-level methodologies. NIST SP 800-115 remains the standard reference for planning and conducting technical security testing and assessment, covering review techniques, target identification and analysis, and vulnerability validation, along with the handling of findings [50, 51]. WSTG itself points to SP 800-115 and to OSSTMM as complementary methodology references [52]. In a mature programme these are layered rather than chosen between: SP 800-115 or PTES for the engagement lifecycle, WSTG for the web-tier technique catalogue, ASVS for the verification bar.

NIST SSDF (SP 800-218)

The Secure Software Development Framework, published as SP 800-218 v1.1 in February 2022, occupies a different slot again: it is an outcome-based catalogue of practices for the producing organisation rather than a test specification for a given application [53, 54]. It organises 19 practices and 42 tasks into four groups — Prepare the Organization (PO), Protect the Software (PS), Produce Well-Secured Software (PW), and Respond to Vulnerabilities (RV) — and is explicitly designed to be integrated into whatever SDLC model an organisation already runs rather than replacing it [55, 56, 57].

SSDF acquired weight through procurement. Executive Order 14028 in May 2021 directed federal action on software supply chain security and tasked NIST with producing guidance; SP 800-218 followed; OMB Memorandum M-22-18 in September 2022, amended by M-23-16 in June 2023, then required agencies to obtain self-attestations of SSDF conformance from software producers, with CISA publishing a standard attestation form [53, 56, 57]. The effect is that a framework with no legal force of its own became the de facto vocabulary of vendor security questionnaires well beyond the federal market. NIST later extended it for AI development in SP 800-218A, a community profile covering generative AI and dual-use foundation models [58].

Reading the stack Four documents, four different questions.

OWASP Top 10 — what risks should we be aware of? ASVS — what must this application do to pass? WSTG — how do we test each of those requirements? SSDF — what must our organisation do, repeatedly, across every application? Confusing the fourth with the second is the most common failure: SSDF conformance says nothing about whether any particular application is secure, and ASVS conformance says nothing about whether the next one will be.

06
Regulation

When the standard becomes an obligation

For most of its history AppSec argued for budget on the basis of expected loss. That argument is now increasingly supplemented by a simpler one: specific controls are legally or contractually mandatory, with deadlines that have already passed or are imminent.

PCI DSS 4.0.1 and the client side

PCI DSS version 4.0 designated 51 requirements as future-dated best practices; that transition period ended on 31 March 2025, and every one of them is now a mandatory, assessable control [59, 60]. Two are of direct interest to application security teams because they extend the standard's reach into the browser for the first time in a meaningful way. Requirement 6.4.3 requires that all scripts loaded and executed on a payment page be inventoried, individually authorised with a written business justification, and integrity-assured. Requirement 11.6.1 requires a change-and-tamper detection mechanism that alerts personnel to unauthorised modification of security-impacting HTTP headers and of the payment page contents as received by the consumer's browser [61, 62, 63, 60].

The framing matters. Both requirements exist because of e-skimming: Magecart-style attackers who never breach the server at all, and instead compromise a third-party script that the merchant page loads [62]. A common and incorrect assumption is that hosting card fields in a payment processor's iframe transfers these obligations to the processor. It does not — the iframe is sandboxed but the framing page is not, and analytics, A/B testing, session replay and chat widgets all execute in the same top-level browser context, where a compromised script can read the DOM or overlay a fake input field [62].

The EU Cyber Resilience Act

Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 entered into force on 10 December 2024 and applies in full from 11 December 2027, establishing mandatory cybersecurity requirements for “products with digital elements” placed on the EU market [64]. Its first operational milestone arrives much earlier. From 11 September 2026, manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents affecting their products: an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware, a full notification within 72 hours, and a final report no later than 14 days after a corrective measure is available [65, 66].

Scope trap The reporting duty reaches backwards.

Under Article 71(2) and Article 69(3), the reporting obligations apply from 11 September 2026 to all in-scope products already made available on the EU market before the regulation applies in full — not only to new launches [66, 67]. A manufacturer therefore needs a working vulnerability-monitoring and component-inventory capability across its legacy catalogue more than a year before the SBOM and conformity-assessment obligations themselves bite [68].

Reporting flows through a single ENISA-operated platform to the manufacturer's national CSIRT, with the information made available to ENISA simultaneously [65]. In practice this converts what many organisations run as a discretionary PSIRT function into a regulated process with evidentiary requirements, and it makes the AppSec team's ability to answer “which of our shipped products contains this component?” a compliance dependency rather than an engineering nicety.

Federal directives as a template

CISA's binding operational directives apply only to US federal civilian agencies, but their risk models are widely borrowed. BOD 26-04, issued 10 June 2026, replaced the single fixed remediation deadline of BOD 22-01 with four windows — 3 days, 14 days, 60 days, or deferral to the next upgrade cycle — assigned by scoring internet exposure, KEV membership, exploit-automation feasibility and post-exploitation impact [30, 32, 33]. The directive's own rationale acknowledged the failure mode of the previous model: deadlines that landed before patches shipped, and vendors that sometimes declined to fix at all [33].

Programme design

Maturity models: prescriptive, descriptive, and the difference that matters

Verification standards evaluate an application. Maturity models evaluate the organisation producing applications. Two dominate, they share an ancestor, and they answer opposite questions.

Both descend from OpenSAMM, created by Pravir Chandra and released in beta in 2008 with funding from Fortify. OpenSAMM was donated to OWASP in 2009 and became OWASP SAMM. In the same period a separate group — Gary McGraw, Sammy Migues and Brian Chess — released BSIMM1, taking inspiration from the OpenSAMM beta but pursuing a fundamentally different design goal [69, 70].

OWASP SAMMBSIMM
PosturePrescriptive — what you should do Descriptive — what peer organisations do do

BasisCommunity consensus; open source, freely available Longitudinal observation of participating firms since 2008; commercially maintained
Best forBuilding or restructuring a programme from a defined roadmap Benchmarking an existing programme against industry practice

SAMM defines measurable maturity levels across its streams and does not expect an organisation to reach the same level everywhere — it is explicitly risk-driven, and it is not a compliance framework [71, 69, 72]. BSIMM's levels are largely a function of how widespread a given activity is across the observed population, which makes it a strong answer to “are we unusual?” and a weak answer to “what should we do next?” [73, 74]. The two projects maintain a published mapping between them, mapping BSIMM activities onto SAMM streams rather than onto individual SAMM activities, precisely because the granularity does not align [75].

Empirical work on maturity models is thinner than the marketing suggests, but the available studies are instructive. A survey of Norwegian public-sector organisations using a BSIMM-derived questionnaire found enormous variance — the most mature organisation implemented 87 of 112 activities, the least mature implemented nine, with a mean of 44 — and follow-up interviews found that what was typically missing was not tooling but a comprehensive strategy and any means of setting and evaluating security objectives [76]. Longitudinal BSIMM analysis also documents a structural shift: security activities have become diluted across organisations as DevSecOps practices spread, moving from the exclusive preserve of security specialists toward developers [76]. That is the security champion model showing up in the data.

Maturity models fail in a characteristic way: an organisation optimises for the assessment rather than the outcome, and ends up with excellent coverage of activities that do not reduce the number of exploitable defects reaching production.

The defence against that failure is to pair the maturity assessment with outcome metrics — escape rate, time to remediate, security debt trend — and to treat a divergence between rising maturity scores and flat outcome metrics as evidence that the programme is measuring the wrong things.

08
Automation

The toolbox, and what each tool structurally cannot see

Every automated technique in AppSec is defined by its vantage point: what artefact it inspects, at what point in the lifecycle, and therefore what class of defect it is capable of noticing at all. Choosing a stack is less about product comparison than about arranging vantage points so that their blind spots do not overlap.

Static application security testing (SAST)

SAST analyses source or compiled code without executing it, typically building a control-flow and data-flow model and searching for paths from untrusted input to a dangerous operation. Its advantage is coverage: it can reason about code paths no test case exercises, including error handlers and rarely used branches. Its structural limitation is that it has no notion of runtime configuration, deployment topology, or whether the vulnerable path is reachable in production. It reports what the code permits, not what the system does.

Software composition analysis (SCA)

SCA resolves the dependency graph and matches component versions against vulnerability databases. It is the single highest-yield tool in most estates, and also the one most prone to producing unactionable volume, because a CVE in a library is only relevant if the vulnerable function is called on a reachable path with attacker-influenced input. Veracode's data has repeatedly found third-party code to be the dominant source of unresolved risk: roughly 70% of critical security debt originates in third-party code and the software supply chain, and fixing third-party flaws takes about 50% longer than first-party flaws [77, 78].

Dynamic application security testing (DAST)

DAST exercises a running application from the outside, sending crafted requests and inspecting responses. It sees the deployed configuration, the web server, the load balancer and the authentication flow — things no static tool can observe. It is limited by coverage: a DAST tool finds vulnerabilities only in the parts of the application it can reach, which makes crawling and authenticated session handling the decisive quality factor. Modern API-heavy applications, where much of the surface is not linked from any page, are where naive DAST fails hardest.

Interactive testing (IAST) and runtime protection (RASP)

IAST instruments the running application and observes data flow from inside while functional or DAST-driven traffic exercises it. That combination gives it materially better precision than either static or black-box testing alone: it sees the actual value reaching the actual sink. Its limitations are operational — language and framework support, agent overhead, and the same coverage ceiling as whatever is driving the traffic. RASP shares the instrumentation model but serves a different goal: blocking exploitation at runtime rather than reporting the defect. RASP and WAF are mitigations. Treating them as remediation is how organisations accumulate the security debt described later.

Secret scanning, IaC and container scanning

Credentials committed to repositories, over-permissive infrastructure-as-code, and base images with known-vulnerable packages are all high-frequency, low-ambiguity findings, which is why they belong at the earliest possible gate — pre-commit hooks and pull-request checks. They are also the categories where a hard blocking gate is defensible, because the false-positive rate is low and the fix is unambiguous.

The practical consequence of Figure 6 is that tool selection should be evaluated on the shape of a tool's errors rather than on its detection count. Two SAST engines that make the same mistakes add volume without adding assurance. A SAST engine plus a fuzzer plus a bug bounty programme make largely independent mistakes, and it is that independence — not any individual tool's accuracy — that produces coverage.

Technique

Static analysis: what a taint rule actually asserts

Static analysis is the technique AppSec engineers spend the most time tuning and the one most often misunderstood by the developers receiving its output. Nearly every security-relevant static rule is a taint rule, and a taint rule is a claim about three sets.

Consider a search endpoint that supports a wildcard filter. The developer has done more work than a textbook example — there is an allowlist on the sort column and an integer cast on the page size — which is exactly the situation where reviewers relax and analysers earn their place.

# app/search.py — the vulnerable version
from flask import request
import sqlite3

ALLOWED_SORT = {"created_at", "title", "score"}

def search(conn: sqlite3.Connection):
    term  = request.args.get("q", "").strip()          # SOURCE
    sort  = request.args.get("sort", "created_at")
    limit = int(request.args.get("limit", "20"))      # sanitised by int()

    if sort not in ALLOWED_SORT:                    # sanitised by allowlist
        sort = "created_at"

    pattern = f"%{term}%"                              # PROPAGATOR: taint survives
    sql = (f"SELECT id, title FROM documents "
           f"WHERE title LIKE '{pattern}' "             # SINK: string-built predicate
           f"ORDER BY {sort} LIMIT {limit}")
    return conn.execute(sql).fetchall()

Two of the three parameters are handled correctly. limit passes through int(), which is a genuine sanitiser for this sink because a successful cast cannot produce SQL syntax. sort passes through set membership, which is the strongest form of input validation available. term passes through .strip() and an f-string, neither of which neutralises anything, and lands inside a quoted literal in the generated statement. The analyser's job is to distinguish these three cases, and it can only do so if its sanitiser set is accurate.

Expressed as a Semgrep-style rule, the assertion is explicit:

# .semgrep/sqli-python-sqlite3.yml
rules:
  - id: py-sqlite3-taint-to-execute
    mode: taint
    message: >-
      Untrusted request data reaches sqlite3 execute() without parameterisation.
      Use a bound parameter (?) rather than string interpolation.
    languages: [python]
    severity: ERROR
    metadata:
      cwe: "CWE-89: Improper Neutralization of Special Elements used in an SQL Command"
      owasp: "A05:2025 Injection"
      confidence: HIGH
    pattern-sources:
      - pattern: request.args.get(...)
      - pattern: request.form.get(...)
      - pattern: request.json
    pattern-sanitizers:
      - pattern: int(...)
      - pattern: |
          $X if $X in $ALLOWLIST else $DEFAULT
    pattern-sinks:
      - patterns:
          - pattern: $CONN.execute($SQL, ...)
          - pattern-not: $CONN.execute("...", ...)

Read the rule as an argument rather than a configuration file. It asserts that these three expressions produce attacker-controlled values; that a successful integer cast or an allowlist membership test breaks the taint; and that reaching execute() with anything other than a fully literal statement is a defect. Each clause is falsifiable, and each false positive that reaches a developer is evidence that one of them is wrong — almost always a sanitiser the codebase uses that the rule does not know about.

Remediation Parameterisation, not escaping.

The fix is to stop building the predicate as text. Bind the value and let the driver handle quoting; keep the allowlist for the identifier, because identifiers cannot be bound.

# app/search.py — the fixed version
def search(conn: sqlite3.Connection):
    term  = request.args.get("q", "").strip()
    sort  = request.args.get("sort", "created_at")
    limit = min(int(request.args.get("limit", "20")), 100)
    if sort not in ALLOWED_SORT:
        sort = "created_at"

    sql = f"SELECT id, title FROM documents WHERE title LIKE ? ORDER BY {sort} LIMIT ?"
    return conn.execute(sql, (f"%{term}%", limit)).fetchall()

Soundness, completeness and the tuning treadmill

Static analysis inherits a hard theoretical limit: deciding whether a given program path is feasible is undecidable in general, so every practical analyser approximates. An analyser that over-approximates — assuming paths are feasible when they may not be — reports vulnerabilities that cannot occur. One that under-approximates stays quiet about real ones. Vendors choose a point on that spectrum and then adjust it per rule.

The operational consequence is that a SAST deployment is a continuous curation exercise, not an installation. The work consists of teaching the analyser the codebase's own sanitisers and framework idioms, suppressing rules whose preconditions do not hold in this architecture, and maintaining suppressions as auditable artefacts with expiry dates rather than as permanent silence. Where this is done well, developers treat the tool's output as signal. Where it is not, the tool becomes a compliance formality that everyone learns to click past — which is worse than not running it, because the organisation now believes it has coverage.

What static analysis cannot find

No taint analyser will find a business logic flaw, because there is no sink. If an endpoint correctly parameterises every query and correctly escapes every output, but permits a user to apply another tenant's discount code by supplying its identifier, every rule in the ruleset passes. That class — broken access control, which remains the highest-ranked risk in the OWASP list with 40 mapped CWEs [25] — is found by humans who understand what the application is supposed to mean, or it is not found at all before an attacker finds it.

Technique

Dynamic testing and fuzzing: letting the program answer

Dynamic techniques give up the static analyser's total visibility in exchange for the one thing it can never have: ground truth. A crash is not a hypothesis about a path being feasible. It is the path, executed.

DAST in practice

The quality of a dynamic scan is dominated by coverage, and coverage is dominated by two unglamorous problems: authentication and discovery. A scanner that cannot maintain an authenticated session tests the login page and the marketing pages. A scanner that discovers endpoints only by crawling rendered HTML will miss most of a modern application's surface, because that surface is an API described by an OpenAPI document, a GraphQL schema, or nothing at all. Serious DAST deployment therefore looks less like pointing a tool at a URL and more like feeding the tool a machine-readable inventory of endpoints, a set of working credentials for each role, and a seeded corpus of valid requests captured from functional tests.

The second discipline is environment separation. DAST is intrusive by nature — NIST's guidance is explicit that testing provides a more accurate picture of security posture than examination but carries real potential to affect the target systems [51] — which is why scans belong against a production-like staging environment with production-like data volumes and synthetic records, and why the scope, timing and rollback plan should be agreed in writing before the first request.

Coverage-guided fuzzing

Fuzzing occupies a different niche: it is the highest-yield automated technique for memory-safety and parser defects in native code, and it works by treating the program's own instrumentation as a reward signal.

A fuzz target is a function that maps an arbitrary byte string onto one execution of the code under test. Writing good ones is the whole skill: the target must be fast, deterministic, and positioned at a genuine trust boundary.

// fuzz/parse_header_fuzzer.cc — a libFuzzer entry point
#include <stddef.h>
#include <stdint.h>
#include "header_parser.h"

extern "C" int LLVMFuzzerTestOneInput(const uint8_t *data, size_t size) {
  // Reject inputs that cannot represent a header; keeps the corpus focused.
  if (size < 4 || size > 64 * 1024) return 0;

  Header h;
  // The parser is the trust boundary: this data arrives off the network.
  if (header_parse(data, size, &h) == HEADER_OK) {
    // Exercise the accessors too — bugs often live past the parse.
    (void)header_field_count(&h);
    header_free(&h);
  }
  return 0;  // non-zero is reserved for future use
}

# build with AddressSanitizer + UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer, then run
clang++ -g -O1 -fsanitize=fuzzer,address,undefined \
        -fno-omit-frame-pointer \
        fuzz/parse_header_fuzzer.cc header_parser.c -o parse_header_fuzzer

./parse_header_fuzzer corpus/ -max_total_time=3600 -print_final_stats=1

Without sanitisers, a fuzzer detects only the bugs that happen to crash the process; a heap overflow that quietly corrupts adjacent memory will run to completion and be recorded as a pass. AddressSanitizer catches spatial and temporal memory errors, MemorySanitizer catches uninitialised reads, UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer catches undefined behaviour, and ThreadSanitizer catches data races. The combination of instrumentation and sanitisation is what makes the technique work; either alone is substantially weaker.

Evidence Continuous fuzzing at ecosystem scale.

Google's OSS-Fuzz has run since December 2016. As of May 2025 it had helped identify and fix over 13,000 vulnerabilities and 50,000 bugs across more than 1,000 projects, supporting libFuzzer, AFL++ and Honggfuzz in combination with sanitisers, and extending beyond C and C++ to Go, Rust, Python, Java and JavaScript [79, 80]. An empirical study of 23,907 OSS-Fuzz bugs across 316 projects found that the service is often effective at finding bugs quickly and that developers are usually quick to patch them [81].

The counterpoint is equally important. GitHub Security Lab has documented serious vulnerabilities surviving for years in projects enrolled in OSS-Fuzz for more than three years — typically because researchers and harness authors concentrate on the obvious attack surface, such as media decoding, while the encoding side receives far less attention [82]. Continuous fuzzing measures the harnesses you wrote. Code that no harness reaches is not being fuzzed, no matter how long the campaign has run.

The technique is also now partly automated at the harness-writing layer. Google reported using large language models to generate and improve fuzz targets, adding coverage across 272 C and C++ projects and surfacing 26 vulnerabilities, including an out-of-bounds write in OpenSSL (CVE-2024-9143) that had likely been present for two decades and was not reachable by any existing human-written target [83].

The human layer

Threat modelling, code review and penetration testing

Every automated technique described so far shares a limitation: it can only find defects that someone has already characterised as a pattern. The categories that matter most in current data — broken access control, insecure design, authorisation failures — are precisely the categories that resist characterisation, because whether a given operation should be permitted is a fact about the business, not about the code.

Threat modelling

Threat modelling is the only AppSec activity that can run before any code exists, and the only one that reliably finds design-level flaws. The workable version is short and structured: agree on what is being built and where the trust boundaries are; enumerate what can go wrong, usually with a mnemonic such as STRIDE to force breadth; decide what to do about each; and check that the decisions survived contact with the implementation.

Its effect shows up in the data. OWASP attributes the fall of Insecure Design from fourth to sixth place in the 2025 list to noticeable industry improvement in threat modelling and a greater emphasis on secure design [25]. That is a rare instance of a proactive practice producing a measurable movement in a prevalence-based ranking.

The two failure modes are symmetrical. Models produced once, at project inception, by a security engineer working alone, describe a system that no longer exists within two quarters. Models that attempt exhaustive enumeration of every conceivable threat produce documents nobody reads. A model that fits on one page, is owned by the team, and is revisited when the architecture changes outperforms both.

Secure code review

Manual review is expensive and should therefore be targeted rather than uniform. The highest return comes from reviewing the code that decides things: authentication and session handling, authorisation checks, cryptographic operations, and anything that parses input from a trust boundary. Review of code that merely moves data between well-typed structures is rarely worth the hour.

The most useful discipline in review is to read for the absence of code rather than the presence of bad code. Injection flaws are visible; a missing authorisation check is invisible, because nothing is there to see. Reviewers who work from the specification — “who is allowed to call this, and where is that enforced?” — find defects that reviewers working from the diff do not.

Penetration testing

A penetration test is a time-boxed adversarial engagement conducted by people, and its value is almost entirely a function of how well it is scoped. NIST SP 800-115 remains the standard reference for the engagement lifecycle, covering planning, target identification and analysis, vulnerability validation and the handling of findings [50, 51]; PTES describes a comparable lifecycle from pre-engagement through intelligence gathering, threat modelling, vulnerability analysis, exploitation, post-exploitation and reporting [49]. WSTG sits inside that lifecycle as the web-tier technique catalogue rather than as a competing methodology [52, 49].

Engagement typeQuestion it answersTypical cadenceFailure mode

The report is not the deliverable. The deliverable is the set of tickets in the development backlog, each with an owner and a date, and the regression test that will fail if the fix is ever reverted.

Two scoping decisions determine most of the value. Grant testers source code and credentials for every role unless the specific objective is to measure external discoverability — a black-box test of a system whose source you could have provided spends its first third rediscovering your architecture. And require reproduction steps precise enough to be converted into an automated regression test, so that the finding becomes a permanent property of the test suite rather than a line in a PDF.

External researchers

Bug bounty and coordinated disclosure

Every organisation running internet-facing software has external people testing it. The only decision is whether those people have a way to tell you what they found, and what happens when they do. That is the entire premise of the vulnerability disclosure programme, and it is why VDPs and bug bounties are treated here as an AppSec function rather than a marketing one.

A thirty-year adoption curve

The first formal programme was Netscape's, launched on 10 October 1995, offering cash rewards for security bugs found in the Navigator 2.0 beta [84, 85]. It ran until the final release and then the idea lay largely dormant for seven years. iDefense launched a broker-style programme in 2002, paying researchers and acting as an intermediary with vendors; Mozilla launched a direct programme in 2004 offering $500 for critical vulnerabilities in its software, and that programme is still running [85, 86]. TippingPoint's Zero Day Initiative followed in 2005 as a competing intermediary [85].

Mainstream adoption arrived between 2010 and 2013. Google's Vulnerability Reward Program launched in 2010, initially scoped to Chrome, followed by Facebook in 2011; HackerOne and Bugcrowd were both founded in 2012, converting programme operation into a service that organisations could buy rather than build; Microsoft launched its first bounty in 2013 after what its architect describes as three years of internal work on data analysis, game theory and economics rather than a single blog post and a budget line [84, 85, 87]. Government adoption followed with Hack the Pentagon in 2016, and with it the normalisation of disclosure programmes in regulated and public-sector contexts [87].

What a programme is actually buying

The economics are frequently misunderstood. A bounty does not buy vulnerabilities; the vulnerabilities exist either way. It buys a channel through which a researcher who has already found one chooses to tell you rather than to sell, publish or ignore. The competing options are real, and the programme is competing on total experience — response time, quality of triage, absence of legal risk, and public credit — at least as much as on price.

This has direct consequences for programme design:

  • Do not start with a bounty. Start with a published disclosure policy, a monitored security contact, and a stated response time. A bounty attached to a queue nobody reads converts goodwill into public complaints.
  • Safe harbour language is the highest-leverage clause. A researcher who is unsure whether testing your product is legally safe will test something else. State plainly what is in scope, what techniques are permitted, and that good-faith research within the policy will not be met with legal action.
  • Triage capacity is the binding constraint. An open programme generates a substantial volume of duplicates, out-of-scope reports and low-quality submissions. Budget the engineering hours before opening the channel.
  • Bounties are the last layer, not the first. Paying market rate for defects an in-house scanner would have caught in CI is an expensive way to learn that the scanner is not wired into the pipeline.

The internal counterpart is coordinated disclosure discipline: a reserved CVE, a fix developed and regression-tested under embargo, an advisory that names affected versions precisely, and a release that lands simultaneously with the advisory. For EU-market products, from 11 September 2026 that process also acquires a statutory clock, with an early warning within 24 hours of awareness of an actively exploited vulnerability [65, 66].

Operations

Running the programme: triage, prioritisation, metrics

The tools generate findings. The programme decides which findings become work. That decision procedure — not the tool stack — is what distinguishes an effective AppSec function from an expensive one.

A prioritisation function, written down

Prioritisation is frequently held in individual heads, which makes it unauditable and inconsistent between triagers. Writing it as code has three benefits: it can be reviewed, it can be argued with using specific counter-examples, and it produces the same answer at 2am as at 2pm. The following implements a risk-tiered model in the shape CISA adopted in BOD 26-04 — exposure, observed exploitation, exploit automatability and post-exploitation impact, mapped onto four remediation windows [30, 32, 33].

"""Risk-tiered triage for application security findings.

Turns raw scanner output into an ordered work queue by combining severity with
the three facts that only the owning organisation holds: is the affected code
reachable, is the asset exposed, and what does an attacker gain.

Mirrors the four-window model CISA adopted in BOD 26-04.
"""
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Iterable, Optional

# Remediation windows, in days. None means "defer to next upgrade cycle".
WINDOWS = {"P1": 3, "P2": 14, "P3": 60, "P4": None}


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Finding:
    ident: str            # CVE id, or an internal id for first-party defects
    component: str
    cvss: float           # CVSS v4.0 base score
    epss: Optional[float] # 30-day exploitation probability; None for first-party defects
    in_kev: bool          # observed exploitation in the wild
    internet_facing: bool # is the hosting asset publicly reachable
    reachable: bool       # is the vulnerable function on an executed code path
    automatable: bool     # can exploitation be scripted without target-specific work
    impact: str           # "full", "partial" or "limited" post-exploitation control


def exploitation_likelihood(f: Finding) -> str:
    """Collapse the exploitation signals into one ordinal judgement."""
    if f.in_kev:
        return "observed"
    if f.epss is None:
        # First-party defects have no CVE and therefore no EPSS score. The
        # public feeds are silent about your own code; judge it directly.
        return "likely" if (f.automatable and f.reachable) else "possible"
    if f.epss >= 0.10 or (f.automatable and f.cvss >= 8.0):
        return "likely"
    if f.epss >= 0.01:
        return "possible"
    return "unlikely"


def tier(f: Finding) -> str:
    """Assign a remediation window. Unreachable code is never P1."""
    likelihood = exploitation_likelihood(f)
    exposed = f.internet_facing and f.reachable
    severe = f.impact == "full" or f.cvss >= 9.0

    if not f.reachable:
        # Present in the artefact but not on any executed path: real debt,
        # not an incident. Fix it on the normal upgrade cadence.
        return "P3" if likelihood == "observed" else "P4"
    if exposed and likelihood == "observed":
        return "P1"
    if exposed and likelihood == "likely" and severe:
        return "P1"
    if exposed and likelihood in ("likely", "possible"):
        return "P2"
    if likelihood == "observed":
        return "P2"
    if severe or likelihood == "likely":
        return "P3"
    return "P4"


def queue(findings: Iterable[Finding]) -> list[tuple[str, Finding]]:
    order = {"P1": 0, "P2": 1, "P3": 2, "P4": 3}
    scored = [(tier(f), f) for f in findings]
    return sorted(scored,
                  key=lambda t: (order[t[0]], -(t[1].epss or 0.0), -t[1].cvss))


BACKLOG = [
    Finding("CVE-2021-44228", "log4j-core 2.14.1", 10.0, 0.944, True,
            True, True, True, "full"),
    Finding("CVE-2017-5638", "struts2-core 2.5.10", 9.8, 0.945, True,
            False, True, True, "full"),
    Finding("CVE-2024-XXXX1", "image-codec 1.2.0", 8.8, 0.004, False,
            True, False, False, "partial"),
    Finding("CVE-2024-XXXX2", "auth-middleware 3.1.2", 7.5, 0.180, False,
            True, True, True, "partial"),
    Finding("APPSEC-4471", "reports/export.py (IDOR)", 6.5, None, False,
            True, True, True, "partial"),
    Finding("CVE-2023-XXXX3", "build-tooling 0.9.4", 9.1, 0.002, False,
            False, True, False, "full"),
    Finding("CVE-2024-XXXX4", "xml-parser 4.0.1", 5.3, 0.031, False,
            True, True, False, "limited"),
]

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"{'TIER':<5} {'WINDOW':<9} {'FINDING':<22} {'CVSS':>5} {'EPSS':>6}"
          f"  {'LIKELIHOOD':<10} COMPONENT")
    print("-" * 96)
    for t, f in queue(BACKLOG):
        w = WINDOWS[t]
        window = f"{w}d" if w else "upgrade"
        epss = f"{f.epss:.3f}" if f.epss is not None else "  n/a"
        print(f"{t:<5} {window:<9} {f.ident:<22} {f.cvss:>5.1f} {epss:>6}"
              f"  {exploitation_likelihood(f):<10} {f.component}")

    counts = {}
    for t, _ in queue(BACKLOG):
        counts[t] = counts.get(t, 0) + 1
    print("-" * 96)
    print("distribution:", "  ".join(f"{k}={counts.get(k, 0)}"
                                     for k in ("P1", "P2", "P3", "P4")))

Three results are worth reading carefully, because each contradicts a common heuristic.

  • The highest CVSS score in the backlog after Log4Shell — an 8.8 in an image codec — lands in the lowest tier, because the vulnerable function is not on any executed path. Severity without reachability is not urgency.
  • A known-exploited Struts flaw sits at P2 rather than P1 only because the affected asset is not internet-facing. That is a defensible deferral and a standing invitation to re-check the exposure assumption, which is exactly the kind of assumption that was wrong at Equifax [15, 17].
  • A first-party access-control defect with no CVE, no EPSS score and a middling CVSS reaches P2 on the strength of reachability and automatability alone. None of the public feeds have anything to say about your own code, which is precisely why the identifier stack in Figure 3 cannot be the whole prioritisation model.

The metrics that survive scrutiny

AppSec metrics fail when they measure activity. Number of scans run, findings opened and tools deployed all rise when the programme is working and when it is failing. The metrics worth reporting describe outcomes and their trend.

Flaw half-lifeDays to remediate 50% of flaws in a cohort Remediation capacity is not scaling with detection capacity
Escape rateShare of production findings that pre-production testing missedThe gates are in the wrong place, or tuned to silence

Veracode's fifteenth annual analysis, drawn from 1.3 million applications and 126.4 million raw findings, put the average time to fix a security flaw at 252 days — a 47% increase over five years and a 327% increase over the report's fifteen-year history — with half of organisations carrying critical security debt [88, 77]. The following year's edition recorded 82% of organisations carrying some security debt and 60% carrying critical debt [89]. Public-sector organisations fare worse, with a 315-day half-life against a 252-day cross-industry average [78].

Fifteen years of shift-left has substantially improved detection and has not improved remediation throughput. The queue is the problem, and no additional scanner shortens it.

Gates, guardrails and the politics of blocking

The most consequential design choice in a programme is which checks can fail a build. A hard gate is appropriate where the false-positive rate is near zero and the fix is unambiguous: a committed credential, a dependency with a known-exploited CVE on a reachable path, an unsigned build artefact. Everything else should be a guardrail that annotates the pull request, creates a tracked ticket, and escalates on a schedule.

The reason is organisational rather than technical. A gate that fires wrongly on a Friday afternoon deploy teaches an entire engineering organisation that AppSec is an obstacle, and that lesson persists for years. Application security posture management tooling exists largely to make this tractable at scale — correlating findings across scanners into a single view and prioritising by what is exploitable, reachable and urgent rather than by raw severity [77]. The underlying principle predates the product category: the programme's credibility is spent every time it blocks, so it should block rarely and be right when it does.

Security champions

The champion model is the only known way to scale a central team past a few dozen services. A champion is a developer on a product team with allocated time, additional training, and a direct line to the central function. They run the threat model, own the first pass at triage for their service, and translate central policy into local architecture. The BSIMM longitudinal data shows this as a measured shift rather than an aspiration: security activities have become more distributed across organisations as DevSecOps practices spread, moving from security specialists toward developers [76].

Two conditions determine whether it works. Champions need protected time, written into their team's capacity plan rather than assumed as goodwill; and the role needs to be career-positive, with recognition and progression attached. Programmes that skip both get a list of names and no change in behaviour.

2026

The frontier: generated code, agentic testing, and eliminating classes

Three developments are reshaping the discipline faster than the standards documents can track them. Two of them concern AI on both sides of the problem; the third is a slow structural change that may matter more than either.

Code the organisation did not write

The volume of AI-generated and AI-assisted code entering production has grown faster than the review capacity attached to it. Reported vulnerability rates in generated code vary substantially by methodology — studies published through 2026 span roughly 19% to 62% depending on whether the definition includes code quality issues alongside security flaws, with narrower SAST-plus-manual-validation studies clustering lower [90]. Early academic work found approximately 40% of programs generated by an AI coding assistant contained vulnerabilities, with higher rates in C than in Python, and a subsequent user study found that developers with AI assistance wrote less secure code while reporting greater confidence in its security — a false sense of security that is arguably the more dangerous finding [91].

The governance gap Generated code is being held to a lower standard than written code.

Survey data from 2026 reports that a large majority of organisations now use AI-generated code in their development workflows while only a small minority apply the same security standards to it as to human-written code, and that most struggle to supply provenance data for it [90]. AppSec teams are consequently extending scope beyond application code to pretrained models treated as dependencies, vector stores holding sensitive embeddings, inference APIs, and agent tool-use behaviour [92].

The concrete vulnerability classes are already appearing in the CVE stream. Prompt injection embedded in a pull request description has been shown to achieve remote code execution through a coding assistant, and agentic AI CVEs grew sharply year over year alongside an entirely new category of tool-server vulnerabilities [93]. Accountability lags badly: one 2026 survey found only 7.2% of organisations had a named individual with formal accountability for AI agent behaviour [94].

Machines that find bugs

The offensive-research side has moved faster than most defenders expected. The International AI Safety Report 2026 assesses the evidence for AI assistance in discovering software vulnerabilities as particularly strong, citing Google's Big Sleep agent identifying a critical memory-corruption vulnerability in production software [95]. Survey work counted 39 or more open-source AI penetration-testing agents across six architectural patterns by April 2026, while being clear that genuine zero-day discovery in production code remains rare and that the well-publicised results are outliers rather than the norm [96].

Verizon's analysts were careful to note that the surge in vulnerability exploitation observed in the 2026 DBIR predates the current wave of AI-assisted vulnerability research, and attribute it instead to organisations failing to patch known vulnerabilities quickly or thoroughly enough — while expecting AI-driven discovery to add to that burden [5]. The near-term implication for AppSec is not that attacks become unstoppable; it is that the interval between disclosure and weaponisation compresses further, which raises the value of everything that shortens the remediation path and lowers the value of everything that merely detects.

Eliminating classes rather than instances

Against that backdrop, the most consequential long-term shift is the least dramatic: removing entire weakness classes from the ecosystem rather than finding their instances one at a time. Approximately 70% of security vulnerabilities in memory-unsafe languages that receive a CVE designation are attributable to memory safety issues, and the ONCD's 2024 technical report made the case that adopting memory-safe programming languages is the highest-leverage available intervention — a class of vulnerability that had by then persisted for over thirty-five years [6, 97]. NSA's 2025 guidance reinforced the position while being explicit that achieving memory safety requires language-level protections, library support, tooling and training rather than developer discipline alone [98].

The same logic applies beyond memory safety. A framework that makes authorisation declarative and fails closed eliminates a category of access-control defects more reliably than any amount of testing. Build provenance under SLSA — whose v1.0 in April 2023 restructured the specification into tracks, beginning with a Build track defining levels of protection against tampering — eliminates a category of supply chain compromise rather than detecting it [99, 100, 101, 102]. Parameterised query APIs eliminate injection at the interface level. In every case the AppSec contribution is not to find more instances but to make the instance impossible, and then to redirect the testing capacity at whatever class is now on top.

In closing

The discipline's actual product

An application security programme does not produce secure software. Developers produce software; the programme produces justified belief about it, and a queue of specific, owned, dated corrections where that belief fails. Everything in this article — the identifier vocabulary, the verification standards, the maturity models, the scanners, the fuzzers, the pentests, the bounty programme — exists to make that belief more accurate and that queue shorter.

The honest summary of where the field stands in 2026 is that it has become far better at the first half and has not improved at the second. Detection coverage is broader than at any previous point: continuous fuzzing at ecosystem scale, taint analysis running on every pull request, dependency graphs resolved automatically, external researchers testing continuously. And yet the average flaw takes 252 days to fix, security debt affects 82% of organisations, only 26% of known-exploited vulnerabilities are fully remediated by the organisations that hold them, and exploitation of vulnerabilities has become the leading route into a breached network [5, 88, 89].

That gap is the field's central problem, and it is not a tooling problem. It is a problem of prioritisation quality, remediation capacity, and the organisational standing to insist that a specific fix ships this sprint rather than next quarter. The programmes that close it are the ones that spend their effort on the middle of the funnel — reachability, exploitability, ownership, deadlines — rather than on the top of it.

Which returns to the definition this article opened with. AppSec is the discipline that refuses to take the codebase's word for it. The refusal is only useful if it terminates in a change to the code.
