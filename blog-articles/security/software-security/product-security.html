01SCOPE

The unit of analysis is the product

Application security asks whether a piece of code can be made to misbehave. Product security asks a harder and less comfortable question: whether the thing a customer bought — silicon, firmware, mobile app, cloud tenancy, update channel, support process, and the promise printed on the box — can be made to harm them, and what the company is obliged to do about it once the answer turns out to be yes.

The distinction is not academic. A cross-site scripting flaw in a web console is an application security finding: it lives in a repository, it is fixed by a commit, and the fix reaches every user the moment the service redeploys. A flaw in the pairing protocol of a battery-powered sensor is a product security problem: the fix is a firmware image that must be signed by a key held in a hardware security module, delivered over a network the vendor does not own, to devices that may be asleep, out of warranty, or installed in a ceiling. Deciding whether to ship that fix means weighing the probability of exploitation against the probability that a bricked device causes a service call, and — increasingly — against a statutory reporting clock that started running the moment the company became aware of the problem.

FIRST, the international body that has done more than any other to codify this work, draws the line institutionally rather than technically. A computer security incident response team (CSIRT) defends the organisation's own estate. A product security incident response team (PSIRT) exists to identify, assess and dispose of the risks associated with security vulnerabilities in the products, solutions, components and services that the organisation produces or sells.1 When FIRST published its CSIRT Services Framework in 2016 it quickly became clear that PSIRTs provide quite different services in quite different environments, and the community split the work into a separate document.2 That split is the cleanest available definition of the field: product security is the discipline whose constituency is not your employer, but your customers.

What sits inside the envelope

In practice the scope covers at least six categories of surface, each with failure modes invisible from the one inside it. Source code and application runtime are the traditional AppSec core. Device software — bootloader, kernel, drivers, over-the-air update agent — introduces the possibility of a defect that cannot be fixed remotely at all. Silicon and physical access add debug interfaces, unblown fuses and side channels. The service plane behind the product adds multi-tenancy, identity and the update infrastructure itself, which is the most valuable target in the whole system because it is the one component authorised to change every other component. And around all of it sits the lifecycle: how long the vendor promises to ship patches, how a researcher reaches a human being, how a recall is executed, and what the interface tells a customer to do at three in the morning.

The FIRST framework is explicit that a well-constituted PSIRT is not a separate group divorced from product development but part of a broader push for secure engineering, integrated into the secure development lifecycle.1 This is the structural claim that distinguishes mature programmes from immature ones. In an immature organisation, product security is a queue that receives complaints. In a mature one, the same engineers who run the queue are in the room when the feature is specified, because everything they learn from the queue is evidence about which design decisions generate cases.

The three-way conversation

The daily work of a product security engineer looks less like penetration testing than like a negotiation conducted in three languages at once. To the product manager, the engineer must express risk in terms of roadmap cost: this authentication design adds four weeks and removes an entire class of account takeover. To the engineering team, the same risk becomes a set of concrete requirements: mutual TLS with pinned certificates, no shared factory secrets, a rollback counter in the bootloader. To the outside world — researchers, customers, regulators — it becomes a published policy, an advisory, a support window and, in Europe since 2024, a legal filing. The skill that separates competent practitioners is not finding bugs. It is knowing which of these three audiences to address first when a decision has to be made this week.

02ORIGINS

How the discipline was forced into existence

Nothing about product security was planned. Every institution in the field — the coordination centre, the severity score, the disclosure standard, the incident response team, the conformity mark — was created in the aftermath of an event that made the previous arrangement untenable. The timeline below separates the events that caused the changes from the governance that followed, because the lag between them is one of the more useful things to understand about the discipline.

From patch culture to process

The modern era of vendor-side product security begins with a memo. On 15 January 2002, in the wake of the Code Red and Nimda worms, Bill Gates told Microsoft that trustworthy computing was now the company's highest priority.3 The consequences were structural rather than rhetorical: the Windows division halted feature work for a security push, and the practices developed during that stand-down were formalised as the Security Development Lifecycle, made mandatory across the company, and then published so that other organisations could copy it.4 Microsoft also consolidated its patch releases into the first Patch Tuesday in 2003, which introduced the idea — now universal — that predictability is itself a security property for customers.4 Analysts at the time correctly identified the SDL as a costly new process whose purpose was to catch security flaws during development rather than after shipping.5

The other half of the field grew from the opposite direction: outsiders. Netscape launched the first cash-for-bugs programme in October 1995 for the Navigator 2.0 beta, and then the idea lay dormant for the better part of a decade.6 iDefense began paying for vulnerabilities in 2002 as a middleman, Mozilla started its own programme in 2004, and TippingPoint's Zero Day Initiative followed in 2005.6 The inflection point was 2010, when Google offered a memorable $1,337 per bug in Chrome and demonstrated that a bounty could be launched with a blog post and a budget line rather than three years of internal negotiation.7 By the time Google formed Project Zero, the terms of engagement had hardened into policy: vendors get ninety days to ship a fix, with a further thirty days before technical details are published, and a seven-day clock instead of ninety when a vulnerability is already being exploited against real users.8

The events that changed the scope

Three incidents did more than any standards body to widen the definition of a product security problem.

In July 2015, Charlie Miller and Chris Valasek remotely took control of a Jeep Cherokee travelling on a public highway, reaching the vehicle's CAN bus through its cellular-connected infotainment head unit.9 Fiat Chrysler recalled about 1.4 million vehicles days later, distributing a software patch — a recall in which, as one contemporary analysis dryly noted, the manufacturer maintained that no defect had been found.10 It was regulators, not the manufacturer, who converted a research result into a formal recall action.11 For the first time, a software defect had produced a physical product recall at automotive scale.

In late 2016, the Mirai botnet assembled a peak of roughly 600,000 infected embedded devices and used them to attack targets including a major DNS provider. The academic post-mortem is unusually blunt about the cause: the malware succeeded by brute-forcing a dictionary of default username and password pairs specific to consumer routers and cameras, combined with a fast stateless scanner.12 The authors described the result as a lens into the fragile ecosystem of IoT devices, and argued that novice techniques had compromised enough low-end devices to threaten some of the best-defended targets on the internet.12 Almost every consumer-device regulation written since has a clause about default passwords, and Mirai is the reason.

In August 2017, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved a firmware update for radio-frequency-enabled cardiac pacemakers made by Abbott, formerly St. Jude Medical, and classified it explicitly as a recall and corrective action.13 Roughly 465,000 implanted devices in the United States were affected; the update required an in-person clinical visit of about three minutes, carried a small risk of the device losing functionality during the process, and neither the FDA nor the manufacturer recommended prophylactic removal.14 Observers noted at the time that it was the first such action to affect implanted devices.15 Nothing clarifies the difference between an application security bug and a product security bug quite like a patch that has to be installed by a cardiologist.

The disclosure that broke its own embargo

The Meltdown and Spectre speculative-execution flaws, reported to CPU vendors on 1 June 2017, were scheduled for coordinated disclosure on 9 January 2018.16 The embargo collapsed a week early after journalists and kernel developers inferred the existence of the flaws from public patch activity, and Google published its technical details on 3 January, explicitly citing existing public reports and growing speculation as the reason for moving early.17 CERT-EU's advisory noted flatly that many details had become known in advance of the planned date.18 A Black Hat panel the following year described a response effort that stretched more than six months across dozens of organisations, complicated at one point by an extraordinary miscommunication about who had actually been notified.19 The episode remains the field's canonical lesson that an embargo is a coordination mechanism, not a secrecy mechanism, and that it fails in proportion to the number of parties and the visibility of the fix.

03SURFACE

The product as a distributed system

Consumers buy an object. Product security teams defend a distributed system with at least three distinct owners: the customer, who controls the local network and the physical device; the public internet, which nobody controls; and the vendor's own estate, which includes the API, the data platform, the support desk and — critically — the build and signing infrastructure.

The diagram is worth reading as a list of questions rather than a picture. Can the device be reached from the local network without authentication, on the assumption that everything behind the home router is friendly? What happens to pairing credentials when the companion app is uninstalled, or the phone is sold? Does the transport pin certificates, and if it does, how does the product recover when the pinned authority is rotated? Is the update channel authenticated in both directions, so that a device cannot be persuaded to install an older, vulnerable image? Who can trigger a factory reset from the support console, and what does that reset actually erase?

The asymmetry that defines the job

Web application security enjoys a structural advantage that product security does not: the defender controls deployment. If a service is vulnerable, the fix is live within a deploy cycle and the vulnerable version ceases to exist. Shipped products invert every term of that equation.

Composition risk

Most of what ships in a modern product was not written by the company whose name is on it. Firmware pulls in a real-time operating system, a TLS stack, a JSON parser, a Bluetooth stack and a bootloader, each with its own upstream and its own patch cadence. The security consequence is that the vendor's exposure is defined by a dependency graph it did not design and often cannot fully enumerate. This is precisely the gap that the software bill of materials was invented to close, and it is why SBOM requirements have propagated from executive orders into medical device submissions and European product law rather than remaining a procurement nicety.20

Composition risk also runs in the other direction. A vendor that integrates a component inherits not only its vulnerabilities but its disclosure timeline: when the upstream project publishes, the clock on every downstream product starts whether or not those products are ready. Practically, this means a product security function needs a component inventory that can answer, within hours, the only question that matters during an industry-wide event — which of our shipped products contain this thing, in which versions, and can we reach them?

04DESIGN

Design time: the roadmap is the control point

Everything expensive in product security is decided before any code is written. Whether a device has a hardware root of trust, whether every unit ships with a unique credential, whether the update mechanism can be revoked, whether telemetry is separable from personal data — these are architectural commitments that are cheap in a design document and close to unfixable in a manufactured fleet. This is why the defining organisational feature of a product security team is not its tooling but its calendar: whether its engineers are present at roadmap planning, or briefed after it.

Requirements before threats

The most common failure mode in early-stage product security is to begin with a threat model. Threat modelling is indispensable, but it presupposes a design to model. The more useful first artefact is a short set of security requirements attached to the feature specification itself, in the same document and with the same status as the performance and usability requirements: what must be true of this feature for us to be willing to ship it. NIST's Secure Software Development Framework encodes this at the level of practice groups — Prepare the Organisation, Protect the Software, Produce Well-Secured Software and Respond to Vulnerabilities — precisely so that requirements, tooling and response can be discussed as organisational commitments rather than as engineering preferences.21 The framework was written to be integrated into any lifecycle model, from waterfall to continuous deployment, which is what makes it usable as a common vocabulary between a security engineer and a product manager.22

Threat modelling that survives contact with a roadmap

A threat model earns its place when it changes a design decision. In practice the models that do this share three properties. They are drawn at the level of trust boundaries rather than components, because boundaries are where the interesting failures live. They enumerate the attacker's goals in the product's own vocabulary — unlock the door, suppress the alarm, read the neighbour's telemetry, keep the subscription after cancelling — rather than in abstract categories. And they end with a list of decisions, each assigned to a person and a sprint, rather than a list of risks assigned to a register.

The corresponding discipline on the engineering side is the elimination of vulnerability classes rather than individual bugs. This is the most defensible long-term investment a product security team makes, and it has become a public commitment: CISA's Secure by Design pledge, launched in May 2024 with an initial cohort of software manufacturers and since grown into a large signatory list, asks vendors to demonstrate measurable reductions in entire classes of vulnerability, along with default multi-factor authentication, an end to default passwords and improved customer-visible logging.23 Signatories have published concrete results against those goals — Cloudflare, for instance, reported reductions in secrets committed to repositories and in injection-class findings after introducing blocking rulesets in its pipelines.24 The pledge is voluntary and carries no penalty, which is exactly why the published progress reports are interesting: they are the closest thing the industry has to comparable evidence about which controls actually move numbers.

Secure defaults are a product decision, not a security one

The single highest-leverage design choice in consumer products is what happens when the buyer does nothing. Mirai did not exploit a subtle memory corruption bug; it logged in.12 The regulatory response has been to make the default the object of law rather than of guidance: the United Kingdom's product security regime prohibits universal default passwords outright, requires a published contact point for reporting security issues, and requires manufacturers to state the minimum period for which the product will receive security updates.25 Those three requirements are, in effect, a legislature telling product managers that certain trade-offs are no longer theirs to make.

Designing good defaults is genuinely difficult because the cost lands on the user experience and the benefit is invisible. Unique per-device credentials mean a provisioning step and a label on the box. Certificate pinning means a recovery path when authorities rotate. Mandatory account creation improves recoverability and destroys the offline use case. The correct response is not to always choose the more secure option; it is to make the trade-off explicit, record who made it, and revisit it when the deployed population changes. A decision that was reasonable for 5,000 pilot units is frequently indefensible at five million.

05BUILD

Build time: inventory, provenance and signatures

Between a design decision and a shipped product sits a build pipeline, and the pipeline has become a first-class part of the product's attack surface. Three artefacts now define competent practice: an inventory of what is inside the release, evidence of how the release was produced, and a signature that binds the two to the vendor.

The bill of materials

A software bill of materials is a formal record of the components used in building software and the supply-chain relationships between them.26 The United States National Telecommunications and Information Administration published the minimum elements in July 2021, pursuant to Executive Order 14028, and settled on a small set of per-component data fields — supplier, component name, version, other unique identifiers, dependency relationship, the author of the SBOM entry and a timestamp — plus expectations about automation support and practices.26 Two machine-readable formats dominate: SPDX, stewarded by the Linux Foundation and standardised as ISO/IEC 5962:2021, which grew out of licence compliance and carries deep file-level provenance; and CycloneDX, stewarded by OWASP and standardised as ECMA-424, which was designed for security use cases and extends naturally to VEX statements and other bill-of-materials types.20 Both encode the same minimum elements, and most toolchains can emit either.27

The reason SBOM survived the hype cycle is operational rather than regulatory. During an industry-wide event, the question a customer asks is not whether a vendor has good practices; it is whether this specific product, in the version they are running, contains the affected component. A team with queryable SBOMs answers that in an afternoon. A team without one convenes a fire drill.20 That difference is why the artefact migrated from guidance into binding requirements for medical devices and, in Europe, into product law.

Provenance and the build itself

An inventory says what went in. It says nothing about whether the binary customers received was actually produced from that input. Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts (SLSA) — an OpenSSF project — addresses that gap with incrementally adoptable levels describing how strongly a build platform resists tampering, and with signed provenance metadata that ties an artefact back to its sources and build process.28 The version 1.0 release reorganised the specification into tracks, starting with the Build track, so that organisations could adopt build integrity without simultaneously solving source integrity.29 The pairing is easy to remember: the SBOM is the ingredient list, the provenance attestation is the record of who cooked and where.

The signing infrastructure that makes any of this meaningful is, in most product companies, the highest-value asset in the estate. A private key that can sign firmware is an authorisation to modify every device in the field. Competent practice keeps it in a hardware security module, requires multiple approvals to use it, logs every signature, keeps a revocation path, and — the part that is usually missing — has a rehearsed plan for what happens if the key is compromised, since for many product categories key compromise is unrecoverable without physical access to every unit.

Where AI development fits

Products increasingly embed models rather than only code, and the same lifecycle logic applies with a wider definition of component. NIST addressed this directly by publishing SP 800-218A in July 2024, an SSDF community profile that adds practices, tasks and references specific to generative AI and dual-use foundation model development across the software development lifecycle.30 For a product security team the practical consequence is that model weights, training data provenance and inference endpoints join the component inventory, and prompt-mediated behaviour joins the threat model as a new class of untrusted input.

06FIELD

Hardware and firmware: the parts you cannot redeploy

The defining property of a physical product is that the vendor's ability to change it is finite, mediated by a mechanism the vendor built in advance, and revocable by the customer. Two chains determine whether a product can be defended at all after it leaves the factory: the boot chain, which decides what is allowed to run, and the signing and update chain, which decides whether anything can ever be changed.

The boot chain

A hardware root of trust is an immutable starting point — typically a hash of a public key burned into fuses or mask ROM — against which the first mutable stage is verified. Each stage then verifies the next, so that a compromise anywhere in the chain is detected by the stage above it. Rollback counters prevent an attacker from installing an older, correctly signed but vulnerable image. Measured boot extends this into attestation, where the device can prove to a remote service which software it is actually running before being granted access to anything interesting.

Getting this right is unglamorous and consequential. Debug interfaces left enabled in production, keys shared across an entire product line, secrets stored in readable flash, and bootloaders that accept unsigned images in recovery mode are the recurring findings of hardware assessments, and each of them converts a device-level compromise into a fleet-level one. The industrial sector has codified the process side of this work in IEC 62443-4-1, which specifies secure product development lifecycle requirements for products used in industrial automation and control systems, including a structured process for managing vulnerabilities in those products.31 Manufacturers commonly combine it with the ISO/IEC vulnerability standards to cover both the engineering process and the disclosure interface.32

The update chain

An update mechanism is a product feature with an unusual property: it is the only feature whose absence is unrecoverable. Designing one means answering a list of questions that sound mundane and are not. Are images signed and verified on-device rather than merely served over TLS? Is the update atomic, with an A/B partition scheme, so that a power loss mid-write does not produce a brick? Can the rollout be staged and halted when telemetry shows failures? Is there a fallback for devices that have been offline for two years and are several versions behind? What is the failure rate the business will accept, and who signs off on it?

The pacemaker case remains the clearest illustration of these constraints in their most severe form: the corrective firmware required a clinical visit, took about three minutes, and carried a small but non-zero risk that the device would lose functionality during the update — a risk that had to be weighed against a vulnerability with no known exploitation in the field.1413 Most products are not implanted, but the structure of the decision repeats everywhere: the patch itself has a failure rate, and the correct action depends on the ratio between that rate and the probability of exploitation.

Support windows and the end of the product

The moment a vendor stops shipping security updates, every remaining device becomes a permanent liability for its owner and, increasingly, for the internet. Regulators have concluded that this cannot be left implicit. The UK regime requires manufacturers to publish the defined support period — the minimum length of time, with an end date, for which security updates will be provided — alongside the ban on universal default passwords and the requirement to publish a vulnerability reporting contact.3325 NIST's consumer IoT profile expresses similar expectations as outcomes that apply to the whole product, including its backend and companion applications, rather than to the device in isolation.34 For a product organisation, a published support window converts an open-ended obligation into a plannable one — and forces a conversation with finance about the cost of maintaining a build environment for a product that stopped selling four years ago.

07RESPOND

The PSIRT: running the queue that never closes

Every product organisation eventually receives an email that begins I think I found something in your device. What happens next is the most visible expression of a company's product security maturity, and it is almost entirely a matter of process design rather than technical skill.

Structure

FIRST's PSIRT Services Framework observes that there is no single one-size-fits-all product security incident response strategy, but that three organisational models cover most companies: distributed, centralised and hybrid.1 The distributed model runs a small core team that sets policy, maintains the triage and communication processes, and works through a matrix of tiered product security representatives embedded in each product team.1 The centralised model concentrates deep expertise in one place — efficient for smaller or homogeneous portfolios, and poorly scalable as the portfolio grows and diversifies. Hybrid models select characteristics of both. The framework organises the work into service areas covering stakeholder management, vulnerability discovery, triage, remediation, disclosure and training, and there is a companion maturity document that arranges those services into levels so a team can plan what to build first.3536

Intake, triage, remediation, disclosure

The four stages are simple to name and unforgiving in operation.

Intake fails most often for want of a front door. A monitored security address, published on the vendor's site and in the product documentation, with an automatic acknowledgement and a stated response expectation, converts a hostile interaction into a manageable one. Under the UK regime this is no longer optional for consumer connectable products: the manufacturer must provide a public point of contact for reporting security issues.25

Triage is where most programmes lose credibility, because it requires answering a question the reporter cannot answer: is this exploitable in a configuration our customers actually run? Triage needs a reproducible environment for every supported product version, which is an infrastructure investment that no one budgets for until the first time a critical report cannot be reproduced because the affected hardware revision no longer exists in the building.

Remediation is an engineering negotiation. The fix competes with the roadmap, and the product security team rarely controls the engineers who will write it. This is where scoring frameworks earn their keep — not because a number is truth, but because a shared, published basis for urgency removes the argument from the realm of personality.

Disclosure is a publishing operation with a legal dimension. It requires an advisory that states affected versions precisely, describes the impact without providing a recipe, gives customers something to do before the patch is available, and credits the finder accurately.

Coordination under embargo

Some cases cannot be handled by one vendor. When a flaw sits in a shared component, a protocol, or silicon, the response requires an embargo across organisations that are otherwise competitors, and the coordination cost scales badly. Meltdown and Spectre are the reference case: reported to CPU vendors in June 2017 with a coordinated date of 9 January 2018, the embargo broke a week early once public patch activity made the existence of the flaws inferable.1617 Participants later described a response that spanned more than six months and dozens of organisations, with at least one significant failure in who-told-whom.19 The practical lessons are durable: keep the participant list as small as the fix allows, assume the embargo will leak in proportion to the visibility of the patch, write the advisory early rather than at the deadline, and have a shortened-timeline plan ready before you need it.

What the PSIRT owes the rest of the company

A queue that only produces patches is a cost centre. A queue that produces evidence is an engineering input. The cases arriving each quarter are a sample of the organisation's real defect distribution, weighted by what attackers and researchers find worth their time. Feeding that distribution back into design review, secure coding guidance and test tooling is the mechanism by which the FIRST framework's claim — that product security response belongs inside the development lifecycle rather than beside it — becomes true in practice.1

08DISCLOSE

Coordinated disclosure and the finder relationship

Product security is the only engineering discipline whose quality assurance is partly performed by strangers with no contract and no obligation to be patient. Managing that relationship is a discipline in its own right, and it is now standardised.

The two standards

ISO/IEC 29147:2018 covers the external half: how a vendor receives vulnerability reports and publishes remediation information, including requirements and recommendations for the disclosure policy itself and for communication with reporters.37 Its companion, ISO/IEC 30111:2019, covers the internal half — investigating, prioritising, developing and testing remediations, and improving secure development as a result.38 The division is deliberate and useful: 29147 is the promise a vendor makes in public, 30111 is the machinery that has to exist for the promise to be kept.39 The standards explicitly acknowledge that no single fixed process can apply to every disclosure event, and confine themselves to minimum requirements for a functional capability.39

Their influence is now indirect but pervasive. The UK's product security regulations grant deemed compliance to manufacturers meeting specified provisions of ETSI EN 303 645 and ISO/IEC 29147, which is an unusual and telling move: a legislature pointing at an international standard as the definition of an adequate disclosure process.33

Policy as an engineering artefact

A vulnerability disclosure policy is a short document that does an unreasonable amount of work. It tells a researcher what is in scope, how to reach a human, what response times to expect, what testing is authorised, and what the vendor will and will not do — most importantly, whether it will pursue legal action against good-faith research. The presence of an explicit safe harbour is, in practice, the strongest single signal of programme maturity, because it is the clause that costs a company something to write.

Bounty programmes sit on top of a disclosure policy, never underneath it. The historical arc is instructive: a decade of near-dormancy after Netscape's 1995 experiment, a middleman market in the early 2000s, then broad vendor adoption after 2010.640 The lesson organisations most often learn late is that paying for bugs increases inbound volume immediately and remediation capacity not at all. A bounty launched before triage and engineering capacity exists produces a backlog of paid-for, unfixed findings — the worst of both worlds, and a reputational liability when researchers eventually publish.

Deadlines

Disclosure deadlines exist because vendors, left to themselves, historically did not ship fixes. Project Zero's published policy is the clearest statement of the modern bargain: ninety days for a patch, thirty further days before technical details are released, a shortened seven-day clock when a vulnerability is already being exploited in the wild, and defined grace periods for vendors who are close to shipping.8 Vendors who dislike deadlines should note the counterfactual the deadline replaced, and vendors who like them should note that a deadline is only fair when the finder's report was clear enough to act on.

The uncomfortable truth for product organisations is that hardware and embedded timelines rarely fit a ninety-day window. A fix may require a silicon change, a supplier's release cycle, an automotive validation programme or a regulatory re-submission. The way out is not to argue for longer defaults; it is to demonstrate, case by case, a credible plan with dates — which is only possible if the internal machinery described by ISO/IEC 30111 already exists.38

09SIGNAL

Naming, scoring and telling: the advisory supply chain

A fixed vulnerability that customers never hear about protects nobody. The machinery that converts an internal fix into an action taken by a stranger's operations team is a supply chain of its own, and each link answers a different question.

Naming: the CVE programme and its fragility

The Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures programme provides the shared identifier that lets a vendor advisory, a scanner, a regulator and a customer's ticketing system refer to the same defect. Its operational reality is a global network of CVE Numbering Authorities — CISA counted more than 460 of them when describing the programme's growth era — that assign identifiers within their own scope.41 Most sizeable product companies are now CNAs for their own products, which is a meaningful commitment: it means owning the accuracy of the record rather than negotiating it.

The programme's institutional fragility became a public issue in April 2025, when MITRE warned the CVE Board that the contracting pathway to develop, operate and modernise CVE and related programmes would expire the following day.42 CISA executed an option period on the contract hours before the lapse, extending operations for eleven months, while a group of board members announced a separate foundation intended to give the programme independence from a single government sponsor.4344 CISA subsequently published a strategic document framing a shift from the programme's growth era to a quality era focused on record completeness, accuracy, timeliness and governance, and stated that privatising the programme would dilute its value as a public good.41 Funding was secured again in early 2026, though reporting at the time noted that the underlying dependence on a single funder remained unresolved.45 For a product security team the lesson is concrete: identifiers are shared infrastructure, and shared infrastructure with a single point of failure belongs on the risk register.

Scoring: severity is not urgency

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System supplies a common way to describe a vulnerability's technical severity. Version 4.0 was published on 1 November 2023, the first major revision in more than eight years, and it reorganised the model into Base, Threat, Environmental and Supplemental metric groups, replacing the old Temporal group and adding metrics for safety impact and automatable exploitation.4647 The supplemental safety metric is a direct concession to product security: a memory-corruption bug in an infusion pump and the same bug in a photo viewer do not have the same consequences.

Severity, however, was never a patching queue, and treating it as one has been the field's most durable mistake. Two complements have emerged. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, maintained by FIRST, estimates the probability that a publicly disclosed vulnerability will be exploited in the wild within the next thirty days, using models built on vulnerability characteristics, temporal features and threat-intelligence signals.48 EPSS has been revised several times since its 2021 public release, with successive versions adding telemetry sources, gradient-boosted models and contextual threat feeds.49 Where EPSS is predictive, CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog is observational: it records the subset of CVEs known to have been used to compromise real systems.50 The catalog was established under Binding Operational Directive 22-01 in November 2021, which required federal civilian agencies to remediate listed vulnerabilities on accelerated timelines and encouraged everyone else to use the catalog as an input to prioritisation.51 CISA has been consistent that the catalog is an input, not the whole triage function, and recommends pairing it with a decision framework such as Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorization, developed with Carnegie Mellon's Software Engineering Institute, which resolves a vulnerability to one of four actions — track, track*, attend or act.505253

The 2026 turn: risk-tiered remediation

In June 2026 CISA replaced its earlier directives with Binding Operational Directive 26-04, Prioritizing Security Updates Based on Risk, which supersedes both BOD 19-02 and BOD 22-01 and rewrites federal vulnerability management around four variables: whether the affected asset is publicly exposed, whether the vulnerability is in the KEV catalog, whether exploitation is automatable, and the technical impact an attacker obtains after exploitation.5455 The most urgent combinations carry a three-day remediation deadline; the lowest-risk cases may be deferred to the next planned system upgrade.56 The directive also obliges agencies to perform forensic triage to determine whether affected systems have already been compromised, and sets staged implementation milestones.56 Analysts described it as a shift from a severity checklist to a defensible risk model, with the open question being whether audit practice will convert it back into a checklist.57 The knock-on effects reach vendors directly: FedRAMP announced that cloud service providers must adopt its new vulnerability detection, response and reporting rules by December 2026 to give agency customers the assurance the directive now demands.58

Directives that bind only federal agencies matter to product companies for a simple commercial reason: agency customers will push the timelines into contracts, and the four questions become the questions vendors are asked about their own products.

Telling: machine-readable advisories

The last link is the advisory itself, and it has been quietly transformed. The Common Security Advisory Framework version 2.0 was approved as an OASIS standard in November 2022; it defines a JSON format for security advisories along with the mechanism by which advisories are distributed and discovered, and it incorporates the Vulnerability Exploitability eXchange profile.5960 VEX solves a problem SBOMs created: a component being present in a product does not mean the vulnerability in that component is exploitable in that product, and VEX lets a supplier assert the status of a specific vulnerability in a specific product — including the negative assertion that customers do not need to act.6162 In 2025 CSAF v2.0 was published as an ISO/IEC international standard, giving regulators a normative reference for structured advisories.63

The operational argument for machine-readable advisories is unsentimental. A vendor that publishes only human-prose advisories forces every customer to re-derive applicability by hand, at exactly the moment when thousands of them are doing it simultaneously. A vendor that publishes CSAF documents lets customer tooling compare advisories against asset inventories automatically. As the volume of disclosed vulnerabilities keeps rising, that difference is the difference between an advisory that is read and one that is queued.

10GOVERN

Conformity: when product security becomes market access

For twenty years product security was a matter of reputation and contract. Since 2024 it has become, in several major markets, a condition of being allowed to sell. The change of category matters more than any individual requirement: a reputational risk is managed by a security team, while a market-access risk is managed by the people who decide what gets built.

The European Cyber Resilience Act

Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 sets horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements — hardware and software placed on the Union market, including components sold separately. It entered into force on 10 December 2024 and applies in phases: reporting obligations under Article 14 from 11 September 2026, and the main body of obligations, including essential requirements, conformity assessment, technical documentation and CE marking, from 11 December 2027.6465 The reporting duty is the sharp edge for product teams: manufacturers must notify actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents to ENISA and their designated national CSIRT through a single reporting platform, on a clock that starts when the manufacturer becomes aware — a threshold the regulation does not define in detail, which puts the burden on internal awareness processes.66 Reporting applies to in-scope products already made available before full application, not only to new ones.66 Penalties for non-compliance with the essential requirements reach €15 million or 2.5% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.67 Microenterprises and small enterprises are shielded from fines for missing the 24-hour reporting deadline, and open-source stewards from CRA infringements generally.65 The compliance chain also runs through standards and classification work with its own deadlines during 2026.68

The United Kingdom's minimum floor

The UK took the opposite approach: a small number of duties, in force early. Under the Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure Act 2022 and the 2023 Regulations, from 29 April 2024 manufacturers of consumer connectable products must avoid universal default passwords, publish a point of contact for reporting security issues, and publish the minimum period for which the product will receive security updates.3325 Compliance can be deemed by meeting specified provisions of ETSI EN 303 645 and ISO/IEC 29147.33 Enforcement sits with the Office for Product Safety and Standards, with sanctions up to £10 million or 4% of worldwide revenue.69

Vehicles, medical devices, and everything with a sector regulator

Automotive got there first. UN Regulation No. 155, adopted under the UNECE World Forum for Harmonization of Vehicle Regulations, requires manufacturers to operate a certified Cyber Security Management System and to obtain cybersecurity type approval; its companion R156 governs software update management systems. Both entered into force in January 2021, became mandatory for new vehicle types in July 2022, and extended to all new vehicles produced from July 2024.7071 ISO/SAE 21434 is the engineering standard used in practice to satisfy them: where the regulation defines what must be achieved, 21434 defines the threat analysis, risk assessment and lifecycle engineering used to achieve it.71

Medical devices followed a similar path in the United States. Section 3305 of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 amended the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act by adding section 524B, Ensuring Cybersecurity of Devices, effective 29 March 2023.72 The FDA issued a refuse-to-accept policy under which, from 1 October 2023, premarket submissions for cyber devices lacking the required cybersecurity information may be rejected outright.72 Sponsors must supply a software bill of materials covering commercial, open source and off-the-shelf components, a plan for postmarket vulnerability monitoring and disclosure, and evidence of a secure product development framework.7374 The mechanism is worth noting: not a fine after an incident, but a submission that never gets reviewed.

Labels and the American divergence

The United States chose a voluntary, market-signal approach for consumer devices. The FCC's U.S. Cyber Trust Mark programme builds on NIST's consumer IoT profile, which expresses baseline outcomes — asset identification, secure configuration, data protection, software update capability, documentation of the support period — as properties of the whole product rather than the device alone.3475 Implementation has proceeded through administrator selection and standards development rather than prohibition.76

At the same time, federal software assurance policy moved in the opposite direction from Europe. Executive Order 14028 (2021) had driven SBOM into federal procurement and produced a standardised self-attestation regime under OMB memoranda M-22-18 and M-23-16.2077 Executive Order 14306 in June 2025 rescinded large parts of the successor order while retaining NIST guidance based on SP 800-218, and on 23 January 2026 OMB issued M-26-05, which rescinded the attestation memoranda and directed agencies to adopt tailored, risk-based assurance requirements instead.7879 Agencies may still require attestations or SBOMs, but on their own assessment rather than by government-wide mandate.80 Vendors selling into both markets therefore face a widening divergence: a harmonised, legally binding conformity regime in Europe, and a decentralised, agency-specific set of expectations in the United States. The practical hedge is to build the evidence once, to the stricter standard, and map it to each jurisdiction's paperwork.

11JUDGE

The trade-offs that make it a job rather than a checklist

If security were the only objective, every product decision would be easy and most products would be unusable. The reason product security is staffed by engineers embedded with product managers rather than by auditors is that almost every meaningful control has a cost that lands on someone other than the attacker.

Four recurring tensions

Security against usability. Mandatory account creation makes a device recoverable and revocable, and destroys the offline use case. Unique per-device credentials eliminate the Mirai failure mode and add a provisioning step that generates support calls. The honest framing is not which is more secure but what fraction of users will abandon setup, and is that acceptable given who is harmed if we do nothing. The Mirai measurements are a reminder that the harm is often not to the device's owner at all: the traffic that took down high-profile targets came from hundreds of thousands of the internet's least powerful hosts, whose owners noticed nothing.81

Security against time-to-market. A hardware root of trust must be selected before tape-out. A per-device key injection process must exist before the first production run. Neither can be added in a later sprint, so both compete for schedule at exactly the moment when the schedule is least negotiable. The only reliable counter is to convert them into a small number of non-negotiable gates agreed at programme start, rather than to argue them individually under deadline pressure.

Patch risk against exploitation risk. Every update has a failure rate. The pacemaker recall is the extreme case — an update requiring a clinical visit, with a small chance of the device losing functionality, deployed against a vulnerability with no known exploitation.14 The same arithmetic applies to a thermostat with a 0.1% bricking rate across three million units. This is where predictive and observational evidence earn their keep: EPSS supplies the probability side, the KEV catalog supplies the confirmation side, and both are more decision-relevant than a severity score alone.4850

Transparency against exploitation. Advisories that are precise enough to be actionable are also precise enough to guide an attacker. The convention that has emerged — state affected versions exactly, state impact plainly, omit the exploitation recipe, publish alongside the fix — is a workable compromise that the disclosure standards encode as minimum requirements.39

Making the trade-off legible

The organisational technique that distinguishes teams whose advice is taken is not better analysis; it is better framing. Three habits do most of the work. Express risk in units the audience already manages: support calls, warranty claims, regulatory exposure, weeks of schedule. Offer a menu rather than a verdict — the secure option, the cheap option, and the one you would ship, with the residual risk of each stated in one sentence. And record the decision, including who made it and what evidence existed at the time. That record is what turns a disagreement into a reviewable engineering decision, and it is also, when a regulator asks three years later why a product shipped as it did, the only artefact that will help.

There is a version of product security that consists of saying no persuasively. It does not survive contact with a roadmap. The version that works treats every control as a proposal with a price, argues for the ones whose price is justified by the harm they prevent, and spends its credibility deliberately — because credibility, not authority, is what the function actually runs on.

12OPERATE

Running the function: models, metrics and maturity

A product security programme is an internal service with customers who did not ask for it. Its survival depends on being measurably useful, which means choosing metrics that describe outcomes rather than activity.

Organisational shape

The three models FIRST describes — distributed, centralised, hybrid — map onto portfolio structure more than company size.1 A single product line with shared architecture is well served by a central team of specialists. A diverse portfolio, especially one assembled through acquisition, needs a small core that owns policy and process plus a network of embedded representatives who own their product's cases, because no central team can hold the domain knowledge of a dozen unrelated products. FIRST's maturity document arranges the framework's services into levels, which is the most practical available answer to the question every new team asks: what do we build first.36

Reporting lines matter less than two structural conditions. The team must be able to escalate a shipping decision without going through the person whose schedule the escalation threatens, and it must have a standing seat in roadmap planning rather than a review slot before release. Programmes that lack the first condition eventually stop escalating; programmes that lack the second are limited to finding, at the highest possible cost, the flaws that a design conversation would have prevented.

Two of these deserve emphasis. Patch adoption is the only metric that measures the thing customers actually experience, and it is the one most often missing, because it requires telemetry that privacy review may constrain and that no one budgets for. Weakness-class recurrence is the metric that connects response back to engineering: if the same class keeps arriving, the control that was supposed to eliminate it did not.

Benchmarks and models

For comparison against peers, the two long-running instruments are BSIMM, which observes and reports the activities organisations actually perform and publishes adoption percentages across a grouped set of practices, and OWASP SAMM, a prescriptive maturity model covering governance, design, implementation, verification and operations.82 Recent research has begun mapping these against newer supply-chain-specific frameworks and against regulatory regimes including the CRA and SSDF, which is a useful development for product organisations that must satisfy several at once.82 Used well, a maturity model is a planning tool. Used badly, it becomes a scoring exercise in which a programme optimises for the assessment rather than for the defect distribution its own queue is describing.

13PRACTICE

A field guide

What follows is a compressed version of the decisions that most reliably separate product organisations that handle their first serious vulnerability well from those that do not. It is ordered by lifecycle stage, because that is the order in which the decisions become expensive.

Before the architecture is fixed

  • Choose the root of trust, the key hierarchy and the per-device identity scheme. These are unfixable after tape-out.
  • Specify the update mechanism as a product feature with an owner, a failure budget and an A/B partition scheme — not as a task in the firmware backlog.
  • Write the security requirements into the same specification as the functional ones, and give them the same review.
  • Decide the support period and write it down. It is a regulatory requirement in the UK today and a documentation expectation under the NIST consumer profile.2534

Before the first unit ships

  • Generate an SBOM in SPDX or CycloneDX for every release artefact, and make it queryable across the whole shipped portfolio rather than per-build.26
  • Sign release artefacts from an HSM-backed service, retain provenance, and adopt build-integrity levels incrementally rather than waiting for a perfect pipeline.2829
  • Publish a vulnerability disclosure policy that names a contact, states response expectations and includes an explicit safe harbour for good-faith research, following ISO/IEC 29147.37
  • Stand up the internal handling process the policy implies, per ISO/IEC 30111, before the first report arrives.38
  • Eliminate universal default credentials. There is no configuration in which they are defensible.12

Once the product is in the field

  • Become a CNA for your own products, or establish a reliable route to one.41
  • Publish advisories in CSAF, and use VEX to tell customers which components present in your SBOM do not require them to act.5961
  • Triage on exposure, known exploitation, automatability and impact rather than on severity alone; the 2026 federal directive is a usable template even for organisations it does not bind.54
  • Measure patch adoption, not patch availability.
  • Rehearse a coordinated multi-vendor disclosure before you are in one. The Meltdown and Spectre post-mortems are the cheapest available training material.19

Where to read further

Four documents repay direct reading rather than summary. FIRST's PSIRT Services Framework is the closest thing the field has to a job description.35 NIST SP 800-218 supplies the vocabulary that procurement, regulators and engineering can all use without translation.21 ISO/IEC 29147 and 30111 together define the disclosure interface and the machinery behind it.3738 And the Cyber Resilience Act's legislative summary is the shortest route to understanding what the next decade of product security obligations will look like outside the United States.65

The through-line of all of it is a change in who bears the cost of insecurity. For most of computing's history, that cost fell on whoever bought the product. The institutions built over the last twenty-five years — the coordination centres, the disclosure standards, the incident response teams, the scoring systems, and now the conformity regimes — are all mechanisms for moving some of that cost back to the organisation that shipped the defect. Product security is the function that absorbs the move, and does it early enough that the shift is paid for in design decisions rather than in recalls.
