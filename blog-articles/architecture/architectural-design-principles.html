Architectural Design Principles

Introduction

The oldest surviving treatise on architecture, written by the Roman architect Vitruvius in the first century BC, says that buildings must be made "with due reference to durability, convenience, and beauty" (firmitas, utilitas, venustas).[1] The principles in this article belong mostly to the third term. They are the means by which a designer arranges form and space so that a building is legible, coherent and satisfying to move through.

Vitruvius also gave the first list of compositional terms: order (ordinatio), arrangement (dispositio), eurythmy, symmetry, propriety and economy.[1] Modern textbooks reorganise the same territory. Ching groups the ordering principles as axis, symmetry, hierarchy, rhythm, datum and transformation, and treats proportion and scale in a chapter of their own.[2] Rasmussen approaches the subject through perception, with chapters on solids and cavities, scale and proportion, rhythm, texture, daylight, colour and sound.[3]

How to read this article. The principles overlap. Rhythm depends on repetition; emphasis is usually produced by contrast; human scale is a special case of scale. Each section states where its principle stops and the next begins. Diagrams are drawn as inline SVG and the code samples are plain JavaScript that runs in the interactive boxes on this page.

01Balance

Definition. Balance is the perceived equilibrium of visual weight about an axis or a point. A balanced composition does not appear to tip, slide or demand correction.

Arnheim, writing on the psychology of perception, argued that every element in a visual field exerts "perceptual forces" and that viewers sense whether those forces settle into equilibrium. Visual weight rises with size, darkness or saturation, isolation, and distance from the centre, much as torque rises with lever arm.[4] Architects use the same intuition when they judge whether a tower, an entrance or a large opening "holds" a façade.

Three kinds of balance

  • Symmetrical (formal) balance. Equivalent elements are mirrored about an axis. Equilibrium is immediate and static.
  • Asymmetrical (informal) balance. Unlike elements are arranged so that their weights cancel. A large, light mass near the axis can balance a small, dark or distant one.
  • Radial balance. Elements are arranged around a central point, as in a domed centralised church or a circular plaza.

Examples

The classical temple front and the Palladian villa rely on symmetrical balance (section 2). Picturesque and modern compositions often use asymmetrical balance: a low wing is set against a tower, or a blank wall against a band of glazing. Unwin observes that architects frequently use a building's geometry to relate it to a centre or axis that the visitor may never see drawn, yet still senses.[5]

A computable model

Visual weight cannot be measured exactly, but a rough model is useful for teaching. Treat each façade element as a rectangle whose weight is area multiplied by a tone factor (0 for white, 1 for black), then find the weighted centroid. If the centroid lies close to the intended axis, the composition will tend to read as balanced.

// Approximate the "visual centroid" of a façade.
// Each element: {x, y, w, h, tone}  (tone 0..1, darker = heavier)
function visualCentroid(elements) {
  let W = 0, cx = 0, cy = 0;
  for (const e of elements) {
    const weight = e.w * e.h * (0.2 + 0.8 * e.tone); // pale forms still weigh something
    W  += weight;
    cx += weight * (e.x + e.w / 2);
    cy += weight * (e.y + e.h / 2);
  }
  return { x: cx / W, y: cy / W };
}

Exercise 1.1

A façade has a pale stair tower (area 60 m², tone 0.1) whose centre is 4 m left of the entrance axis. On the right, a dark brick panel of area 15 m² (tone 0.9) is to be placed. Using the weight model above, how far right of the axis should the panel's centre be for balance?

Show solution

Weight of tower = 60 × (0.2 + 0.8 × 0.1) = 60 × 0.28 = 16.8. Weight of panel = 15 × (0.2 + 0.8 × 0.9) = 15 × 0.92 = 13.8. Balance requires 16.8 × 4 = 13.8 × d, so d = 67.2 / 13.8 ≈ 4.9 m. The smaller, darker panel must sit slightly farther out than the tower. The model is a teaching heuristic, not a law of perception; the designer still judges the result by eye.

02Symmetry and asymmetry

Definition. Symmetry is the balanced arrangement of equivalent forms and spaces about a common line (bilateral symmetry) or point (radial symmetry). Asymmetry is the deliberate absence of such correspondence, usually combined with informal balance.

Ching distinguishes bilateral symmetry, in which equivalent elements are arranged about a single axis, from radial symmetry, in which they are arranged about two or more axes intersecting at a centre.[2] He also notes that a building can be symmetrical in only part of its organisation, organising a portion of the plan around an axis while the rest responds to site or programme.[2]

A note on the word. Vitruvius's symmetria does not mean mirror symmetry. He defines it as "a proper agreement between the members of the work itself, and relation between the different parts and the whole general scheme, in accordance with a certain part selected as standard", that is, commensurability through a module.[1] The modern sense of mirror correspondence came later. Section 4 returns to the Vitruvian sense.

What symmetry does, and what asymmetry does

ReadingInstant, static, ceremonialGradual, dynamic, exploratory
Typical useCivic, religious, commemorative buildings; formal gardensHouses, buildings on irregular sites, programmes with unequal parts
RiskForcing unequal functions into equal boxesVisual instability if weights are not balanced

Palladio's villas show symmetry used as an ordering discipline: the plates of his Four Books present plans in which rooms of matching size flank a central hall.[6] Twentieth-century modernism often rejected that discipline in favour of plans shaped by use, which produced asymmetrical compositions that still needed to be balanced in the sense of section 1.[2]

Exercise 2.1

A library must contain a 400 m² reading room, a 120 m² office and a 60 m² café, all on one floor with a single main entrance. Sketch (in words) a symmetrical and an asymmetrical parti. Which is more honest to the programme?

Show solution

Symmetrical: entrance on a central axis into a hall; reading room straight ahead as the terminating element; office and café as flanking wings. Because the wings differ in area (120 vs 60 m²), you must either equalise their outer envelopes (padding the café with storage or a terrace) or accept a "broken" symmetry. Asymmetrical: entrance off-centre between the large reading room and a lower wing containing office and café; the large volume is balanced by the darker, more articulated small wing. The asymmetrical parti reflects unequal sizes directly; the symmetrical one gives the library a civic presence. Neither is wrong; the choice depends on whether ceremony or programmatic fit matters more on this site.

Exercise 2.2

What is the difference between Vitruvius's symmetria and the modern meaning of "symmetry"?

Show solution

For Vitruvius, symmetria is the agreement of all parts with each other and with the whole through a chosen module (for a temple, the column diameter).[1] It concerns commensurable proportion and could apply to an asymmetrical plan. The modern sense is mirror or rotational correspondence about an axis or point.[2]

03Proportion

Definition. Proportion is the relation of one part to another, or of a part to the whole, expressed as a ratio. Scale (section 4) compares a thing with something outside it; proportion compares the parts within it.[2]

Ching surveys the main proportioning systems: the golden section, regulating lines, the classical orders, Renaissance theories, the Modulor, the Japanese ken, and anthropometry.[2] The common aim is to set up a consistent set of visual relationships so that parts of very different sizes seem to belong to one family.

Renaissance ratios and the three means

In the first of his Four Books (1570), Palladio recommends seven room shapes: circular, square, the diagonal of the square (1 : √2), and the ratios 3 : 4, 2 : 3, 3 : 5 and 1 : 2.[6] For the height of vaulted rooms he gives three rules, which correspond to the arithmetic, geometric and harmonic means of length and width.[6,7] Wittkower showed that these ratios were tied to the consonant intervals of Pythagorean music theory, so that "harmony" in architecture was meant literally.[7]

// Palladio's three rules for the height of a vaulted room
// of length L and width W, as the classical means.
const arithmetic = (L, W) => (L + W) / 2;
const geometric  = (L, W) => Math.sqrt(L * W);
const harmonic   = (L, W) => (2 * L * W) / (L + W);

// Room 6 × 12: arithmetic 9, geometric ≈ 8.49, harmonic 8.
// Note: harmonic ≤ geometric ≤ arithmetic for any positive L, W.

The golden section

A line is divided in the golden section when the whole is to the larger part as the larger part is to the smaller. The ratio is φ = (1 + √5) / 2 ≈ 1.618, and the ratios of successive Fibonacci numbers converge to it.[8] Le Corbusier used regulating lines (tracés régulateurs), including golden-section constructions, to check his façades, and argued that such lines protect the designer against arbitrariness.[9]

// Golden ratio and Fibonacci convergence
const phi = (1 + Math.sqrt(5)) / 2;          // 1.618033988749895
let [a, b] = [1, 1];
for (let i = 0; i < 12; i++) {
  [a, b] = [b, a + b];
  console.log(`${b}/${a} = ${(b / a).toFixed(6)}  error ${(b / a - phi).toExponential(2)}`);
}
Fact check: the golden ratio myth. Many popular claims that the Parthenon, the Great Pyramid or the United Nations Secretariat were designed on the golden ratio do not survive scrutiny. Markowsky showed that such claims depend on choosing which points to measure and on ignoring measurement error.[10] Livio reached the same conclusion for the Parthenon and noted that there is no documentary evidence that its designers intended the ratio.[8] The golden section is a legitimate design tool, as Le Corbusier's work shows, but it is not a hidden law of all beautiful buildings.

Exercise 3.1

A vaulted room measures 4 m × 9 m. Compute Palladio's three candidate heights. Which one is also a whole-number geometric proportion, and why might Palladio have favoured it here?

Show solution

Arithmetic: (4 + 9) / 2 = 6.5 m. Geometric: √(4 × 9) = √36 = 6 m. Harmonic: 2 × 4 × 9 / 13 = 72 / 13 ≈ 5.54 m. The geometric mean gives a whole number, so 4 : 6 : 9 forms a continued proportion (4 : 6 = 6 : 9 = 2 : 3). Each step is the musical fifth, the kind of relationship Wittkower identified as central to Palladio's thinking.[7]

Exercise 3.2

A façade is 18.0 m wide. Divide it in the golden section. Then explain why measuring an existing building to "find" φ is weak evidence of intent.

Show solution

Larger part = 18.0 / φ ≈ 11.12 m; smaller part = 18.0 − 11.12 ≈ 6.88 m (and 11.12 / 6.88 ≈ 1.618). Measured buildings offer many candidate points (plinth or not, cornice or not, centre lines or edges). With enough choices, some pair will approximate 1.618 within a few per cent, so a match proves little without documentary evidence.[8,10]

04Scale

Definition. Scale is the perceived size of a thing in relation to a known standard or to other things. Mechanical scale is measured size; visual scale is how large something appears relative to nearby elements whose size we already know.[2]

We judge size by comparison. Doors, steps, bricks, window panes and handrails are "scale-givers" because their sizes are familiar. Changing those cues changes the apparent size of a whole building, even when its dimensions are fixed.[2]

Rasmussen gives the interior of St Peter's in Rome as an example of scale cues working against expectation. Its details, including the figures of putti, are so large that the eye reads them as normal-sized, so the vast interior seems smaller than it is until a person walks up to them.[3]

Scale in practice

  • Monumental scale enlarges the cues (giant orders, tall doors) to make a building read as civic or sacred.
  • Intimate scale keeps cues small and close to the body; see section 14.
  • Urban scale relates a building to its street, neighbours and skyline rather than to the body alone.

Exercise 4.1

A 12 m high single-volume community hall feels "barn-like" and intimidating to elderly users. Without changing its height, list three design moves that would reduce its perceived scale.

Show solution

Possible moves: (1) introduce a datum at about door-head height (roughly 2.1 to 2.4 m), such as a timber wainscot, a picture rail or a low canopy, so the lower zone reads at body size; (2) subdivide large openings with mullions and transoms into familiar pane sizes; (3) add elements at human scale, such as an inglenook, window seats or a lowered ceiling over the entrance and edges, so people occupy smaller "rooms within the room." Each adds familiar scale-givers.[2]

05Rhythm

Definition. Rhythm is a pattern of recurrence, of lines, forms, openings, colours or spaces, that is perceived over time or across distance. Repetition is its raw material; rhythm is repetition organised by interval.[2]

Rasmussen gives a whole chapter to rhythm and compares the experience of a regularly spaced building to the experience of music or of walking in step. We feel a rhythm in a façade because we can grasp its interval and anticipate the next beat.[3] Ching describes rhythm as the regular or harmonious recurrence of lines, shapes, forms or colours, and shows that the repeated elements can vary in size, shape and detail while still reading as one series.[2]

Types of rhythm

Generating rhythms from a pattern string

A façade rhythm can be written as a string, a convention borrowed from describing bays, where A is a narrow bay, B a wide bay and - a solid pier. The generator below draws the pattern so you can compare rhythms quickly.

function drawRhythm(pattern, unit = 12) {
  const widths = { A: 2, B: 4, C: 1, '-': 1 };       // in modules
  let x = 0, out = '';
  for (const ch of pattern) {
    const w = (widths[ch] ?? 1) * unit;
    if (ch !== '-') out += `<rect x="${x}" y="0" width="${w}" height="${ch === 'B' ? 60 : 40}"/>`;
    x += w + unit / 2;                                // constant joint
  }
  return `<svg viewBox="0 0 ${x} 60">${out}</svg>`;
}

Exercise 5.1

Write pattern strings for: (a) a Palladian or Serlian motif repeated along an arcade (narrow, wide arched, narrow, repeated); (b) a façade whose bays get steadily wider toward the centre. Which rhythm type is each?

Show solution

(a) A-B-A-A-B-A-A-B-A: an alternating rhythm whose unit is the group "A B A". (b) Using C (1), A (2), B (4) as increasing widths: C-A-B-A-C, which is progressive toward the centre and symmetrical overall. Progressive rhythms like this also create emphasis at the centre (section 8).

06Repetition

Definition. Repetition is the recurrence of the same or similar elements, such as structural bays, windows, materials or room types. It is how a building tells the viewer that parts belong together.

Ching notes that we group elements in a composition by proximity and by shared visual characteristics, such as size, shape and detail, and that repetition draws on both.[2] Those grouping tendencies were described by Gestalt psychology; Arnheim summarises them as rules of similarity, by which like elements are seen together.[4]

Repetition, modules and economy

Repetition is also economic. A structural grid of identical bays allows standard beams, panels and formwork. Vitruvius's notion of symmetria, all parts derived from one module, is an early statement of the same idea.[1] The danger is monotony. Alexander's pattern language argues for repetition of patterns, not of identical objects: the same pattern (for example, light on two sides of every room) can produce rooms that differ in size and shape.[11]

Exercise 6.1

A housing block uses 60 identical windows in a uniform grid and residents describe it as monotonous. Propose two changes that keep the economy of repetition but reduce monotony.

Show solution

(1) Keep one window type but vary grouping: pair or triple windows at living rooms and single them at bedrooms, so proximity creates a second-order rhythm. (2) Keep the structural grid but vary the infill: alternate solid panels and balconies in a syncopated pattern, or differentiate the ground floor, typical floors and top floor (base, middle, top). Both keep repetition of components while introducing hierarchy and rhythm.[2]

07Hierarchy

Definition. Hierarchy is the articulation of importance or significance among forms and spaces, so that a viewer can rank them.

Ching identifies three ways to make a form or space dominant: by an exceptional size, by a unique shape, and by a strategic location, such as the termination of an axis, the centre of a centralised organisation, or a position set apart from a series.[2] Hierarchy is how a plan tells you which room is the hall, which door is the front door, and which volume is the sanctuary.

Classical façades show hierarchy in elevation as well: a rusticated base, a principal floor (piano nobile) with the tallest windows and richest surrounds, and a lesser attic storey. Palladio's plates show this arrangement repeatedly.[6]

Exercise 7.1

In a primary school plan, list four spaces in order of hierarchy and state which of Ching's three means you would use to express each rank.

Show solution

One reasonable answer: (1) assembly hall, expressed by size (largest, tallest volume) and placement (on the entrance axis); (2) library, by shape (a distinct, perhaps circular or top-lit form); (3) classrooms, a repeated series of equal rooms, deliberately non-dominant; (4) stores and services, minimised and placed at the back. The point is that only one or two elements should be dominant; if every space is emphasised, there is no hierarchy.

08Emphasis

Definition. Emphasis is the creation of a focal point, the element that the eye finds first or returns to. Hierarchy ranks a whole set of elements; emphasis concerns the top of that ranking and the means used to draw attention to it.

Techniques for emphasis include contrast of form, material or colour (section 9), isolation, convergence of lines on one point, and placing the element at the end of a view. Ching describes how an axis can be terminated at each end by a significant form or space, which gives the axis a destination.[2] At the scale of the city, Lynch calls such elements landmarks: point references, often visible from far away, that people use to orient themselves.[12]

Exercise 8.1

A long corridor in an office has twenty identical doors and no focal point; visitors get lost. Propose an emphasis strategy using at least two techniques.

Show solution

Terminate the corridor with a window or a top-lit meeting space so the corridor has a destination (termination). Mark the reception or main meeting room door with a recessed, wider entrance in a contrasting material and a change in ceiling height (contrast and size). Lynch's research suggests that distinctive, well-placed landmarks improve orientation.[12]

09Contrast

Definition. Contrast is the juxtaposition of opposing qualities: solid and void, light and dark, rough and smooth, large and small, open and enclosed, old and new.

Rasmussen devotes chapters to "solids and cavities" in architecture and to the contrasting effects between them, arguing that we experience a space partly by its difference from what surrounds it.[3] Contrast sharpens perception: a bright room feels brighter after a dim passage, and a smooth plane looks smoother against rough masonry.

The Pantheon

The Pantheon in Rome (rebuilt under Hadrian and completed about AD 125) is a classic case of contrast. Visitors pass from a columnar, shadowed portico into a single domed rotunda lit only by the open oculus at its crown. The rotunda's interior height equals its diameter, about 43 m (150 Roman feet), so a sphere of that diameter would fit exactly inside it.[13]

Contrast can also be stylistic. Venturi's Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture (1966) argued against the reduction of modernism and for "messy vitality", answering Mies van der Rohe's "less is more" with "less is a bore."[14] His point was that tension and contrast between elements can produce richer meaning than uniformity.

Exercise 9.1

The Pantheon rotunda has interior diameter D ≈ 43.3 m and height to the oculus equal to D. What is the volume of the inscribed sphere, and why does this geometry make the interior feel unified rather than merely large?

Show solution

V = (4/3) π r³ with r ≈ 21.65 m, so V ≈ (4/3) × 3.1416 × 10 148 ≈ 42 500 m³. Because height equals diameter, the space has a single, centralised geometry; a visitor standing anywhere perceives the same governing figure. The contrast with the portico intensifies it, and the oculus provides one dominant light source that reinforces the centre.

10Unity and harmony

Definition. Unity is the quality of a composition whose parts are perceived as belonging to one whole. Harmony is the agreeable relationship among those parts, through shared proportions, materials, forms and details.

Alberti's definition of beauty, in his fifteenth-century treatise On the Art of Building, remains the classic statement: beauty is the reasoned harmony (concinnitas) of all the parts within a body, so that nothing may be added, taken away or altered without making it worse.[15] Wittkower showed that Alberti and Palladio understood this harmony through the ratios of musical consonance: the octave (1 : 2), the fifth (2 : 3) and the fourth (3 : 4).[7]

Means of achieving unity

  • Shared proportion system: one module, grid or set of ratios across plan, section and elevation (section 3).
  • Consistent material palette: a limited set of materials used with consistent jointing and detailing.
  • Repeated formal motifs: the same window type, roof pitch or column profile (section 6).
  • A dominant element to which others are subordinate (section 7). Arnheim notes that a structure is easiest to perceive when it is organised as a whole with clear subordination of parts.[4]

Unity is not sameness. Unity with variety, a clear whole with differentiated parts, is generally the aim; total uniformity produces monotony and total variety produces chaos.

Exercise 10.1

An extension to a nineteenth-century brick house is designed in glass and steel. A planning officer says it "lacks harmony." Give two strategies that keep the contrasting materials but achieve unity.

Show solution

(1) Shared proportion and datum: align the extension's floor levels, eaves or parapet, and mullion spacing with the existing storey heights and window widths, so the new work uses the old building's measures. (2) Subordination: keep the extension lower and set back so the original house remains dominant; the contrast then reads as a deliberate secondary element within one hierarchy. Shared proportion and clear hierarchy can unify parts that differ in material.

11Order

Definition. Order is a condition in which each part of a whole is properly disposed in relation to the other parts and to its purpose. It is broader than geometric regularity.[2]

Vitruvius placed order (ordinatio) first among his compositional terms and defined it as giving due measure to the members of a work, considered separately and in relation to the whole.[1] Ching lists the devices by which order is established: axis, symmetry, hierarchy, rhythm, datum (a line, plane or volume that gathers a random pattern of elements by its continuity and regularity) and transformation (manipulating a prototype while keeping its identity).[2] Unwin adds that architecture begins with the "identification of place", and that the geometry of a building, whether a grid, a circle or a line, is one of the main tools for giving places identity and order.[5]

Snapping to a modular grid

Ordering by grid is easy to automate, and most CAD software does it. The function below snaps a dimension to the nearest multiple of a planning module, here the 600 mm module common in European construction (any module works).

const snap = (value, module = 600) => Math.round(value / module) * module;

// A sketched room of 4 130 × 3 270 mm on a 600 mm grid:
snap(4130); // 4200
snap(3270); // 3000  (also try 300 mm: 3300)

Exercise 11.1

Name Ching's six ordering devices and give one built example or plan situation for "datum" and one for "transformation."

Show solution

Axis, symmetry, hierarchy, rhythm, datum, transformation.[2] Datum: a continuous colonnade or arcade around a town square that unifies buildings of different styles and heights behind it. Transformation: the Palladian villa type varied across many villas, keeping a central hall and flanking rooms while changing porticoes, wings and dimensions; Palladio's own book presents such variations.[6]

12Movement

Definition. Movement concerns how people physically travel through buildings and cities and how architecture guides the eye and body along a route. It includes circulation (actual paths) and implied movement (forms that suggest direction).

Ching analyses circulation as five components: the approach to the building, the entrance, the configuration of the path (linear, radial, spiral, grid, network or composite), the path–space relationship (paths may pass by, pass through, or terminate in spaces), and the form of the circulation space (enclosed, open on one side, or open on both).[2] Lynch's study of Boston, Jersey City and Los Angeles found that people's mental images of cities are built from paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks, with paths often the dominant element.[12]

Implied movement

Forms can suggest motion even when nothing moves. Rasmussen notes that a regular rhythm draws the eye along a façade like a beat in music.[3] Diagonals, ramps, tapering forms and progressive rhythms point the viewer in a direction; static, centralised forms ask the viewer to stop.

Exercise 12.1

For a small museum with five galleries, compare a linear "enfilade" route and a radial route from a central hall. Which gives curators more control over the visitor's sequence, and which gives visitors more freedom?

Show solution

A linear route (paths passing through each gallery) fixes the order, so curators control the narrative, but visitors cannot skip galleries easily. A radial plan (each gallery terminating a path from the hub) lets visitors choose, and returns them to a central node that orients them, but weakens any intended sequence. Many museums combine the two (a composite configuration).[2]

13Spatial sequence

Definition. Spatial sequence is the ordered succession of spaces experienced along a route, designed so that their differences in size, light, enclosure and view produce a deliberate progression, often toward a climax.

Cullen called the townscape version of this serial vision: as a pedestrian walks at a steady pace, the scene is revealed as a series of "jerks or revelations", an existing view followed by emerging views, and the designer can shape those revelations into a dramatic sequence.[16] Le Corbusier spoke of the promenade architecturale, most famously at the Villa Savoye, where a ramp carries the visitor up through the house from the entrance to the roof garden; Samuel traces how this idea runs through his work.[17]

Public to private

Alexander's A Pattern Language gives two patterns that govern domestic sequence. Entrance Transition (pattern 112) proposes a transitional space, with a change of light, level, surface or view, between the street and the inside of a building. Intimacy Gradient (pattern 127) proposes laying out spaces in sequence from the most public, near the entrance, to the most private, deepest inside.[11]

Scoring a sequence

A sequence can be described numerically, for example by ceiling height and light level along a route. The function below flags each step whose ratio of change is large enough to be strongly felt. The 1.5 threshold is a teaching assumption, not an established perceptual constant.

// rooms: [{name, height_m, lux}] in route order
function sequenceContrasts(rooms, threshold = 1.5) {
  const out = [];
  for (let i = 1; i < rooms.length; i++) {
    const h = rooms[i].height_m / rooms[i - 1].height_m;
    const l = rooms[i].lux / rooms[i - 1].lux;
    const strong = Math.max(h, 1 / h) >= threshold || Math.max(l, 1 / l) >= threshold;
    out.push(`${rooms[i - 1].name} → ${rooms[i].name}: height ×${h.toFixed(2)}, light ×${l.toFixed(2)}${strong ? '  ◀ strong' : ''}`);
  }
  return out;
}

14Human scale

Definition. Human scale is the sizing of architectural elements and spaces in relation to the dimensions, reach, senses and movement of the human body.[2]

Vitruvius based the proportions of temples on the proportions of the well-formed human body, and described a man with outstretched limbs whose navel is the centre of a circle that touches his fingers and toes, and whose height equals his arm span, forming a square.[1] Leonardo's drawing of "Vitruvian Man" illustrates that passage.

The Modulor

Le Corbusier's Modulor (published 1950, English edition 1954) is a proportioning system derived from a man 1.83 m tall. It gives two interlocking series: a "red" series based on the navel height of 113 cm, and a "blue" series based on the height of the raised hand, 226 cm (twice 113). Successive terms in each series are related by the golden ratio.[18] Ching discusses the Modulor alongside the Japanese ken, a unit used to standardise construction, to which the dimensions of the 1 : 2 tatami mat are related.[2]

// Generate Modulor series (cm) from Le Corbusier's anchors.
const PHI = (1 + Math.sqrt(5)) / 2;
function series(anchor, up = 2, down = 6) {
  const s = [anchor];
  for (let i = 1; i <= up; i++)   s.push(anchor * PHI ** i);
  for (let i = 1; i <= down; i++) s.unshift(anchor / PHI ** i);
  return s.map(v => Math.round(v * 10) / 10);
}
const red  = series(113);   // ... 26.7, 43.2, 69.8, 113, 182.8, 295.8
const blue = series(226);   // ... 53.4, 86.3, 139.7, 226, 365.7, 591.7
// Le Corbusier's published values round these, e.g. 43, 70, 113, 183 and 86, 140, 226.

Distance, the senses and speed

The anthropologist Edward T. Hall defined four zones of interpersonal distance: intimate (to about 18 in, or 0.45 m), personal (1.5 to 4 ft), social (4 to 12 ft) and public (12 ft and beyond).[19] These distances shape furniture layouts, seating in public space, and the size of rooms meant for conversation.

Gehl argues that cities should be designed for the speed of walking, about 5 km/h, at which people can perceive detail, rather than for the speed of cars, which calls for large signs and coarse detail.[21] Alexander's Four-Story Limit (pattern 21) makes a related claim, that buildings much taller than four storeys lose the connection between the people in them and the ground.[11] Pallasmaa reminds designers that human scale is not only visual: we measure buildings with the whole body, through touch, sound, smell and movement.[22]

Stairs

The stair is the most direct case of fitting architecture to the body. A long-standing rule, often attributed to the seventeenth-century French architect François Blondel, is that twice the riser plus the tread should equal about one comfortable stride; Ching gives 2R + T = 24 to 25 in (about 610 to 635 mm).[20] Building codes set their own limits, which take precedence.

// Check a stair against the 2R + T rule (mm)
function stairCheck(R, T, lo = 610, hi = 635) {
  const v = 2 * R + T;
  return { value: v, ok: v >= lo && v <= hi };
}
stairCheck(175, 280); // { value: 630, ok: true }

Exercise 14.1

A floor-to-floor height of 3 000 mm must be climbed by a straight stair. Choose the number of risers so that R is between 165 and 185 mm, then find a tread that satisfies 2R + T ≈ 630 mm. How long is the stair's run?

Show solution

3 000 / 17 ≈ 176.5 mm (17 risers) fits the range. T = 630 − 2 × 176.5 = 277 mm. A straight flight has one fewer tread than risers, so run = 16 × 277 ≈ 4 432 mm. Check 2R + T = 353 + 277 = 630 mm. Local building codes would also need checking.[20]

Exercise 14.2

Using Hall's zones, what is a reasonable distance between two facing sofas in a living room designed for conversation, and why?

Show solution

Faces roughly 1.5 to 2.5 m apart puts the sitters in Hall's social zone (1.2 to 3.6 m), where normal conversation is comfortable without intruding into personal space.[19] With seat depths of about 0.8 m each, the sofa fronts would sit about 1.0 to 1.2 m apart, leaving room for a low table.

Synthesis and summary table

The fourteen principles are not a checklist to be satisfied one by one; they are lenses that reveal different aspects of the same design. A strong building typically shows a clear order (a grid or axis), within which repetition and rhythm organise the many parts, hierarchy and emphasis single out the few important ones, contrast sharpens the experience, and proportion and human scale tie everything to a consistent measure and to the body.[2,3]

Capstone exercise

Choose a building you know well. In no more than 300 words, analyse it using at least six of the principles in this article. For each, identify the specific device used (for example "hierarchy by placement at the end of the entrance axis") and judge whether it succeeds.

Show a model answer outline

A model answer (for a hypothetical town library): Order: 7.2 m structural grid. Hierarchy: reading room expressed by size and a raised roof. Rhythm: regular fins on the long façade, syncopated at the entrance. Contrast: heavy brick base against a light glazed upper storey. Spatial sequence: low entrance canopy, then a tall top-lit reading room (compress and release). Human scale: window seats and a timber datum at 2.4 m in the reading room.
