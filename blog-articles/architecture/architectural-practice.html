1Introduction: the shape of a practice

Architectural practice is the professional and business activity through which an architect turns a client's needs into a constructed building, under contract and within a framework of law. The American Institute of Architects (AIA) treats practice as several kinds of knowledge working together: managing a firm, managing projects, delivering design services, and handling the legal and contractual relationships that bind owners, architects, consultants, and contractors.1

In the United States the title "architect" is protected by law in every jurisdiction. Licensure is administered by state and territorial boards, which coordinate through the National Council of Architectural Registration Boards (NCARB). The usual path has three parts: an accredited education, documented experience, and an examination. NCARB's Architectural Experience Program (AXP) requires 3,740 hours across six experience areas.2 The Architect Registration Examination (ARE 5.0) is organised into six divisions with almost the same names.3 NCARB revised how AXP experience is reported and described from 18 November 2025. Under the revision, experience up to a year old earns full credit and older experience earns 75 percent, but the total and per-area hours stayed the same.4

The architect's contract with the owner sets out the sequence of a typical project. AIA Document B101–2017, the standard owner–architect agreement, divides basic services into five phases: Schematic Design, Design Development, Construction Documents, Procurement, and Construction.5,6 Programming usually comes before these phases and is often a separate or supplemental service. 2Architectural programming

Programming is the research and decision-making that defines the problem a building has to solve. It comes before design. Peña and Parshall put the distinction simply: programming is problem seeking and design is problem solving, and the two should be kept apart so that the client's requirements are agreed before anyone commits to a form.8 The method started at Caudill Rowlett Scott (CRS) in the 1960s and is still the best-known framework in North American practice.

The five steps and four considerations

The Problem Seeking method moves through five steps:8

  1. Establish goals: what the client wants to achieve, and why.
  2. Collect and analyse facts: what is known, such as site, users, codes, budget, and climate.
  3. Uncover and test concepts: how the goals might be reached. These are programmatic concepts, such as "centralised security" or "flexible teaching space," and not physical design ideas.
  4. Determine needs: how much space, of what quality, at what cost, and on what schedule.
  5. State the problem: short statements of the main conditions a design has to meet.

Each step is examined against four considerations: Function (people, activities, relationships), Form (site, environment, quality), Economy (initial budget, operating cost, life-cycle cost), and Time (past, present, and future). Crossing the five steps with the four considerations gives a 20-cell information index (Figure 2), which works as a checklist for completeness.8

Quantifying needs: net, gross, and efficiency

Programs list net assignable area, which is the area of each room as used, and turn the total into gross area by adding circulation, walls, mechanical shafts, and similar space. The conversion uses an efficiency ratio, net ÷ gross, which varies by building type. Peña and Parshall stress that space, quality, budget, and time are linked, so a programmer cannot fix all four independently. If the budget is fixed, then space or quality must give.8 This is the arithmetic that sits behind the budget conversations in every later phase.

Worked example A clinic program totals 12,600 ft² net. With a target efficiency of 0.70, the gross area is 12,600 ÷ 0.70 = 18,000 ft². At an estimated construction cost of $420/ft², the cost of the work is 18,000 × 420 = $7.56 million. If the owner's budget is only $6.5 million, the programmer has three options: reduce the area (6.5M ÷ 420 ≈ 15,476 ft² gross, or about 10,833 ft² net), lower the unit cost by reducing quality, or raise the budget.

3Conceptual (schematic) design

Schematic Design (SD) is the first contracted design phase. The architect turns the agreed program into a spatial idea and tests it against the site, the codes, and the budget. Under B101–2017, the architect first reviews the program and the owner's budget, then prepares a preliminary evaluation and discusses alternative approaches with the owner. The architect then produces schematic design documents showing the scale and relationship of the building components, which are typically a site plan, preliminary floor plans, sections, and elevations. The architect also considers environmentally responsible design approaches and submits an estimate of the cost of the work for the owner's approval.5

What schematic design produces

  • Diagrams and parti: the organising idea, such as a courtyard, a bar, or a cluster.
  • Massing and site strategy: orientation, access, and zoning envelope.
  • Preliminary code analysis: occupancy classification, construction type, allowable height and area, and egress concept.9
  • Preliminary systems choices: structural grid, mechanical approach, and envelope strategy.
  • Cost estimate: usually by area or by building system, reconciled with the owner's budget.

Schematic design is where design ideas get tested against reality. The companion article on design principles covers order, proportion, and spatial composition. In practice, those ideas must also satisfy the program, the codes, and the budget at the same moment. The architect presents alternatives so that the owner makes the defining choices while changes are still cheap.

Why early decisions matter

The AIA's guide to Integrated Project Delivery popularised the MacLeamy curve (Figure 3). It shows that the ability to influence cost and performance is highest early in a project, while the cost of making a change rises steadily over time.10 This is why owners are asked to approve the schematic design formally before design development begins.5

4Design development

Design Development (DD) fixes the size and character of the project. Under B101–2017, DD documents illustrate and describe the development of the approved schematic design. They are drawings and other documents including plans, sections, elevations, typical construction details, and diagrammatic layouts of building systems. They fix and describe the size and character of the project as to architectural, structural, mechanical, and electrical systems and the other appropriate elements. They also include outline specifications that identify major materials and systems and establish their general quality levels. The architect updates the cost estimate and the owner approves the DD documents.5

Coordination becomes the core activity

In DD the consultants (structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, fire protection, civil, landscape, and others) turn their diagrams into sized systems. The architect is responsible for coordinating them, and B101 includes the consultants' services within the architect's basic services.5 Typical DD issues include:

  • Structural depth against required ceiling heights and duct sizes, which determines floor-to-floor height.
  • Shaft locations and the space needed for mechanical rooms.
  • Wall types, fire-resistance ratings, and how rated assemblies continue through penetrations.9
  • Envelope assemblies: thermal performance, air and water barriers, and window systems.

Model maturity: Level of Development

When the design is built as a building information model (BIM), its maturity is often described with the BIMForum Level of Development (LOD) scale. LOD describes how reliable a model element is, not simply how much detail it shows. At LOD 100 an element is symbolic. At LOD 200 it is generic, with approximate size and location. At LOD 300 it is specific, with quantity, size, shape, location, and orientation all measurable from the model. LOD 350 adds interfaces with other systems, and LOD 400 is detailed enough for fabrication.11 As a rough guide, many DD models reach LOD 200 to 300 for the main systems, but the target for each element and phase should be set in the project's BIM execution plan.

Practice note Design development is where cost overruns are cheapest to catch. If an estimate exceeds the owner's budget, B101 gives the owner a defined set of responses: approve a budget increase, rebid or renegotiate, terminate, or work with the architect to revise the program, scope, or quality. If the owner chooses to revise after the Construction Documents estimate, the architect must modify the documents without additional compensation. Finding the problem in DD is far cheaper for everyone.5

5Technical documentation

The Construction Documents (CD) phase produces the drawings and specifications that set out in detail the requirements for building the project. These form part of the Contract Documents on which contractors price and build. AIA A201–2017 defines the Contract Documents as the Agreement between owner and contractor, the Conditions of the Contract (general, supplementary, and other), the Drawings, the Specifications, Addenda issued before the agreement is signed, other documents listed in the agreement, and Modifications issued after it is signed.12 A201 also says the Contract Documents are complementary: what one requires is as binding as if all required it.12

Drawings and specifications: a division of labour

A common rule of thumb is that drawings show quantity, location, and relationship, while specifications describe quality, materials, workmanship, and procedure. In North America, specifications are organised with CSI's MasterFormat, a numbering system of divisions 00 to 49 (some numbers are reserved). Each section commonly follows a three-part format: Part 1 General, Part 2 Products, and Part 3 Execution.13 Drawing sets follow the US National CAD Standard. Its Uniform Drawing System sets the order of disciplines and the conventions for sheet identification.14

What a good CD set does

  • Code compliance shown on the drawings: a code analysis sheet (occupancy, construction type, area, occupant load, egress), life-safety plans, and rated-assembly schedules. Building departments review these for a permit.9
  • Consistency: each piece of information appears once and is referenced elsewhere. Saying the same thing in two places invites conflicts.
  • Constructability: details that can be built in a sensible sequence with the trades available.
  • Quality control: structured internal reviews before permit and bid issue. The AIA Handbook treats quality management as an obligation running through all phases.1

Procurement

In the Procurement phase, the architect helps the owner get bids or negotiate proposals. This includes answering bidders' questions and issuing addenda, which become part of the Contract Documents.5,12 Substitution requests made during bidding are typically decided by addendum, so that every bidder is pricing the same documents.

6Construction administration

During construction the architect becomes the owner's representative and the person who interprets the documents. The duties are set out in A201–2017 §4.2 and repeated in B101. The key point is that the architect administers the contract but is not the builder. A201 makes the contractor solely responsible for construction means, methods, techniques, sequences, and procedures, and for site safety.12

The submittal loop

Submittals are where the design meets fabrication, and they are where some of the worst failures in building history began. Figure 5 traces the standard workflow.

Case: Hyatt Regency, Kansas City (1981) On 17 July 1981 two suspended walkways in the hotel atrium collapsed, killing 114 people. The National Bureau of Standards found that the hanger-rod connection as originally designed did not meet code requirements, and that a change made during shop-drawing development roughly doubled the load on the critical connection. The original design used one continuous rod; the change substituted two offset rods.15 The case is still the standard lesson that submittal review, changes made during construction, and responsibility for connection design must all be explicit, and that public safety depends on how that process is administered.

Three kinds of change

A201–2017 Article 7 separates three instruments.12 A Change Order is signed by owner, contractor, and architect, and agrees the change in the work, the cost, and the time. A Construction Change Directive is signed by owner and architect and directs a change before cost or time is agreed. A minor change in the work is ordered by the architect alone and must not change the contract sum or time and must be consistent with the intent of the documents.

7Project delivery

A project delivery method sets who contracts with whom, when the builder joins the team, and how risk is shared. The AIA Handbook and the AIA's family of contract documents cover four main approaches.1,10

The AIA's IPD guide defines integrated project delivery as an approach that brings people, systems, business structures, and practices into one process, so that all participants use their insights and talents together to improve results, increase value for the owner, reduce waste, and work efficiently through every phase.10 No method is best everywhere. The choice depends on how much schedule pressure the owner faces, how far the scope is defined, how much risk the owner will accept, procurement law (many public owners must use design-bid-build), and the owner's own capacity to manage the project.

8Professional ethics

Architects are bound by two layers of ethical rules. The first is the law of licensure: each jurisdiction's rules of conduct, many modelled on NCARB's Model Rules of Conduct, which a licensing board can enforce by suspending or revoking a license.16 The second is the AIA Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct, which binds AIA members and is enforced by the AIA's National Ethics Council.17

Structure of the AIA Code

The Code has three tiers. Canons are broad principles. Ethical Standards are more specific goals that members should aim for. Rules of Conduct are mandatory, and breaking one is grounds for discipline.18 There are six canons:17,18

  1. General Obligations
  2. Obligations to the Public
  3. Obligations to the Client
  4. Obligations to the Profession
  5. Obligations to Colleagues
  6. Obligations to the Environment

Ethics and competition law

Professional codes have limits, and antitrust law sets some of them. In National Society of Professional Engineers v. United States (1978), the US Supreme Court held that a professional society's ban on competitive bidding violated the Sherman Act. The court rejected the argument that the ban protected public safety.19 In 1990 the AIA entered a consent decree with the US Department of Justice. As a result, the AIA Code does not regulate fees, competitive bidding, or how members compete for work, apart from prohibiting false or misleading statements.18 This is why a modern ethics course talks about honest representation rather than fee schedules.

An ethical reasoning sequence (1) Identify everyone affected: the public, the client, the profession, colleagues, and the environment. (2) Identify the rule that applies, if any. Mandatory rules come before aspirational standards. (3) Ask whether the law is at stake, because the law sets a minimum. (4) Look for a solution that meets the client's goal and the rule. (5) Write down what you advised and what was decided.

9Public health, safety, and welfare

Protecting public health, safety, and welfare (HSW) is the reason architects are licensed at all. Licensing exists to protect the public, not to protect the profession. NCARB states that its member boards' mission is to protect the public health, safety, and welfare, and the content of the ARE and the AXP is defined around that duty.2,3

The regulatory stack

The building code's purpose

The International Building Code (IBC) states its purpose in §101.3. It sets minimum requirements to provide a reasonable level of safety, health, and general welfare through structural strength, means of egress, stability, sanitation, adequate light and ventilation, and energy conservation. It also aims to provide a reasonable level of life safety and property protection from fire, explosion, and other emergencies, and a reasonable level of safety for firefighters and emergency responders.9 The word "minimum" matters. Compliance with the code is the floor of professional responsibility, not its ceiling.

Core HSW topics in design

  • Occupancy and construction type (IBC Chapters 3 and 6). These determine allowable height and area (Chapter 5).9
  • Fire and smoke protection (Chapters 7 and 9): rated assemblies, compartmentation, sprinklers, and alarms.9
  • Means of egress (Chapter 10): occupant load, egress width, number of exits, travel distance, and common path.9
  • Accessibility: IBC Chapter 11 and the ICC A117.1 standard, together with the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, which are federal civil-rights requirements enforced separately from local code review.9,20
  • Structural safety (Chapter 16 onward), envelope and moisture (Chapter 14), and interior environment such as light, ventilation, and sound (Chapter 12).9

Occupant load and egress width

Egress design starts with the occupant load: the floor area divided by the occupant load factor for its use, taken from IBC Table 1004.5. Some factors apply to gross area and some to net area. Representative values are given below.9 The required egress width is then the occupant load times a capacity factor: 0.3 inch per occupant for stairways and 0.2 inch per occupant for other egress components (IBC §1005.3.1–1005.3.2), subject to code minimums and the code's exceptions.9

Under IBC §1006.3, the number of exits or access-to-exit points from a story rises with the occupant load. The minimum is two for 1 to 500 occupants, three for 501 to 1,000, and four above 1,000, with exceptions for small spaces with a single exit.9

10Computation in practice: fees and occupant load

Much of practice is quantitative. The two scripts below show calculations that come up on almost every project. They are teaching tools, not compliance software, and the inputs used in a real project must come from the executed contract and the adopted code.

10.1 Fee and phase billing (Python)

B101–2017 allows compensation as a percentage of the owner's budget for the cost of the work, with progress payments for each phase calculated from the most recent budget.6,7 The script below computes a fee and splits it by phase. The phase percentages are whatever the parties write into the agreement; the defaults here are illustrative.

# fee_schedule.py — percentage-of-budget fee split by B101 phase
PHASES = {                      # illustrative; set by agreement, not by B101
    "Schematic Design":       0.15,
    "Design Development":     0.20,
    "Construction Documents": 0.40,
    "Procurement":            0.05,
    "Construction":           0.20,
}

def fee_schedule(cost_of_work: float, fee_rate: float, phases=PHASES):
    assert abs(sum(phases.values()) - 1.0) < 1e-9, "phase shares must total 100%"
    total = cost_of_work * fee_rate
    rows, cumulative = [], 0.0
    for name, share in phases.items():
        amount = total * share
        cumulative += amount
        rows.append((name, share, amount, cumulative))
    return total, rows

def earned_to_date(rows, pct_complete: dict):
    """Fee earned given % completion of each phase (0..1)."""
    return sum(amt * pct_complete.get(name, 0.0) for name, _, amt, _ in rows)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    total, rows = fee_schedule(cost_of_work=8_000_000, fee_rate=0.07)
    print(f"Total fee: ${total:,.0f}")
    for name, share, amt, cum in rows:
        print(f"{name:<24}{share:>6.0%}  ${amt:>10,.0f}  cum ${cum:>10,.0f}")
    progress = {"Schematic Design": 1.0, "Design Development": 1.0,
                "Construction Documents": 0.5}
    print(f"Earned to date: ${earned_to_date(rows, progress):,.0f}")

10.3 Occupant load and egress width (Python)

# egress.py — occupant load (IBC Table 1004.5 factors) and egress capacity
import math

OLF = {                         # ft² per occupant; (factor, basis)
    "assembly_concentrated":   (7,   "net"),
    "assembly_unconcentrated": (15,  "net"),
    "classroom":               (20,  "net"),
    "mercantile":              (60,  "gross"),
    "business":                (150, "gross"),
}

def occupant_load(spaces):
    """spaces: list of (use, area_ft2). Each space rounded up separately."""
    return sum(math.ceil(area / OLF[use][0]) for use, area in spaces)

def egress_width(load, stair=False):
    """Capacity factor: 0.3 in/occupant stairs, 0.2 in/occupant other (IBC 1005.3)."""
    return load * (0.3 if stair else 0.2)

def min_exits(load):
    """Minimum exits per story (IBC 1006.3), ignoring single-exit exceptions."""
    return 2 if load <= 500 else 3 if load <= 1000 else 4

floor = [("business", 9_000), ("assembly_unconcentrated", 1_800)]
n = occupant_load(floor)                    # 60 + 120 = 180
print(n, egress_width(n, stair=True), egress_width(n), min_exits(n))
# 180  54.0 in of stair width  36.0 in other components  2 exits

The calculated width is only a capacity check. Code minimum widths, such as the minimum clear width of stairways and doors, can control instead, and so can separation rules for exits.9

11Exercises and solutions

Exercise 1 — Programming matrix

Place each statement in the Problem Seeking matrix (step × consideration): (a) "The library must support evening study for 300 students." (b) "The site slopes 4 m from north to south." (c) "Separate staff and public circulation." (d) "Construction funds of $12 million are available." (e) "Allow for a 30 percent enrollment increase in ten years."

Solution

(a) Goals × Function: it states what the building must do and for whom. (b) Facts × Form: it is a given site condition. (c) Concepts × Function: it is a programmatic idea about how people move, not a design form. (d) Facts × Economy: it is known financial information. (e) This one could go two ways. The intent to allow for growth is a Goal × Time. The projected enrollment increase on its own is a Fact × Time. Peña and Parshall stress that the information matters more than which box it sits in.8

Exercise 2 — Net-to-gross and budget

A program requires 24,000 ft² of net assignable area. The efficiency ratio is 0.65 and the estimated construction cost is $380/ft². The owner's budget for the cost of the work is $13,000,000. Is the project feasible? If not, what net area can the budget support?

Solution

Gross area = 24,000 ÷ 0.65 = 36,923 ft². Cost = 36,923 × 380 ≈ $14,030,800, about $1.03 million (8 percent) over budget. The budget supports 13,000,000 ÷ 380 = 34,211 ft² gross, which is 34,211 × 0.65 ≈ 22,237 ft² net. The options are to cut about 1,760 ft² of net area, improve efficiency (24,000 ÷ 34,211 = 0.70 would be needed), lower the unit cost by changing quality, or increase the budget. This is the trade-off between space, quality, budget, and time.

Exercise 3 — Phase billing

Using the illustrative phase split in Figure 1, a 6.5 percent fee, and a $5,400,000 budget for the cost of the work: what is the total fee, and what has been earned when SD is complete, DD is complete, and CD is 60 percent complete?

Solution

Total fee = 5,400,000 × 0.065 = $351,000. SD = 0.15 × 351,000 = $52,650. DD = 0.20 × 351,000 = $70,200. CD = 0.40 × 351,000 = $140,400, and 60 percent of that is $84,240. Earned = 52,650 + 70,200 + 84,240 = $207,090. In code: earned_to_date(rows, {"Schematic Design":1, "Design Development":1, "Construction Documents":0.6}).

Exercise 4 — Occupant load and exits

One floor contains 7,500 ft² gross of business area, a 2,100 ft² net training room with tables and chairs, and a 1,400 ft² net lecture area with loose chairs only. Find the occupant load, the stair width required by the capacity factor, and the minimum number of exits.

Solution

Business: 7,500 ÷ 150 = 50. Training (unconcentrated assembly): 2,100 ÷ 15 = 140. Lecture (concentrated assembly): 1,400 ÷ 7 = 200. Total = 390 occupants. Stair capacity width = 390 × 0.3 = 117 inches in total, divided among the stairs, and each stair must still meet its code minimum. Other components: 390 × 0.2 = 78 inches. Because the load is 500 or fewer, the minimum is 2 exits. The assembly rooms also need their own exit analysis, and the training and lecture rooms each have more than 49 occupants, which triggers further requirements such as door swing and number of exits from the room.9

Exercise 5 — Change instruments

Classify each under A201–2017 Article 7: (a) The owner adds a skylight. Price and time are agreed, and all three parties sign. (b) The owner orders a revised roof drain layout before the contractor has priced it. (c) The architect moves a light switch 300 mm along the same wall, with no change in cost or time.

Solution

(a) A Change Order, because it is signed by owner, contractor, and architect and agrees the change, cost, and time. (b) A Construction Change Directive, which owner and architect issue to direct the work while cost and time are still unresolved. (c) An order for a minor change in the work, which the architect may issue alone because it does not change the contract sum or time and is consistent with the intent of the documents.12

Exercise 6 — Construction administration

A contractor's Application for Payment says the storefront is 100 percent installed. On your last site visit, two weeks ago, you saw it about half installed, and you won't be on site again before certifying. The owner's manager tells you to "just approve it so we stay friendly." What should you do?

Solution

Under A201–2017 §9.4, certifying payment represents that the work has progressed to the point indicated, to the best of the architect's knowledge, information, and belief. You have direct evidence that contradicts the claim. Certify only the amount you can justify, or ask for photographs or another site visit before certifying, and tell both owner and contractor why. The architect administers the contract impartially for both parties (§4.2.12), so going along with an inflated application to keep the peace would be a misrepresentation.12

Exercise 7 — Delivery method selection

A private hospital system needs a clinic open in 18 months. The scope is only partly defined, the owner has a strong in-house construction team, and it wants contractor input on cost early. Which delivery method would you recommend, and why not design-bid-build?

Solution

CM at-Risk is the strongest fit. The CM joins during design, provides cost and constructability advice, can release early packages (site work and structure) while design continues, and later guarantees a maximum price. The owner keeps a direct contract with the architect, and its experienced in-house team can manage the GMP process. Design-bid-build would put bidding after the documents are complete, which would probably miss the 18-month opening and give no contractor input during design. Design-build would also be fast, but it would reduce the owner's direct control over the design of a complex clinical building. A mature team could also justify IPD.1,10

Exercise 8 — Ethics and HSW

During CD, the developer client tells you to remove the one-hour rating from the corridor walls of a residential building to save money. The code requires the rating. The client says the building official "never checks." What does the AIA Code require of you, and how is this an HSW issue?

Solution

This falls directly under AIA Rule 2.105. The decision violates the code and would materially and adversely affect public safety in the finished building. You must (a) advise the client against it, (b) refuse to consent, and (c) report it to the building official, unless you can resolve the matter satisfactorily another way, for example by persuading the client or finding a code-compliant alternative of equal cost. Rule 2.101 also forbids knowingly violating the law, and state rules of conduct impose similar duties on licensees. Corridor ratings protect the exit path that occupants rely on during a fire. That is exactly the life-safety purpose stated in IBC §101.3. Keep a written record of your advice.9,16,17

Exercise 9 — Submittal review

A steel fabricator's shop drawing changes a hanger connection from a continuous rod to two offset rods "for ease of erection." The contractor has stamped it approved. What are the architect's and the engineer's responsibilities, and what does the Hyatt Regency collapse teach?

Solution

The contractor's approval stamp does not move responsibility for the design to anyone else. A change to a structural connection is a design change, not a fabrication detail. It must be sent to the structural engineer of record, who checks the load path and either accepts the change by calculation or rejects it. The architect coordinates this and must not approve deviations from the Contract Documents that nobody has evaluated (A201 §3.12, §4.2.7).12 At the Hyatt Regency, the two-rod change roughly doubled the load on the critical connection, and the collapse killed 114 people.15 The lesson is that submittal review is part of protecting public safety, not a clerical task.
