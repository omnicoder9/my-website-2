1Introduction: why hardware is changing

For half a century, progress in computing rested on two compounding regularities. In 1965 Gordon Moore observed that the number of components that could be economically placed on an integrated circuit was doubling at a regular rate1, an observation later recast as a doubling roughly every two years and sustained by the industry for decades2. In 1974 Robert Dennard and colleagues at IBM showed that if a transistor's dimensions and voltages were reduced together by a constant factor, its power density stayed constant while its speed increased3. Together these meant that each new manufacturing generation delivered more transistors, faster transistors and roughly constant power per unit area, all at once.

That second regularity has broken down. Threshold and supply voltages could not keep falling without unacceptable leakage current, so power density began to rise with each generation and clock frequencies stalled in the mid-2000s4,5. Meanwhile, the energy cost of moving data came to dominate the energy cost of computing on it: in a 45 nm process, reading 32 bits from off-chip DRAM costs on the order of 640 pJ, more than a hundred times the energy of a 32-bit floating-point multiplication5. The gap between processor speed and memory speed, the so-called memory wall, had been predicted as early as 19956.

The technologies surveyed here are largely responses to these two pressures. Some extend the reach of silicon by changing how chips are built and assembled (advanced manufacturing, chiplets, 3D integration). Some reduce data movement by bringing computation and memory together (processing-in-memory, compute-in-memory, CXL, new non-volatile memories) or by moving data with light (optical interconnects). Some abandon the conventional digital model for a different physics of computation (neuromorphic, quantum and photonic computing). Others specialise hardware for the workload that now dominates demand, machine learning, whether in the data centre or on a battery-powered sensor. Finally, flexible electronics and brain-computer interfaces take computing into new physical settings: onto skin and into the nervous system.

2Chiplets and advanced packaging

History

The integrated circuit was born as a way to replace discrete components wired together by hand with a single monolithic piece of semiconductor7. For decades the economic logic of Moore's law favoured ever-larger monolithic dies that integrated more functions. That logic weakens at the leading edge: defect density means the yield of a die falls as its area grows, lithography tools limit maximum die size to a reticle field, and the newest process nodes are expensive for circuits such as analogue and I/O that gain little from shrinking8,9.

A chiplet architecture partitions a system into several smaller dies, each potentially manufactured in the most suitable process, and reassembles them in a package so that they behave as one chip. AMD's EPYC server processors are the best-documented commercial example: the first generation combined four identical dies in one package, and the second generation separated 7 nm compute chiplets from a central I/O die made in an older, cheaper process, which the designers report improved cost and allowed product variety from a small set of dies9. NVIDIA's Blackwell GPU, announced in 2024, joins two reticle-sized dies through a 10 TB/s chip-to-chip link so that software sees a single GPU with 208 billion transistors10.

Key concepts

Chiplets are only viable if the connections between them are dense, fast and energy-efficient enough not to erase the gains. Advanced packaging supplies those connections at several levels of density8:

  • Organic substrate (2D) multi-chip modules place dies side by side on a conventional laminate; wiring pitch is coarse, so bandwidth between dies is modest.
  • 2.5D silicon interposers place dies on a passive slab of silicon carrying fine-pitch wiring and through-silicon vias, the approach used to connect GPUs to stacks of high-bandwidth memory.
  • Embedded bridges such as Intel's Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge (EMIB) embed a small piece of silicon only under the die edges that need high-density links, avoiding the cost of a full-size interposer11.
  • 3D stacking, covered in the next section, places dies on top of one another.

A second requirement is a common language between dies. The Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express (UCIe) specification, announced in March 2022 by a consortium that included AMD, Arm, ASE, Google Cloud, Intel, Meta, Microsoft, Qualcomm, Samsung and TSMC, defines a die-to-die physical layer, protocol stack, software model and compliance testing, and maps the PCIe and CXL protocols natively12,13. The goal is an ecosystem in which chiplets from different vendors can be mixed in one package, much as boards accept standard expansion cards.

Challenges and outlook

Die-to-die links still cost more energy and latency than on-die wires, so architects must partition systems along boundaries where traffic is modest9. Testing is harder, because a single faulty chiplet can scrap a whole assembled package; heat must be removed from denser packages; and a multi-vendor marketplace requires agreement on mechanical, electrical and security standards beyond the link itself8,13. Despite this, chiplets have become the default architecture for high-end processors and AI accelerators, and advanced packaging capacity is now regarded as strategically important as wafer fabrication.

3Three-dimensional integrated circuits

History

Stacking active layers vertically has been studied since the 1980s, but it became practical with reliable through-silicon vias (TSVs), vertical copper connections etched through a thinned silicon die. Reviews from the mid-2000s laid out the motivation: in a stack, a wire that would have run millimetres across a chip can instead run tens of micrometres vertically, reducing delay and power and allowing heterogeneous layers such as logic and memory to be combined14,15.

The first high-volume success was memory. High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) stacks several DRAM dies on a logic base die, connected by TSVs and microbumps, and places the stack beside the processor on an interposer. An early HBM device described in 2014 delivered 128 GB/s across eight channels from a single stack16. HBM is now standard on AI accelerators and high-end GPUs17. Logic-on-logic stacking followed: AMD's 3D V-Cache bonds a 64 MB SRAM die directly on top of a 7 nm processor die using hybrid bonding18.

Key concepts

Three broad approaches exist8,15. TSV-based die stacking joins separately fabricated dies with microbumps at pitches of tens of micrometres. Hybrid bonding fuses copper pads and surrounding dielectric directly, without solder, enabling pitches below ten micrometres and correspondingly higher connection density18. Monolithic 3D integration builds successive device layers sequentially on one wafer, connected by vias as dense as ordinary on-chip wiring; the constraint is that upper layers must be fabricated at temperatures low enough not to damage the layers beneath. A 2017 demonstration built carbon-nanotube transistors and resistive memory in interleaved layers over silicon logic in a single monolithic chip19.

Challenges and outlook

Heat is the central problem: stacking concentrates power, and inner layers are far from the heat sink, so thermal design limits which dies may be stacked and how hard they can be driven14. Stacks also complicate testing, since a defective layer can ruin the assembly, and they require new design tools that model electrical and thermal coupling between tiers15. Monolithic 3D remains a research topic because of the low-temperature processing constraint, but it promises the greatest gains in density and communication energy19. Meanwhile, vertical integration already underpins mainstream memory: 3D NAND flash, discussed in Section 12, builds dozens to hundreds of cell layers in a single monolithic process20,21.

4Neuromorphic computing

History

The term neuromorphic was introduced by Carver Mead, who argued in 1990 that analogue silicon circuits operating in the subthreshold regime could emulate the physics of neurons and synapses far more efficiently than digital simulation, and that the brain's energy efficiency came from such collective analogue computation22. In parallel, computational neuroscience developed spiking neural networks, in which neurons communicate by discrete, timed pulses; Maass described these as a third generation of neural network models, computationally at least as powerful as networks of conventional threshold or sigmoid units23.

Large digital neuromorphic systems appeared in the 2010s. The University of Manchester's SpiNNaker project built a machine of up to a million ARM cores optimised to route small spike packets in real time24. IBM's TrueNorth chip, reported in 2014, implemented one million spiking neurons and 256 million synapses in 4,096 cores, consuming less than a tenth of a watt in real-time operation25. Intel's Loihi, published in 2018, added on-chip learning through programmable synaptic plasticity rules across 128 neuromorphic cores in a 14 nm process26. In 2024 Intel announced Hala Point, a system of 1,152 Loihi 2 processors supporting up to 1.15 billion neurons and 128 billion synapses, deployed at Sandia National Laboratories27.

Key concepts

Neuromorphic hardware departs from conventional computers in three main ways28. First, computation is event-driven: a neuron consumes energy mainly when it receives or emits a spike, so activity that is sparse in time and space translates directly into energy savings. Second, memory and processing are co-located: each core stores the synaptic weights for its own neurons, avoiding the shuttling of weights from a distant memory. Third, information may be encoded in spike timing as well as rates, and many chips support local learning rules such as spike-timing-dependent plasticity26.

The most common neuron model in hardware is the leaky integrate-and-fire neuron, whose membrane potential integrates weighted input spikes, decays toward rest, and emits an output spike and resets when it crosses a threshold (Figure 4).

Examples and applications

Demonstrated applications include low-power keyword spotting and gesture recognition, sensory processing paired with event-based cameras, constraint satisfaction and graph optimisation, and real-time simulation of brain circuits for neuroscience24,28. Intel reports that Hala Point achieves more than 15 trillion 8-bit operations per second per watt on sparse, event-driven deep neural networks27. A related design philosophy appears in IBM's NorthPole chip, which is not spiking but borrows the neuromorphic principle of intertwining memory and compute; on ResNet-50 it reported roughly 25 times higher energy efficiency than a GPU in a comparable 12 nm technology29.

Challenges

The principal obstacle is software. Training spiking networks is harder than training conventional networks because spikes are not differentiable, and there is no dominant programming framework comparable to those for GPUs28. Benchmarks that fairly compare spiking systems with conventional accelerators are still maturing, and the energy advantages hold mainly for workloads with sparse, temporally structured data.

5Quantum computing hardware

History

In 1982 Richard Feynman argued that simulating quantum systems on classical computers requires resources that grow exponentially with system size, and proposed instead a computer built from quantum-mechanical elements30. The field gained urgency when Peter Shor showed that a quantum computer could factor integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time, problems on which widely used public-key cryptography depends31. In 2000 David DiVincenzo set out widely used criteria for a physical quantum computer: scalable, well-characterised qubits; the ability to initialise them; coherence times much longer than gate times; a universal set of gates; and qubit-specific measurement32.

Physical proposals followed rapidly. Cirac and Zoller described quantum logic with laser-cooled trapped ions in 199533. Nakamura, Pashkin and Tsai demonstrated coherent control of a superconducting circuit, a single-Cooper-pair box, in 199934, and the transmon, a variant engineered to be insensitive to charge noise, became the workhorse of superconducting processors after 200735. In 2019 Google reported that its 53-qubit Sycamore processor sampled from a random quantum circuit in about 200 seconds, a task it estimated would take a state-of-the-art supercomputer around 10,000 years36; a photonic experiment in China made a similar quantum-advantage claim using boson sampling in 202037. Preskill named the current period the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era, in which devices have tens to hundreds of imperfect qubits without full error correction38.

Key concepts

A qubit can occupy a superposition of the states |0⟩ and |1⟩, conveniently pictured as a point on the Bloch sphere (Figure 5). Multi-qubit gates create entanglement, so that the state of n qubits is described by 2n complex amplitudes. The practical difficulty is that these states are fragile: interaction with the environment causes decoherence, and every gate and measurement has a finite error rate.

Quantum error correction addresses this by encoding one logical qubit in many physical qubits and repeatedly measuring parity checks that reveal errors without disturbing the encoded information. The surface code, which needs only nearest-neighbour interactions on a 2D grid, is the leading candidate because it tolerates relatively high physical error rates of around 1%39. If physical errors are below this threshold, increasing the code distance suppresses logical errors exponentially. In results published in 2025, Google reported surface-code memories on its Willow processor in which the logical error rate fell by a factor of about 2.14 each time the code distance increased by two, up to distance seven, with the logical qubit outliving the best physical qubit40.

The engineering frontier has shifted from qubit counts to logical qubits and error rates. 6Photonic computing

History

Optical computing has a long history. Lenses perform Fourier transforms in free space at the speed of light, and optical correlators for pattern recognition were explored decades ago; but general-purpose digital optical computers never competed with transistors because optical logic gates were large and power-hungry45,46. Interest revived in the 2010s for a narrower goal: accelerating the linear algebra at the heart of neural networks. A key enabling result predates this revival: in 1994 Reck and colleagues showed that any unitary matrix can be implemented by a triangular mesh of beam splitters and phase shifters47.

Key concepts

Most of the arithmetic in a neural network is matrix–vector multiplication. In an integrated photonic processor, an input vector is encoded in the amplitudes of light in several waveguides. A mesh of Mach–Zehnder interferometers (MZIs), each a programmable beam splitter set by thermo-optic or electro-optic phase shifters, then mixes the light so that the output amplitudes are the product of the input with a programmed matrix47,48. Once the phases are set, the multiplication occurs as the light propagates, with latency set by time of flight and little energy dissipated in the mesh itself. Other approaches use many wavelengths in parallel, with each wavelength carrying an independent channel49, or passive diffractive layers that perform a fixed computation as light passes through50.

Examples

In 2017 Shen and colleagues used a programmable silicon-photonic processor of 56 MZIs to run a small neural network that recognised spoken vowels48. Feldmann and colleagues built a photonic tensor core that used phase-change materials as non-volatile weights and a chip-scale frequency comb to perform convolutions on many wavelengths in parallel49. Lin and colleagues showed all-optical inference with 3D-printed diffractive layers operating at terahertz frequencies50. In 2025 a team from the company Lightmatter reported a photonic processor, packaged with electronic control chips, that ran unmodified models including ResNet and BERT with accuracy close to that of conventional electronic hardware51.

Challenges

Photonic computing is analogue, so noise, fabrication variation and thermal drift limit effective precision. Converting between electronic and optical domains at the inputs and outputs costs energy and area that can cancel the efficiency of the optical core, and non-linear activation functions remain difficult to implement optically45,46. Optical components are also much larger than transistors, limiting the size of matrix that fits on a chip. Many researchers therefore see the nearer-term opportunity in moving data optically, discussed in Section 10, rather than in replacing electronic arithmetic.

7Processing-in-memory

History

The idea of placing logic inside memory is as old as the von Neumann bottleneck it seeks to relieve. Harold Stone proposed a "logic-in-memory" computer in 197052. In 1997 Patterson and colleagues at Berkeley argued for "intelligent RAM", merging a processor and DRAM on one die to exploit the enormous internal bandwidth of DRAM arrays53. These early efforts foundered on manufacturing reality: DRAM processes are optimised for dense capacitors and low leakage, and produce slow logic.

Key concepts

The case for processing-in-memory (PIM) rests on the energy and bandwidth cost of data movement. Fetching an operand from DRAM costs far more energy than the arithmetic performed on it5, and for data-intensive workloads such as databases, graph analytics, genomics and recommendation models, a large fraction of total system energy is spent moving data between memory and processor54. PIM places simple compute units next to or within memory banks so that bulk operations happen where the data lives, and only results travel over the external bus. Mutlu and colleagues distinguish two flavours: processing-near-memory, which adds logic close to memory arrays (for example in the base die of a 3D stack or beside each DRAM bank), and processing-using-memory, which exploits the analogue behaviour of the memory cells themselves to compute54. The latter overlaps with compute-in-memory, discussed next.

Examples

Two products have made PIM commercially real. UPMEM integrates small general-purpose processors, called DPUs, into standard DDR4 DRAM modules, one beside each memory bank; an independent characterisation found large speedups for memory-bound workloads with simple arithmetic, and poor performance for workloads needing complex operations such as floating-point multiplication or frequent communication between DPUs55. Samsung's HBM-PIM places programmable SIMD compute units inside the banks of an HBM2 stack and provides a software stack so that existing machine learning frameworks can offload memory-bound kernels56.

Challenges

PIM requires changes throughout the stack: memory interfaces, operating-system support for data placement, programming models and compilers. Logic built in a DRAM process is slow and area-constrained, so PIM units are usually simple, and the benefit depends heavily on the application's arithmetic intensity54,55. Standardisation of PIM-capable memory interfaces is still at an early stage.

8Compute-in-memory

History and concepts

Compute-in-memory (CIM), also called in-memory computing, goes a step further than PIM: rather than placing a processor near memory, it uses the memory array itself as the computing element57,58. The concept is most powerful for the matrix–vector multiplications of neural networks. Store each weight of a matrix as the conductance G of a memory cell at the crossing of a row and column. Apply the input vector as voltages V on the rows. By Ohm's law each cell passes a current I = G·V, and by Kirchhoff's current law the currents on each column add, so the column currents are exactly the matrix–vector product, computed in one step in place (Figure 8)57,59.

The idea draws on the memristor, a two-terminal element whose resistance depends on its history, postulated by Leon Chua in 197160 and identified in a titanium-dioxide device by HP Labs in 200861. Resistive RAM (RRAM), phase-change memory (PCM), ferroelectric devices and flash cells can all store analogue conductance levels, and SRAM-based CIM performs similar charge- or current-domain summation using conventional transistors58.

Examples

Prezioso and colleagues trained a small neural network directly in a 12×12 crossbar of metal-oxide memristors in 201562. IBM researchers showed in 2018 that mixed analogue–digital training using phase-change memory could match software accuracy on standard benchmarks63, and in 2023 reported an analogue-AI chip with 35 million PCM devices in 34 tiles that ran keyword spotting at software-equivalent accuracy and a large speech-transcription model at near-software accuracy, with up to 12.4 trillion operations per second per watt sustained64. The NeuRRAM chip, published in 2022, integrated RRAM CIM cores with reconfigurable peripheral circuits and ran image, speech and image-recovery tasks65.

Challenges

Analogue CIM trades precision for efficiency. Device-to-device variation, conductance drift over time, read noise and non-linear programming all introduce errors, and the analogue-to-digital converters at the array periphery can dominate energy and area57,58. Practical systems therefore combine analogue arrays with digital correction, noise-aware training and careful calibration64. Digital SRAM-based CIM sacrifices some density and efficiency for deterministic results and is closer to commercial deployment58.

9Advanced AI accelerators

History

Modern AI hardware grew from two roots. The first is the systolic array, proposed by H. T. Kung in 1982: a grid of simple processing elements through which data flows rhythmically, each element performing a multiply–accumulate and passing operands to its neighbours, so that each value fetched from memory is reused many times66. The second is the graphics processing unit (GPU), which evolved from fixed-function graphics hardware into a programmable, massively parallel processor67. The training of the AlexNet image classifier on two GPUs, which won the 2012 ImageNet competition, demonstrated that GPUs made deep learning practical68.

Google's Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) revived the systolic array. The first TPU, deployed in Google's data centres from 2015 and described in 2017, used a 65,536-element 8-bit multiply–accumulate matrix unit offering a peak of 92 trillion operations per second, and on Google's production inference workloads ran on average 15 to 30 times faster than a contemporary CPU or GPU with 30 to 80 times better performance per watt69.

Key concepts

Because deep neural networks are dominated by dense linear algebra with high data reuse, accelerators gain efficiency by (i) providing very large arrays of multiply–accumulate units; (ii) using reduced-precision number formats, since inference and much of training tolerate 8-bit or even 4-bit arithmetic; (iii) organising on-chip memory and dataflow to maximise reuse and minimise DRAM accesses; and (iv) exploiting sparsity70. The Eyeriss accelerator made the dataflow argument explicit, showing that the choice of which operand stays stationary in each processing element largely determines energy efficiency70,71.

Examples of current designs

Data-centre accelerators have converged on a recipe of huge matrix engines, low-precision formats and stacked memory. NVIDIA's H100 GPU, built on an 80-billion-transistor die with HBM3 memory, added a Transformer Engine that dynamically uses 8-bit floating-point arithmetic for transformer models17; its successor Blackwell uses two dies in one package and adds 4-bit floating-point inference10. Cerebras took the opposite approach to chiplets, building a single wafer-scale processor with hundreds of thousands of cores and tens of gigabytes of on-chip SRAM to keep entire models close to compute72. Google's TPU v4 connected 4,096 chips through reconfigurable optical circuit switches, which the designers report accounted for less than 5% of system cost and less than 3% of system power73. At the inference end, IBM's NorthPole interleaves compute with on-chip memory and eliminates off-chip memory access during inference29.

Challenges

The dominant constraints are now memory bandwidth and capacity, interconnect between chips, and power delivery and cooling, rather than arithmetic throughput17,73. Accelerators are also locked into a co-design race with algorithms: architectures specialised for today's models risk obsolescence if model structures change, which favours programmable designs and flexible dataflows70.

10Optical interconnects

History

Optical communication over long distances became practical after Kao and Hockham showed in 1966 that glass fibre could carry light with low enough loss if impurities were removed74, and fibre now carries essentially all long-haul and most data-centre traffic. The question for computing has been how close to the processor light should go. In 2000 David Miller set out the physical case: electrical wires suffer losses and bandwidth limits that grow with length and data rate, whereas optical links have loss nearly independent of distance and can carry many wavelengths on one waveguide75. Silicon photonics, which builds waveguides, modulators and detectors with CMOS-compatible processes, made it plausible to integrate optics alongside electronics76.

Key concepts

Data-centre links are moving through a progression of tighter integration77,78. Today, most optical links use pluggable transceivers at the front panel of a switch or server, connected to the switch chip by centimetres of electrical trace. As per-lane data rates rise, driving those traces costs a growing share of power. Co-packaged optics moves the optical engines into the same package as the switch or processor die, shortening the electrical path to millimetres. In-package optical I/O chiplets go further, giving a processor optical inputs and outputs directly through a die-to-die interface79. Wavelength-division multiplexing allows several data streams on different colours of light in a single waveguide or fibre, multiplying bandwidth density at the package edge46.

Examples

In 2015 Sun and colleagues demonstrated a single-chip microprocessor that communicated with memory using light, with photonic devices fabricated in an unmodified commercial 45 nm silicon-on-insulator process alongside the electronics80. That work led to the TeraPHY optical I/O chiplet, designed to sit inside a processor package and connect over a die-to-die interface79. At system scale, Google's TPU v4 supercomputer uses optical circuit switches to reconfigure the topology linking thousands of accelerators, improving availability and allowing the network to be tailored to each job73.

Challenges

Energy per bit is the critical metric: to replace short electrical links, optical links must reach the order of a picojoule per bit or below, including lasers, modulators, drivers and receivers46. Silicon does not emit light efficiently, so lasers must be integrated from other materials or supplied externally; and optical components are sensitive to temperature, requiring control circuits. Packaging, fibre attachment, serviceability and testing at volume remain significant engineering challenges77,78.

11CXL memory expansion and pooling

History

Servers traditionally attach memory directly to each processor through DDR channels, so memory capacity and bandwidth are fixed by the number of channels and slots, and memory cannot be shared between servers. Several industry efforts sought a cache-coherent interconnect to address this, including CCIX, Gen-Z and OpenCAPI. Compute Express Link (CXL), introduced in 2019, became the industry's consolidating standard; it reuses the PCI Express physical layer so that the same slots and lanes can carry either PCIe or CXL traffic81. CXL 2.0 added switching and memory pooling, and CXL 3.0, released in August 2022, doubled the data rate to 64 GT/s and added multi-level switching, fabric capabilities and memory sharing across hosts81,82.

Key concepts

CXL multiplexes three protocols over one link81: CXL.io, essentially PCIe, for discovery and configuration; CXL.cache, which lets a device coherently cache host memory; and CXL.mem, which lets a host access memory attached to a device using ordinary load and store instructions. Devices are classified as Type 1 (accelerators with caches but no exposed memory), Type 2 (accelerators with their own memory) and Type 3 (memory expanders). A Type 3 memory expander appears to the operating system as additional memory, typically as a NUMA node without local CPU cores, with higher latency than local DRAM but far more capacity per socket83,84.

Memory pooling connects several hosts to a shared set of CXL memory devices, often through a switch, and assigns capacity to hosts dynamically. This targets memory stranding, in which memory sits idle on one server while another runs short84.

Examples and evidence

Researchers at Microsoft Azure analysed production clusters and found substantial stranded memory, and designed Pond, a pooling system that uses machine-learning predictions to decide how much of each virtual machine's memory can be placed in a CXL pool without hurting performance84. Meta engineers developed TPP, a Linux kernel mechanism that keeps frequently used ("hot") pages in local DRAM and migrates cold pages to CXL-attached memory, transparently to applications83. Measurements on early commercial CXL memory devices confirmed latencies substantially higher than local DRAM and showed that performance depends strongly on workload and on how software places data85.

Challenges

Added latency means CXL memory is best treated as a tier rather than a drop-in replacement for local DRAM, so the operating system and applications must manage placement83,85. Pooling raises new failure-domain and security questions, since a pooled device or switch failure can affect many hosts. Sharing memory coherently across hosts, enabled in principle by CXL 3.0, will require new software models81.

12Non-volatile memory research

History

Non-volatile semiconductor memory began with the floating-gate transistor, proposed by Kahng and Sze at Bell Labs in 1967: charge stored on an electrically isolated gate shifts the transistor's threshold voltage and is retained without power86. Fujio Masuoka and colleagues at Toshiba introduced flash memory in 1984, allowing blocks of floating-gate cells to be erased electrically at once87. As planar flash approached its scaling limits, Toshiba proposed building cells in vertical strings through a stack of layers in 200720, and Samsung's vertical NAND products followed; a 2017 Samsung device stored 256 Gb using 48 stacked word-line layers and three bits per cell21.

Key concepts: emerging memories

Researchers have long sought a storage-class memory that combines the speed and endurance of DRAM with the non-volatility and density of flash, filling the large gap between them in the memory hierarchy88. The main candidates store information in physical states other than trapped charge (Figure 12):

  • Phase-change memory (PCM) switches a chalcogenide material between a high-resistance amorphous phase and a low-resistance crystalline phase by controlled heating89.
  • Resistive RAM (RRAM) forms and ruptures nanoscale conductive filaments, often of oxygen vacancies, in a metal-oxide layer90.
  • Magnetoresistive RAM (MRAM) stores bits in the relative magnetic orientation of two layers in a magnetic tunnel junction; spin-transfer torque, predicted in 1996, allows the free layer to be switched with current rather than an external field91,92.
  • Ferroelectric memories store bits in the polarisation of a ferroelectric material; the 2011 discovery of ferroelectricity in thin hafnium-oxide films, a material already used in CMOS gate stacks, revived interest in ferroelectric transistors and capacitors compatible with modern processes93.

Examples and lessons

Each technology has found a niche rather than a universal role. MRAM has been commercialised as a fast, high-endurance non-volatile memory and is a candidate to replace embedded flash and some SRAM92. The most ambitious attempt at storage-class memory, Intel's Optane products based on 3D XPoint memory, reached the market but was discontinued in 2022, with Intel writing down US$559 million of inventory94. The episode illustrates that a new memory must be not only technically superior on some metrics but also cheaper per bit or uniquely enabling, and supported by software that exploits it. PCM and RRAM have meanwhile become leading candidates for analogue compute-in-memory, where their multi-level conductance is an asset (Section 8)57.

Challenges

Every candidate involves trade-offs between switching energy, speed, endurance, retention, variability, density and process compatibility88. PCM requires high programming currents and drifts over time; RRAM is variable from cycle to cycle; MRAM has a small resistance ratio and limited density; and hafnium-oxide ferroelectrics face endurance and wake-up effects89,90,92,93. Competing with the relentless cost reduction of 3D NAND and DRAM remains the biggest barrier.

13Advanced semiconductor manufacturing

History

Until the mid-2000s, transistor scaling followed Dennard's recipe of shrinking a planar MOSFET3,4. As gate lengths fell, the gate lost electrostatic control of the channel and leakage soared. The response was to wrap the gate around more of the channel. The FinFET, in which the channel is a thin vertical fin gated on three sides, was described by Hisamoto and colleagues at Berkeley in 200095, and Intel introduced it into high-volume manufacturing at the 22 nm node in 2012 under the name tri-gate96.

Key concepts

Gate-all-around transistors. The next step surrounds the channel completely. In nanosheet or gate-all-around (GAA) transistors, the channel consists of several thin horizontal sheets stacked vertically, each wrapped by the gate, which gives better electrostatics than a fin and allows the sheet width to be tuned for power or performance97. Samsung began production of its first 3 nm GAA process in June 2022, claiming up to 45% lower power, 23% higher performance and 16% smaller area than its 5 nm process98. Research beyond GAA includes the complementary FET (CFET), which stacks n-type and p-type transistors on top of one another to shrink standard cells further99.

Extreme ultraviolet lithography. Patterning has moved from 193 nm deep-ultraviolet light, which required multiple exposures per layer at advanced nodes, to extreme ultraviolet (EUV) light at 13.5 nm100. EUV light is absorbed by air and glass, so the tools use reflective multilayer mirrors in vacuum and a tin-plasma light source100. The next generation, high-numerical-aperture EUV, raises the numerical aperture from 0.33 to 0.55 and improves resolution from 13 nm to 8 nm; ASML shipped the first such system to Intel in December 2023101.

Backside power delivery. In conventional chips, power and signal wires share the metal layers above the transistors, competing for space. Backside power delivery moves power wiring beneath the transistor layer. Intel reported that its PowerVia test chip achieved more than 90% cell density and more than 5% frequency improvement102.

Challenges and outlook

Moore's original argument was economic as much as technical: integration paid because the cost per component fell1,2. Each new node now demands more process steps, more expensive tools and more design effort, so the cost of leading-edge silicon area has risen8,9. This economic pressure is a major reason for the rise of chiplets, which let designers reserve the most advanced and expensive nodes for the circuits that benefit most8,9. Beyond silicon, two-dimensional semiconductors and carbon nanotubes are being researched as future channel materials, and monolithic 3D integration points toward vertical scaling19.

14Low-power edge AI

History

Edge computing processes data near where it is produced rather than in a distant data centre, reducing latency, bandwidth use and privacy exposure103. For machine learning, this initially meant running inference on smartphones, enabled by compact network designs such as MobileNets, which replaced standard convolutions with cheaper depthwise-separable convolutions104. The frontier has since moved to "TinyML": running neural networks on microcontrollers with only hundreds of kilobytes of memory and milliwatt power budgets, such as those found in sensors, wearables and appliances105,106.

Key concepts

Edge AI relies on co-design of models, software and hardware to fit within tight memory, energy and cost limits.

  • Model compression. Pruning removes redundant weights, and quantisation reduces their precision. Han, Mao and Dally combined pruning, trained quantisation and Huffman coding to shrink AlexNet's storage 35-fold, from 240 MB to 6.9 MB, without loss of accuracy107. Integer-only 8-bit inference, now standard in mobile frameworks, lets networks run on simple integer hardware108.
  • Memory-aware design. On a microcontroller the binding constraint is often on-chip SRAM for intermediate activations, not arithmetic. MCUNet jointly searched network architectures and optimised the inference engine for memory, and was the first to exceed 70% ImageNet top-1 accuracy on an off-the-shelf commercial microcontroller105.
  • Dedicated low-power hardware. Small neural processing units, near-memory and in-memory designs, and event-driven neuromorphic chips all target the same goal of minimising data movement, which dominates energy at small scale as well as large5,28,58.
  • Benchmarks. MLPerf Tiny, developed by a consortium of more than 50 organisations, standardises measurement of accuracy, latency and energy on four tasks: keyword spotting, visual wake words, image classification and anomaly detection106.

Why it matters

Running inference locally means raw audio or video need not be transmitted, saving radio energy and improving privacy and responsiveness103. Always-on applications such as wake-word detection, predictive maintenance on industrial machinery and health monitoring in wearables are typical targets106. Processing data where it is produced also reduces the bandwidth and energy spent transmitting it103.

Challenges

The space of hardware is fragmented, making it hard to deploy a model across devices; aggressive quantisation can reduce accuracy, particularly for small models; and on-device learning, needed to personalise or adapt models, remains much harder than inference within kilobyte-scale memory105,106.

15Flexible and wearable electronics

History

Conventional electronics are built on rigid crystalline silicon wafers. Flexible electronics builds circuits on thin plastic, metal foil or elastomer substrates using materials that can be deposited at low temperature. Two material developments were pivotal around 2004. Nomura and colleagues demonstrated transparent, flexible thin-film transistors made at room temperature from an amorphous indium–gallium–zinc oxide (IGZO) semiconductor on plastic, with much higher mobility than amorphous silicon109; oxide transistors of this kind now drive many flat-panel displays. In the same year Someya and colleagues built a large-area, flexible pressure-sensor matrix read out by organic transistors as an "electronic skin" for robots110.

Key concepts

Making electronics that bend, stretch and conform to the body involves both new materials and mechanical design111:

  • Thinness. Bending strain in a film is proportional to its thickness divided by the bending radius, so very thin devices can bend sharply without cracking. Kaltenbrunner and colleagues built organic circuits on foils about 2 µm thick that could be crumpled112.
  • Neutral mechanical plane. Placing brittle layers at the plane within a stack where bending strain is zero protects them111.
  • Stretchable interconnects. Rigid device islands connected by serpentine or wavy metal traces allow the whole system to stretch while the islands themselves deform little111.
  • Soft interfaces with tissue. Devices that match the mechanical properties of skin or organs make more stable, comfortable and lower-noise contact113,114.

Examples

"Epidermal electronics", reported in 2011, laminated ultrathin, skin-like circuits containing sensors, transistors, LEDs and antennas directly onto the skin like a temporary tattoo, recording electrophysiological signals113. Gao and colleagues integrated flexible sweat sensors with silicon signal-processing chips in a wristband that simultaneously measured glucose, lactate, sodium, potassium and skin temperature115. In 2021 Biggs and colleagues reported PlasticARM, a 32-bit Arm microprocessor built from metal-oxide thin-film transistors on a polyimide substrate, showing that flexible processes can now produce complete processors116. Soft, conformable devices are also being developed as implantable bioelectronics114.

Challenges

Flexible transistors are slower and much larger than silicon transistors, so most practical wearables are hybrids that combine flexible sensors and interconnects with small rigid silicon chips115. Durability under repeated strain, sweat and washing; reliable power sources; encapsulation against moisture; and biocompatibility for long-term skin contact or implantation all remain active research areas111,114.

16Brain-computer interface hardware

History

Hans Berger recorded the first human electroencephalogram (EEG) from the scalp and published his findings in 1929117. In 1973 Jacques Vidal coined the term "brain-computer communication" and asked whether EEG signals could be used to control external devices118. By 2002, a review by Wolpaw and colleagues described a growing field of non-invasive and invasive brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) for communication and control by people with paralysis119.

Implanted interfaces were made possible by microelectrode arrays. The Utah array, a 10 × 10 grid of silicon needle electrodes developed at the University of Utah, could record from many individual neurons in the cortex120. In 2006 the BrainGate team reported that a person with tetraplegia implanted with such an array could control a computer cursor, open e-mail and operate a prosthetic hand by thought121.

Key concepts: the hardware chain

A BCI is a signal-processing chain (Figure 16). Electrodes pick up voltages produced by neural activity; their location trades invasiveness against signal quality, from scalp EEG through electrocorticography (ECoG) grids on the brain surface and electrodes delivered through blood vessels, to penetrating microelectrodes that record individual neurons' action potentials119,122. Front-end amplifiers must amplify microvolt-level signals with very low noise and power, so that heat does not damage tissue; Harrison and Charles's 2003 low-power, low-noise CMOS amplifier became a reference design for implantable recording circuits123. Digitisation and processing follow, often including on-chip spike detection to reduce data rates, and wireless telemetry and power remove the need for wires through the skin124. Finally, decoders, increasingly based on machine learning, translate neural activity into cursor movements, text or speech125,126.

Examples of recent progress

Electrode density has risen sharply. Neuropixels probes, developed for neuroscience research in animals, integrate 960 recording sites with on-probe amplification and digitisation on a single shank127. Neuralink described a system of flexible polymer threads carrying 3,072 electrodes, inserted by a surgical robot and connected to custom low-power recording chips124. A less invasive approach, the Stentrode, is delivered through blood vessels to lie in a vein adjacent to the cortex, avoiding open brain surgery122; an early clinical study reported its safety in four patients with severe paralysis who used it to operate digital devices128.

Decoding performance has advanced in parallel. In 2021 a participant with paralysis wrote text at 90 characters per minute by imagining handwriting, decoded from intracortical arrays125. Two 2023 studies decoded attempted speech: one, using intracortical arrays, reached 62 words per minute with a 23.8% word error rate on a 125,000-word vocabulary126; the other, using a high-density ECoG grid, decoded text at a median of 78 words per minute and also drove a speaking digital avatar129. In 2024 a speech neuroprosthesis achieved about 97.5% word accuracy with a large vocabulary130. BCIs have also been combined with spinal cord stimulation: a "brain–spine interface" allowed a man with chronic spinal cord injury to regain natural control of walking131.

Challenges

Long-term reliability is the central hardware problem: the body's foreign-body response, mechanical mismatch between stiff electrodes and soft brain tissue, and degradation of insulating materials can degrade recordings over months to years114,124. Implanted electronics must operate within strict power and heat limits while handling growing channel counts, pushing designers toward on-chip compression and spike detection123,124. Surgical risk, regulatory approval, data privacy and equitable access are equally important for clinical adoption.

17Synthesis and outlook

The fifteen technologies surveyed here are not independent. Several recurring themes link them.

Data movement is the common enemy. Processing-in-memory, compute-in-memory, neuromorphic designs, CXL tiering, HBM, 3D stacking and optical interconnects are all, at root, strategies to reduce the energy and latency of moving bits5,46,54. The energy figures in Figure 7 explain why these approaches attract such attention.

Integration has gone vertical and modular. As planar transistor scaling slows and grows more expensive, gains increasingly come from packaging: chiplets, interposers, bridges and hybrid bonding8,9. Standards such as UCIe and CXL are building the interfaces for an ecosystem of interoperable components13,81.

Machine learning drives demand and shapes design. AI accelerators, edge AI, compute-in-memory and photonic matrix engines are all specialised around the linear algebra of neural networks45,57,70, and AI decoders are central to modern BCIs126.

New physics, slower timelines. Quantum, photonic and neuromorphic computing promise qualitative changes for specific problem classes but face long engineering roads, especially quantum error correction at scale28,39,40.
