Introduction and a working method

When IBM announced its Personal Computer on 12 August 1981, the machine shipped with a 4.77 MHz Intel 8088 processor and Microsoft's MS-DOS operating system1,2. Today's PCs remain modular: processors, memory, storage, expansion cards and power supplies come from different vendors and are joined by published standards, several of which are described in section 1. That modularity is what lets a technician build, repair and upgrade a PC, and it is also why many faults are mismatches between parts, firmware and software rather than outright broken components.

Seven years earlier, Gary Kildall had already made a design decision that still shapes troubleshooting today. When he wrote the CP/M operating system, first running in 1974, he separated the hardware-dependent code into its own component, the Basic Input/Output System or BIOS, so that the same operating system could run on different manufacturers' machines without modification2. The split between platform firmware and the operating system, and the drivers that bridge them, is the frame through which every section of this article looks at hardware.

The article follows a simple, repeatable method. It is an editorial framework rather than a formal standard, and each later section supplies the specific tools that fit into it.

1. Component selection and compatibility

Background

The modern desktop is governed by a handful of interlocking standards. Intel's ATX specification defines the physical board and chassis. A full-size ATX board measures 12 by 9.6 inches (305 by 244 mm), and the specification fixes the rear I/O aperture at 6.25 by 1.75 inches along with the positions of the mounting holes3. The standard evolves with the parts it carries. Version 2.2, for example, changed the main power connector from 20 to 24 pins to meet the requirements of PCI Express3, and the ATX version 3 power supply design guide introduced the 12V-2x6 connector, which can deliver up to 600 W through a single cable4.

Key concepts

Intel's own build guide puts the rule plainly: make a build list before buying anything, because every component must be compatible with every other component5. In practice, compatibility is a chain of dependencies, shown in Figure 2.

  • Processor and motherboard. The CPU socket and chipset must match. Intel provides a Product Compatibility Tool that lists third-party motherboards validated for a given boxed processor6.
  • Memory generation and speed. JEDEC's DDR5 standard, published on 14 July 2020, launched at 4.8 Gbps, 50% above DDR4's end-of-life speed of 3.2 Gbps, doubled the bank count from 16 to 32 and added on-die ECC7. RAM rated faster than the platform supports will run at the platform's lower speed, and Intel advises against mixing kits from different manufacturers even when they are sold at the same speed5.
  • Expansion and storage. PCI Express doubles its per-lane rate each generation. PCIe 6.0, released in January 2022, reaches 64 GT/s per lane and up to 256 GB/s across an x16 link by moving to PAM4 signalling with flit-based encoding and lightweight forward error correction8. Lanes are shared, so installing an M.2 SSD can disable some SATA ports or PCIe add-in slots on a given board5.
  • Power. The power supply must provide the connectors and sustained power the parts need. Under ATX 3, the 12V-2x6 connector is rated for 600 W sustained at a maximum RMS current of 55 A4.
  • Operating system. Software sets a floor of its own. Windows 11, for instance, requires a compatible 64-bit processor with at least two cores at 1 GHz, 4 GB of RAM, 64 GB of storage, UEFI firmware that is Secure Boot capable, TPM 2.0 and a DirectX 12 GPU with a WDDM 2.0 driver9.

Worked example

A small office wants a desktop that will run Windows 11 and take a modern graphics card. The checklist below turns the dependencies above into decisions.

2. PC assembly and disassembly

Preparation

Assembly begins with the work area. Intel's build guide recommends standing on an uncarpeted surface to avoid electrostatic discharge and suggests an anti-static wrist strap, noting that straps are cheap insurance5. Before opening any existing system, disconnect it from AC power; Intel's CMOS reset guidance and Dell's maintenance guidance both make this the first step10,11.

A typical assembly order

Installing the processor, storage and memory on the motherboard before it goes into the case gives the most working room. Intel's guide describes each step5:

  1. Processor. Hold the CPU by its edges and never touch the contacts. Align the arrow on the chip with the arrow on the socket and lower it in. The CPU fits only one way and needs no force; the retention lever may need some force, but seating the chip never does.
  2. Cooler and thermal interface material (TIM). Many boxed coolers come with TIM already applied, in which case no extra is needed. When reusing a cooler, remove the old compound with isopropyl alcohol on a lint-free wiper, let it dry, and always apply fresh TIM rather than adding new material over old12.
  3. M.2 SSD. Press the drive down and secure it with its small screw. If it is not detected later, it may need to be enabled in firmware setup.
  4. Memory. The notch between the gold contacts is off-centre, so modules only fit one way. If a new build will not start, reseating the RAM is the first thing to try.
  5. Power supply. Orient the PSU so its fan draws through a case vent. Semi-modular units suit most builds because they reduce unused cables.
  6. Motherboard into case. Check that cables are threaded to the correct places first, and avoid dragging the board across the standoffs.
  7. Graphics card, front-panel and power connectors. Then the first power-on, entering firmware setup with the key shown on the first screen and setting the USB installer first in the boot order.

Disassembly

Disassembly reverses the build: power off, disconnect AC, discharge yourself to a grounded point, then remove expansion cards, cables, storage, memory and cooler before the board. Photographs of cable routing before removal make reassembly faster. Laptops add one hazard worth special mention. Dell warns that a swollen lithium-ion battery should not be used; the system should be run on battery until it will no longer power on, the battery must not be crushed, dropped or punctured, and it should be returned through the manufacturer or an approved recycling centre13.

3. Electrostatic discharge (ESD) protection

Why it matters

Electrostatic charge is created when materials touch and separate, a process called triboelectric charging, in which one material gives up electrons and becomes positive while the other gains them and becomes negative14. People generate a great deal of it. The ESD Association's figures for walking across a carpet are about 35,000 V at 10–25% relative humidity and about 1,500 V at 65–90%14, while many electronic components can be damaged by less than 100 V and some disk drive components by less than 10 V14.

Damage takes two forms. A catastrophic failure permanently damages the circuit so the device stops working, which is usually caught in testing. A latent failure partly degrades the device so it keeps working for a while and then fails early in service, which makes it much harder to detect14. A technician who handles a memory module carelessly may therefore create a fault that only appears weeks later.

Standards and principles

Industrial ESD control is governed by ANSI/ESD S20.20, which grew out of a 1995 request from the US Department of Defense and was first published in 1999; it was most recently revised in 202115. Its first principle is that all conductors in the environment, including people, are bonded or electrically connected so that they sit at the same potential, and it defines requirements for an ESD protected area (EPA)15. The ESD Association summarises control as designing in protection, reducing charge generation, safely dissipating or neutralising the charge that does occur, and preventing discharges from reaching sensitive parts. Personnel are grounded with wrist straps or a flooring and footwear system, and parts moved outside the EPA travel in packaging with discharge shielding16.

Practical rules

  • Work on a hard floor where possible and use a wrist strap connected to a common ground point5,16.
  • Keep parts in their shielding bags until the moment of installation, and return removed parts to shielding packaging16.
  • Do not clean inside a PC with a household vacuum or blower; Dell notes that these build up static charge and recommends compressed air instead11.
  • Remember that humidity reduces but does not eliminate charge; charge generation still occurs at high relative humidity14.

4. Cable management

Cable management is often treated as cosmetic, but it serves three engineering goals: airflow, reliable connections and serviceability. The ATX specification calls for venting that gives unimpeded, well-directed airflow over components such as the processor, and suggests a system fan for overall cooling3. Intel's build guide links cables directly to that goal, warning that too many unused cables obstruct airflow, recommending semi-modular power supplies, and advising builders to use the case's cable management features5.

Good practice

  • Plan before mounting. Thread cables to their destinations before installing the motherboard, as Intel's guide recommends5.
  • Seat high-current connectors fully. The 12V-2x6 graphics connector is specified for up to 55 A RMS4, so a partly inserted plug concentrates that current on fewer contacts. Push it fully home and avoid sharp bends right at the connector.
  • Secure data cables at both ends. Intel's Ethernet troubleshooting guidance starts with confirming that the cable is securely attached at both ends and within length limits17; the same principle applies to SATA and display cables.
  • Label and record. In managed environments, labelled cables and ports speed up tracing, and the asset records described in section 15 should note what connects to what18.

5. Firmware configuration

From BIOS to UEFI

The term BIOS dates to Kildall's CP/M, where it named the layer of hardware-specific code beneath a portable operating system2. Today's platforms use the Unified Extensible Firmware Interface. The UEFI Forum publishes the specification, which reached version 2.11 in 2024 and includes a dedicated chapter on Secure Boot and driver signing19. The forum also maintains ACPI, the interface through which the operating system rather than the firmware directs power management and device configuration, a model the specification calls OS-directed configuration and power management (OSPM)20.

Secure Boot and the 2026 certificate rollover

Secure Boot ensures a PC boots only software trusted by its manufacturer. At start-up the firmware checks the signature of each piece of boot software, including option ROMs on expansion cards and the operating system loader, and hands over control only if the signatures are valid21. Driver signing ties into this: on Windows 10 version 1607 and later with Secure Boot on, new kernel-mode drivers must chain to Microsoft root certificates, while cross-signed drivers are still accepted when Secure Boot is off22.

This chain has a shelf life. Microsoft's original 2011 Secure Boot certificates expire during 2026: the KEK CA 2011 on 24 June, the UEFI CA 2011 (third-party loaders and option ROMs) on 27 June, and the Windows Production PCA 2011 (the Windows boot loader) on 19 October. Each is replaced by a 2023 certificate. Devices without the new certificates keep starting normally but stop receiving new early-boot protections such as boot manager and revocation-list updates23. For a technician this makes firmware updates and certificate status part of routine maintenance rather than a one-off task.

Resetting firmware settings

When a bad setting prevents booting, the configuration can be cleared. Intel describes two methods on boards that use a jumper and a coin-cell battery. With AC power disconnected, move the CMOS jumper from its default pins 1–2 to pins 2–3, wait, and return it; or remove the battery for one to five minutes and refit it. Jumper location, wait time and procedure vary by board, so the motherboard manual takes precedence10. A firmware warning such as "CMOS Battery Low" signals that the coin cell is failing and settings may be lost at the next power-off24.

6. Operating system hardware detection

Plug and Play

Plug and Play (PnP) removes the need to assign hardware resources by hand. Microsoft describes its PnP subsystem as providing automatic and dynamic recognition of installed hardware, allocation and reallocation of resources such as I/O ports, interrupts, DMA channels and memory ranges, loading of appropriate drivers, and notification of hardware events to drivers and applications25. ACPI complements PnP by describing motherboard devices and power states to the operating system20.

Linux: the driver model and sysfs

Linux uses a unified device model that describes every bus and the devices on it with common structures. Bus layers discover devices while probing, and the model is exposed to user space through sysfs, a virtual file system in which every device inserted into the tree gets its own directory26. The lspci utility reads this information: -nn shows vendor and device IDs as both numbers and names, and -k shows the kernel driver in use for each device and the modules able to handle it27.

Example: an "unknown device" after a fresh install

On Windows, a network adapter appears under "Other devices" with a yellow warning. Its Properties show Code 28, meaning no driver is installed28. The hardware IDs on the Details tab identify the vendor and device, which leads to the correct driver package. On Linux, lspci -nnk would show the same device with its numeric IDs and no "Kernel driver in use" line, pointing to a missing module or firmware file27.

7. Device drivers

A driver translates generic operating system requests into commands for a particular device. Because kernel-mode drivers run with full privilege, Windows restricts which ones may load. Microsoft's driver signing policy requires signed kernel drivers on 64-bit Windows Vista and Windows 7, and from Windows 10 version 1607 Windows will not load new kernel-mode drivers that are not signed through Microsoft's Dev Portal, with exceptions for upgraded systems, systems with Secure Boot off, and older certificates issued before 29 July 201522. Graphics drivers carry a further requirement: Windows 11 expects a WDDM 2.0 driver9.

Driver troubleshooting practice

  • Prefer drivers from the device or system manufacturer, and check Windows Update first, as Microsoft's display guidance suggests30.
  • For a frozen or black display, Windows can restart the graphics driver with Windows+Ctrl+Shift+B30.
  • Treat Code 52 as a security signal rather than an obstacle; disabling signature enforcement to load an unknown driver weakens the protections described in section 522.
  • Code 43 is reported by the driver after the device fails, so it frequently points to hardware or connection faults rather than to the driver itself28.

8. Diagnostic tools and POST codes

Power-on self-test

Before any operating system loads, firmware runs a power-on self-test (POST) and loads its settings; this pre-boot phase ends when the firmware finds a valid system disk31. When POST fails, the display may not yet be working, so firmware has several other ways to report progress and faults.

  • On-screen messages, such as Intel's "CMOS Battery Low" and similar firmware warnings24.
  • Beep codes. Dell explains that if the display cannot show an error, the computer may beep a pattern instead. On Dell XPS desktops, for example, one beep indicates a BIOS ROM checksum failure, two beeps mean no RAM was detected, and three indicate a chipset error; newer OptiPlex models use 1-3-2 for a memory problem32.
  • Port 80h POST codes. During POST the firmware writes progress codes to I/O port 80h, and if POST fails, the last code stays there, identifying the stage that failed33. Boards with a two-digit display or a plug-in POST card show these values. Sun's Ultra 20 workstation, for instance, documented codes such as 52h ("test all memory") and FFh ("boot attempt") on a motherboard LED34.
  • Diagnostic LEDs on many business desktops and laptops, which encode the same information as colour or blink patterns32.

Operating system tools

Once Windows loads, Device Manager (section 7), the Windows Memory Diagnostic (section 9) and Startup Repair (section 11) cover most first-line diagnosis. On Linux, lspci, the sysfs tree and the hardware-monitoring interface expose similar information26,27,35. Manufacturers also ship pre-boot diagnostics; Dell, for example, directs users with certain beep codes to run its Preboot Diagnostic Test32.

Example: two beeps and a blank screen

A Dell XPS desktop gives two beeps after a memory upgrade. Dell's table maps two beeps to "no RAM detected"32. Reseating the new modules, Intel's first recommendation for a system that will not start5, restores normal POST. Had a POST display been present, a halt in the memory range would have pointed to the same place33.

9. Memory testing and storage diagnostics

How often memory fails

Memory errors are more common than many technicians assume. A 2009 field study by Schroeder, Pinheiro and Weber, covering Google's server fleet over two and a half years, found DRAM error rates orders of magnitude higher than earlier reports; about a third of machines and more than 8% of DIMMs saw at least one correctable error per year. The errors were dominated by hard (repeatable) faults rather than soft errors, and temperature had a surprisingly small effect36. Hard faults are exactly what a memory test can find, and they are a good reason to test rather than reinstall when a system crashes randomly.

Memory testing tools

The original MemTest-86 was written by Chris Brady between 1994 and 2002. Its open-source descendant Memtest86+ is released under the GNU GPL version 2, runs from legacy BIOS or UEFI, and gained a new code base in 2022 under Martin Whitaker and Sam Demeulemeester37. The commercial MemTest86, maintained by PassMark, describes two core algorithms38:

  • Moving inversions. Fill memory with a pattern; then, from the lowest address upward, check each location and write its complement; then repeat from the highest address downward. This catches cells that flip or influence their neighbours.
  • Modulo-X. Write a pattern to every 20th location and the complement everywhere else, then verify. The algorithm was designed to defeat caching and buffering, which can hide errors from simpler tests.

Windows includes its own tool. Running mdsched.exe schedules the Windows Memory Diagnostic at the next restart; pressing F1 during the test selects a Basic, Standard or Extended test mix, and the results appear after the next sign-in. Windows may also prompt for a memory test after detecting a crash caused by failing memory39.

Storage health

Hard drives and SSDs report health data through S.M.A.R.T. (Self-Monitoring, Analysis and Reporting Technology) on SATA, and through the SMART/Health Information log on NVMe. Google's landmark 2007 study of more than 100,000 drives showed both the value and the limits of this data. After a drive's first scan error it was 39 times more likely to fail within 60 days, and after its first reallocated sector over 14 times more likely; yet more than 36% of failed drives showed zero counts on all the SMART variables studied, so SMART alone cannot reliably predict an individual drive's failure40.

NVMe drives expose a structured health log. Microsoft's documentation of the NVMe health structure lists critical warning bits for available spare falling below threshold, temperature out of range, reliability degraded, media placed in read-only mode, and volatile memory backup failure, alongside a composite temperature in kelvin, an available spare percentage, a "percentage used" endurance estimate that may exceed 100, and a count of unrecovered media errors41.

10. Temperature and fan monitoring

Processor temperatures

Every modern processor protects itself. Intel defines Tjunction max as the highest junction temperature a processor will allow, typically around 100–110 °C depending on the model. As a core approaches its limit, the processor reduces frequency and power, which can cost performance, and if throttling cannot hold the temperature it shuts down to prevent permanent damage. Intel adds that reaching maximum temperature under a sustained workload is not necessarily cause for concern42. The practical point is that a hot processor is usually a slow processor long before it is a broken one, so unexplained slowdowns under load deserve a temperature check.

Temperature and other components

Field data complicate the simple rule that cooler is always better. In Google's disk study, lower temperatures were associated with higher failure rates over most of the observed range40, and in the DRAM study temperature had a surprisingly small effect on error rates36. Neither result is a licence to run hot; they show that temperature should be judged against each component's specification rather than by intuition.

How PWM fan control works

Four-pin fans follow an Intel specification, "4-Wire Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) Controlled Fans" (revision 1.3, September 2005). The motherboard drives a square-wave PWM signal into pin 4 at a target frequency of 25 kHz (acceptable range 21–28 kHz), and the fan's tachometer output produces two pulses per revolution, so speed in RPM equals the tach frequency in hertz multiplied by 60 and divided by 243.

Monitoring in practice

  • Firmware. Setup screens show live temperatures and fan speeds and let you set fan curves and minimum-speed alarms (section 5).
  • Linux. The kernel's hwmon interface exposes sensors as sysfs files with standard names, including temp*_input, temp*_crit, fan*_input, pwm* controls and alarm flags in which 1 means an alarm condition exists35.
  • Storage. NVMe drives report a composite temperature and set a critical warning bit when it is out of range41.
  • Physical checks. Dust is the commonest cause of rising temperatures. Dell recommends cleaning vents on all sides with compressed air, with the system off and unplugged11.

Example. A laptop slows sharply during video exports. Monitoring shows the CPU pinned at its Tjunction limit and clock speeds falling, consistent with thermal throttling42. Cleaning the vents with compressed air11 and, on a desktop, replacing dried thermal compound with fresh TIM12 restores headroom.

11. Power and boot failures

How a PC powers on

An ATX power supply is never fully off while it is plugged in. Its +5 V standby rail (+5VSB) is always enabled whenever AC power is present, which lets the motherboard watch the power button. Pressing the button causes the board to pull PS_ON#, an active-low signal, to turn the main rails on. Once +12 V, +5 V and +3.3 V are within regulation, the supply asserts PWR_OK, its "power good" signal, after a delay that the ATX 3 guide requires to fall between 100 and 250 ms (100–500 ms on legacy designs). The supply must also ride through brief outages, with a required hold-up time of 12 ms at full load4. Only then does the processor leave reset and firmware begin POST.

Repairing the boot loader

Microsoft's advanced start-up troubleshooting guide recommends booting from Windows installation media and opening Repair your computer, Troubleshoot, Advanced options, Startup Repair, which fixes many common problems and logs its work to %windir%\System32\LogFiles\Srt\Srttrail.txt. From a command prompt (Shift+F10), bootrec /fixmbr repairs the master boot code, bootrec /fixboot repairs the boot sector, and bootrec /scanos and bootrec /rebuildbcd address Boot Configuration Data errors31.

Example: a desktop that is completely dead

No lights or fans appear when the button is pressed. Following Intel's sequence, the technician confirms the power cable and PSU switch, then shorts the power-switch header pins with a screwdriver; the system starts, so the fault lies in the case button or its cable rather than the PSU or board44.

12. Display and peripheral failures

Displays

Display faults divide into "no signal" (the link never forms) and "wrong picture" (resolution, flicker or artefacts). Microsoft's troubleshooting steps for external monitors cover the most common causes: make sure the cable is secure and try another cable; press Windows+P to check the projection mode; try a different video output such as HDMI or DisplayPort; reset the graphics driver with Windows+Ctrl+Shift+B; and update the display adapter driver through Device Manager30. On a newly built desktop, a blank screen most often means a missing power cable, and Intel's guide advises checking that all cables, especially power cables, are connected5.

USB and USB-C peripherals

USB-C combines data, power and video over one connector, so failures can come from any of the three. Microsoft documents the typical cases45:

  • Slow or no charging often happens when the charger goes through a hub or dock; connect it directly to the PC's charging port with the original charger and cable.
  • Limited display connection means the port or dongle does not support the needed Alternate Mode, such as DisplayPort; use a port that does.
  • Device might need more power: use the device's own power supply, plug the laptop in, or unplug other USB devices.
  • USB device not recognised: open the device's properties in Device Manager and follow the steps for its error code.

Device Manager codes again narrow the cause. Code 45 simply means a previously seen device is not connected now, while Code 43 means the device reported a failure to its driver28.

Example: docking station with no external display

A laptop charges through its dock but the monitor attached to the dock stays dark. Windows reports that the display connection might be limited. The dock is plugged into a USB-C port without DisplayPort Alternate Mode; moving it to the port that supports the mode restores video45.

13. Network troubleshooting

Background

The core diagnostic tool of networking is older than the PC. RFC 792, written by Jon Postel and published in September 1981, defined the ICMP Echo (type 8) and Echo Reply (type 0) messages used by ping, in which the receiver returns the sender's data so reachability can be tested46. RFC 1122 (October 1989) then required that every Internet host implement an ICMP Echo server47.

Work from the bottom up

  1. Physical. Intel's Ethernet guidance starts with confirming the cable is secure at both ends, within length limits and tested, and with using the best available cabling for the intended data rate. It also warns that a link light can be on even when communication with the switch has not been properly established17.
  2. Data link. The adapter and switch must both auto-negotiate, or both be set manually to the same speed and duplex. A mismatch allows a link but produces poor or failed communication17.
  3. Network. ipconfig displays each adapter's IPv4 and IPv6 addresses, subnet mask and default gateway; /all shows the full configuration, and /release and /renew request a fresh DHCP lease48. An address in 169.254.0.0/16 is a self-assigned link-local address, used when no DHCP or manual address is available and valid only on the local link49. Seeing one almost always means the DHCP server was not reached.
  4. Name resolution. If a host answers by address but not by name, DNS is the suspect. ipconfig /flushdns clears the resolver cache, including cached negative answers48.
  5. Application. Only when the lower layers work does it make sense to look at the application, its port or a proxy.

Example: "the internet is down" for one desk

ipconfig shows 169.254.23.7 with no default gateway. The link-local address shows the PC never received a DHCP lease49. The link light is on, but a cable test fails on one pair, consistent with Intel's warning that a light alone does not prove a working link17. Replacing the patch cable and running ipconfig /renew48 restores a normal address and connectivity.

Upgrade or replace?

Upgrades make sense when a single bottleneck is clear and the platform still meets the software baseline. When the platform itself falls short, for example a PC without TPM 2.0 or a supported processor for Windows 119, replacing the system is usually the better path. Section 16 returns to this decision.

Example: SSD upgrade for an older laptop

A technician clones a laptop's hard drive to a new SSD. Before handing the old drive to recycling, it is sanitised. NIST SP 800-88 Revision 2 defines media sanitisation as making access to the data infeasible for a given level of effort and helps organisations choose sanitisation techniques based on data sensitivity50.

15. Preventive maintenance and asset management

Physical maintenance

Dell's maintenance guidance covers the basics: turn the computer off and unplug it from both the computer and the wall before cleaning; clean vents on all sides with compressed air; avoid vacuums and blowers because they build up static; and keep vents clear, since blocked vents restrict airflow and cause overheating11. Replacing thermal compound when a cooler is removed12 and checking batteries for swelling13 complete the physical routine.

Health monitoring

Preventive maintenance increasingly means watching data rather than opening cases. SMART and NVMe health logs give early warning for some drives40,41; firmware and operating system sensors track temperatures and fan speeds35; and logged correctable memory errors identify DIMMs that may harden into failures36. Firmware upkeep belongs here too, as the 2026 Secure Boot certificate changes show23.

Quarterly to yearly (by environment)Clean vents and fans with compressed air; inspect cables and batteries11,13
At least twice a yearReconcile the hardware inventory against what is actually on the network51,52

Intervals are suggestions. Dusty, hot or shared environments need more frequent cleaning; Dell's guidance says regular cleaning extends a computer's life but does not set an interval11.

Asset management

Maintenance only works if you know what you have. The Center for Internet Security makes this its first control: actively manage, meaning inventory, track and correct, all enterprise assets so the organisation knows the totality of what it must monitor and protect18. Its first safeguard calls for a detailed inventory of all enterprise assets, reviewed and updated at least twice a year51. NIST SP 800-53 includes the same requirement as control CM-8, system component inventory52, and NIST's National Cybersecurity Center of Excellence published SP 1800-5 in 2018 as a practice guide showing how commercial and open-source tools can be combined into an IT asset management system53. A useful hardware record holds at least the asset tag and serial number, model, purchase date and warranty, location and user, firmware version, and a history of repairs, which is exactly what step 6 of the troubleshooting loop produces.

16. Hardware inventory and lifecycle planning

The lifecycle

ISO/IEC 19770-1:2017 specifies requirements for an IT asset management system covering all types of IT asset in organisations of any size; it was reviewed and confirmed in 2024 and amended that year to add climate action changes54. In practice, every hardware asset passes through the same stages.

Planning refresh dates

Two forces set when hardware should be replaced: how likely it is to fail, and whether it can still run supported software.

Failure over time. Reliability engineers often draw failure rates as a "bathtub curve", with early defects, a long stable middle and a rise as parts wear out. Backblaze's 2025 analysis of its large hard-drive fleet found the curve incomplete rather than wrong: drives under one year old failed at only about 1.3% a year, and the wear-out peak arrived later and lower than in earlier data, at about 4.25% at ten years and three months compared with earlier peaks of around 14%55. Drives, in other words, are lasting longer, but the rising tail still exists.

Software support. Hardware often outlives its supported software. Windows 10 Home and Pro reached end of support on 14 October 202556, and many older PCs could not move to Windows 11 because they lacked TPM 2.0, UEFI Secure Boot capability or a supported processor9. A lifecycle plan should record each asset's eligibility for the next operating system alongside its age and warranty so that refreshes can be budgeted years ahead.

Responsible retirement

Retirement has two duties. The first is data: every storage device must be sanitised to a level appropriate to the sensitivity of its data, following NIST SP 800-88 Revision 2, published in September 202550. The second is the environment. The UN's Global E-waste Monitor 2024 reported a record 62 million tonnes of e-waste generated in 2022, of which less than a quarter (22.3%) was documented as properly collected and recycled, and projected 82 million tonnes by 203057. Redeploying working machines, harvesting parts for spares, and using certified recyclers keep hardware out of that stream.

Example: planning a three-year refresh

An organisation exports its asset inventory and sorts it by Windows 11 eligibility9, warranty end date and drive health warnings40,41. Ineligible machines are scheduled first; eligible machines with failing storage get an SSD upgrade rather than replacement; and every retired drive is sanitised and recorded before the hardware goes to a certified recycler50. The inventory, kept as CIS Control 1 recommends, makes each of these decisions a query rather than a guess18.

Conclusion

Hardware work rests on a few durable ideas. Kildall's separation of hardware-specific code from the operating system2 still explains why problems fall into firmware, driver and OS layers. The open standards that followed the IBM PC1, from ATX to PCI Express, DDR5 and UEFI3,7,8,19, explain why compatibility checking matters more than brand. And the field studies of disks and memory36,40 explain why monitoring, testing and backups beat intuition. A technician who checks compatibility before buying, protects parts from static, reads the codes the hardware provides, works through faults layer by layer, and records everything in a living inventory will solve most problems quickly and see many of them coming.
