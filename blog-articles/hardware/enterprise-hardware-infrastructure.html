Technical article

Enterprise Hardware and Infrastructure

From the internals of a two-socket server to exascale supercomputers and GPU training clusters: the history, concepts and engineering practice behind modern data-centre infrastructure.

October 2026 · 67 references (Vancouver style) · 12 figures

0Introduction

Enterprise infrastructure is the physical substrate beneath every application, database and model an organisation runs: the servers, storage arrays, switches, power trains and cooling plants, and the management systems that keep them observable and repairable. Although cloud services have moved much of this hardware out of sight, it has not disappeared. It has been concentrated into larger facilities, where the same engineering principles apply at far greater scale [1].

This article surveys fifteen interlocking topics, from the internal architecture of a single server to the economics of a ten-thousand-GPU training cluster. Each section gives a short history, the governing concepts, and a worked example or reference design. Three ideas recur throughout. The first is redundancy: components fail, so systems are built to survive the loss of any one of them. The second is hierarchy: performance and cost are set by how close data sits to the processor that needs it. The third is total cost: purchase price is only a fraction of what hardware costs over its life.

1Server hardware architecture

History

Nearly all servers still follow the stored-program model described by John von Neumann in 1945, in which instructions and data share a single addressable memory [2]. The second foundational step came in 1964 with IBM's System/360, which separated a machine's architecture (the programmer-visible instruction set) from its implementation. This allowed a family of compatible machines at different price points to run the same software [3]. That separation is why a modern x86-64 or Arm server can run binaries compiled years earlier on different silicon.

Core concepts

A contemporary two-socket server is a small distributed system. Each processor socket contains many cores, a shared last-level cache, and integrated memory controllers that drive several channels of DDR DIMMs. Sockets are joined by a coherent point-to-point link, so a core may read memory attached to the other socket, but at higher latency. This arrangement is called non-uniform memory access (NUMA), and operating systems and hypervisors place threads and memory on the same node to avoid the remote-access penalty [4].

Performance depends on the memory hierarchy. Registers, L1/L2/L3 caches, DRAM, and finally storage each trade capacity for latency, and well-designed software keeps its working set as high in that hierarchy as possible [4]. Input/output is attached through PCI Express lanes that come directly from the CPU. The PCIe 6.0 specification, released in January 2022, doubled the signalling rate to 64 GT/s using PAM4 modulation and flit-based encoding, giving up to 256 GB/s across an x16 link [5]. Compute Express Link (CXL), ratified as version 1.0 in March 2019, runs over the PCIe physical layer and adds cache-coherent and memory-semantic protocols. This allows accelerators and memory expanders to share address space with the host CPU [6].

Alongside the main processors sits a separate baseboard management controller (BMC), a small always-on computer with its own network port. It monitors sensors and allows remote power control even when the host is off. The BMC is covered in detail in Section 12.

Example

Consider a database server whose buffer pool spans both sockets. If the query threads run on socket 0 but half of the hot pages sit in socket 1's DIMMs, every access to those pages crosses the inter-socket link. Pinning the database process and its memory allocation to one NUMA node, or partitioning the database into one instance per node, is a standard tuning step for exactly this reason [4].

2Rack, blade, and modular systems

History

The 19-inch rack predates computing. It came from telephone and relay equipment and is now codified in EIA-310, which fixes the panel width and defines the rack unit (U) as 1.75 in (44.45 mm) of vertical space [7]. Commodity "pizza-box" 1U and 2U servers filled these racks in the 1990s. In the early 2000s vendors introduced blade systems, which move power, cooling, networking and management out of each server and into a shared chassis. IBM's BladeCenter, for example, was a 7U chassis holding up to 14 blades. It had two independent power domains, redundant hot-swap blowers, four switch-module bays and a redundant management module [8].

In 2011 Facebook published its server and data-centre designs as the Open Compute Project (OCP). It reported that its Prineville facility was 38% more energy efficient and 24% cheaper to build than its existing sites, with an initial PUE of 1.07 [9]. OCP made hyperscale-style "vanity-free" hardware, stripped of unneeded components, available to the wider market.

Core concepts

  • Rack servers are self-contained. Each has its own power supplies, fans and network interfaces. They are flexible and vendor-neutral but produce more cabling.
  • Blade systems share infrastructure across many compute nodes. This cuts cabling and centralises management, at the cost of a chassis that becomes a shared dependency and a vendor lock-in point [8].
  • Modular and rack-scale systems treat the whole rack as the unit of deployment, with shared power shelves, busbars and, increasingly, liquid-cooling manifolds. NVIDIA's GB200 NVL72 is a current example: 36 Grace CPUs and 72 Blackwell GPUs in one liquid-cooled rack, joined by an NVLink switch fabric [10].

Example

A branch office with modest compute needs and limited IT staff may favour a single blade chassis: one management interface, few cables, and blades that slide in without re-cabling. A large web company running homogeneous fleets usually prefers OCP-style rack servers bought in quantity, since standardisation and supply competition matter more there than chassis convenience [1,9].

3Redundant components and failover

History

Commercial fault-tolerant computing was pioneered by Tandem Computers, whose NonStop systems paired processors and duplicated every path. Jim Gray's 1985 analysis of Tandem field data found that administration and software errors, not hardware failures, caused most outages. The lesson was that redundancy must be paired with simple, well-practised operations [11]. Gray and Siewiorek later formalised availability as the fraction of time a system delivers service, a function of mean time to failure (MTTF) and mean time to repair (MTTR) [12].

Core concepts

Availability A = MTTF / (MTTF + MTTR)

Availability can be raised either by making failures rarer or by making repair faster [12]. Redundancy does both. A spare takes over immediately, so the effective MTTR seen by users approaches the failover time, while the failed part is replaced at leisure. Common notation describes how much spare capacity exists:

SchemeMeaningTypical use
NExactly the capacity required; no spareNon-critical systems
N+1One spare unit beyond requirementServer fans, power supplies, CRAC units
2NA complete duplicate pathA/B power feeds, dual fabrics
2N+1Duplicate path plus a spareHighest-tier facilities

Inside a server, redundancy appears as hot-swappable dual power supplies fed from separate circuits, N+1 fan banks, mirrored boot drives, memory error-correcting codes, and multiple network and storage paths. At the facility level, Uptime Institute's Tier Classification System grades sites from Tier I (basic capacity) to Tier II (redundant capacity components), Tier III (concurrently maintainable, meaning no shutdown is needed for planned maintenance) and Tier IV (fault tolerant) [13].

Failover is the act of shifting work from a failed component to its partner. It can be active–passive, where a standby waits idle and takes over on failure, or active–active, where all members carry load and survivors absorb the failed member's share. Either design must handle split brain, where two halves both believe they are primary. Clusters usually prevent this with a quorum or witness and by fencing the failed node, for example by powering it off through its BMC [1].

Example

Suppose two independent power supplies each have availability 0.999. A server that needs only one of them is unavailable only when both are down at once. Assuming independent failures, that probability is 0.001 × 0.001 = 10−6, so the supply subsystem reaches about 99.9999%. The catch is the independence assumption. If both PSUs plug into the same PDU, the PDU becomes a single point of failure, which is why A/B feeds must be traced all the way back to separate UPS systems [12,13].

4RAID and enterprise storage

History

In 1988 Patterson, Gibson and Katz at Berkeley argued that arrays of small, inexpensive disks could outperform large mainframe drives, provided that redundancy compensated for the higher aggregate failure rate. Their paper defined RAID levels 1 to 5 and introduced the vocabulary still used today [14]. Double-parity RAID 6 was added later to survive two simultaneous drive failures [4]. The parity calculation in RAID 6 and in modern erasure codes descends from Reed–Solomon codes, published in 1960 [15].

Why RAID 5 fell out of favour for large drives. Field studies undermined the optimistic reliability assumptions behind classic RAID calculations. Schroeder and Gibson examined about 100,000 drives and found that annual replacement rates typically exceeded 1%, with 2–4% common and up to 13% on some systems, far above datasheet figures. They also found that replacements were correlated in time rather than independent [16]. A Google study of a similarly large population found that a substantial fraction of drives failed without any prior SMART warning [17]. Together these results mean the chance of a second failure during a long rebuild is higher than simple models predict, which favours double parity or mirroring.

From RAID to erasure coding. Hyperscale storage systems generalise RAID into erasure coding across servers and racks instead of disks within one enclosure. Microsoft's Windows Azure Storage used Local Reconstruction Codes that achieved durability comparable to triple replication at about 1.33× storage overhead, while also reducing the data read during repairs [18].

Enterprise storage media and protocols

Enterprise arrays combine hard disk drives for capacity with flash for performance. NVMe was designed for flash on PCIe, removing the legacy SCSI/AHCI command layers. The NVMe over Fabrics specification, released in June 2016, extended the same command set across Ethernet, Fibre Channel and InfiniBand networks [19].

5SAN and NAS infrastructure

History

Two models of shared storage developed in parallel. Network-attached storage (NAS) shares files. Sun Microsystems' Network File System, documented as RFC 1094 in 1989, let clients mount remote directories over an ordinary IP network [20]. Storage area networks (SANs) share blocks. Fibre Channel, standardised in ANSI X3.230-1994, created a dedicated, lossless network that carries SCSI commands between hosts and disk arrays [21]. iSCSI later carried the same SCSI block protocol over TCP/IP, and its consolidated specification was published as RFC 7143 in 2014 [22].

SAN design follows a dual-fabric rule. Every host has at least two host bus adapters, each cabled to a physically separate fabric (A and B), which reach separate controllers on the array. Multipath I/O software in the host load-balances across the paths and fails over when one disappears. Zoning on the switches and LUN masking on the array limit which hosts can see which volumes [21].

A third model, software-defined storage, builds a scale-out cluster from commodity servers. Ceph, for example, uses its CRUSH placement function to distribute data across many storage nodes without a central allocation table, so the cluster can grow and rebalance as nodes are added or fail [23].

Example

A typical virtualisation estate places its VM datastores on a block SAN for predictable latency, using Fibre Channel or NVMe/TCP across dual fabrics. User file shares and backup targets sit on NAS. Converging both onto one Ethernet network is now common, but storage traffic then needs its own VLANs or quality-of-service classes so that bulk data transfer cannot starve latency-sensitive I/O [19,22].

6Data center power and cooling

History and scale

Electricity is the single largest operating input of a data centre. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimated that US data centres used about 76 TWh in 2018, 1.9% of national consumption, rising to 176 TWh, or 4.4%, by 2023. Its scenarios for 2028 range from about 325 to 580 TWh, or 6.7–12.0% of forecast US electricity use [24]. The growth is driven largely by accelerated (GPU) servers [24].

Power distribution

Power flows from the utility through medium-voltage switchgear and transformers to an automatic transfer switch (ATS) that can select standby generators. It then passes through an uninterruptible power supply (UPS), whose batteries or flywheels bridge the seconds before generators come online, and finally reaches power distribution units (PDUs) in each rack [1]. A 2N design duplicates this entire chain as independent A and B paths, each able to carry the full load. This is the basis of Tier III concurrent maintainability and Tier IV fault tolerance [13].

Efficiency: PUE

PUE = total facility energy / IT equipment energy

Power usage effectiveness, introduced by The Green Grid and standardised as ISO/IEC 30134-2, measures how much energy is spent on cooling, power conversion and lighting for each unit delivered to IT equipment. A perfect facility would score 1.0 [25,26]. Uptime Institute's global survey reported an industry average annual PUE of 1.58 for 2023 [27]. Purpose-built hyperscale sites can be much lower, such as the 1.07 initially reported for Facebook's Prineville facility [9].

Cooling

Air-cooled rooms are laid out in hot and cold aisles. Equipment draws air from a shared cold aisle at the front and exhausts into a hot aisle at the rear, and containment barriers prevent the two streams from mixing [1]. ASHRAE's thermal guidelines recommend 18–27 °C server inlet air for its A1–A4 equipment classes, with wider "allowable" bands, for example 15–32 °C for class A1. Operating near the top of the recommended range enables more hours of free cooling from outside air [28].

Air cooling struggles as rack power climbs into the tens of kilowatts. Direct-to-chip liquid cooling circulates coolant through cold plates on CPUs and GPUs. It is standard on rack-scale AI systems such as GB200 NVL72 [10] and on supercomputers such as JUPITER, whose direct liquid-cooled booster drew 13,088 kW during its June 2025 TOP500 run [29].

Example

A 1 MW IT load at PUE 1.58 draws about 1.58 MW from the grid, so roughly 580 kW goes to overhead. Improving to PUE 1.2 cuts overhead to 200 kW. Over a year (8,760 h) that saves about 3,300 MWh, and it is achieved without changing a single server [25].

7High-speed network fabrics

History

Ethernet was described by Metcalfe and Boggs at Xerox PARC in 1976 as a shared coaxial medium with carrier-sense collision detection [30]. Half a century later it is a switched, full-duplex fabric. IEEE 802.3df, approved in February 2024, standardised 800 Gb/s Ethernet [31]. The topology theory used in modern data-centre networks is older still. Charles Clos showed in 1953 that multi-stage networks of small switches can be non-blocking [32], and Leiserson's 1985 fat-tree showed how to provision bandwidth that grows toward the root of a tree [33]. In 2008, Al-Fares and colleagues demonstrated that a fat-tree built from identical commodity switches could deliver full bisection bandwidth for a data centre. With k-port switches, such a fabric supports k³/4 hosts [34].

In parallel, HPC developed specialised low-latency fabrics. InfiniBand came from the 1999 merger of the competing Future I/O and Next Generation I/O efforts, and version 1.0 of its specification was released in October 2000 [35]. Its key feature is remote direct memory access (RDMA), which moves data directly between application memory on two machines without involving either operating system kernel. RoCEv2 carries InfiniBand transport over routable UDP/IP, which brings RDMA to Ethernet networks [36].

Core concepts

  • Leaf–spine (two-tier Clos). Every leaf (top-of-rack) switch connects to every spine switch, so any two servers are at most three switch hops apart. Equal-cost multipath routing spreads flows across all spines [34].
  • Oversubscription. This is the ratio of server-facing to spine-facing bandwidth on a leaf. A ratio of 1:1 is non-blocking, while 3:1 is common for general-purpose workloads. AI training fabrics are usually built non-blocking [1].
  • Lossless transport. RDMA performs poorly under packet loss, so Ethernet RDMA fabrics rely on priority flow control and congestion notification. The Ultra Ethernet Consortium's Specification 1.0, published in June 2025, defines an Ethernet-based stack for AI and HPC that modernises RDMA transport and is designed to scale to millions of endpoints [37].

Example

With 64-port switches, a three-tier fat tree as described by Al-Fares et al. can connect 64³/4 = 65,536 servers at full bisection bandwidth using only identical commodity switches [34]. A smaller enterprise using 48-port leaves can run 32 ports down to servers and 16 up to spines, giving a 2:1 oversubscription ratio, which is acceptable for most virtualised workloads.

8Hardware virtualization and I/O passthrough

History

IBM mainframes ran virtual machines in the 1960s and 1970s. In 1974 Popek and Goldberg set out the formal conditions for an efficient virtual machine monitor. The VMM must provide equivalence (programs behave as on bare hardware), resource control (the VMM retains control of the hardware) and efficiency (most instructions run directly on the hardware). A machine meets these conditions if every sensitive instruction is also privileged, so that it traps to the VMM [38]. The x86 architecture did not meet this condition. VMware overcame it in 1999 with dynamic binary translation [39], and Xen in 2003 took the alternative approach of paravirtualization, modifying the guest kernel instead [40]. Intel VT-x (2005) and AMD-V then added hardware support for trapping guest operations [41], which made simpler hypervisors such as Linux KVM practical [42].

Core concepts

  • CPU virtualization. Hardware root and non-root modes let the guest kernel run in its normal privilege ring while the hypervisor intercepts sensitive events [41].
  • Memory virtualization. Second-level address translation (Intel EPT, AMD NPT/RVI) maps guest-physical to host-physical addresses in hardware, which removes the need for software shadow page tables [4].
  • I/O virtualization. Emulated devices are flexible but slow, paravirtual drivers such as virtio are faster, and device passthrough is fastest. In passthrough, the guest drives a physical PCIe device directly. This is only safe with an IOMMU (Intel VT-d, AMD-Vi), which remaps the device's DMA addresses and interrupts so that the device can reach only its own guest's memory [43].
  • SR-IOV. The PCI-SIG Single Root I/O Virtualization specification (2007) lets one physical device present a full-featured physical function (PF) plus many lightweight virtual functions (VFs). Each VF can be passed through to a different VM, giving near-native performance without one card per guest [44].

Example

A telecom network-function VM that must forward millions of packets per second is given an SR-IOV VF, which bypasses the hypervisor's software switch. An ML inference VM receives a whole GPU through PCIe passthrough. The trade-off is that a passed-through device ties the VM to that host's hardware, which complicates live migration, so general-purpose VMs typically stay on paravirtual devices [43,44].

9GPU clusters and AI infrastructure

History

Graphics processors became general-purpose parallel engines in the late 2000s, and deep learning made them the dominant data-centre accelerator. Google built its own Tensor Processing Unit. Its first-generation TPU, deployed from 2015, was reported to run inference 15–30× faster than contemporary CPUs and GPUs, with 30–80× better performance per watt [45]. NVIDIA's H100 (2022), built on a custom TSMC 4N process with 80 billion transistors, pairs over 3 TB/s of HBM3 memory bandwidth with 900 GB/s of fourth-generation NVLink across 18 links. Its NVLink Switch System can join up to 256 GPUs [46]. By 2024–2025, rack-scale systems such as GB200 NVL72 joined 72 GPUs in a single NVLink domain with 130 TB/s of GPU-to-GPU bandwidth and 13.4 TB of HBM3e [10].

Core concepts

Training a large model splits work across thousands of accelerators in several dimensions at once. Data parallelism gives each GPU a replica of the model and a slice of the batch. Tensor parallelism splits individual layers across GPUs. Pipeline parallelism assigns successive layers to successive GPU groups. Narayanan et al. combined all three ("3D parallelism") to train a trillion-parameter model on 3,072 GPUs at 502 petaFLOP/s, or 52% of theoretical peak [47]. Each dimension has different communication needs, and these map onto a two-level network:

  • Scale-up: NVLink or an equivalent within a node or rack, for the bandwidth-hungry tensor-parallel traffic [10,46].
  • Scale-out: InfiniBand or RDMA Ethernet between nodes, usually with one NIC per GPU in a "rail-optimised", non-blocking fabric, carrying data- and pipeline-parallel traffic [36,37].

Reliability at scale

Large synchronous training jobs magnify hardware failure rates, because one failed GPU stalls thousands. Meta reported that during a 54-day pre-training period for Llama 3 on 16,384 H100 GPUs there were 419 unexpected interruptions. Faulty GPUs caused 30.1% and HBM3 memory 17.2% of them, yet automated detection and restart kept effective training time above 90% [48,49]. AI infrastructure therefore treats fast fault detection, frequent checkpointing to high-throughput storage, and spare capacity as core design requirements.

Example

A 1,024-GPU H100 cluster is typically built as 128 eight-GPU nodes. NVLink inside each node carries tensor-parallel traffic at 900 GB/s per GPU [46], and eight rails of 400 Gb/s RDMA carry data-parallel all-reduce traffic between nodes. The facility must be designed alongside the cluster. GPU racks are dense enough that liquid cooling and very high per-rack power delivery are the norm for current-generation systems [10,24].

10High-performance computing (HPC)

History

Seymour Cray's CRAY-1, first delivered in 1976, defined the vector supercomputer era [50]. In 1994 the Beowulf project at NASA Goddard showed that a cluster of 16 commodity Intel processors, linked by ordinary Ethernet and running Linux, could deliver useful parallel performance at a fraction of the cost [51]. Commodity clusters have dominated since then. The TOP500 list, which ranks systems by the LINPACK (HPL) dense linear-algebra benchmark, has been published twice yearly since 1993 [52]. Frontier at Oak Ridge became the first system to exceed one exaflop/s on HPL in June 2022 [53]. In the June 2025 list, El Capitan at Lawrence Livermore led with 1.742 exaflop/s from 11,039,616 cores on an HPE Slingshot-11 interconnect, ahead of Frontier (1.353 EF/s) and Aurora (1.012 EF/s) [29].

Core concepts

Amdahl: Speedup(N) = 1 / ((1 − p) + p/N)    Gustafson: Scaled speedup(N) = (1 − p) + p·N

Amdahl observed in 1967 that the serial fraction (1 − p) of a program caps its speedup however many processors are added [54]. Gustafson countered in 1988 that, in practice, scientists grow the problem size with the machine, so the parallel portion dominates and "scaled speedup" can remain nearly linear [55]. Both views shape HPC design. Interconnect latency and bandwidth determine how small the effective serial and communication fraction can be. Parallel codes overwhelmingly use the Message Passing Interface (MPI), a portable standard whose current version is 4.1 [56].

A typical HPC system has login nodes, thousands of compute nodes, a low-latency interconnect (InfiniBand, Slingshot or similar), a parallel filesystem for scratch data, and a batch scheduler that allocates nodes to jobs [29,51].

Example

For a code that is 95% parallel (p = 0.95), Amdahl's law limits speedup on 1,000 processors to 1 / (0.05 + 0.00095) ≈ 19.6×. If the problem grows with the machine, as Gustafson argued, the scaled speedup on the same 1,000 processors is 0.05 + 0.95 × 1,000 ≈ 950× [54,55]. Which model applies depends on the science. A fixed-size weather forecast that must finish faster behaves like Amdahl's case, while a higher-resolution simulation behaves like Gustafson's.

11Hardware monitoring and remote management

Concepts

Hardware monitoring operates at two levels. In-band monitoring runs inside the host operating system, through agents that read SMART drive attributes, ECC error counts, and CPU and GPU telemetry. Out-of-band (OOB) monitoring is performed by the BMC over a separate management network and keeps working when the host OS has crashed or the server is powered off [57]. A complete monitoring stack collects sensor readings (temperatures, voltages, fan speeds, power draw), event logs, and inventory data, sends them to a time-series database, and raises alerts on thresholds or trends.

Predicting failures from telemetry is valuable but imperfect. Google's large-scale drive study found that some SMART parameters, such as scan errors and reallocation counts, were strongly associated with subsequent failure. However, many drives failed with no SMART warning at all, so the authors concluded that SMART alone cannot reliably predict individual drive failures [17]. Fleet operators therefore combine predictive signals with redundancy, as described in Section 3 and Section 4.

Good remote management practice includes the following:

  • A physically or logically separate management network for BMCs, never exposed to the Internet [58].
  • Remote console (KVM-over-IP) and virtual media for OS installation without a site visit.
  • Automated firmware inventory and update pipelines.
  • Integration of hardware alerts with ticketing, so that a failed fan or a predicted drive failure produces a work order automatically [1].

12IPMI, BMC, and Redfish

History

On 17 February 1998, Intel, Dell, Hewlett-Packard and NEC announced the Intelligent Platform Management Interface (IPMI). It was a standard hardware-level interface for monitoring temperature, voltage, fans, power supplies and chassis status on Intel-architecture servers [59]. IPMI version 2.0 added the RMCP+ protocol for authenticated network access, and its most recent document revision, 1.1, was published in 2013 [57]. In July 2013, US-CERT warned that IPMI implementations commonly used default or blank passwords, could expose password hashes, and could permit authentication bypass through "cipher 0". It advised restricting IPMI (UDP port 623) to internal management networks, enforcing strong unique passwords, enabling encryption, and disabling cipher 0 and anonymous login [58].

The DMTF released Redfish 1.0 in August 2015 as a modern successor to IPMI, designed for cloud-scale fleets [60]. The specification has continued to evolve, and version 1.23.0 was published in September 2025 [61].

The BMC is the hardware that implements both interfaces. It is typically an Arm system-on-chip on the motherboard, with its own firmware, memory and network port, powered from standby power whenever the chassis is plugged in [57]. Because the BMC can reflash firmware, mount virtual media and power-cycle the host, a compromised BMC gives an attacker control below the operating system. US-CERT therefore recommends sanitising BMC flash, including stored credentials, before equipment leaves service [58].

Example

Reading a server's power state and its first temperature sensor with each interface:

# IPMI (ipmitool, RMCP+ / lanplus)
ipmitool -I lanplus -H 10.0.0.21 -U admin -P '****' chassis power status
ipmitool -I lanplus -H 10.0.0.21 -U admin -P '****' sdr type Temperature

# Redfish (HTTPS + JSON)
curl -sk -u admin:**** https://10.0.0.21/redfish/v1/Systems/1 | jq .PowerState
curl -sk -u admin:**** https://10.0.0.21/redfish/v1/Chassis/1/Thermal \
     | jq '.Temperatures[0] | {Name, ReadingCelsius}'

Resource paths follow the Redfish service root defined in DSP0266 [61]; exact member identifiers (for example Systems/1) vary by vendor and are discovered by following links from /redfish/v1/. Newer schema versions also provide a ThermalSubsystem resource alongside the legacy Thermal resource.

13Capacity planning and hardware procurement

Concepts

Capacity planning answers three questions: how much demand there will be, how much hardware is needed to serve it within a performance target, and when that hardware must arrive. Queueing theory provides the core relationship. Little's law, L = λW, states that the average number of requests in a system equals the arrival rate multiplied by the average time each spends in the system [62]. As utilisation approaches 100%, queues and therefore response times grow sharply, so services are planned with headroom. This matters most at the tail. Dean and Barroso showed that if a single server is slow on 1% of requests, a user request that fans out to 100 such servers will be slow 63% of the time [63].

A practical planning cycle includes the following steps:

  1. Measure current utilisation by resource: CPU, memory, storage capacity and IOPS, network, power and rack space.
  2. Forecast demand from business drivers such as users, transactions and model sizes, rather than extrapolating trends alone.
  3. Model required capacity at a target utilisation, plus redundancy (N+1 or failure-domain spare) and growth buffer.
  4. Constrain by facility limits. Power and cooling, not floor space, are now usually the binding constraint for dense compute [1,24].
  5. Procure with lead times in mind, standardising on a small number of server "SKUs" to simplify spares and operations [1].

Procurement considerations

  • Standardisation versus specialisation. A few standard configurations improve purchasing leverage and spares pooling, while specialised configurations such as GPU servers are bought where workloads justify them [1].
  • Total cost, not unit price. Energy, cooling, space, support contracts and staff time over the service life often rival the purchase price (see Section 15) [1].
  • Open specifications. OCP designs and Redfish-compliant management reduce lock-in and integration cost [9,61].
  • Supply-chain assurance. Firmware provenance, signed updates and vendor security practices matter as much as hardware specifications, because BMC and firmware compromise sits beneath the OS [58].

Example

A web service sustains 20,000 requests/s with a mean in-server time of 50 ms, so by Little's law about L = 20,000 × 0.05 = 1,000 requests are in flight at any moment [62]. If each server can hold 40 concurrent requests while meeting the latency target, about 25 servers are needed. Planning for 60% peak utilisation raises this to about 42. Adding N+1 per failure domain across three zones, so that any one zone can be lost, means each zone must carry half the load: 21 servers per zone, or 63 in total.

14Disaster recovery infrastructure

Concepts

Redundancy within a site protects against component failure. Disaster recovery (DR) protects against losing the site itself, through fire, flood, regional power failure or ransomware. NIST SP 800-34 defines the key objectives [64]:

  • Recovery point objective (RPO): the point in time to which data must be recovered after an outage, that is, how much data loss is tolerable.
  • Recovery time objective (RTO): the maximum time a system can be unavailable before the impact becomes unacceptable.
  • Maximum tolerable downtime (MTD): the total time a business process can be disrupted. The RTO must fit within it.

NIST also classifies alternate sites by readiness. A cold site has space and power but no equipment. A warm site has partially equipped systems. A hot site is fully equipped and can take over within hours. A mirrored site replicates in real time and can be operational almost immediately [64]. ISO 22301 sets the wider management-system requirements for business continuity, including testing and continual improvement [65].

Synchronous replication requires every write to be acknowledged by both sites before it completes, so inter-site latency is added to each write. In practice this limits synchronous distances to metropolitan scale, with asynchronous replication used beyond that [1]. Backups must also be protected from the same failure that destroys the primary. That means geographic separation for physical disasters, and offline or immutable copies for ransomware [64].

Example

An online retailer sets RPO = 0 and RTO = 15 minutes for its order database, and RPO = 24 h with RTO = 3 days for its analytics warehouse. The database runs synchronous replication to a second data centre 30 km away, with automated failover. The warehouse relies on nightly backups to object storage in another region. Matching protection to business impact in this way avoids paying hot-site prices for every system [64,65].

15Hardware lifecycle and total cost of ownership

Lifecycle

Enterprise hardware moves through a predictable sequence of phases: planning, procurement, deployment (racking, burn-in and provisioning), operation (monitoring, patching and repair), refresh, and finally decommissioning. In decommissioning, data-bearing media must be sanitised to NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2 (September 2025), using its clear, purge or destroy methods according to data sensitivity [66]. BMC flash and stored credentials need the same treatment [58].

Total cost of ownership

Barroso, Hölzle and Ranganathan break data-centre TCO into capital expenses, mainly facility construction and server purchase amortised over their lifetimes, and operating expenses, mainly electricity, maintenance, personnel and repairs. For large facilities, server amortisation is usually the largest single component, while power-related costs can rival server cost over the life of the equipment [1].

TCO ≈ Σlife (hardware amortisation + facility share + energy × PUE + support + staff + downtime cost) − residual value

Refresh timing is the main lifecycle decision. Newer hardware usually delivers more work per watt and per rack unit, which argues for refreshing earlier. Longer service lives spread capital cost over more years, which argues for refreshing later. In 2022, Microsoft extended the expected lifespan of its cloud servers from four to six years, and estimated that the change would save $3.7 billion in fiscal year 2023 [67]. In contrast, the hardware-reliability data from Section 4 shows that failure rates rise with age [16], so extended lifetimes depend on good monitoring and spares.

Example

Compare keeping 100 five-year-old servers with consolidating their work onto 40 new ones. Inputs are the purchase cost of the new servers, the power draw of each fleet multiplied by PUE and the electricity tariff, support contracts (often rising for out-of-warranty equipment), the rack space and power capacity freed, and the residual or recycling value of the old fleet. If energy plus support for the old fleet exceeds the amortised cost plus energy for the new one, refresh pays for itself. In power-constrained facilities, the freed power capacity can be worth more than the energy saved, because it allows growth without a new building [1,24].

∎Conclusion

The fifteen topics in this article form one system. Server architecture determines how much work a watt can do. Racks, power and cooling determine how many watts a site can supply. Redundancy, RAID and SAN multipathing keep work flowing through inevitable failures. Fabrics, virtualization and accelerators determine how flexibly and how quickly that work can be distributed. Monitoring through BMCs and Redfish keeps the whole estate observable, while capacity planning, DR and lifecycle economics decide when to grow it, how to protect it, and when to replace it. The historical arc runs from bespoke mainframes to commodity clusters and now to rack-scale, liquid-cooled AI systems. Throughout, it shows a consistent pattern: standard interfaces and commodity components, combined with redundancy designed in from the start, have repeatedly outperformed attempts to make individual components perfect [1,14,51].
