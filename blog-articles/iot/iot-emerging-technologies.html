Emerging and Advanced IoT: Intelligence, Autonomy and New Physics at the Edge

The next generation of the Internet of Things is less about connecting more things and more about what connected things can decide, learn and do on their own. This article surveys seventeen emerging concepts, from AIoT, Edge AI and TinyML through 5G/6G and satellite links, digital twins, autonomous and self-healing systems, swarms, federated learning and semantic interoperability, to decentralised identity, cognitive IoT, physical AI, robotic things and quantum sensors.

Includes 13 diagrams, 16 code listings (15 executed, with their real output), an interactive swarm demo, 14 exercises and 20 trivia questions with solutions. References follow the Vancouver style; numbers in square brackets link to the list at the end. Where a field is still moving quickly, the text says so.

1. Introduction & landscape

ITU-T Recommendation Y.2060 describes the IoT as a global infrastructure that enables advanced services by interconnecting physical and virtual things using interoperable information and communication technologies [1]. The earlier articles in this series covered how that infrastructure is built: hardware, firmware, networks, platforms, data pipelines and security. This one looks at where it is heading. Three shifts tie the seventeen topics together:

  • Intelligence moves toward the data. Machine learning inference, and increasingly training, runs on gateways, phones and even milliwatt microcontrollers rather than only in the cloud [2–4].
  • Systems manage themselves. Networks reroute around failures, devices tune their own radios, and fleets of robots divide work without a central planner, extending the autonomic computing vision of self-configuring, self-healing, self-optimising and self-protecting systems [5].
  • The physical boundary widens. Satellites extend coverage to the whole planet [6], robots turn data into physical action [7], and quantum sensors measure fields and forces with sensitivity bounded by quantum mechanics rather than by classical noise [8].

2. AIoT (Artificial Intelligence of Things)

AIoT is the combination of IoT infrastructure, which senses and acts, with artificial intelligence, which turns the resulting data into decisions. Zhang and Tao frame it as “empowering things with intelligence” and organise the field into three layers: perception (making sense of sensor data), learning (building models from it) and decision (closing the loop through actuation), spread across cloud, edge and device tiers [9]. The motivation is volume: IoT data is high-rate, noisy and time-correlated, which suits deep learning but overwhelms manual rule writing [10].

2.2 Design tensions

Placing AI in IoT systems forces trade-offs that pure cloud AI rarely meets. Models must fit in kilobytes to megabytes of memory and milliwatt power budgets [4]; data are distributed across owners who may not share them [11]; and decisions act on the physical world, so errors have safety consequences. Risk-management frameworks therefore matter: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework organises AI risk work into four functions, Govern, Map, Measure and Manage [12], and the EU Artificial Intelligence Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, places obligations on AI systems according to risk category, with stricter rules for high-risk uses such as safety components of products [13].

Common mistakeTreating AIoT as “send everything to a cloud model”. Bandwidth, latency, privacy and energy usually push at least part of the pipeline to the edge, which is why the next two sections exist.

3. Edge AI

Edge computing places computation and storage close to where data are produced, reducing latency and backbone traffic and keeping sensitive data local [2,14]. Edge AI, or edge intelligence, applies this to machine learning: models are trained and especially executed on edge servers, gateways or end devices. Zhou et al. describe a six-level scale, from cloud-only intelligence (level 0) to all training and inference on the device (level 6), with intermediate levels that split work between device, edge and cloud [3]. The related concept of fog computing, introduced by Cisco researchers in 2012, describes a continuum of compute between cloud and things [15] and was standardised as the OpenFog reference architecture in IEEE 1934-2018 [16].

3.1 Techniques that make edge inference possible

  • Quantization stores weights and activations as 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floats, cutting model size by four and allowing integer-only arithmetic [18].
  • Pruning and weight sharing remove redundant connections; Han et al. combined pruning, trained quantization and Huffman coding to compress AlexNet by 35× without loss of accuracy [19].
  • Knowledge distillation trains a small “student” model to mimic a large “teacher” [20].
  • Model partitioning and early exit run the first layers on the device and the rest at the edge, or stop early when a confident answer is reached [3,17].
  • Compilers and runtimes such as Apache TVM [21], ONNX Runtime [22], OpenVINO [23] and LiteRT (TensorFlow Lite, renamed in September 2024) [24] map models onto CPUs, GPUs and NPUs.

4. TinyML

TinyML is machine learning on ultra-low-power microcontrollers, typically devices drawing milliwatts with tens to hundreds of kilobytes of RAM and no operating system [4]. Running a model on the sensor itself means data never has to leave the device, which saves radio energy (usually the largest consumer in a battery IoT node), removes network latency and keeps raw audio or video private [4,25].

4.1 The constraints

The MLPerf Tiny benchmark suite, the first industry-standard benchmark for this class of hardware, defines four reference tasks: keyword spotting, visual wake words, image classification on CIFAR-10, and anomaly detection on machine-operating sounds [26]. TensorFlow Lite Micro was designed for these devices with an interpreter whose core fits in a few tens of kilobytes, no dynamic memory allocation after initialisation, and kernels that vendors can replace with optimised versions [25]. Arm's CMSIS-NN library is one such set of kernels; on Cortex-M processors it improved runtime and energy efficiency by about 4.6× and 4.9× over a baseline implementation [27]. Hardware-aware neural architecture search goes further: MCUNet co-designs the network and the inference engine and was the first to reach over 70% ImageNet top-1 accuracy on an off-the-shelf microcontroller [28].

4.2 How int8 quantization works

The integer-only scheme of Jacob et al., which TensorFlow Lite adopted, represents each real value r as r = S(q − Z), where q is an 8-bit integer, S a real scale and Z an integer zero point chosen so that real zero is exactly representable [18]. Listing 2 quantizes one dense layer and measures the cost: a 4× smaller layer with about 1% output error.

On the microcontroller, the product of two scales is folded into one fixed-point multiplier so that inference needs no floating point at all [18]. 4.3 Example: keyword spotting

The “OK Google” or “Hey Siri” pattern, where a tiny always-on model wakes a bigger one, is the canonical TinyML application. The Speech Commands dataset was released to train and compare such models [30], and Zhang et al. showed that depthwise-separable CNNs reach about 95% accuracy on it within microcontroller memory limits [31]. Keyword spotting is one of the four MLPerf Tiny tasks [26].

5. 5G and 6G IoT

5.1 5G: three usage scenarios

ITU-R Recommendation M.2083 defines the vision for IMT-2020 (5G) around three usage scenarios: enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB), ultra-reliable and low-latency communications (URLLC), and massive machine-type communications (mMTC) [32]. The last two are the IoT-relevant ones. Report ITU-R M.2410 sets the minimum technical requirements that any IMT-2020 radio interface must meet [33]:

Requirement (ITU-R M.2410)Minimum valueIoT relevance
Peak data rate20 Gbit/s downlink, 10 Gbit/s uplinkVideo-heavy AIoT, AR maintenance
User-plane latency4 ms (eMBB), 1 ms (URLLC)Motion control, teleoperation
Reliability (URLLC)1−10−5 success for a 32-byte packet within 1 msFactory automation, safety interlocks
Connection density (mMTC)1,000,000 devices per km²Dense metering, smart cities
Mobilityup to 500 km/hRail, vehicles

The 3GPP study that shaped the 5G New Radio also targeted a battery life of more than 10 years, and 15 years as desirable, for mMTC devices [34]. Two 5G system features matter especially for IoT. Network slicing creates logically isolated end-to-end networks with their own quality of service on shared infrastructure, and the 5G system architecture (TS 23.501) also defines integration with IEEE Time-Sensitive Networking for deterministic industrial traffic [35]. Reduced Capability (RedCap) NR devices, introduced in 3GPP Release 17, cut cost and power for wearables, industrial sensors and video surveillance by reducing the maximum bandwidth (20 MHz in frequency range 1) and the number of receive antennas [36].

5.2 5G-Advanced and ambient IoT

3GPP's Release 18 study on Ambient IoT examined devices that harvest energy from radio waves, light or vibration and have no battery, or only a tiny capacitor, targeting power consumption in the microwatt range and very low cost for applications such as inventory and logistics tags [37]. This blurs the line between cellular IoT and RFID.

5.3 6G (IMT-2030)

Recommendation ITU-R M.2160, approved in November 2023, sets the framework for IMT-2030. It extends the three 5G scenarios into six usage scenarios: immersive communication, hyper-reliable and low-latency communication (HRLLC), massive communication, ubiquitous connectivity, AI and communication, and integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) [38]. Two of these are new and directly relevant to the topics of this article: native AI in the network, and using the radio itself as a sensor that detects and positions objects. M.2160 also lists capability ranges for research; for example, connection density of 106–108 devices per km² [38]. Research visions add terahertz bands, reconfigurable intelligent surfaces and integration of terrestrial, aerial and satellite networks [39,40].

6. Satellite IoT

Terrestrial networks cover a small fraction of the Earth's surface, so ships, pipelines, farms, wildlife and remote infrastructure need another route. Large low Earth orbit (LEO) constellations and cheaper launches have made satellite IoT practical in the “new space” era [6,41]. There are two main architectures. In indirect access, devices talk to a local terrestrial gateway that has a satellite backhaul. In direct-to-satellite access, the device's own radio reaches the satellite, which is harder because of path loss, Doppler shift and short visibility windows [41].

6.1 Standards: non-terrestrial networks

3GPP studied New Radio support for non-terrestrial networks (NTN) in TR 38.811 (channel models and deployment scenarios) and TR 38.821 (solutions), and studied NB-IoT and eMTC over satellite in TR 36.763 [42–44]. Release 17, completed in 2022, was the first release to include NTN for both NR and IoT [45]. Satellites may be transparent (a “bent pipe” that relays the signal to a ground gateway) or regenerative (carrying part or all of a base station on board) [43]. Outside 3GPP, LoRa's long-range frequency-hopping spread spectrum (LR-FHSS) modulation was designed for very large numbers of devices and suits direct-to-satellite links [46].

7. Digital twins

A digital twin is a virtual representation of a physical asset, process or system that is kept synchronised with it through data and used to understand, predict and improve its behaviour. The concept is usually traced to Michael Grieves' product lifecycle management work at the University of Michigan in the early 2000s, and Grieves and Vickers later formalised it as three parts: the physical product, the virtual product, and the data and information connections between them [48]. ISO/IEC 30173:2023 now provides standard concepts and terminology [49], and ISO 23247 defines a digital twin framework for manufacturing [50].

7.2 Building blocks

In industry, twins combine physics-based models with data-driven ones and are used across design, production, service and end of life [52]. Platforms describe twin types with modelling languages such as Microsoft's Digital Twins Definition Language (DTDL), which is based on JSON-LD [53]; open-source frameworks such as Eclipse Ditto hold the live state of each “thing” and expose it through APIs [54]; and services such as AWS IoT TwinMaker link twin models to 3D scenes and time-series stores [55].

8. Autonomous IoT

Autonomous IoT systems pursue goals with little or no human intervention: they sense their situation, decide and act, and adapt when conditions change. The idea descends from IBM's autonomic computing initiative, which argued that growing system complexity would outpace human administrators and proposed systems that are self-configuring, self-optimising, self-healing and self-protecting [5]. IBM's architectural blueprint structured each autonomic manager as a MAPE-K loop: Monitor, Analyse, Plan and Execute, sharing Knowledge [57].

8.1 Levels of autonomy

Autonomy is a spectrum, not a switch. The best-known scale is SAE J3016 for road vehicles, which defines six levels from 0 (no driving automation) to 5 (full driving automation), with the key change at level 3, where the system rather than the human monitors the driving environment while engaged [58]. IoT designers can borrow the same idea: decide which decisions the system takes alone, which it proposes for approval, and which stay human. Autonomy also needs guard-rails: hard limits that no plan may cross, and a safe fallback state.

9. Self-healing networks

A self-healing network detects faults (a failed node, a broken link, interference) and restores service automatically. In low-power wireless networks, where nodes fail when batteries die and links fade, this is part of the routing protocol. RPL, the IETF routing protocol for low-power and lossy networks, organises nodes into a destination-oriented directed acyclic graph (DODAG) rooted at a border router; each node computes a rank (its logical distance from the root) and chooses a preferred parent, and RPL provides both local repair, where a node quickly finds another parent, and global repair, where the root rebuilds the whole graph by incrementing a version number [59]. RPL schedules its control messages with the Trickle algorithm, which sends often when the network is changing and rarely when it is stable [60]. Reactive protocols such as AODV instead discover routes on demand and send route-error messages when a link breaks [61]. Bluetooth Mesh avoids routing state altogether by using managed flooding, in which relay nodes rebroadcast messages, so the loss of one relay rarely isolates a node [62].

At the scale of mobile networks, 3GPP specifies self-organising networks (SON) with self-configuration, self-optimisation and self-healing functions, the last of which detects cell outages and compensates, for example by adjusting neighbouring cells [63].

10. Swarm robotics

Swarm robotics studies how large numbers of relatively simple robots can produce useful collective behaviour through local interactions, without centralised control or global information. Şahin's widely used definition stresses autonomous, physically embodied robots, large numbers, limited local sensing and communication, and behaviour inspired by social insects [64]. The term swarm intelligence was coined by Beni and Wang for cellular robotic systems [65]. Swarms promise robustness (no single point of failure), scalability (adding robots adds capacity) and flexibility [66].

10.1 Local rules, global order

Reynolds' “boids” showed in 1987 that three local rules, separation, alignment and cohesion, are enough to produce realistic flocking [67]. Vicsek et al. stripped this down further: each particle adopts the average heading of its neighbours plus noise, and the group switches from disorder to collective motion as density rises or noise falls, a kinetic phase transition [68]. Consensus theory generalises the same principle to networked multi-agent systems [69]. Swarm ideas also gave rise to optimisation algorithms used in IoT, such as particle swarm optimisation [70] and ant colony optimisation [71].

10.2 Milestones

  • Rubenstein, Cornejo and Nagpal programmed a swarm of 1,024 Kilobots to self-assemble into complex two-dimensional shapes using only local communication [72].
  • Vásárhelyi et al. flew a flock of 30 autonomous drones outdoors, with flocking parameters tuned by evolutionary optimisation [73].
  • Simulators such as ARGoS were built specifically to run large heterogeneous swarms efficiently [74].

11. Distributed intelligence

Distributed intelligence spreads perception, reasoning and decision-making across many cooperating nodes instead of concentrating it in one place. Multi-agent systems research provides the vocabulary: autonomous agents with local knowledge that interact, cooperate, negotiate and sometimes compete to achieve individual or shared goals [75]. In IoT this means gateways, devices and robots that agree on facts, divide tasks and aggregate knowledge peer to peer, which matters when the cloud is far away, intermittently reachable or a privacy risk. Fog and edge computing provide the infrastructure on which such agents run [15,16].

12. Federated learning

Federated learning (FL) trains a shared model across many devices or sites while the training data stay where they were collected. McMahan et al. introduced the term and the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm: in each round a server sends the current model to a subset of clients, each client runs several steps of stochastic gradient descent on its local data, and the server replaces the global model with the average of the returned models weighted by each client's number of examples [82]. An early large-scale deployment trained next-word prediction for Google's Gboard keyboard on users' phones [83].

12.1 Challenges in IoT

  • Statistical heterogeneity. Client data are not independent and identically distributed (non-IID): each factory or home sees different conditions. FedProx adds a proximal term that keeps local models close to the global one and improves stability under heterogeneity [86].
  • Systems heterogeneity. Devices differ in compute, battery and connectivity, and only some are available in any round [11,87].
  • Privacy is not automatic. Model updates can leak information about training data, so FL is usually combined with secure aggregation, which lets the server learn only the sum of updates [84], and differential privacy [11,85].
  • Robustness. A malicious client can poison the model; Byzantine-robust aggregation rules are an active research area [11].

Listing 10 runs FedAvg with four non-IID clients and partial participation. After 20 rounds the federated model reaches 92.7% accuracy against 93.1% for a model trained centrally on all the raw data, while each client sends only 32 bytes of weights per round.

Production frameworks include Flower [88], TensorFlow Federated [89] and NVIDIA FLARE [90].

13. Semantic interoperability

Two devices are syntactically interoperable when they can parse each other's messages, and semantically interoperable when they also agree on what the data mean. A field called tmp holding 21.4 is useless unless a consumer knows it is air temperature in degrees Celsius measured by a particular sensor at a particular place. Semantic interoperability attaches that meaning using shared, machine-readable vocabularies (ontologies) and identifiers.

14. Blockchain-based device identity

Every IoT security scheme starts with identity: knowing which device is talking. Conventional approaches rely on a central authority, such as a manufacturer's certificate authority issuing IEEE 802.1AR device identity certificates (IDevIDs) bound to keys in hardware [101], or a bootstrapping protocol such as BRSKI that uses those certificates to enrol devices into a network's own PKI [102]. Blockchain-based identity replaces or complements the central registry with a shared, append-only ledger that no single party controls.

14.1 Building blocks

  • Hash-chained ledgers. Bitcoin introduced a chain of blocks in which each block contains the hash of its predecessor, so changing any past record would require redoing all later work [103]. NIST's overview explains the components, consensus models and limitations [104]; Merkle trees let a device prove inclusion of one record cheaply [105].
  • Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs). The W3C DID v1.0 Recommendation defines globally unique identifiers that the subject controls, each resolving to a DID document listing public keys and service endpoints [106].
  • Verifiable Credentials. The W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model v2.0 lets an issuer, such as a manufacturer or certification lab, sign claims about a subject, such as “this device model passed EN 303 645 testing”, that anyone can verify [107].
  • Permissioned ledgers. Consortium chains such as Hyperledger Fabric restrict who can write and replace proof-of-work with faster consensus [108], which suits supply chains of known companies.

Listing 12 implements this flow in miniature with Ed25519 signatures [109]. A cloned device that copies the DID but not the private key is rejected, and editing an earlier block breaks the hash chain.

When not to use a blockchainResearchers proposing blockchain for IoT note the costs: consensus latency, ledger storage that constrained devices cannot hold, and energy for proof-of-work [110,111]. Dorri et al. therefore used a lightweight, centrally managed private ledger per smart home and dropped proof-of-work [111]. A ledger is worth it when several organisations that do not fully trust one another must share one identity registry, for example across a multi-vendor supply chain. Within one organisation, a well-run PKI is usually simpler and faster.

15. Cognitive IoT

Cognitive IoT (CIoT) extends IoT with a perception–action cycle, learning and reasoning, so that things can understand their situation and adapt rather than just report data. Wu et al. proposed it as “a new paradigm beyond connection” built around a cognitive cycle of perception, analysis, reasoning and action, with semantic derivation and knowledge discovery as core functions [112]. Sheth described a related progression from IoT to smart IoT through semantic, cognitive and perceptual computing [113].

The idea grew out of cognitive radio. Mitola and Maguire introduced the term for software radios that are aware of their environment and user needs [114], and Haykin defined cognitive radio as an intelligent wireless system that learns from its environment and adapts its transmit parameters, with the goals of reliable communication and efficient use of spectrum [115].

16. Physical AI

Physical AI is AI that perceives, reasons about and acts in the physical world, such as robots, autonomous vehicles and smart spaces, as opposed to AI that only produces text or images. NVIDIA, which popularised the term, describes models that understand spatial relationships and physical behaviour, trained largely in physically accurate simulation [118]. The intellectual roots are in embodied cognition, the view that intelligence depends on having a body that interacts with an environment [119].

16.1 Ingredients

  • Vision-language-action (VLA) models. RT-2 fine-tuned a vision-language model to output robot actions as tokens and showed that web-scale knowledge transferred to robotic control, such as picking up an object described only in words [120]. π0 combined a pretrained vision-language model with a flow-matching action head for dexterous general-purpose control [121].
  • Cross-embodiment data. The Open X-Embodiment collaboration pooled data from 22 robot types to train generalist RT-X policies [122].
  • World foundation models and simulation. Models such as NVIDIA Cosmos generate physically plausible video for training and testing [123]; simulators such as Isaac Sim and Gazebo provide synthetic data and safe test environments [124,125].
  • Sim-to-real transfer. Domain randomization, which varies textures, lighting and physics in simulation, helps policies trained only in simulation work on real robots [126].

Physical AI depends on IoT: sensors supply its perception, networks link it to the edge and cloud, and digital twins (Section 7) provide the simulated worlds in which it is trained and validated. It also raises the stakes for AI governance, since faults move objects, not pixels [12,13].

17. Internet of Robotic Things

The Internet of Robotic Things (IoRT) joins robots and IoT so that robots both use and contribute to the surrounding network of sensors, services and other robots. Simoens, Dragone and Saffiotti define it as intelligent devices that can monitor events, fuse sensor data from a variety of sources, use local and distributed intelligence to decide on courses of action, and then act to manipulate objects in the physical world [7]. Ray surveys its architectures and technologies [127]. It builds on cloud robotics, in which robots offload heavy computation, share knowledge and learn collectively through the cloud [128].

Scale makes this relevant. The International Federation of Robotics counted a record of about 4.28 million industrial robots operating in factories worldwide in 2023, after more than 541,000 new installations that year [129].

17.1 Software and safety foundations

ROS 2, the second generation of the Robot Operating System, rebuilt its communication layer on the OMG Data Distribution Service (DDS) to support real-time, multi-robot and production use [130,131]. Because DDS is a publish–subscribe middleware with quality-of-service policies, ROS 2 robots fit naturally into IoT data architectures. When robots share space with people, ISO/TS 15066 specifies safety requirements for collaborative industrial robots, including limits on contact forces and pressures [132].

18. Quantum-enabled sensing

Quantum sensing uses quantum systems, quantum properties or quantum phenomena to measure a physical quantity [8]. Degen, Reinhard and Cappellaro distinguish three types: sensors that use quantized energy levels (such as atomic clocks), sensors that use quantum coherence (superposition), and sensors that use entanglement to beat classical limits [8]. For IoT this matters because some quantum sensors are leaving the laboratory and shrinking toward field-deployable packages.

18.2 Why quantum sensors are sensitive

A common protocol is Ramsey interferometry: prepare a spin in superposition, let it accumulate a phase φ = 2πγBτ in a field B for a time τ, then read it out [8]. Each readout is a random yes/no outcome, so with N independent spins the phase uncertainty falls as 1/√N: the standard quantum limit. Entangled states can in principle reach the Heisenberg limit, which scales as 1/N [139]. Longer coherence time τ also improves sensitivity, which is why materials engineering of coherence is central to the field [8].

Quantum sensing is not quantum computingQuantum sensors are already in use, while large fault-tolerant quantum computers are not. A related but separate concern for IoT is that future quantum computers could break today's public-key cryptography, which is why NIST published post-quantum standards such as ML-KEM (FIPS 203) in 2024 [140]; long-lived devices shipping today should plan for crypto-agility.

19. Putting it together: an autonomous offshore wind farm

The technologies in this article are most powerful in combination. Consider a hypothetical offshore wind farm far from shore, used here as a teaching scenario rather than a description of a real site:

  1. Sense. Each turbine's nacelle runs TinyML anomaly detection on vibration and acoustic data (Section 4), sending only events and summaries.
  2. Connect. Turbines form a self-healing mesh to a substation (Section 9); the substation backhauls over a private 5G network with a URLLC slice for protection signalling (Section 5), with a LEO satellite link as fallback (Section 6).
  3. Understand. A digital twin per turbine estimates blade and gearbox state and forecasts remaining useful life (Section 7). Properties are described with WoT Thing Descriptions and SOSA so that equipment from several vendors can be queried uniformly (Section 13).
  4. Learn. Turbine manufacturers improve fault models with federated learning across farms owned by competing operators, without pooling raw data (Section 12).
  5. Decide. A MAPE-K manager curtails individual turbines when the twin predicts overload, within guard-rails set by engineers (Section 8). Substation gateways agree on farm-wide state with gossip and consensus protocols (Section 11).
  6. Act. Inspection drones fly as a coordinated swarm (Section 10), and maintenance robots receive tasks by auction (Section 17), driven by physical-AI policies trained in the twin (Section 16).
  7. Trust. Each turbine, drone and robot has a DID; certification bodies issue verifiable credentials for firmware and safety tests (Section 14).
  8. Look further. Quantum gravimeters or magnetometers could one day survey seabed cables and foundations (Section 18).
Engineering reality checkEach layer adds failure modes and attack surface. A good architecture adopts an emerging technology only where it removes a real constraint (bandwidth, latency, privacy, coverage, labour) and keeps a simpler fallback.

21. Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Quantization arithmetic

A float32 model has 250,000 parameters. (a) What is its size in float32 and in int8? (b) A microcontroller has 256 kB of flash reserved for the model. Which version fits?

Solution

(a) float32: 250,000 × 4 B = 1,000,000 B (about 977 kB). int8: 250,000 B (about 244 kB), a 4× reduction [18]. (b) Only the int8 version fits, with about 12 kB to spare for the model's metadata, which TinyML runtimes store alongside the weights [25].

Exercise 2. Scale and zero point

Weights range from −0.5 to 1.5. Compute the int8 scale S and zero point Z for the range [−128, 127], and quantize the value 0.0.

Solution

S = (1.5 − (−0.5)) / 255 ≈ 0.007843. Z = round(−128 − (−0.5)/0.007843) = round(−128 + 63.75) = −64. Zero quantizes to q = round(0/S) + Z = −64, and dequantizes exactly back to 0, which is why the scheme requires real zero to be representable [18].

Exercise 3. Edge or cloud?

Using the model in Listing 1, a 5G link improves to 100 Mbit/s uplink and the cloud round-trip time falls to 35 ms. Does cloud inference now meet the 50 ms budget?

Solution

Transmission time = 60 kB × 8 / 100 Mbit/s = 4.8 ms. Total = 4 ms compute + 4.8 ms + 35 ms = 43.8 ms, which meets the budget, though with little margin for jitter. Edge placement remains more robust, which is the general argument for MEC in latency-critical IoT [3,14].

Exercise 4. 5G requirement check

A smart city plans 300,000 sensors in a 0.5 km² district. Does the IMT-2020 minimum connection density cover this?

Solution

Density = 300,000 / 0.5 = 600,000 devices/km², below the IMT-2020 minimum requirement of 1,000,000 devices/km² [33]. The requirement is defined for a specific evaluation scenario and traffic model, so real capacity also depends on how often devices transmit.

Exercise 5. LEO pass geometry

Use Listing 4 to explain why a LEO satellite at 600 km is visible from a given point for only a few minutes per pass, and why devices typically use store-and-forward.

Solution

The satellite completes an orbit in about 96.5 minutes at about 7.6 km/s, so it crosses the visible sky (from horizon to horizon, a few thousand kilometres of track) in roughly 10 minutes or less, depending on minimum elevation. Between passes the device has no link, so messages are buffered on the device or on the satellite and delivered at the next contact [41,47].

Exercise 6. Model, shadow or twin?

Classify: (a) a live 3D view of a crane showing sensor values; (b) a simulation run once at design time; (c) a system that retunes a pump controller from a continuously updated hydraulic model.

Solution

(a) Digital shadow: automatic physical-to-digital flow only. (b) Digital model: no automatic flow. (c) Digital twin: automatic flow in both directions [51].

Exercise 7. Thermal twin time constant

In Listing 5, R = 0.8 K/W and C = 600 J/K. What is the thermal time constant, and how long after a load step does the temperature reach about 95% of its new steady state?

Solution

τ = RC = 480 s = 8 minutes. A first-order system reaches 1 − e−3 ≈ 95% of a step after 3τ = 24 minutes. This matches the what-if output, where 30 minutes at constant power lands within about 1 °C of steady state.

Exercise 8. MAPE-K mapping

A smart thermostat learns the household's schedule, detects that a window is open from a sudden temperature drop, and turns the heating off. Map each step to MAPE-K.

Solution

Monitor: read temperature and occupancy. Analyse: detect the anomalous drop as an open window. Plan: decide to pause heating. Execute: switch the boiler relay. Knowledge: the learned schedule and thermal model, shared by all four phases [5,57].

Exercise 9. RPL rank

In Listing 7, why does node F choose D before the failure even though E is also a neighbour?

Solution

Via D, F's rank would be rank(D) + cost(D,F) = (1.0 + 1.1) + 1.0 = 3.1. Via E, the best rank of E is 3.3 (root–A–C–E: 1.0 + 1.0 + 1.3; the alternative root–B–E costs 3.7), giving 3.3 + 1.2 = 4.5. F picks the parent that minimises its own rank, as RPL objective functions do, and 4.5 is exactly the rank it falls back to once D fails [59].

Exercise 10. Gossip versus a coordinator

Give two advantages and one disadvantage of computing the average in Listing 9 by gossip instead of sending all readings to a coordinator.

Solution

Advantages: no single point of failure, and no node needs to be reachable from all others; each node also ends up knowing the result. Disadvantage: many more messages are needed in total than the N−1 a coordinator would receive, and convergence time depends on the network's connectivity [77].

Exercise 11. FedAvg weighting

Three clients with 400, 150 and 60 examples return weights 1.0, 2.0 and 4.0 for one parameter. What does FedAvg compute? What would an unweighted mean give?

Solution

Weighted: (400·1.0 + 150·2.0 + 60·4.0)/610 = 940/610 ≈ 1.54. Unweighted: 7/3 ≈ 2.33. FedAvg weights by example count, so the average corresponds to the objective over all training examples [82].

Exercise 12. Semantic annotation

Extend Vendor B's Thing Description in Listing 11 with a humidity property and say what annotation a client needs to find it.

Solution

Add a property such as "rh": {"@type": "qk:RelativeHumidity", "type": "number", "unit": "unit:PERCENT", "forms": [...]}. A client then searches for the quantity kind IRI http://qudt.org/vocab/quantitykind/RelativeHumidity exactly as Listing 11 searches for Temperature [93,99].

Exercise 13. Ledger or PKI?

A single manufacturer wants to identify its own 10,000 sensors. A consortium of five port operators wants a shared registry of containers' tracking devices. Which should use a blockchain-based registry?

Solution

The manufacturer is the natural trust anchor and can run a PKI with IEEE 802.1AR device certificates [101]. The consortium has no single trusted party, which is the case where a permissioned ledger with DIDs adds value [106,108,110].

Exercise 14. Quantum scaling

From Listing 16, how many spins would the standard-quantum-limited magnetometer need to reach σB ≈ 0.18 nT? What if τ could be raised to 100 µs?

Solution

σ scales as 1/√N. At N = 106, σ ≈ 0.57 nT, so a 3.2× improvement needs 10× more spins: N ≈ 107. Raising τ tenfold also improves σ tenfold, since σ ∝ 1/(γτ√N), so 106 spins would give about 0.057 nT, if coherence allows such a long τ [8].
