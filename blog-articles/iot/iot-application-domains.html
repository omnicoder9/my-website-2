IoT Application Domains: Homes, Cities, Farms, Hospitals, Vehicles, Grids, Supply Chains and Spacecraft

The same few building blocks (sensors, radios, gateways, cloud platforms and control loops) turn up in very different places. This article tours eight application domains and 32 use cases, showing for each one what is measured, how it is connected, which standards apply, what the evidence says about benefits, and where the risks lie.

Includes 10 diagrams, 17 tested code listings, an interactive traffic-signal calculator, 16 exercises and 24 trivia questions with solutions. References follow the Vancouver style; numbers in square brackets link to the list at the end. Facts were checked against the cited sources on 16 November 2026.

1. Introduction: one stack, many domains

ITU-T Recommendation Y.2060 describes the Internet of Things as a global infrastructure that enables advanced services by interconnecting physical and virtual things using interoperable information and communication technologies [1]. The early surveys that shaped the field grouped its uses into application domains such as transportation and logistics, healthcare, smart environments (home, office, plant) and personal and social uses [2], and later added utilities, agriculture and smart cities [3,4]. The phrase itself came from supply chains: Kevin Ashton used it in 1999 to describe linking RFID-tagged goods at Procter & Gamble to the internet [5].

What changes from one domain to the next is not the basic architecture but the constraints: how long a battery must last, how far a radio must reach, how quickly a decision must be made, what happens if it is wrong, and which regulator cares. Those constraints decide everything else. A smart bulb can tolerate a lost message; a pacemaker telemetry link, a railway signal or a satellite command link cannot.

Domain 12. Smart homes

The smart home is the most visible IoT domain and, for a long time, the most fragmented one: Zigbee, Z-Wave, Wi-Fi and proprietary hubs each formed their own islands. The Connectivity Standards Alliance (formerly the Zigbee Alliance) launched Matter to give devices a common IP-based application layer that runs over Wi-Fi, Ethernet and Thread, with Bluetooth Low Energy used for commissioning [6]. Thread is an IPv6 mesh built on IEEE 802.15.4 radios, so battery devices such as sensors and locks can join the home network without a proprietary bridge [29,30]. A Thread border router (often built into a smart speaker or hub) connects the mesh to the home's Wi-Fi/Ethernet network.

2.1 Lighting

Connected lighting builds on the efficiency of LEDs, which according to the U.S. Department of Energy use up to 90% less energy and last up to 25 times longer than incandescent lamps [31]. Networking adds control on top: scheduling, dimming, scenes, occupancy and daylight sensing, and tunable white. In homes this usually means Zigbee or Matter bulbs and switches [32,6]; commercial buildings more often use the wired Digital Addressable Lighting Interface (DALI, IEC 62386), in which every driver on a two-wire bus has its own address and can report status and faults [33].

Two design rules matter more than the radio. First, a smart bulb must still work from the wall switch when the network is gone; the best designs keep a physical control path. Second, group commands (multicast or scenes stored in each bulb) beat sending one message per bulb, because the mesh would otherwise flicker as 20 bulbs switch one after another.

2.2 Thermostats

Heating and cooling are the largest energy use in many homes, which is why thermostats were among the first connected devices to show measurable savings. The U.S. Department of Energy notes that turning a thermostat back by 7–10 °F (about 4–6 °C) for 8 hours a day can save as much as 10% a year on heating and cooling [37]. ENERGY STAR certifies smart thermostats on the basis of measured field savings from installed products rather than on features alone [38].

The control law inside most thermostats is still on/off control with hysteresis (a dead band around the setpoint), which stops the boiler or compressor from short-cycling. Connectivity adds schedules, occupancy (“away”) detection from phones or motion sensors, weather forecasts and utility signals. In demand response programmes the utility sends a price or event signal, for example via OpenADR (standardised as IEC 62746-10-1), and the thermostat shifts load away from the peak [39].

Notice that at 08:00 (20.6 °C) heating stays on even though the temperature is within 0.5 °C of the setpoint: inside the band the controller keeps its previous state. That is the whole point of hysteresis.

2.3 Security systems

Home security combines door and window contacts, passive infrared motion detectors, glass-break and smoke sensors, cameras, video doorbells and smart locks. These devices are attractive targets in their own right. The Mirai botnet of 2016 recruited about 600,000 cameras, DVRs and routers at its peak, mostly by logging in with factory-default passwords, and used them for some of the largest denial-of-service attacks then seen [40].

Regulators responded. ETSI EN 303 645 sets baseline requirements for consumer IoT, beginning with “no universal default passwords” and including a vulnerability disclosure policy and software updates [7]. In the United States, the FCC created the voluntary U.S. Cyber Trust Mark label for consumer wireless IoT products, adopted in 2024 and launched in January 2025 [8]. Matter requires each device to carry a Device Attestation Certificate so that a controller can check during commissioning that the product is genuine and certified [6].

Design guidanceKeep alarms local: a siren and a local hub rule should not depend on a cloud round trip. Treat camera video as sensitive personal data, encrypt it end to end where possible, and give users a clear way to delete it.

2.4 Appliances

Connected washing machines, dishwashers, ovens, refrigerators and robot vacuums offer remote start, end-of-cycle notifications, diagnostics and, increasingly, energy flexibility (for example delaying a dishwasher until solar output or cheap tariffs arrive). Interoperability has been a problem because each manufacturer used its own cloud and app. Two efforts address it at different levels. ETSI's SAREF ontology (TS 103 264) gives a shared vocabulary for devices, their functions, commands and measurements, so that an energy manager can reason about an appliance it has never seen before [41]. Matter 1.2 (October 2023) added nine device types, including refrigerators, room air conditioners, dishwashers, laundry washers and robot vacuum cleaners [42].

Domain 23. Smart cities

A smart city uses networked sensing and control to run public services (traffic, parking, waste, lighting, water, air quality) more efficiently and transparently. Zanella et al. described an urban IoT built around a city-wide network, open data and services, using the Padova Smart City pilot as an example [43]. Large test beds followed: SmartSantander in Spain deployed thousands of sensors for parking, environmental monitoring and irrigation of parks [44], and Chicago's Array of Things mounted sensor nodes on light poles to collect air-quality, climate and pedestrian data as open research data [45]. ISO 37120 defines indicators that let cities measure and compare their services [9].

Cities favour low-power wide-area networks (LPWANs) because they need many cheap, battery-powered sensors spread over kilometres. LoRaWAN uses a star-of-stars topology: devices transmit to any gateway in range, gateways forward frames to a network server, and Class A devices open two short receive windows only after each uplink, which keeps batteries going for years [46]. NB-IoT offers the same kind of service on operators' licensed cellular spectrum in a 180 kHz carrier [47]. For data, ETSI's NGSI-LD API and the FIWARE open-source platform give cities a common way to publish “context information” such as the current state of a parking spot [10,48].

3.1 Traffic management

Adaptive traffic control is one of the oldest urban IoT applications. Sydney's SCATS (Sydney Coordinated Adaptive Traffic System), developed in the 1970s, uses inductive loop detectors at stop lines to adjust cycle length, phase splits and offsets between neighbouring junctions in real time [49]. The British SCOOT system takes a model-based approach, measuring flows upstream and continuously making small changes to minimise predicted delay and stops [50]. Modern deployments add video analytics, radar, Bluetooth/Wi-Fi travel-time sensors and connected-vehicle data.

The classic starting point for timing a fixed-time junction is Webster's formula. For lost time L per cycle (seconds) and the sum Y of the critical flow ratios y = q/s (flow divided by saturation flow) of the conflicting phases, the delay-minimising cycle is C0 = (1.5L + 5)/(1 − Y), and effective green time is shared in proportion to each phase's y [51]. Adaptive systems in effect re-solve this kind of problem every cycle with live detector data. Microscopic simulators such as SUMO let engineers test such strategies before they touch real signals [52].

3.2 Smart parking

Drivers searching for kerbside parking add measurably to congestion. Reviewing 16 studies from 1927 to 2001, Shoup found that cruising for parking accounted for between 8% and 74% of traffic in the areas studied, about 30% on average, with average search times of around 8 minutes [53]. Smart parking attacks the information gap with in-ground or surface-mounted sensors (magnetometers that detect the disturbance a car's steel causes in the Earth's magnetic field, sometimes combined with radar or infrared), camera-based occupancy detection, and apps or roadside signs that guide drivers. The same data supports demand-responsive pricing, where meter prices are adjusted block by block to keep a target share of spaces free.

A parking sensor is a classic LPWAN device: it only needs to report a state change and an occasional heartbeat, so a few bytes per uplink are enough. LoRaWAN network servers such as The Things Stack let you attach a JavaScript payload formatter that turns those bytes into JSON [54]. Listing 4 is one for a hypothetical 4-byte payload.

3.3 Waste management

The World Bank estimated that the world produced 2.01 billion tonnes of municipal solid waste in 2016 and projected 3.40 billion tonnes by 2050, with at least a third of it not managed in an environmentally safe way [55]. Collection is a large part of the cost, and fixed routes empty half-full bins while overflowing ones wait. Fill-level sensors (usually ultrasonic or time-of-flight distance sensors under the lid, plus temperature for fire detection and a tilt switch for lid opening) allow dynamic routing: trucks visit only bins forecast to be full. Folianto et al. described an early design of such a system with mesh-networked ultrasonic sensors [56].

A real system feeds the “collect” list into a vehicle-routing solver together with truck capacity, depot location and road network, but the forecasting step is the IoT-specific part.

3.4 Street lighting

Street lighting is often the first city-wide IoT network because the poles already have power, height and a regular spacing that suits radio coverage. Converting from high-pressure sodium (HPS) to LED gives most of the saving; adding a controller to each luminaire adds remote dimming, adaptive lighting (brighter when people or vehicles are present), fault reporting that replaces night-time patrols, and accurate energy metering. Two interfaces standardise the pieces: Zhaga Book 18 defines a connector on top of the luminaire for plug-in sensing and communication nodes [57], and the TALQ protocol lets a city's central management software control outdoor lighting networks from different vendors [58]. Because the network is already on every street, it often carries other city sensors too.

Domain 34. Agriculture

“Smart farming” or precision agriculture applies sensing, data and automation to manage variability within fields and herds rather than treating them as uniform [59]. Tzounis et al. reviewed IoT uses across open-field crops, greenhouses, livestock and the food chain and identified rural connectivity, power, cost and interoperability as the main barriers [60]. The economic and environmental stakes are high: FAO reports that agriculture accounts for roughly 70% of global freshwater withdrawals [61].

4.1 Soil monitoring

Soil probes measure volumetric water content, temperature, salinity (electrical conductivity) and sometimes nutrients. Most moisture sensors are dielectric: water has a relative permittivity of about 80 compared with 3–5 for dry soil minerals, so the apparent permittivity Ka of soil rises with water content. Topp, Davis and Annan's empirical polynomial, derived with time-domain reflectometry (TDR), remains the standard conversion for mineral soils: θ = −5.3×10−2 + 2.92×10−2Ka − 5.5×10−4Ka2 + 4.3×10−6Ka3 [62]. Cheaper capacitance probes need site-specific calibration, especially in clay or saline soils. Probes are typically installed at several depths through the root zone and report every 15–60 minutes over LoRaWAN or cellular.

4.2 Irrigation

FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 is the reference method for crop water requirements. It computes reference evapotranspiration ET0 with the FAO Penman–Monteith equation from weather data, multiplies it by a crop coefficient to get crop evapotranspiration (ETc = Kc × ET0), and tracks root-zone depletion as a daily water balance [11]. The total available water (TAW) is the water held between field capacity and wilting point over the root depth; only a fraction p of it, the readily available water (RAW = p × TAW), can be used before the crop suffers stress. Irrigation is triggered when depletion reaches RAW [11]. IoT closes the loop: soil probes initialise and correct the balance, a local weather station or forecast supplies ET0, and valves or pivot controllers apply the dose.

The 12 mm of rain on day 4 delays irrigation by about a day, and the dose on day 6 refills the root zone to field capacity. In practice, efficiency losses mean the gross amount applied is larger than the net requirement [11].

4.3 Livestock tracking

Electronic identification of animals is mandated in many places. ISO 11784 defines the 64-bit code structure of animal RFID transponders and ISO 11785 the 134.2 kHz air interface used by ear tags, boluses and implanted transponders [63,64]. The European Union made electronic identification of sheep and goats compulsory under Council Regulation (EC) No 21/2004 [12]. Beyond identity, wearable sensors on collars, ear tags or in the rumen monitor activity, rumination, body temperature and location, and can flag oestrus, lameness or illness earlier than visual checks [65]. GPS collars with geofences track grazing animals across large properties, and virtual fencing collars go further, using an audio cue followed by a mild electrical stimulus to keep cattle within a boundary drawn on a map; a trial by Campbell et al. found that cattle learned to respond to the audio cue alone [66].

The haversine formula gives great-circle distance on a spherical Earth and is accurate enough for farm-scale tracking [67]. For polygons spanning large distances, project coordinates to a planar system first.

4.4 Crop monitoring

Crop health shows in how leaves reflect light. Healthy vegetation absorbs red light for photosynthesis and strongly reflects near-infrared (NIR), so the normalised difference vegetation index, NDVI = (NIR − Red)/(NIR + Red), rises with green biomass [68,69]. NDVI and related indices are computed from satellite images, drone cameras and tractor-mounted sensors. ESA's Sentinel-2 satellites provide free multispectral imagery in 13 bands with up to 10 m resolution and a revisit of five days at the equator with two satellites [70], and Google Earth Engine makes multi-year archives of such data available for analysis at planetary scale [71]. In-field IoT adds what satellites cannot see: leaf wetness and microclimate for disease models, insect trap counts from camera traps, and yield monitors on combines.

Domain 45. Healthcare

The Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) spans consumer wellness wearables, regulated medical devices and hospital infrastructure. The boundary matters: software intended to diagnose, treat or monitor a disease can itself be a medical device. The International Medical Device Regulators Forum defines Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) as software intended for one or more medical purposes that performs them without being part of a hardware medical device [13]. Manufacturers manage risk under ISO 14971 [72], and in the United States premarket submissions must now document cybersecurity, as set out in FDA's 2023 guidance [14].

5.1 Wearables

Smartwatches and fitness bands mostly measure heart rate with photoplethysmography (PPG): an LED shines into the skin and a photodiode records the small changes in light absorption with each pulse of blood [76]. Accelerometers add activity and sleep; some devices add a single-lead ECG, skin temperature or blood oxygen (SpO2). The Apple Heart Study, with 419,297 participants, tested whether an irregular-pulse algorithm on a smartwatch could flag possible atrial fibrillation: 0.52% of participants received a notification, and when an ECG patch was worn at the same time, the positive predictive value of an irregular tachogram was 0.84 [77]. In 2018 the FDA granted a De Novo classification to Apple's watch-based ECG app, an example of a consumer wearable function regulated as a medical device [78].

Measurement biasOptical sensors do not perform equally for everyone. In a study of paired measurements in hospital patients, pulse oximetry missed occult hypoxaemia nearly three times as often in Black patients as in White patients [79]. Validation across skin tones, body types and motion conditions is part of building a responsible wearable.

Most wearables talk to phones over Bluetooth Low Energy using GATT services. The standard Heart Rate Service (UUID 0x180D) exposes a Heart Rate Measurement characteristic (0x2A37) whose first byte is a flags field that says whether the value is 8 or 16 bits and whether sensor-contact, energy-expended and RR-interval fields follow [73,80]. 5.2 Remote monitoring

Remote patient monitoring (RPM) sends measurements from home (weight, blood pressure, SpO2, glucose, ECG, implant data) to a clinical team who act on trends before a crisis. Evidence is strongest where a clear physiological signal precedes deterioration and a team responds quickly. In the TIM-HF2 trial of 1,538 heart-failure patients in Germany, daily transmission of weight, blood pressure, ECG and self-rated health to a telemedical centre staffed around the clock reduced the percentage of days lost to unplanned cardiovascular admissions or death, and reduced all-cause mortality [82]. In the CHAMPION trial, an implanted wireless pulmonary-artery pressure sensor whose readings guided therapy reduced heart-failure hospitalisations compared with standard care [83].

Interoperability standards make RPM scalable. IEEE 11073 personal health device standards define data models and an optimised exchange protocol for devices such as scales and blood-pressure monitors [74], and FHIR provides REST APIs and resources (Observation, Device, Patient) for exchanging the results with electronic health records [75]. Open-source servers such as HAPI FHIR are a convenient way to prototype [84].

5.3 Medical equipment

Hospitals connect infusion pumps, patient monitors, ventilators, imaging systems and laboratory analysers to clinical networks so that data reaches the record automatically and equipment can be tracked and maintained. Connecting them changes their risk profile. IEC 80001-1 assigns responsibilities for managing safety, effectiveness and security when medical devices are placed on IT networks [15], and ASTM F2761 describes an integrated clinical environment in which devices can be coordinated, for example pausing an opioid pump when a monitor detects respiratory depression [85]. Common hospital practices include network segmentation for medical devices, inventories with software bills of materials, and patch processes agreed with manufacturers, all of which FDA's premarket guidance now asks manufacturers to support [14].

5.4 Patient tracking

Real-time locating systems (RTLS) track patients, staff and equipment inside hospitals using active RFID, Wi-Fi, infrared, ultrasound or Bluetooth tags, with accuracy from room level to under a metre depending on technology [86]. Uses include finding infusion pumps and wheelchairs, measuring patient flow through emergency departments, protecting infants and patients with dementia with door-exit alarms, staff duress buttons and contact tracing. Kamel Boulos and Berry point out that room-level accuracy often needs infrared or ultrasound because radio signals pass through walls [86]. The same privacy rules that cover health records apply to location data about patients and staff.

Domain 56. Transportation

A modern car is a network of dozens of electronic control units joined by in-vehicle buses, most commonly the Controller Area Network (CAN, ISO 11898) [87]. Connectivity links that network to the outside world: a telematics control unit with a cellular modem and GNSS receiver, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth for phones, and increasingly vehicle-to-everything (V2X) radios. That outside link brings services and also exposure. Checkoway et al. showed in 2011 that remote attack surfaces included Bluetooth, cellular telematics and even malicious audio files [88], and in 2015 Miller and Valasek demonstrated remote control of an unmodified Jeep Cherokee over the cellular network [89], which led to a recall of about 1.4 million vehicles [90].

6.1 Connected vehicles

Connected-vehicle services range from emergency calls to over-the-air updates. In the EU, eCall has been mandatory in new types of passenger cars and light vans since 31 March 2018: after a serious crash the car dials 112 automatically and sends a minimum set of data including location [91]. V2X adds short-range, low-latency links. Dedicated short-range communications (DSRC), based on IEEE 802.11p, defined a Basic Safety Message broadcast about ten times per second with a vehicle's position, speed and heading [92]. In 2020 the FCC reallocated the lower 45 MHz of the 5.9 GHz band to unlicensed use and designated the upper 30 MHz (5.895–5.925 GHz) for cellular V2X [18].

Regulation now treats vehicle cybersecurity and software updates as type-approval requirements. UN Regulation No. 155 requires a certified cyber-security management system and UN Regulation No. 156 a software-update management system; in the EU they apply to all newly registered vehicles from July 2024 [16,93].

6.2 Fleet tracking

Fleet tracking combines a GNSS receiver, a cellular modem and a connection to the vehicle's power and data buses. GPS alone is accurate to within about 4.9 m (95%) for a smartphone under open sky, and less near tall buildings, trees or tunnels [94], so trackers fuse GNSS with wheel speed and inertial sensors and snap tracks to the road map. Typical uses are dispatch, proof of delivery, geofencing of depots and customer sites, theft recovery, utilisation reports and route optimisation. Regulation is a strong driver: the U.S. electronic logging device (ELD) rule requires most commercial drivers who keep hours-of-service records to use an ELD connected to the engine [95]. Open-source servers such as Traccar support hundreds of tracker protocols [96].

6.3 Telematics

Telematics is the collection and use of vehicle data: location, speed, engine data, fault codes, fuel use and driving behaviour. Light vehicles expose standardised diagnostic data through the OBD-II port using the service modes and parameter IDs (PIDs) of SAE J1979 [97]; heavy trucks use SAE J1939 over CAN [98]. Fleet operators use the data for preventive maintenance, fuel management and driver coaching, and insurers use it for usage-based insurance. 6.4 Autonomous systems

SAE J3016 defines six levels of driving automation, from Level 0 (no automation) to Level 5 (full automation under all conditions). The key line falls between Level 2, where the human driver must supervise at all times, and Level 3, where the system performs the entire driving task within its operational design domain and the human must take over only when asked [17]. UN Regulation No. 157 on Automated Lane Keeping Systems, in force since January 2021, was the first binding international regulation for Level 3 automation on motorways [99].

Autonomous vehicles are IoT systems in their own right: cameras, radar, lidar, ultrasonic sensors, GNSS and inertial units feed perception and planning computers, while connectivity supplies high-definition maps, remote assistance, fleet learning and over-the-air software. The same pattern applies to autonomous shuttles, delivery robots and drones. Because the consequences of failure are physical, safety standards, cybersecurity management (UN R155) and controlled software updates (UN R156) are as important as the algorithms [16,93].

Domain 67. Energy

The electricity grid was designed for one-way flow from large power stations to passive consumers. Rooftop solar, batteries, heat pumps and electric vehicles make it two-way and much more variable, and IoT supplies the visibility and control needed to manage it. NIST's smart grid framework divides the system into seven conceptual domains (bulk generation, transmission, distribution, customers, markets, operations and service providers) and catalogues the standards that let them interoperate [19].

7.1 Smart meters

A smart meter records consumption in intervals (often 15 or 30 minutes), communicates it automatically, and supports remote connection and disconnection, outage detection (the “last gasp” message sent when power is lost), voltage monitoring and time-of-use tariffs. The DLMS/COSEM suite (IEC 62056) is the most widely used data model and protocol family for meters; each quantity is identified by an OBIS code, for example 1.8.0 for total imported active energy [100]. The EU's Third Energy Package required member states, subject to a positive cost-benefit assessment, to fit at least 80% of consumers with intelligent metering by 2020 [102], and EIA data show that smart (AMI) meters now make up the majority of U.S. electricity meters [103].

7.2 Smart grids

Further up the grid, IoT looks like industrial automation. Substations use IEC 61850, which models protection and control functions as logical nodes and uses fast GOOSE messages over Ethernet for trip signals between protection relays [20]. Phasor measurement units (PMUs) timestamp voltage and current phasors against GPS time, following IEEE C37.118.1, so operators can see the grid's dynamic state across a wide area tens of times per second [104,105]. Distribution operators use sensors on feeders, automated switches and fault indicators to locate faults and restore supply to most customers automatically. The same connectivity increases exposure: on 23 December 2015, attackers used remote access to open breakers at three Ukrainian distribution companies, cutting power to about 225,000 customers [106].

7.3 Renewable energy

The International Energy Agency estimates that almost 510 GW of renewable capacity was added worldwide in 2023, about 50% more than in 2022, with solar PV making up three-quarters of it [107]. Every one of these plants is heavily instrumented. Wind turbines report through IEC 61400-25, which applies the IEC 61850 modelling approach to wind power plants [108]; solar inverters commonly expose standardised Modbus register maps defined by the SunSpec Alliance [109,110]. IEEE 1547-2018 requires inverter-based distributed energy resources to ride through grid disturbances, to support voltage and frequency, and to provide a communication interface using IEEE 2030.5, IEEE 1815 (DNP3) or SunSpec Modbus [21]. At high solar penetration, net demand dips at midday and ramps steeply in the evening, the “duck curve” described by the California ISO, which makes flexible, controllable loads and storage valuable [111].

7.4 Power monitoring

Power monitoring ranges from plug-level energy meters in homes to permanent power-quality analysers in factories and data centres. Power-quality instruments measure voltage dips and swells, harmonics, flicker and unbalance, and IEC 61000-4-30 defines measurement methods so that results from different instruments are comparable [112]. Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM), proposed by Hart, infers which appliances are running from step changes in real and reactive power measured at a single point, avoiding a sensor on every appliance [113]. Listing 12 applies the simplest form of Hart's idea: matching the size of each step to known appliance signatures. Open-source tools such as pandapower let engineers model networks and test the effect of new loads and generators before connecting them [114].

Domain 78. Logistics

Logistics was the first IoT domain in name: the term came from RFID in consumer-goods supply chains [5]. Today it spans tags and trackers on items, pallets, containers and vehicles, and the systems that share those events between companies. GS1's EPCIS standard defines how to capture and share visibility events, answering what, when, where and why for each object; version 2.0 added JSON/JSON-LD and REST bindings and sensor data [22].

8.1 Asset tracking

The right technology depends on the asset's value and how precisely and how often it must be located. Passive UHF RFID (RAIN RFID, ISO/IEC 18000-63) tags cost cents and can be read several metres away without a battery, but only near a reader [115]. Bluetooth beacons give room-level location inside buildings. Ultra-wideband (IEEE 802.15.4z) measures time of flight and can locate tags to tens of centimetres, which suits tools, forklifts and work-in-progress in factories [116]. For assets on the move, battery-powered trackers combine GNSS with LTE-M, NB-IoT, LoRaWAN or satellite links and report on a schedule or when motion, shock or a geofence crossing occurs [47,46].

8.2 Inventory management

Inventory records are surprisingly wrong. DeHoratius and Raman examined nearly 370,000 inventory records at a large retailer and found that about 65% did not match what was physically in the store [117]. Automated identification attacks the problem directly: in an early study of item-level RFID at Walmart, stores using RFID had about 16% fewer out-of-stocks than comparable control stores [118]. Other IoT techniques are load-cell shelves that infer the count from weight, camera-based shelf monitoring, and smart bins in factories that reorder parts automatically when nearly empty. Better and more frequent information also dampens the bullwhip effect, in which order variability grows as it moves upstream in a supply chain [119].

8.3 Cold-chain monitoring

Temperature-sensitive goods (vaccines, biologics, fresh food) must stay inside a range from production to use. CDC guidance for refrigerated vaccines is 2–8 °C, recorded with a calibrated digital data logger [121]. The EU's Good Distribution Practice guidelines for medicines require temperature-mapped storage and monitored transport [23], and the U.S. FDA's sanitary transportation rule covers temperature control of food in transit [24]. Losses are large: FAO estimated that around 14% of the world's food is lost between harvest and retail [122], and UNEP estimated that a further 17% of food is wasted in households, food service and retail [123].

IoT loggers replace USB loggers that are read only on arrival, when it is too late to save the shipment. Live loggers send temperature, location, door openings and shock over cellular, LoRaWAN or BLE, so an excursion can be handled in transit. For pharmaceuticals, a common summary statistic is the mean kinetic temperature (MKT), a single temperature that would cause the same total thermal degradation as the actual varying profile. Haynes introduced the concept and it is used in stability and storage assessments, typically with an activation energy of 83.144 kJ/mol [124,125]. Because degradation rises exponentially with temperature, MKT is always at least the arithmetic mean.

8.4 Supply chains

At supply-chain scale, IoT data becomes valuable when it is shared. Traceability systems link EPCIS-style events from many parties so that the origin and handling history of a product can be queried quickly [22]. In a widely reported pilot, Walmart and IBM used a permissioned blockchain to cut the time to trace a package of sliced mangoes to its farm from almost seven days to 2.2 seconds [126]; the speed came mainly from digitising and sharing the records, which any shared ledger or database could provide. Regulation is pushing the same way: the EU's Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation introduces a digital product passport that will carry information about a product's composition, repair and recycling along its life [127]. Sensor data on containers, trailers and pallets (location, temperature, shock, door events) is the raw material for such records and for predicting late arrivals.

Domain 89. Aerospace

Aerospace was doing “IoT” before the name existed: aircraft and spacecraft have streamed telemetry to the ground for decades, under tight constraints on bandwidth, power, mass and reliability. What is new is the scale (tens of thousands of aircraft and satellites), the use of commercial technologies such as CubeSats and open-source flight software, and the idea of satellites as a backhaul for terrestrial IoT devices.

9.1 Aircraft monitoring

Aircraft report themselves in several ways. ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast) transponders broadcast GNSS position, velocity, identification and status about twice a second on 1090 MHz (1090ES) or, for some U.S. general aviation, on 978 MHz UAT. Since 1 January 2020, aircraft in most controlled U.S. airspace must carry ADS-B Out [26]. Because the signals are unencrypted, anyone with a cheap software-defined radio can receive them; the OpenSky Network aggregates data from a crowd-sourced network of such receivers for research [128]. Each 112-bit message ends with a 24-bit CRC parity field, and identification messages carry an eight-character callsign in a 6-bit character set [129].

Airlines also receive operational data from the aircraft. ACARS (ARINC 618) carries short text messages such as out/off/on/in times, engine reports and maintenance messages over VHF, HF and satellite links [130]. Engine manufacturers analyse this condition-monitoring data to plan maintenance, and long-term service contracts priced per flying hour, such as Rolls-Royce's “power by the hour”, depend on it [131]. The disappearance of flight MH370 in 2014, which investigators could only locate approximately by analysing hourly satellite “handshake” signals between the aircraft's terminal and an Inmarsat satellite [132], led ICAO to require operators to track aircraft over oceanic areas at least every 15 minutes [25].

9.2 Satellite telemetry

Spacecraft telemetry and telecommands are usually exchanged as CCSDS Space Packets. Every packet starts with a 6-octet primary header containing a version number, a type bit (telemetry or telecommand), a secondary-header flag, an 11-bit Application Process Identifier (APID) that names the source or destination on board, sequence flags, a 14-bit sequence count, and a packet data length field equal to the number of octets in the data field minus one [27]. ESA missions build on this with the Packet Utilisation Standard (PUS, ECSS-E-ST-70-41C), which defines standard services such as housekeeping reporting, event reporting and time-tagged commands [28].

Ground segments decommutate these packets into named parameters, check limits and archive everything. Open-source mission-control tools such as Yamcs and NASA's Open MCT are used for this by small missions and test benches [133,134].

9.3 Remote sensing

Earth-observation satellites are the widest-area sensors in the IoT. The Landsat programme has imaged the Earth's land surface continuously since Landsat 1 in 1972, and the 2008 decision to make the archive free and open transformed its scientific use [135]; Landsat 9 was launched in September 2021 [136]. ESA's Sentinel-2 provides 13-band multispectral imagery with a 290 km swath [70]. Remote sensing and in-situ IoT complement each other: satellites cover everywhere but infrequently and only what can be seen from above, while ground sensors measure continuously at a few points. Agricultural NDVI (Section 4.4), flood mapping, wildfire detection and methane monitoring all combine the two.

Satellites are also becoming a direct link for IoT devices in places without terrestrial coverage, such as oceans, deserts and remote pipelines [137]. 3GPP Release 17 added non-terrestrial network (NTN) support to NB-IoT and LTE-M, so standard cellular IoT modules can connect via satellites [138]. Low Earth orbit keeps path loss and latency manageable but means each satellite is visible from a ground point for only a few minutes per pass, so devices must store data and transmit in contact windows. Listing 17 estimates those quantities using Kepler's third law and the Friis free-space path loss [139,140].

The 400 km result (about 92.6 minutes, 15.6 orbits per day) matches the International Space Station, which circles the Earth about 16 times a day [141].

9.4 Space systems

The CubeSat standard turned small satellites into something close to a commodity IoT platform: a 1U CubeSat is a 10 cm cube, and the CubeSat Design Specification limits mass to 2.00 kg per unit [142]. Missions use off-the-shelf processors and radios, and open-source flight software frameworks such as NASA's core Flight System (cFS) and JPL's F Prime [143,144]. Constellations of such satellites now carry IoT payloads, AIS ship tracking and ADS-B receivers, and sensors for weather and Earth observation.

Further out, the network itself becomes the problem. Links to other planets have one-way delays of many minutes and are regularly interrupted, so ordinary end-to-end TCP/IP does not work [145]. Delay-tolerant networking (DTN) replaces it with store-and-forward of “bundles” that nodes hold until the next contact [145]; Bundle Protocol version 7 is specified in RFC 9171 [146]. The same store-carry-forward idea appears on Earth in IoT devices that only occasionally reach a gateway, such as sensors on animals, ships or LEO satellite links.

10. Cross-cutting lessons

Design for disconnection

Thermostats, alarm panels, traffic controllers, vehicles and satellites all keep working when the cloud is unreachable. Local control, store-and-forward and clear failsafe states are universal patterns [145,49].

Security is a regulatory requirement

Consumer labels (ETSI EN 303 645, the U.S. Cyber Trust Mark), medical premarket rules, UN R155 for vehicles and NERC CIP-style rules for grids all make security mandatory [7,8,14,16].

Data models beat radios

Matter, SAREF, NGSI-LD, FHIR, DLMS/COSEM, EPCIS and CCSDS/PUS all standardise meaning rather than transport; that is what makes data reusable [6,41,10,75,100,22,28].

Evidence over claims

The best-documented gains (thermostat setbacks, telemonitoring in heart failure, RFID inventory accuracy, adaptive signals) come from field trials, not datasheets [37,82,118,49].

12. Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Constraint table

A city wants to monitor 3,000 storm drains for blockage. Fill in the columns of Table 1 for this application and propose a connectivity option.

Solution

Power: battery, ideally 5+ years (no power in drains). Range: km, underground or under a metal grate. Latency: minutes is fine, except during storms. Failure: local flooding. Option: LoRaWAN or NB-IoT, both designed for battery devices sending a few bytes; NB-IoT's deep-coverage design helps underground, LoRaWAN lets the city own the network [46,47]. Send a daily heartbeat so a dead sensor is not mistaken for a clear drain.

Exercise 2. Hysteresis

Using Listing 2, what happens if band_c is set to 0? Why do real thermostats avoid it?

Solution

The heater turns on whenever the temperature is at or below the setpoint and off at or above it, so sensor noise around the setpoint causes rapid on/off cycling. Short-cycling wears relays, boilers and compressors and wastes energy. A dead band trades a small temperature swing for far fewer switching events.

Exercise 3. Webster

A junction has two critical phases with y = 0.40 and 0.35 and 12 s of lost time. Compute the optimum cycle and the effective greens. What happens if flows grow by 30%?

Solution

Y = 0.75, so C0 = (1.5×12 + 5)/(1 − 0.75) = 23/0.25 = 92 s. Effective green = 80 s, split 80×0.40/0.75 = 42.7 s and 37.3 s [51]. With 30% more traffic, Y = 0.975 and C0 = 920 s, which is impractical: the junction is near saturation and needs more capacity or demand management, not just retiming. Try it in the calculator.

Exercise 4. Parking payload

Decode the bytes 03 FF 9C 0A with Listing 4.

Solution

Flags 0x03: occupied and low battery. Deviation 0xFF9C = −100, so −10.0 µT. Battery 2.0 + 10×0.02 = 2.2 V. A low-battery flag combined with an odd reading is a good reason to schedule a sensor visit.

Exercise 5. Soil moisture

A TDR probe reads Ka = 20. Use the Topp equation to find θ, and say whether irrigation is needed for the soil in Listing 7 (FC 0.30, root depth 600 mm, RAW 48 mm).

Solution

θ = −0.053 + 0.584 − 0.220 + 0.0344 ≈ 0.345 m³/m³ [62]. That is above field capacity (for example just after heavy rain), so depletion is zero and no irrigation is needed; excess water will drain below the root zone [11].

Exercise 6. NDVI interpretation

Pixel A has NIR 0.50 and red 0.05; pixel B has NIR 0.25 and red 0.15. Compute NDVI and interpret.

Solution

A: 0.45/0.55 = 0.82 (dense, healthy canopy). B: 0.10/0.40 = 0.25 (sparse or stressed vegetation, or partly bare soil) [68,69]. B should be scouted on the ground before treatment; NDVI cannot tell water stress from disease or nutrient deficiency.

Exercise 7. BLE heart-rate frame

Decode 01 2C 01 as a Heart Rate Measurement.

Solution

Flags 0x01: bit 0 set, so the value is UINT16 little-endian: 0x012C = 300 bpm. No contact, energy or RR fields [73]. 300 bpm is physiologically implausible for an adult at rest, so a real system should run plausibility checks before alerting a clinician.

Exercise 8. Wearable screening arithmetic

A screening algorithm has 98% specificity and 90% sensitivity for a condition with 1% prevalence in the screened population. Out of 100,000 users, how many alerts are true positives?

Solution

True positives: 1,000 × 0.90 = 900. False positives: 99,000 × 0.02 = 1,980. PPV = 900/2,880 ≈ 31%. Even a good algorithm produces mostly false alarms at low prevalence, which is why studies such as the Apple Heart Study confirm notifications with an ECG patch [77].

Exercise 9. OBD-II

Decode 41 0C 0F A0 and 41 05 32.

Solution

RPM = 0x0FA0/4 = 4000/4 = 1,000 rpm (idle). Coolant = 0x32 − 40 = 50 − 40 = 10 °C, a cold engine [97].

Exercise 10. V2X timing

Two cars approach each other at 100 km/h each. How far do they close between Basic Safety Messages sent at 10 Hz?

Solution

Closing speed 200 km/h = 55.6 m/s; in 0.1 s that is 5.6 m [92]. Applications must extrapolate positions between messages and allow for latency and packet loss.

Exercise 11. Time-of-use

In Listing 12, the kettle/oven element event at 19:15 runs during the peak band. If the 2.0 kW load ran for 30 minutes after 20:00 instead, how much would the bill fall?

Solution

Energy = 2.0 kW × 0.5 h = 1.0 kWh. Saving = 1.0 × (0.38 − 0.14) = 0.24 currency units. Small per event, but automated load shifting across millions of homes is what makes demand response valuable to the grid [39,111].

Exercise 12. NILM ambiguity

Why might Hart's step-change method confuse a 2 kW kettle with a 2 kW oven element, and what extra feature could separate them?

Solution

Both are resistive loads with similar real-power steps and almost no reactive power. Duration (kettles run for minutes, ovens cycle for an hour), on/off patterns (thermostat cycling) and time of day help. Hart proposed using reactive power as a second dimension for loads that differ in it [113].

Exercise 13. Mean kinetic temperature

Why is MKT higher than the arithmetic mean in Listing 14, and why does that matter for a short excursion?

Solution

Degradation rate follows an Arrhenius (exponential) law, so warm periods count for more than cool periods of the same length; MKT is computed from the mean of exp(−ΔH/RT), not of T [124]. A short excursion can raise MKT more than the average suggests, while the product is still within its stability budget. Whether stock can be used is a quality decision based on the product's stability data, not on MKT alone [23].

Exercise 14. Reorder point

In Listing 13, the supplier's lead time rises from 3 to 5 days. Recompute the reorder point.

Solution

Demand during lead time = 40 × 5 = 200. Safety stock = 1.65 × 3.65 × √5 ≈ 13.5. Reorder point = ⌈213.5⌉ = 214 units [120]. Longer lead times increase both terms, which is why lead-time visibility from shipment trackers matters.

Exercise 15. ADS-B integrity

Listing 15 shows that a corrupted frame fails the CRC. Does a correct CRC prove that a message came from the aircraft it claims to be?

Solution

No. The CRC detects transmission errors, not forgery. ADS-B is unauthenticated, so anyone with a transmitter could generate a valid frame. Defences use independent checks such as multilateration (comparing arrival times at several receivers) and plausibility tests, which crowd-sourced networks like OpenSky make possible [129,128].

Exercise 16. LEO contact budget

A sensor sends 20 bytes at 1 kbit/s effective throughput. Using Listing 17's 550 km pass duration, roughly how many messages could it send in one best-case pass? Why is the real number much lower?

Solution

One message takes 160 bits / 1,000 bit/s = 0.16 s; a 7.9 min (474 s) pass allows about 2,960 messages. Real numbers are far lower: many devices share the satellite, most passes are not overhead (shorter and at lower elevation, with higher path loss, as Listing 17's 156 dB at 10° shows), protocol overheads and duty-cycle limits apply [137,140].

Key takeaways

  • All eight domains reuse the same stack; what differs is the constraint set (power, range, latency, consequence of failure, regulation), so start every design with Table 1 [1,3].
  • Low-power wide-area networks (LoRaWAN, NB-IoT) dominate city, farm and asset use cases; mesh (Thread, Zigbee) dominates homes; cellular and V2X dominate vehicles [46,47,29,92].
  • Domain data models (Matter, FHIR, DLMS/COSEM, EPCIS, CCSDS) are what make data portable between vendors and organisations [6,75,100,22,27].
  • Control loops must be safe without the cloud: thermostats, traffic controllers, protection relays and spacecraft all keep local authority [49,20,145].
  • Benefits are real where trials show them, and so are risks: botnets, grid attacks and vehicle hacks show that security is part of the application [40,106,89].
