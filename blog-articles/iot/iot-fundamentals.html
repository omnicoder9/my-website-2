1Definition and history of IoT

1.1 What the standards say

There is no single universal definition of the Internet of Things, but the most widely cited formal one comes from the ITU. Recommendation ITU-T Y.2060 defines the IoT as "a global infrastructure for the information society, enabling advanced services by interconnecting (physical and virtual) things based on existing and evolving interoperable information and communication technologies" [1]. The same Recommendation defines a thing as an object of the physical world (a physical thing) or of the information world (a virtual thing) that can be identified and integrated into communication networks, and a device as equipment with mandatory communication capabilities and optional sensing, actuation, data capture, storage and processing capabilities [1].

NIST deliberately avoids a single definition. NIST SP 800-183 instead describes "Networks of Things" in terms of five primitives (sensor, aggregator, communication channel, external utility and decision trigger) from which any IoT system can be composed and reasoned about [2]. The international reference architecture ISO/IEC 30141 complements these by describing IoT systems through domains, entities and viewpoints so that different implementations can be compared [3].

Working definition used in this article. An IoT system is a set of identifiable physical or virtual things that sense and/or act on the world, communicate over networks, and are integrated with services that turn their data into decisions. This paraphrases the Y.2060 and NIST SP 800-183 framings [1,2].

1.2 A short history

The intellectual roots are older than the term. Mark Weiser's 1991 essay on ubiquitous computing imagined computation that disappears into everyday objects and environments [4]. Practical precursors existed even earlier: in the early 1980s, Carnegie Mellon computer-science students wired a departmental Coke machine to the local network so they could check remotely whether it held cold drinks [5]. In 1991 researchers at the University of Cambridge Computer Laboratory pointed a camera at the "Trojan Room" coffee pot so colleagues could see if it was full; in 1993 the image was put on the World Wide Web [6].

Kevin Ashton recalled using the phrase "Internet of Things" as the title of a 1999 presentation at Procter & Gamble about linking RFID-tagged supply chains to the internet [7]. The ITU's 2005 Internet Report, The Internet of Things, brought the concept to the attention of governments and the telecommunications sector [8], and in 2012 the ITU published Y.2060 as a formal overview [1]. Academic surveys from 2010 onward consolidated the field around three "visions": things-oriented (RFID, sensors), internet-oriented (IP for smart objects) and semantic-oriented (representing and reasoning about the data) [9–11].

1.3 The ITU-T Y.2060 reference model

Y.2060 organises IoT capabilities into four horizontal layers (device, network, service-and-application-support, and application), crossed by two vertical sets of capabilities for management and security that apply to every layer [1]. Many textbooks simplify this to a three-layer "perception, network, application" model [11]; the vertical management and security slices are the part most often forgotten in practice.

2Evolution of machine-to-machine (M2M) communication

Machine-to-machine communication predates the IoT label. Industrial telemetry and supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems have long collected measurements from remote sites and sent commands back to field equipment; NIST SP 800-82 describes how these systems evolved from isolated, proprietary installations toward networks that increasingly use IP and commercial IT components [20]. M2M in this sense is typically vertical: one application, one device type, one network, one vendor stack.

Telecom standards bodies formalised M2M in the 2010s. ETSI published a functional architecture for M2M (TS 102 690) that splits the system into a device-and-gateway domain and a network domain with common service capabilities [12], and 3GPP specified service requirements for machine-type communications (MTC) on cellular networks, covering features such as low mobility, infrequent small data transmissions and group-based handling [21]. Low-power wide-area networks (LPWAN) such as LoRaWAN, Sigfox, NB-IoT and Wi-SUN later extended coverage to battery-powered devices with very small payloads [22].

The move from M2M to IoT is mostly a move from silos to horizontal platforms: shared IP-based networking, common application protocols such as MQTT and CoAP [13,16], reusable device-management standards such as OMA LwM2M [23], and data models that let one application consume data from many vendors [24]. Y.2060 makes this distinction explicit by placing generic support capabilities beneath domain-specific applications [1].

3IoT domains

IoT is usually discussed through application domains. They share building blocks but differ sharply in priorities: safety and determinism in industry, convenience and cost in the home, regulatory assurance in medicine, latency and mobility in vehicles.

3.1 Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT)

The IIoT applies IoT to manufacturing, energy, utilities, oil and gas, and other industrial operations. Boyes and colleagues characterise it as networks of intelligent industrial assets, communication infrastructure and analytics used to improve industrial processes, and they propose an analysis framework that classifies IIoT devices by function, connectivity and security properties [25]. Sisinni and co-authors note that IIoT inherits strict industrial requirements, including real-time behaviour, reliability, and long equipment lifetimes, that consumer IoT rarely faces [26].

The Industrial Internet Reference Architecture (IIRA) describes IIoT systems through business, usage, functional and implementation viewpoints [27]. Because IIoT connects operational technology (OT) to IT networks, security guidance is drawn from OT standards: NIST SP 800-82 for OT security [20] and the IEC 62443 series, which defines security levels and system requirements for industrial automation and control systems [28].

Examples: vibration sensors on motors feeding predictive-maintenance models; energy sub-metering across a plant; condition monitoring of pumps along a pipeline.

3.2 Consumer IoT

Consumer IoT covers smart-home devices, wearables, connected toys, appliances and personal assistants. ETSI EN 303 645 defines baseline security provisions for these products; its 13 headline provisions start with "no universal default passwords" and include implementing a vulnerability-disclosure policy, keeping software updated, communicating securely and making it easy for users to delete personal data [17]. The 2016 Mirai botnet shows why: it spread by logging into IoT devices that still used factory-default credentials and, at its peak, controlled roughly 600,000 devices used for large distributed denial-of-service attacks [15].

Fragmented smart-home ecosystems also motivated Matter, a Connectivity Standards Alliance application-layer standard (version 1.0 in 2022) that runs over IP networks such as Wi-Fi, Thread and Ethernet and uses Bluetooth Low Energy for commissioning [19].

3.3 Enterprise IoT

Enterprise IoT is the use of connected devices by organisations to run their own operations rather than to sell to consumers: smart buildings and HVAC, asset tracking, fleet telematics, retail shelf sensors and connected office equipment. Its distinguishing concerns are integration with existing IT (identity, networks, ERP and maintenance systems) and governance across large device estates. NIST SP 800-213 gives organisations a process for defining the device cybersecurity requirements they should expect from IoT devices before they deploy them [29], built on the manufacturer-facing capability baseline in NISTIR 8259A [30].

3.4 Internet of Medical Things (IoMT)

The IoMT is the network of connected medical devices, sensors and health-information systems that collect and exchange clinical data, from wearable monitors and connected insulin pumps to hospital infusion pumps and remote patient-monitoring platforms [31]. Joyia and colleagues identify benefits such as remote monitoring, earlier intervention and reduced hospital visits, along with challenges of security, privacy and interoperability [31]. Medical IoT is regulated: in the United States, FDA premarket guidance requires manufacturers to describe their cybersecurity risk management, including a software bill of materials (SBOM) and plans for updates and vulnerability handling across the device lifecycle [32].

3.5 Internet of Vehicles (IoV)

The IoV extends vehicular ad hoc networks into an internet-connected system in which vehicles exchange information with other vehicles, roadside infrastructure, pedestrians, sensors and cloud services. Kaiwartya and colleagues propose a five-layer IoV architecture (perception, coordination, artificial intelligence, application and business layers) and a network model built from these interaction types [33]. Vehicle automation itself is described by the six SAE J3016 levels, from Level 0 (no driving automation) to Level 5 (full driving automation) [34]. Vehicle cybersecurity is now a type-approval matter: UN Regulation No. 155 requires manufacturers to operate a certified cyber security management system [35].

3.6 Internet of Robotic Things (IoRT)

The IoRT combines IoT sensing and connectivity with robots that can act physically and autonomously. Simoens, Dragone and Saffiotti review the concept and argue that its added value comes from two directions: robots gain richer perception and knowledge from the surrounding IoT infrastructure, and IoT systems gain the ability to take physical action through robots [36]. Examples include warehouse robots coordinated with shelf and door sensors, and care robots that use smart-home sensors to understand a resident's situation [36].

4Smart environments and smart infrastructure

A smart environment is a physical space (a home, office, hospital ward or campus) instrumented with sensors and actuators so that it can perceive and respond to its occupants. Cook, Augusto and Jakkula describe such environments as the physical substrate on which ambient intelligence operates [37]. Smart infrastructure applies the same approach at urban and utility scale: smart grids, water networks, transport systems, street lighting and structural-health monitoring of bridges and tunnels [10].

Because "smart" is easy to claim and hard to measure, ISO 37122 defines indicators for smart cities, covering areas such as energy, mobility, water, governance and telecommunications, so that cities can quantify progress consistently [38]. Infrastructure IoT also inherits the OT security concerns described for IIoT [20].

5Cyber-physical systems (CPS)

Edward Lee describes cyber-physical systems as "integrations of computation with physical processes", in which embedded computers and networks monitor and control physical processes, usually with feedback loops in which physical processes affect computations and vice versa [39]. NIST's CPS Framework describes CPS through functional, business, human, trustworthiness, timing, data and boundaries concerns, and organises them into conceptualisation, realisation and assurance facets [40].

The IoT and CPS overlap heavily. A useful distinction is emphasis: IoT starts from connectivity and data, while CPS starts from the control loop and its timing and safety guarantees [39,40]. A connected thermostat is an IoT device; the closed temperature-control loop it implements, including its timing and stability, is a CPS.

6Edge computing and pervasive computing

6.1 Edge and fog computing

Cloud computing, as defined by NIST, provides on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources [41]. Sending every sensor reading to a distant data centre, however, costs bandwidth, adds latency and can expose private data. Shi and colleagues define edge computing as the enabling technologies that allow computation to be performed at the edge of the network, on downstream data on behalf of cloud services and upstream data on behalf of IoT services [42]. Satyanarayanan traces the idea through cloudlets, small data centres one wireless hop from mobile devices, and lists its benefits as lower latency, bandwidth reduction, privacy enforcement and resilience to cloud outages [43].

NIST's fog computing conceptual model places a layer of fog nodes between end devices and the cloud, characterised by contextual location awareness, low latency, geographic distribution, heterogeneity, interoperability and support for real-time interaction [44].

6.2 Pervasive (ubiquitous) computing

Pervasive computing descends directly from Weiser's ubiquitous-computing vision [4]. Satyanarayanan's 2001 analysis identified its research challenges as effective use of smart spaces, invisibility, localised scalability and masking uneven conditioning of environments, along with concerns such as context awareness, energy and privacy [45]. IoT can be seen as the networking and infrastructure layer that makes the pervasive-computing vision practical at scale.

7Ambient intelligence (AmI)

Ambient intelligence was promoted in Europe by the Information Society Technologies Advisory Group (ISTAG), whose 2001 scenarios describe people surrounded by intelligent, intuitive interfaces embedded in everyday objects, in an environment that recognises and responds to individuals in a seamless, unobtrusive way [46]. Cook, Augusto and Jakkula characterise AmI systems as sensitive, responsive, adaptive, transparent, ubiquitous and intelligent, and survey applications in smart homes, health monitoring and assistance, hospitals, transport, emergency services, education and workplaces [37].

The relationship between the terms is one of layers: sensors and networks (IoT) supply data; pervasive computing supplies the embedded, context-aware computing fabric; AmI adds reasoning about people and their intentions, so that the environment acts appropriately on their behalf [37,45].

8Digital twins

Grieves and Vickers describe the digital twin as a set of virtual information constructs that fully describe a potential or actual physical product, and identify its three parts as the physical product, the virtual product, and the data and information connections between them. Grieves first presented the underlying model in 2002 in the context of product lifecycle management, and the name "digital twin" is credited in their chapter to Vickers of NASA in 2010 [47]. Tao and colleagues survey industrial use, showing digital twins applied to product design, production, prognostics and health management [48].

For manufacturing, ISO 23247 provides a digital-twin framework built around "observable manufacturing elements" (personnel, equipment, materials, processes, facilities, environment, products and documents) and a reference architecture for representing and synchronising them [49]. In IoT terms, telemetry keeps the twin in sync with reality, and commands computed against the twin flow back to the physical asset through remote control (§9.8).

9Core concepts

9.1 Things and connected devices

In Y.2060 terms, a thing is anything that can be identified and integrated into a network, and a device is the equipment that does the communicating, sensing and acting on its behalf; a gateway connects devices to networks and can translate between protocols [1]. Devices vary enormously in resources. RFC 7228 classifies constrained nodes as Class 0 (far less than 10 KiB of RAM and 100 KiB of flash; usually cannot run a secure IP stack and need a gateway), Class 1 (about 10 KiB RAM and 100 KiB flash; can run a constrained stack such as CoAP over UDP) and Class 2 (about 50 KiB RAM and 250 KiB flash; can run mostly ordinary protocol stacks) [50].

9.2 Sensors and actuators

A sensor measures a physical property and produces data; an actuator receives data and produces a physical effect. NIST SP 800-183 treats the sensor as a primitive with properties such as transmission rate, accuracy and the physical property it reads, and notes that actuators can be modelled with the same properties working in the opposite direction [2]. Typical sensors include temperature, humidity, pressure, accelerometers, gas, light and current; typical actuators include relays, motors, valves, locks and displays.

Sensor pipeline

Transducer → signal conditioning → analog-to-digital conversion → calibration and units → timestamp → transmit or store.

Actuator pipeline

Authorised command → validation and safety interlocks → driver circuit → physical effect → feedback sensor confirms state.

9.3 Device identity

Every thing must be uniquely and verifiably identifiable. NISTIR 8259A lists device identification first among its six baseline capabilities (device identification, device configuration, data protection, logical access to interfaces, software update and cybersecurity state awareness) [30]. Identifiers alone (a MAC address, serial number or URN) are only names; trust requires a cryptographic identity. IEEE 802.1AR defines an IDevID, a manufacturer-installed secure device identifier bound to a key pair, and locally significant LDevIDs issued by the operator [51]. BRSKI (RFC 8995) uses the IDevID to bootstrap a device into an operator's network with zero touch, using a signed voucher that proves ownership [52].

9.4 Device discovery

Discovery answers "what devices and resources are out there, and how do I talk to them?". Options include:

  • mDNS / DNS-SD: devices announce services on the local link using multicast DNS on port 5353 and DNS-based service discovery records [53,54].
  • CoRE Link Format: a CoAP server lists its resources at /.well-known/core in a compact link format [55].
  • CoRE Resource Directory: devices register their links with a directory that clients can query, which suits sleepy devices and multi-hop networks [56].
  • W3C WoT Thing Description directories: machine-readable descriptions of a thing's properties, actions and events that a client can fetch and interpret [24].

9.5 Device interoperability

Interoperability operates at several levels: technical (shared transport and protocols), syntactic (shared data formats) and semantic (shared meaning of the data) [11]. Standards address each: MQTT and CoAP at the protocol level [13,16]; SenML as a common format for sensor measurements in JSON, CBOR or XML [57]; LwM2M's object/resource model, where for example object 3 is "Device" and object 5 is "Firmware Update" [23]; and the WoT Thing Description, which describes existing devices, whatever their protocol, through a common information model [24]. Matter tackles interoperability within the smart home by defining a common application layer across vendors [19].

9.6 Telemetry

Telemetry is the automatic measurement and transmission of data from remote sources. In IoT it means streams of timestamped readings sent from devices to an aggregation point. Good telemetry records what was measured (name), in which unit, when and by whom. SenML encodes exactly this, with fields such as base name (bn), base time (bt), name (n), unit (u, e.g. Cel), value (v) and time (t) [57]. Over constrained or metered links, devices often batch readings, send deltas, or report by exception.

9.7 Remote monitoring

Remote monitoring turns telemetry into situational awareness: dashboards, thresholds, trend analysis and alarms. It is one of the most mature IoT uses, from SCADA in utilities [20] to remote patient monitoring in healthcare [31]. It also covers monitoring the devices themselves; NISTIR 8259A calls this cybersecurity state awareness, the ability to report the device's own security state [30], and ETSI EN 303 645 asks manufacturers to examine system telemetry for security anomalies [17].

9.8 Remote control

Remote control sends commands or set-points from an application to an actuator. Because commands can cause physical effects, they need authentication, authorisation, integrity protection and often confirmation that the effect occurred [30]. Common patterns are a CoAP PUT/POST to a resource [13], an MQTT command topic answered on a response topic (MQTT 5.0 adds explicit response topic and correlation data properties for this request/response pattern) [16], or an LwM2M Write/Execute on a resource [23]. A "device shadow" or digital twin often holds the desired state so that a device that is offline converges when it reconnects [47].

9.9 Event-driven communication

In event-driven systems, components react to events (a door opened, a threshold crossed) instead of polling. Publish/subscribe is the dominant IoT pattern: producers publish to topics and consumers subscribe, decoupled in space (they need not know each other), time (they need not be active at the same time) and synchronisation (publishing does not block) [58]. MQTT is a pub/sub protocol with a broker, hierarchical topics with + and # wildcards, three QoS levels (0 at most once, 1 at least once, 2 exactly once), retained messages and a "Will" message published if a client disconnects ungracefully [16]. CoAP provides a lighter, broker-less event mechanism through Observe, where a client registers interest in a resource and the server pushes notifications when it changes [59].

9.10 Autonomous operation

IoT devices and systems increasingly act without human intervention: a valve closes on a leak, a microgrid islands itself, a robot re-plans its route. Kephart and Chess's autonomic-computing vision framed this as systems that are self-configuring, self-healing, self-optimising and self-protecting, built from autonomic elements that run a monitor–analyse–plan–execute loop over shared knowledge (MAPE-K) [60]. Edge computing makes local autonomy possible when the cloud is unreachable [43], and in vehicles the degree of autonomy is graded formally by SAE J3016 [34]. Autonomy raises the stakes for safety, which is why CPS frameworks treat trustworthiness (safety, security, privacy, reliability and resilience) as a first-class concern [40].

9.11 Device lifecycle management

A device lives far longer than the moment it is sold. NISTIR 8259 recommends that manufacturers plan cybersecurity across the lifecycle, before sale (identifying customers and their needs, determining which capabilities to provide) and after sale (communicating with customers and supporting them) [18]. A practical lifecycle model is shown in Figure 8.

  • Onboarding and provisioning. BRSKI for zero-touch enrolment [52]; Manufacturer Usage Description (MUD), in which a device emits a URL pointing to a manufacturer file that describes the network access it needs, so the network can restrict it to that behaviour [61].
  • Configuration and management. LwM2M defines bootstrap, client registration, device management and information reporting interfaces over CoAP [23].
  • Software update. RFC 9019 describes an architecture in which firmware images are distributed with a signed manifest so that devices can verify authenticity and integrity before installing [62]; NISTIR 8259A lists software update as a baseline capability [30].
  • End of life. Manufacturers should state support periods (ETSI EN 303 645 requires the defined support period to be published) [17], and operators should revoke credentials and erase data before disposal [29].

10.2 Python MQTT telemetry publisher with a Will message

The device publishes SenML telemetry [57], marks itself "online" with a retained status message, and registers a Will so the broker announces "offline" if the device disappears [16].

sensor_pub.py
import json, random, time
import paho.mqtt.client as mqtt

DEVICE_ID = "urn:dev:mac:0024befffe804ff1"
BASE = "site/1/lab"

client = mqtt.Client(mqtt.CallbackAPIVersion.VERSION2, client_id="lab-sensor-01")
# Will: published by the broker if we drop off without a clean DISCONNECT
client.will_set(f"{BASE}/status", payload="offline", qos=1, retain=True)

def on_connect(client, userdata, flags, reason_code, properties):
    print("connected:", reason_code)
    client.publish(f"{BASE}/status", "online", qos=1, retain=True)

client.on_connect = on_connect
client.connect("localhost", 1883, keepalive=30)
client.loop_start()

temp = 21.0
try:
    while True:
        temp += random.uniform(-0.3, 0.3)
        senml = [{
            "bn": DEVICE_ID + ":", "bt": time.time(),
            "n": "temp", "u": "Cel", "v": round(temp, 2)
        }]
        client.publish(f"{BASE}/temp", json.dumps(senml), qos=1)
        time.sleep(5)
except KeyboardInterrupt:
    client.publish(f"{BASE}/status", "offline", qos=1, retain=True).wait_for_publish()
    client.disconnect()
    client.loop_stop()

10.3 Event-driven subscriber with autonomous control

This subscriber reacts to each telemetry event and switches a fan actuator using hysteresis, a simple autonomous control loop (§9.10). It publishes commands to a separate command topic (§9.8).

fan_controller.py
import json
import paho.mqtt.client as mqtt

ON_ABOVE, OFF_BELOW = 24.0, 22.5     # hysteresis band avoids rapid toggling
fan_on = {}                           # room -> bool, our model of actuator state

def on_connect(client, userdata, flags, reason_code, properties):
    client.subscribe("site/1/+/temp", qos=1)

def on_message(client, userdata, msg):
    room = msg.topic.split("/")[2]
    record = json.loads(msg.payload)[0]
    t = record["v"]
    state = fan_on.get(room, False)
    if not state and t > ON_ABOVE:
        state = True
    elif state and t < OFF_BELOW:
        state = False
    else:
        return                         # no change, no command
    fan_on[room] = state
    cmd = {"fan": "on" if state else "off", "reason": f"temp={t}"}
    client.publish(f"site/1/{room}/cmd/fan", json.dumps(cmd), qos=1)
    print(room, cmd)

c = mqtt.Client(mqtt.CallbackAPIVersion.VERSION2, client_id="fan-controller")
c.on_connect, c.on_message = on_connect, on_message
c.connect("localhost", 1883)
c.loop_forever()

10.4 CoAP server with an observable resource and discovery

The server exposes /sensors/temp as an observable resource [59] and lists it at /.well-known/core for discovery [55]. Content-Format 110 is application/senml+json [57].

coap_server.py
import asyncio, json, random, time
import aiocoap
import aiocoap.resource as resource

class Temperature(resource.ObservableResource):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.value = 21.0

    async def render_get(self, request):
        body = [{"bn": "urn:dev:mac:0024befffe804ff1:", "bt": time.time(),
                 "n": "temp", "u": "Cel", "v": round(self.value, 2)}]
        return aiocoap.Message(payload=json.dumps(body).encode(), content_format=110)

async def main():
    root = resource.Site()
    root.add_resource([".well-known", "core"],
                      resource.WKCResource(root.get_resources_as_linkheader))
    temp = Temperature()
    root.add_resource(["sensors", "temp"], temp)
    await aiocoap.Context.create_server_context(root, bind=("::", 5683))
    while True:                       # simulate the physical world changing
        await asyncio.sleep(5)
        temp.value += random.uniform(-0.3, 0.3)
        temp.updated_state()          # notify all observers

asyncio.run(main())
coap_observe_client.py
import asyncio
import aiocoap

async def main():
    ctx = await aiocoap.Context.create_client_context()
    # 1) discovery
    wkc = await ctx.request(aiocoap.Message(code=aiocoap.GET,
                    uri="coap://localhost/.well-known/core")).response
    print("resources:", wkc.payload.decode())
    # 2) observe
    req = aiocoap.Message(code=aiocoap.GET, uri="coap://localhost/sensors/temp", observe=0)
    pr = ctx.request(req)
    first = await pr.response
    print("initial:", first.payload.decode())
    async for note in pr.observation:
        print("update:", note.payload.decode())

asyncio.run(main())

10.6 A minimal digital twin with desired and reported state

This sketch keeps a twin of the fan in sync with telemetry, records history, and computes the delta between desired and reported state so commands can be re-sent when an offline device reconnects (§8, §9.8).

twin.py
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
import time

@dataclass
class FanTwin:
    device_id: str
    reported: dict = field(default_factory=dict)   # what the device says
    desired: dict = field(default_factory=dict)    # what the operator wants
    history: list = field(default_factory=list)
    last_seen: float = 0.0

    def on_telemetry(self, values: dict):
        self.reported.update(values)
        self.last_seen = time.time()
        self.history.append((self.last_seen, dict(values)))

    def delta(self) -> dict:
        return {k: v for k, v in self.desired.items() if self.reported.get(k) != v}

    def is_online(self, timeout=60) -> bool:
        return time.time() - self.last_seen < timeout

twin = FanTwin("fan-lab-01")
twin.desired = {"fan": "on"}
twin.on_telemetry({"fan": "off", "rpm": 0})
print(twin.delta())     # {'fan': 'on'} -> send this command when the device is online

10.7 LwM2M addressing at a glance

LwM2M addresses data as /Object/Instance/Resource [23]. For example, /3/0 is the Device object instance, and /5 is the Firmware Update object, so a management server can read device information and drive a firmware update over the same CoAP-based interface.

12Exercises with solutions

Exercise 1. Classify each as a physical or virtual thing under Y.2060: (a) a smart meter, (b) a software agent representing a parcel in a logistics system, (c) a patient wearing an RFID wristband.

Solution

(a) Physical thing (it is also a device). (b) Virtual thing: an object of the information world that can be identified and integrated into networks. (c) The patient is a physical thing made identifiable through the RFID tag; the tag reader acts as the device [1].

Exercise 2. A node has 8 KiB of RAM and 64 KiB of flash. Which RFC 7228 class is it, and what does that imply about its protocol stack?

Solution

It falls below Class 1's rough 10 KiB/100 KiB, so it is Class 0. Class 0 devices typically cannot communicate directly with the internet in a secure manner and rely on a proxy, gateway or server [50].

Exercise 3. Write MQTT topic filters that match (a) every temperature reading on site 1, regardless of room; (b) every message from any room on site 1, at any depth. Which wildcard can appear only at the end?

Solution

(a) site/1/+/temp. (b) site/1/#. The multi-level wildcard # must be the last character of the filter; + matches exactly one level [16].

Exercise 4. A battery-powered door sensor sends an "opened" event. Losing an event is unacceptable, but duplicates are harmless because the server de-duplicates by timestamp. Which MQTT QoS level do you choose, and why not QoS 2?

Solution

QoS 1 (at least once). It guarantees delivery with a two-packet PUBLISH/PUBACK exchange. QoS 2 (exactly once) requires a four-packet handshake, costing extra airtime and battery for a guarantee the application does not need [16].

Exercise 5. Encode, in SenML JSON, two readings from the same device: temperature 22.1 °C and relative humidity 40 % taken at the same time.

Solution 
[
  {"bn":"urn:dev:mac:0024befffe804ff1:","bt":1.7631e9,"n":"temp","u":"Cel","v":22.1},
  {"n":"hum","u":"%RH","v":40}
]

The base name and base time apply to all subsequent records; Cel and %RH are registered SenML units [57].

Exercise 6. Your fleet of 10,000 sensors each sends 200 bytes every 10 s to the cloud. Estimate the daily upstream volume, then propose an edge strategy that reduces it by at least 90 %.

Solution

10,000 × 200 B × 8,640 messages/day ≈ 17.3 GB/day. An edge gateway can aggregate to one-minute summaries (min, max, mean: about 6× fewer messages) and send only changes beyond a dead-band; combined, these easily exceed a 90 % reduction while raw data stays available locally. This is the bandwidth-reduction benefit of edge computing [42,43].

Exercise 7. Map these lifecycle actions to NISTIR 8259A capabilities: (a) verifying a signed firmware image; (b) a unique per-device credential; (c) reporting a failed login count to the operator.

Solution

(a) Software update. (b) Device identification (and logical access to interfaces). (c) Cybersecurity state awareness [30].

Exercise 8. Extend fan_controller.py so that it ignores readings older than 30 s and alarms if a room's status topic becomes offline.

Solution 
# in on_connect
client.subscribe([("site/1/+/temp", 1), ("site/1/+/status", 1)])

# at the top of on_message
parts = msg.topic.split("/")
room, kind = parts[2], parts[3]
if kind == "status":
    if msg.payload == b"offline":
        print(f"ALARM: {room} sensor offline")
    return
rec = json.loads(msg.payload)[0]
if time.time() - rec.get("bt", 0) > 30:
    return  # stale reading

The offline alarm relies on the retained Will message set by the publisher [16]. Add import time at the top.

Exercise 9. Explain in two sentences why an IoT thermostat is "IoT" while its control loop is a "CPS".

Solution

The thermostat is IoT because it is an identifiable, networked device exposing data and accepting remote commands [1]. The closed loop that measures temperature and drives heating under timing and stability constraints is a cyber-physical system, an integration of computation with a physical process [39].

13Trivia with answers

  1. Which university's networked soft-drink machine is often cited as one of the first internet-connected appliances?

    Answer

    Carnegie Mellon University, in the early 1980s [5].

  2. What everyday object did Cambridge researchers put on camera in 1991 and onto the Web in 1993?

    Answer

    The Trojan Room coffee pot [6].

  3. For which company was Kevin Ashton presenting when he used the phrase "Internet of Things" in 1999?

    Answer

    Procter & Gamble [7].

  4. Which ITU-T Recommendation gives the formal "Overview of the Internet of things"?

    Answer

    Y.2060 (06/2012) [1].

  5. What is the default UDP port for CoAP, and for CoAP over DTLS?

    Answer

    5683 and 5684 [13].

  6. Which international standard number did MQTT 3.1.1 receive at ISO/IEC?

    Answer

    ISO/IEC 20922:2016 [14].

  7. What does an MQTT "Will" message do?

    Answer

    The broker publishes it on the client's behalf if the network connection closes without a normal DISCONNECT [16].

  8. What weakness let the 2016 Mirai botnet spread?

    Answer

    Devices exposing Telnet with factory-default or weak credentials [15].

  9. What is the first provision of ETSI EN 303 645?

    Answer

    No universal default passwords [17].

  10. Who is credited with coining the name "digital twin", and in what year?

    Answer

    John Vickers of NASA, in 2010, according to Grieves and Vickers [47].

  11. What are the four parts of the MAPE-K loop?

    Answer

    Monitor, Analyse, Plan, Execute, over shared Knowledge [60].

  12. How many levels of driving automation does SAE J3016 define?

    Answer

    Six, from Level 0 to Level 5 [34].

  13. At which well-known URI does a CoAP server list its resources?

    Answer

    /.well-known/core [55].

  14. Which IEEE standard defines the manufacturer-installed IDevID?

    Answer

    IEEE 802.1AR [51].
