1Why standards matter

IEEE defines interoperability as "the ability of two or more systems or components to exchange information and to use the information that has been exchanged" [1]. The definition has two halves. Exchanging bits is a radio and networking problem; using them is a data-model and semantics problem. The IoT needs both, across billions of devices made by thousands of vendors, which is why no single body owns IoT standardisation. ITU-T Y.2060 frames the IoT as an infrastructure built on "existing and evolving interoperable information and communication technologies" [2], and ISO/IEC 30141 provides a reference architecture against which different implementations can be compared [3].

The bodies in this article fall into three kinds:

  • Formal standards development organisations such as ISO, IEC, ITU and IEEE, whose output is often referenced in law.
  • Open internet and web bodies, the IETF and W3C, which publish freely available specifications through open participation [4,5].
  • Industry consortia and partnerships such as 3GPP, OASIS, the OPC Foundation, the Connectivity Standards Alliance (CSA), the Thread Group, the LoRa Alliance and oneM2M. Several of these later hand their work to formal bodies: MQTT 3.1.1 became ISO/IEC 20922 [6], OPC UA became IEC 62541 [7], LoRaWAN was approved as ITU-T Y.4480 [8], and oneM2M specifications are transposed by its partner SDOs [9].

2IEEE 802.15.4

IEEE 802.15.4 specifies the physical (PHY) and medium access control (MAC) layers for low-rate wireless personal area networks (LR-WPANs): short-range, low-power, low-data-rate links for battery-operated devices [10]. The current consolidated edition is IEEE Std 802.15.4-2024 [10]. It deliberately stops at layer 2, so many higher-layer stacks sit on top of it: Zigbee, Thread, Wi-SUN, WirelessHART (IEC 62591) and ISA100.11a (IEC 62734) [11–13], as well as IPv6 via 6LoWPAN [14].

2.1 Key concepts

  • Device types. A full-function device (FFD) can act as a PAN coordinator or router; a reduced-function device (RFD) talks only to an FFD and is meant for the simplest, sleepiest nodes [10].
  • Topologies. Star (all devices talk to a PAN coordinator) and peer-to-peer (FFDs talk to each other, enabling mesh and cluster-tree networks built by upper layers) [10].
  • Frames. The classic PHY carries at most 127 octets per frame (aMaxPhyPacketSize), which is why IPv6 over 802.15.4 needs header compression and fragmentation [10,14,15].
  • 2.4 GHz O-QPSK PHY. The most widely deployed PHY gives 250 kb/s on 16 channels (channels 11 to 26) worldwide; sub-GHz PHYs exist for 868 MHz and 915 MHz bands [10].
  • Channel access. Unslotted CSMA-CA in non-beacon networks; beacon-enabled networks add a superframe with guaranteed time slots. Time-Slotted Channel Hopping (TSCH), introduced by the 802.15.4e amendment and now part of the base standard, gives deterministic, frequency-hopping schedules for industrial use [10,16].
  • Other PHYs. The SUN (smart utility network) PHYs underpin Wi-SUN, and the 802.15.4z UWB amendment added secure ranging used for precise location and digital keys [17].
  • Security. The MAC provides AES-CCM* frame protection; key management is left to higher layers [10].

53GPP cellular IoT

3GPP unites seven organisational partners (ARIB, ATIS, CCSA, ETSI, TSDSI, TTA and TTC) to produce the specifications for 2G through 5G and beyond, in numbered Releases [40]. Release 13 introduced the two licensed low-power wide-area (LPWA) technologies used for IoT today: LTE-M (eMTC) and NB-IoT [41]. Both support power-saving mode (PSM) and extended discontinuous reception (eDRX), letting a device stay registered while its radio sleeps for long periods [41]. NB-IoT occupies a 180 kHz carrier and can be deployed standalone, in an LTE guard band or in-band [42].

For 5G, ITU-R defines three usage scenarios: enhanced mobile broadband, ultra-reliable low-latency communications (URLLC) and massive machine-type communications (mMTC) [43]. The IMT-2020 requirement for mMTC is a connection density of 1,000,000 devices per km² [44]. Later releases filled the gap between LPWA and full 5G:

  • Release 17 added NR RedCap (reduced capability) devices for wearables and industrial sensors, and non-terrestrial network (satellite) support including NB-IoT and LTE-M over satellite [45].
  • Release 19 began normative work on Ambient IoT: very low-cost devices that harvest energy and may operate without a battery [46].

Design rule of thumb. Choose LTE-M when you need mobility, voice or firmware updates of moderate size; choose NB-IoT for static devices sending small, infrequent messages in deep indoor coverage; choose RedCap when you need 5G features with lower complexity than full NR [41,45].

6OASIS MQTT

MQTT is a lightweight publish/subscribe protocol in which clients publish messages to hierarchical topics on a broker, which forwards them to subscribers [47]. Version 3.1.1 became an OASIS Standard in 2014 and was adopted as ISO/IEC 20922:2016 [6,48]; MQTT 5.0 became an OASIS Standard on 7 March 2019 [47]. IANA assigns TCP port 1883 for MQTT and 8883 for MQTT over TLS [47].

6.1 Core concepts

  • Topics and wildcards. Levels are separated by /; + matches one level and # matches any remaining levels and must be last [47].
  • Quality of service. QoS 0 at most once, QoS 1 at least once (PUBLISH/PUBACK), QoS 2 exactly once (four-packet handshake) [47].
  • Retained messages and Will. The broker keeps the last retained message per topic for new subscribers, and publishes a client's Will message if it disconnects abnormally [47].

6.2 What MQTT 5.0 added

MQTT 5.0 added reason codes on every acknowledgement, user properties (key–value metadata), shared subscriptions ($share/<group>/<filter>) for load balancing, message and session expiry intervals, topic aliases, request/response via Response Topic and Correlation Data, and flow control via Receive Maximum [47]. MQTT itself says nothing about payload format; the Eclipse Sparkplug specification, standardised as ISO/IEC 20237:2023, adds a topic namespace, Protobuf payloads and state management for industrial use [49]. MQTT-SN adapts MQTT for non-TCP sensor networks [50].

7OPC Foundation standards (OPC UA)

The OPC Foundation's original "OPC Classic" specifications (such as Data Access) relied on Microsoft COM/DCOM. OPC Unified Architecture (OPC UA) replaced that dependency with a platform-independent, service-oriented architecture that combines transport, security and a rich information model [51,52]. OPC UA is published by the Foundation as the OPC 10000 series and internationally as IEC 62541 [7,52].

  • Address space. Everything is a node with a NodeId, belonging to one of eight NodeClasses (Object, Variable, Method, ObjectType, VariableType, ReferenceType, DataType, View), connected by typed references [53].
  • Information models and companion specifications. Domain groups define standard types (for example for machine tools or robotics) so that a client can understand any compliant server [52].
  • Communication patterns. Client/server over the binary opc.tcp protocol (registered port 4840) or HTTPS, and PubSub over UDP multicast or brokers such as MQTT [54,55].
  • Security. Application instance certificates (X.509), secure channels with signing and encryption, and user authentication are built into the protocol [56].
  • OPC UA FX (Field eXchange) extends OPC UA to controller-to-controller and field-level communication, including deterministic transport over TSN [57].

IEC 62443 is the companion security standard series for industrial automation and control systems in which OPC UA is typically deployed [58].

8Matter

Matter is an application-layer smart-home standard from the Connectivity Standards Alliance. It runs over IPv6 on Thread, Wi-Fi and Ethernet, and uses Bluetooth LE for commissioning [23]. The current release at the time of writing is Matter 1.6, announced on 17 June 2026, which added NFC-based setup and better multi-ecosystem management [59]; Matter 1.5 had added cameras, with refinements in 1.5.1 (March 2026) [60].

8.1 Data model

A Matter node contains numbered endpoints. Endpoint 0 is the root node, holding utility clusters such as Descriptor, Basic Information, Access Control, General Commissioning and Operational Credentials. Each application endpoint has a device type (for example On/Off Light) that mandates a set of clusters; each cluster defines attributes, commands and events [23]. Many clusters descend from the Zigbee Cluster Library, which is why the On/Off cluster keeps its Zigbee ID 0x0006 [23].

8.2 Interaction model and security

Clients act on the data model through four interactions: Read, Write, Invoke (commands) and Subscribe [23]. Commissioning uses a setup passcode with the PASE protocol (based on SPAKE2+), verifies the device's Device Attestation Certificate chain back to a CSA-approved root, then installs an operational certificate. Afterwards, nodes authenticate with CASE (a certificate-based SIGMA exchange). A set of nodes sharing a root of trust is a fabric; one device can join several fabrics, which is how a single bulb is controlled from more than one ecosystem ("multi-admin") [23]. The open-source connectedhomeip SDK is the reference implementation [61].

9Thread

Thread is an IPv6 mesh networking protocol from the Thread Group, built on the IEEE 802.15.4 2.4 GHz PHY/MAC with 6LoWPAN adaptation [13,15]. It has no single point of failure, self-heals, and provides only networking, which is why application layers such as Matter run on top of it [13,23].

  • Roles. Routers forward packets; one router is dynamically elected Leader to manage router IDs. Router-eligible end devices (REEDs) can be promoted. End devices attach to a parent router, and sleepy end devices poll their parent for buffered messages. A partition supports up to 32 active routers [13].
  • Border routers connect the Thread mesh to Wi-Fi/Ethernet IP networks and provide service discovery across them [13].
  • Thread 1.4 (2024) tightened border-router requirements and standardised how ecosystems share network credentials, so that border routers from different vendors join one network instead of creating fragmented ones [62].

OpenThread is an open-source implementation used in many commercial products [63].

10LoRa Alliance (LoRaWAN)

LoRaWAN is a MAC-layer protocol for low-power wide-area networks maintained by the LoRa Alliance; it typically runs over the LoRa chirp-spread-spectrum PHY in licence-exempt sub-GHz bands [26,64]. In 2021 ITU-T approved LoRaWAN as Recommendation Y.4480 [8].

  • Architecture. A "star of stars": end devices transmit to any gateway in range; gateways forward packets to a network server, which de-duplicates them and passes application payloads to application servers; a join server handles keys [64].
  • Device classes. Class A (all devices): two short receive windows after each uplink. Class B: additional scheduled receive slots synchronised by gateway beacons. Class C: continuously listening except while transmitting [64].
  • Activation. Over-the-air activation (OTAA) derives session keys through a join procedure; activation by personalisation (ABP) uses pre-provisioned session keys [64].
  • Regional parameters. Channel plans, data rates and limits differ per region and are published separately (RP002) [65]. In Europe's 868 MHz band, regulatory duty-cycle limits such as 1 % apply to many sub-bands [65,66].

11oneM2M

oneM2M is a global partnership project founded in 2012 by regional standards bodies, including ETSI, ARIB, TTC, ATIS, TIA, CCSA and TTA, later joined by India's TSDSI, to specify a common, access-independent M2M/IoT service layer [9]. Its specifications are published in releases; Release 5 is the latest published set [67].

11.1 Architecture

The functional architecture (TS-0001) has three entity types: Application Entities (AE), Common Services Entities (CSE) that provide common service functions such as registration, data management, subscription/notification, security and device management, and Underlying Network Service Entities (NSE). They meet at reference points Mca (AE–CSE), Mcc (CSE–CSE) and Mcn (CSE–NSE). CSEs are deployed in infrastructure nodes (IN), middle nodes (MN, typically gateways) and application service nodes (ASN, devices) [68].

11.2 Resource-oriented API

Every entity is represented as a resource in a tree rooted at CSEBase; an AE registers as an AE resource, stores data in container resources holding contentInstance children, and receives notifications through subscription resources. Requests use CRUD+N (Create, Retrieve, Update, Delete, Notify) primitives that are bound to HTTP, CoAP, MQTT or WebSocket [69,70]. A full HTTP example is in §20.2.

12W3C Web of Things

The W3C Web of Things (WoT) takes the opposite approach from a new protocol: it describes existing devices and protocols in a common, machine-readable way [4]. The central building block is the Thing Description (TD), a JSON-LD document that lists a Thing's interaction affordances (properties, actions and events), the forms that say how to reach each one over a protocol such as HTTP, CoAP or MQTT, and its security schemes [71]. WoT Architecture 1.1, Thing Description 1.1 and WoT Discovery became W3C Recommendations on 5 December 2023 [4,71,72]. Work on Thing Description 2.0 is in progress; its First Public Working Draft appeared on 4 November 2025 [73].

Because the TD can carry semantic annotations via @context, it can reuse ontologies such as W3C SOSA/SSN or ETSI SAREF [71,74,75]. Eclipse Thingweb node-wot implements the WoT Scripting API for producing and consuming Things [76]. A complete TD is in §20.1.

14Interoperability

14.1 Levels and facets

ETSI distinguishes four levels of interoperability: technical (signals and protocols connect), syntactic (data formats and encodings are shared), semantic (the meaning of the data is shared) and organisational (business processes, policies and responsibilities align) [87]. ISO/IEC 21823-1 uses five facets for IoT systems: transport, syntactic, semantic, behavioural and policy [79]. The European Interoperability Framework adds a legal layer above them, which in the IoT now includes data-access and cybersecurity law [88].

14.2 Strategies

Common protocol

Everyone implements the same stack end to end, as in Matter. Highest out-of-the-box interoperability, but legacy devices need bridges [23].

Common service layer

Heterogeneous devices plug into a horizontal middleware with one API, as in oneM2M [68].

Common description

Leave protocols alone and describe them in machine-readable form, as in the W3C WoT Thing Description [71].

Common information model

Agree on types and semantics for a domain, as in OPC UA companion specifications or SAREF [52,75].

Certification turns paper interoperability into tested interoperability: Matter devices must pass CSA certification and appear in the Alliance's distributed compliance ledger before ecosystems will trust their attestation certificates [23].

15Data ownership

Most legal systems do not recognise "ownership" of raw data as a property right. Instead, law allocates rights of access, use and control. The EU Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) is the clearest example for IoT, and it has applied since 12 September 2025 [89]. Its key provisions for connected products are:

  • Access by design (Art. 3(1)). Connected products and related services must be designed so that product data and related-service data are, where relevant and technically feasible, directly accessible to the user. This applies to products placed on the EU market after 12 September 2026 [89].
  • Pre-contract transparency (Art. 3(2)–(3)). Before purchase, the user must be told what data the product can generate, in what format and volume, and how to access it [89].
  • User access and sharing (Arts. 4–5). Where data are not directly accessible, the data holder must make them available to the user, and on the user's request to a third party, without undue delay; designated gatekeepers are not eligible third parties [89].
  • Limits on the data holder (Art. 4(13)). The data holder may use readily available non-personal data only on the basis of a contract with the user [89].
  • Trade secrets are protected through confidentiality measures, and in exceptional cases disclosure may be refused [89].
  • Database right. The sui generis database right does not apply to databases containing data obtained from or generated by a connected product (Art. 43) [89].

Where data relate to an identifiable person, GDPR applies in parallel and prevails in case of conflict [89,90]. The Data Act also requires cloud and edge providers to remove obstacles to switching, which matters for IoT platforms [89].

16Data privacy

IoT data are often personal data even when they look technical: a smart-meter load curve or a car's location trace relates to an identifiable person. EU data protection authorities said so explicitly in their 2014 opinion on the IoT, and also noted that accessing data stored on a user's device engages Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive [91,92].

16.2 Beyond the EU

California's Consumer Privacy Act (as amended by the CPRA) grants rights to know, delete and opt out of sale or sharing [95], and California's SB-327, operative since 1 January 2020, requires connected devices to have "reasonable security features", including unique preset passwords or forced password change on first use [96]. The NIST Privacy Framework offers a voluntary, risk-based way to organise privacy engineering [97], and ISO/IEC 27400 gives IoT-specific privacy and security controls [84].

17Data retention

GDPR's storage limitation principle requires that personal data be kept in identifiable form no longer than necessary for the purposes of processing (Art. 5(1)(e)), and the right to erasure (Art. 17) requires deletion on valid request [90]. Retention therefore has to be designed, not just configured:

  1. Define a schedule per data class. Raw telemetry, aggregates, event logs, security logs and account data usually need different periods tied to a documented purpose.
  2. Aggregate or anonymise early. Fine-grained readings can often be replaced by hourly or daily aggregates, reducing both privacy risk and storage cost.
  3. Delete everywhere. Include device flash, gateways, backups and analytics copies.
  4. Support user-initiated deletion and secure decommissioning. ETSI EN 303 645 provision 5.11 asks that users can easily delete their data from devices and services [98], and the CRA requires products to let users securely and easily remove all data and settings permanently [99].
  5. Sanitise media at end of life. NIST SP 800-88 defines clear, purge and destroy levels of sanitisation [100].

Retention also has minimums. Security logs may need to be kept long enough to investigate incidents and meet CRA reporting duties (§18.1) [101]; sector rules such as medical-device regulation impose their own record-keeping [102]. A worked retention-enforcement script is in §20.6.

18Regulatory compliance

The Mirai botnet of 2016, which spread by logging into devices with default credentials, was a turning point: it showed that insecure consumer IoT harms everyone, not only the device owner [103]. Since then, voluntary baselines such as ETSI EN 303 645 and NISTIR 8259 have been turned into law [98,104].

18.1 European Union

Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), Regulation (EU) 2024/2847. The CRA sets horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements, covering almost all connected hardware and software placed on the EU market [99]. It entered into force on 10 December 2024 and applies in stages: provisions on conformity assessment bodies from 11 June 2026, manufacturers' reporting obligations from 11 September 2026, and all other obligations from 11 December 2027 [99].

  • Essential requirements (Annex I) include secure-by-default configuration, protection against unauthorised access, confidentiality and integrity of data, data minimisation, attack-surface reduction, secure deletion, and vulnerability handling with a software bill of materials (SBOM) and security updates [99].
  • Support period. Manufacturers must determine a support period reflecting expected use, of at least five years unless the product is expected to be used for less [99].
  • Classes. Most products use self-assessment; "important" (Class I and II) and "critical" products face stricter conformity assessment [99].
  • Reporting, live since 11 September 2026. Actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents must be reported through ENISA's Single Reporting Platform: an early warning within 24 hours, a notification within 72 hours, and a final report within 14 days after a corrective measure is available (vulnerabilities) or within one month (incidents) [101].
  • Penalties for breaching essential requirements reach €15 million or 2.5 % of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher [99].

Radio Equipment Directive (RED). Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/30 activated the RED's cybersecurity, privacy and anti-fraud essential requirements for internet-connected radio equipment; it has applied since 1 August 2025 [105]. The harmonised standards EN 18031-1, -2 and -3 were cited in the Official Journal in January 2025 [106]. The CRA is designed to take over this role once fully applicable [99].

Other EU instruments. The Data Act (§15) [89]; NIS2 for operators of essential and important entities [107]; the AI Act where IoT systems embed AI, for example as safety components [108]; and the product-safety regulations in §19.

18.2 United Kingdom

Part 1 of the Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure (PSTI) Act 2022 created a product security regime for consumer connectable products [109]. Its regulations, in force since 29 April 2024, impose three requirements: no universal default or easily guessable passwords, a published way to report security issues, and a published minimum period for security updates [110]. These mirror the first three provisions of ETSI EN 303 645 [98].

18.3 United States

IoT Cybersecurity Improvement Act of 2020 (Pub. L. 116-207) requires NIST to issue standards and guidelines for IoT devices owned or controlled by federal agencies, and bars agencies from procuring devices that do not comply, subject to waivers [111]. NIST implemented it through SP 800-213 and the NISTIR 8259 series [104,112].

U.S. Cyber Trust Mark. In March 2024 the FCC adopted a voluntary cybersecurity labelling programme for consumer wireless IoT products, built on NIST's consumer IoT criteria (NIST IR 8425) and accredited Cybersecurity Label Administrators [113,114]. The programme has been in flux: UL Solutions withdrew as Lead Administrator effective 19 December 2025 [115,116], and in January 2026 the FCC opened new filing windows for Lead Administrator and Label Administrator applications [115]. At the time of writing, manufacturers should check the FCC's current public notices before planning to use the mark.

State law adds to this, notably California SB-327 [96].

18.4 A compliance workflow

  1. Inventory markets and product categories; map each to applicable law (CRA/RED, PSTI, FCC rules, sector rules).
  2. Pick a harmonised or recognised baseline (EN 18031, ETSI EN 303 645, NIST IR 8425, ISO/IEC 27402) and perform a gap analysis [85,98,106,114].
  3. Run threat modelling and a cybersecurity risk assessment; document it in the technical file [99].
  4. Build vulnerability handling: a coordinated disclosure policy, SBOM, signed updates (for example per RFC 9019) and the 24/72-hour reporting process [35,99,101].
  5. Do privacy work in parallel: lawful basis, DPIA, retention schedule, data-access interfaces for the Data Act [89,90].
  6. Choose the conformity route, issue the declaration of conformity and affix the CE marking where required [99].

19Safety and reliability

Dependability is an umbrella for availability, reliability, safety, integrity and maintainability; Avizienis and colleagues' taxonomy separates faults, errors and failures and the means to prevent, tolerate, remove and forecast them [118]. 19.1 Reliability patterns for IoT

  • Fail-safe local control. A thermostat or valve must reach a safe state if the cloud or network is unavailable; Matter, Thread and OPC UA all support local operation without internet [13,23,52].
  • Redundancy and self-healing networks. Thread and RPL meshes reroute around failed nodes [13,27]; several LoRaWAN gateways can receive the same uplink [64].
  • Delivery guarantees. Choose MQTT QoS or CoAP confirmable messages according to the consequence of loss [5,47].
  • Watchdogs, health reporting and Will messages to detect silent failure [47].
  • Robust update. A/B partitions, signed manifests and rollback protection per the SUIT architecture [35].
  • Determinism where needed. TSCH and OPC UA FX over TSN for time-critical industrial control [16,57].

Reliability is quantified with metrics such as mean time between failures (MTBF) and availability A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). For a safety function in high-demand mode, IEC 61508 maps SIL 3 to an average frequency of dangerous failure between 10−8 and 10−7 per hour [119].

    for form in affordance["forms"]:
        ops = form.get("op", [])
        ops = [ops] if isinstance(ops, str) else ops

"""Minimal oneM2M HTTP client (works against an open-source CSE such as ACME)."""
import json, uuid, urllib.request

CSE = "http://localhost:8080/cse-in"
ORIGIN = "Cthermo01"

def m2m(method, path, ty=None, body=None):
    headers = {"X-M2M-Origin": ORIGIN, "X-M2M-RI": uuid.uuid4().hex,
               "X-M2M-RVI": "3", "Accept": "application/json"}
    data = None
    if body is not None:
        headers["Content-Type"] = f"application/json;ty={ty}"
        data = json.dumps(body).encode()
    req = urllib.request.Request(CSE + path, data=data, headers=headers, method=method)
    with urllib.request.urlopen(req, timeout=5) as r:
        return r.headers.get("X-M2M-RSC"), json.load(r)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(m2m("POST", "", 2, {"m2m:ae": {"rn": "thermo01", "api": "Nthermo.example.com",
                                         "rr": False, "srv": ["3"]}}))
    print(m2m("POST", "/thermo01", 3, {"m2m:cnt": {"rn": "temperature", "mni": 100}}))
    print(m2m("POST", "/thermo01/temperature", 4,
              {"m2m:cin": {"cnf": "application/json:0", "con": json.dumps({"t": 21.7})}}))
    print(m2m("GET", "/thermo01/temperature/la"))

20.4 MQTT 5.0 features in Python

User properties, message expiry, a Will message, session expiry, request/response and a shared subscription, using Eclipse Paho 2.x against a local Mosquitto broker [47,125]. The payload is SenML JSON [30].

mqtt5.py
"""MQTT 5.0 features with Eclipse Paho (paho-mqtt >= 2.0):
user properties, message expiry, request/response and shared subscriptions."""
import json, time, uuid
import paho.mqtt.client as mqtt
from paho.mqtt.properties import Properties
from paho.mqtt.packettypes import PacketTypes

BROKER = "localhost"   # e.g. a local Eclipse Mosquitto broker
CLIENT_ID = f"gw-{uuid.uuid4().hex[:6]}"
REPLY_TOPIC = f"site1/replies/{CLIENT_ID}"

def on_connect(client, userdata, flags, reason_code, properties):
    print("connected:", reason_code)            # MQTT 5 reason code, e.g. Success
    # Shared subscription: brokers spread messages across all clients in "workers"
    client.subscribe("$share/workers/site1/+/thermostat/temperature", qos=1)
    client.subscribe(REPLY_TOPIC, qos=1)

def on_message(client, userdata, msg):
    props = msg.properties
    meta = dict(getattr(props, "UserProperty", []) or [])
    corr = getattr(props, "CorrelationData", None)
    print(f"{msg.topic}: {msg.payload.decode()} meta={meta} corr={corr}")

client = mqtt.Client(mqtt.CallbackAPIVersion.VERSION2,
                     client_id=CLIENT_ID, protocol=mqtt.MQTTv5)
client.on_connect, client.on_message = on_connect, on_message

will = Properties(PacketTypes.WILLMESSAGE)
will.MessageExpiryInterval = 3600
client.will_set("site1/gw/status", "offline", qos=1, retain=True, properties=will)

conn = Properties(PacketTypes.CONNECT)
conn.SessionExpiryInterval = 300               # keep session 5 min after disconnect
client.connect(BROKER, 1883, keepalive=30, properties=conn)
client.loop_start()
time.sleep(1)                                  # let on_connect() subscribe first

# Publish a reading that expires after 60 s and carries metadata
pub = Properties(PacketTypes.PUBLISH)
pub.MessageExpiryInterval = 60
pub.UserProperty = [("schema", "senml+json"), ("site", "1")]
pub.ContentType = "application/senml+json"
reading = [{"bn": "urn:dev:thermo01:", "n": "temp", "u": "Cel", "v": 21.7}]
client.publish("site1/office/thermostat/temperature", json.dumps(reading), qos=1, properties=pub)

# Request/response: ask a device for its firmware version
req = Properties(PacketTypes.PUBLISH)
req.ResponseTopic = REPLY_TOPIC
req.CorrelationData = uuid.uuid4().bytes
client.publish("site1/office/thermostat/cmd/get-version", "", qos=1, properties=req)

time.sleep(5)
client.loop_stop(); client.disconnect()

Gotcha we hit while testing. Because each run uses a new client ID and a 300-second session expiry, the broker keeps earlier runs' sessions as members of the $share/workers group. Messages can then be delivered to those offline sessions instead of the live client. This is correct MQTT 5.0 behaviour for shared subscriptions and persistent sessions [47]; use stable client IDs for workers.

20.6 Enforcing a retention schedule

A governance control as code: raw telemetry is rolled into hourly aggregates before it expires, then each data class is deleted according to a documented schedule, implementing storage limitation and erasure [90].

retention.py
"""Enforce a documented retention schedule (GDPR Art. 5(1)(e)) on an IoT store:
roll raw telemetry into hourly aggregates, then delete expired rows."""
import sqlite3, time

SCHEDULE = {            # data class -> retention in days (document the purpose!)
    "raw_telemetry": 30,
    "hourly_aggregate": 730,
    "security_log": 400,
}
DAY = 86_400

def enforce(db, now=None):
    now = now or time.time()
    cur = db.cursor()
    cutoff_raw = now - SCHEDULE["raw_telemetry"] * DAY
    # 1) aggregate raw readings that are about to expire (minimisation)
    cur.execute("""INSERT OR IGNORE INTO hourly_aggregate(device, hour, n, mean, mn, mx)
                   SELECT device, CAST(ts/3600 AS INT)*3600, COUNT(*), AVG(v), MIN(v), MAX(v)
                   FROM raw_telemetry WHERE ts < ? GROUP BY device, CAST(ts/3600 AS INT)""",
                (cutoff_raw,))
    # 2) delete per schedule
    deleted = {}
    for table, col in (("raw_telemetry", "ts"), ("hourly_aggregate", "hour"),
                       ("security_log", "ts")):
        cutoff = now - SCHEDULE[table] * DAY
        cur.execute(f"DELETE FROM {table} WHERE {col} < ?", (cutoff,))
        deleted[table] = cur.rowcount
    db.commit()
    return deleted

def erase_subject(db, device):
    """Right to erasure (GDPR Art. 17) for a device linked to one person."""
    for table in ("raw_telemetry", "hourly_aggregate"):
        db.execute(f"DELETE FROM {table} WHERE device = ?", (device,))
    db.commit()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
    db.executescript("""
      CREATE TABLE raw_telemetry(device TEXT, ts REAL, v REAL);
      CREATE TABLE hourly_aggregate(device TEXT, hour INT, n INT, mean REAL, mn REAL, mx REAL,
                                    PRIMARY KEY(device, hour));
      CREATE TABLE security_log(ts REAL, event TEXT);""")
    t0 = time.time() - 40 * DAY
    db.executemany("INSERT INTO raw_telemetry VALUES(?,?,?)",
                   [("thermo01", t0 + i * 600, 20 + (i % 6) * 0.1) for i in range(12)])
    print(enforce(db))
    print(db.execute("SELECT * FROM hourly_aggregate").fetchall())

22Exercises with solutions

Exercise 1. An IPv6 packet with a 40-byte header must cross an IEEE 802.15.4 link. Explain why plain IPv6 is impractical there and name the two RFCs that fix it.

Solution

The classic 802.15.4 PHY frame is at most 127 octets, and MAC headers and security overhead consume a large part of that, leaving little for a 40-byte IPv6 header plus UDP and payload. IPv6 also requires links to carry 1280-byte packets. RFC 4944 defines 6LoWPAN fragmentation and encapsulation, and RFC 6282 defines the improved IPHC header compression [10,14,15].

Exercise 2. A water utility wants meters in basements that send 50 bytes once a day for 10 years on a battery, in an area with an existing LTE network. Compare NB-IoT and LoRaWAN for this case.

Solution

Both suit small, infrequent uplinks. NB-IoT uses licensed spectrum and the operator's network (no gateways to deploy, subscription per device), supports PSM/eDRX for long sleep, and was designed for deep indoor coverage [41,42]. LoRaWAN uses licence-exempt bands with gateways the utility may own, avoiding per-device fees but subject to duty-cycle limits and interference [64,66]. With LTE coverage already present, NB-IoT avoids building infrastructure; LoRaWAN wins where the utility wants full control of the network.

Exercise 3. Write an MQTT 5.0 subscription that load-balances temperature readings from all rooms of site 1 across a pool of three workers.

Solution

Each worker subscribes to $share/workers/site1/+/thermostat/temperature. The broker delivers each matching message to one member of the share group "workers" [47].

Exercise 4. In §20.4, why might a message never reach the running client, and how do you fix it?

Solution

Previous runs with different client IDs left sessions with a 300 s session expiry that are still members of the share group; the broker may deliver to them. Use a stable client ID per worker, set session expiry to 0 for ephemeral clients, or unsubscribe before disconnecting [47].

Exercise 7. A smart plug exposes endpoint 1 with device type On/Off Plug-in Unit. Which cluster must it contain to be switched, and which interaction turns it on?

Solution

The On/Off cluster (0x0006); the controller uses an Invoke interaction with the On command (or Toggle) on endpoint 1 [23].

Exercise 8. A manufacturer will sell a Wi-Fi camera in the EU from March 2027 and in the UK. List the cybersecurity laws that apply and one concrete obligation from each.

Solution

EU: RED Delegated Regulation 2022/30 (for example, conformity with EN 18031-1, and -2 for personal data) [105,106]; CRA reporting obligations, which already apply, so actively exploited vulnerabilities must be reported to ENISA within 24 hours, with full CRA essential requirements from 11 December 2027 [99,101]; the Data Act, so the camera's data must be accessible to the user by design [89]; and GDPR because video identifies people [90]. UK: PSTI, so no universal default passwords, a published vulnerability-reporting contact and a stated minimum update period [110].

Exercise 9. Your fitness wearable stores heart-rate samples every second. Propose a GDPR-compliant retention schedule and say which principle each step implements.

Solution

Example: raw per-second samples kept on the phone 7 days for live features; minute-level aggregates in the cloud for 2 years for trends; delete everything 30 days after account closure; honour erasure requests immediately. These implement storage limitation and minimisation (Art. 5(1)(c),(e)), erasure (Art. 17) and data protection by default (Art. 25). Health data is special-category data, so Art. 9 also requires an explicit condition such as explicit consent [90].

Exercise 10. A tractor manufacturer refuses to give a farmer's chosen repair shop the machine's operating data. What does the Data Act allow the farmer to do?

Solution

The farmer, as user, can request that the data holder make readily available product data available to a third party of the farmer's choice (Art. 5), under fair terms and without undue delay, subject to trade-secret safeguards [89].

Exercise 11. A valve controller has MTBF 50,000 h and MTTR 10 h. What is its availability, and how many hours of downtime per year does that imply?

Solution

A = 50,000 / 50,010 ≈ 0.99980 (99.98 %). Downtime ≈ (1 − A) × 8,760 h ≈ 1.75 h per year. Availability is one attribute of dependability in the Avizienis taxonomy [118].

Exercise 12. Classify each problem by interoperability level: (a) two devices use different Thread credentials and form separate networks; (b) one sends temperature in Fahrenheit labelled "temp", another expects Celsius; (c) a partner is not contractually allowed to receive the data.

Solution

(a) Technical/transport, addressed by Thread 1.4's credential sharing [62]. (b) Semantic, addressed by explicit units and ontologies such as SAREF in a TD [71,75]. (c) Organisational/policy or legal [79,88].
