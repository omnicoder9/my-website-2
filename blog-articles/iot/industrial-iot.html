1What makes IoT "industrial"

The Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) applies IoT technologies (networked sensing, actuation, connectivity and analytics) to industrial processes such as manufacturing, process plants, utilities, logistics and buildings. Boyes and colleagues define it as a system of networked smart objects, cyber-physical assets, generic information technologies and optional cloud or edge platforms that enable real-time, intelligent and autonomous access, collection, analysis, communication and exchange of process, product and service information within the industrial environment, to optimise overall production value [1]. What sets it apart from consumer IoT is less the technology than the consequences: industrial systems control physical processes where failures can cause injury, environmental damage or large financial loss, and they must meet hard timing, availability and lifetime requirements that consumer devices rarely face [2,3].

Operational technology (OT)

Programmable systems that interact with the physical environment, such as PLCs, SCADA, DCS and safety systems. Priorities: safety, availability, deterministic timing; asset lifetimes of decades [3].

Information technology (IT)

Business systems such as ERP, email and databases. Priorities: confidentiality and data integrity; refresh cycles of a few years. IIoT is largely the story of IT/OT convergence [3,4].

Germany's Industrie 4.0 initiative framed this convergence as a fourth industrial revolution, built on cyber-physical production systems that link machines, storage systems and products so they can exchange information, trigger actions and control each other independently [5]. Survey literature describes IIoT as the enabling infrastructure for that vision [2,4].

1.1 The automation hierarchy: ISA-95 and the Purdue model

Industrial systems are usually described in levels. IEC 62264-1, the international version of ANSI/ISA-95, defines a functional hierarchy: Level 0 is the physical process; Level 1 is sensing and manipulating it; Level 2 is monitoring, supervisory and automated control; Level 3 is manufacturing operations management (dispatching, detailed scheduling, quality, maintenance; the domain of manufacturing execution systems, MES); and Level 4 is business planning and logistics (ERP) [6]. The model descends from the Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture developed at Purdue University in the early 1990s [7]. Security guidance such as NIST SP 800-82 uses the same layering to separate an enterprise zone from a manufacturing zone, with an industrial demilitarised zone (DMZ) between them [3].

Why the pyramid is flattening. IIoT architectures increasingly let field devices publish data directly to edge or cloud services instead of passing everything up level by level. Reference models such as RAMI 4.0 and the Asset Administration Shell were created to give every asset a standard digital representation that any level can query [8,9]. The ISA-95 levels remain essential for describing functions and security zones even when the data paths cross them [3,6].

2Industrial automation

2.1 Programmable logic controllers (PLCs)

IEC 61131-1 defines a programmable controller as a digitally operating electronic system designed for use in an industrial environment, which uses a programmable memory for internal storage of user-oriented instructions to implement specific functions such as logic, sequencing, timing, counting and arithmetic, to control machines or processes through digital or analogue inputs and outputs [10].

History

In 1968 the Hydra-Matic transmission division of General Motors circulated requirements for a solid-state replacement for the hard-wired relay panels that had to be rewired at every model change. Dick Morley of Bedford Associates had already outlined such a controller in a memo dated 1 January 1968; the resulting product was the Modicon 084 (Bedford's 84th project; "Modicon" stands for MOdular DIgital CONtroller), and the first 084 was delivered in November 1969 [11]. Ladder logic, the most famous PLC language, deliberately mimics relay schematics so that electricians could program the new machines [11,12].

The scan cycle

A PLC executes its program cyclically. Each cycle reads all inputs into an input image, executes the user program against that frozen image, writes the output image to the physical outputs, then performs communication and housekeeping [10]. The worst-case reaction time to an input change is therefore roughly two scan times, which is why cycle time is a key specification.

IEC 61131-3 programming languages

IEC 61131-3 standardises the software model (configurations, resources, tasks, programs, function blocks and functions) and the programming languages [12]. The third edition (2013) defined two textual languages, Instruction List (IL) and Structured Text (ST), and graphical Ladder Diagram (LD) and Function Block Diagram (FBD), plus Sequential Function Chart (SFC) elements for structuring sequences; it also added object-oriented features and marked IL as deprecated [12]. The fourth edition, published in May 2025, adds UTF-8 strings and includes an annex listing features added, removed or deprecated relative to 2013; its scope names ST, LD, FBD and SFC [13].

2.2 Supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA)

NIST SP 800-82 describes SCADA systems as systems used to control dispersed assets where centralised data acquisition is as important as control, for example water distribution, wastewater collection, oil and gas pipelines, electric power transmission and rail transport [3]. A SCADA system comprises a control server (historically the master terminal unit), HMI workstations, a data historian, communication links (radio, cellular, leased line, fibre) and field devices: remote terminal units (RTUs), PLCs and intelligent electronic devices (IEDs) that execute local control [3]. Telecontrol protocols designed for these links include DNP3 (IEEE 1815) and IEC 60870-5-104, alongside Modbus [15–17].

2.3 Distributed control systems (DCS)

A DCS controls a production system within a single location, such as a refinery, chemical plant or power station, using multiple integrated controllers under a supervisory level, with tight integration of engineering, operation and historian functions in one vendor environment [3]. Honeywell describes its TDC 2000, introduced in 1975, as the first DCS, while noting that other companies also released DCS products that year [18]. Modern DCSs increasingly incorporate PLC-like controllers and open interfaces such as OPC UA, so the boundary between "DCS" and "PLC plus SCADA" is now one of emphasis rather than technology [3,19].

2.4 Human-machine interfaces (HMI)

The HMI is the software and hardware through which operators monitor and command the process. ANSI/ISA-101.01-2015 sets out a lifecycle for HMI design and recommends a hierarchy of displays from plant-wide overviews down to detailed diagnostic views, with consistent use of colour so that abnormal situations stand out [20]. Alarms are managed separately under ANSI/ISA-18.2 and IEC 62682, which define an alarm lifecycle (philosophy, identification, rationalisation, design, implementation, operation, maintenance, monitoring and assessment) and benchmark alarm rates; ISA-18.2 treats about 150 alarms per operator per day as very likely to be acceptable and treats an alarm flood as more than 10 alarms in 10 minutes [21,22].

2.5 Industrial robots

ISO 8373 defines an industrial robot as an automatically controlled, reprogrammable, multipurpose manipulator, programmable in three or more axes, which can be either fixed in place or fixed to a mobile platform for use in automation applications in an industrial environment [23]. Common kinematic types are articulated arms, SCARA, Cartesian (gantry), parallel (delta) and cylindrical robots [23]. The first Unimate arm, invented by George Devol and commercialised by Unimation, went to a General Motors plant in Ewing Township, New Jersey, where it handled hot die-cast metal [24].

According to the International Federation of Robotics, 542,000 industrial robots were installed worldwide in 2024, and the operational stock reached 4,664,000 units [25]. Safety requirements are set by ISO 10218-1 (robots) and ISO 10218-2 (robot systems and integration) [26]. Collaborative operation, where people and robots share a workspace, is covered by ISO/TS 15066, which recognises safety-rated monitored stop, hand guiding, speed and separation monitoring, and power and force limiting [27]. In IIoT systems, robots are increasingly integrated through OPC UA companion specifications and open middleware such as ROS 2, which uses DDS for publish/subscribe communication [19,28].

2.6 Automated manufacturing

Automation in manufacturing ranges from fixed automation (dedicated transfer lines for one product), through programmable automation (batches with reprogramming between them, as with CNC machines and PLC-controlled lines), to flexible automation that switches between product variants with negligible changeover [29]. Industrie 4.0 adds cyber-physical production systems in which products, machines and logistics exchange information autonomously [5,29]. Lee, Bagheri and Kao proposed a widely cited five-level "5C" architecture for such systems: smart connection, data-to-information conversion, cyber, cognition and configuration [30]. Machine-tool data are often collected with MTConnect, a read-only, HTTP/XML vocabulary for CNC and manufacturing equipment, or with OPC UA [19,31].

Signals: 4–20 mA, HART, IO-Link, wireless

The 4–20 mA current loop, standardised in IEC 60381-1, remains the workhorse analogue signal [36]. Because the signal range starts at a "live zero" of 4 mA, a broken wire (0 mA) is distinguishable from a zero reading. NAMUR recommendation NE 43 builds on this by reserving levels at or below 3.6 mA and at or above 21 mA to signal a transmitter failure [37]. HART superimposes digital communication on the analogue loop, and WirelessHART (IEC 62591) extends it to a wireless mesh [38]. IO-Link (IEC 61131-9) is a point-to-point digital link that carries process data, parameters and diagnostics between a master and individual smart sensors or actuators [34].

2.8 Actuators and control systems

Actuators turn controller outputs into physical action: electric motors driven by variable-speed drives, servo drives for motion control, pneumatic and hydraulic cylinders, solenoid valves and modulating control valves. Drive behaviour on networks is standardised through drive profiles such as CiA 402 and PROFIdrive, collected under IEC 61800-7 [39,40].

Most regulatory control is feedback control. The controller compares a measured process variable (PV) with a set-point (SP) and adjusts the manipulated variable to drive the error to zero. The proportional-integral-derivative (PID) algorithm dominates industrial practice [41]:

u(t) = Kp · e(t) + Ki ∫ e(τ) dτ + Kd · de(t)/dt,   e = SP − PV

Ziegler and Nichols' 1942 tuning rules remain a common starting point: with the ultimate gain Ku (where the loop oscillates steadily) and its oscillation period Tu, a PID controller is set to Kp = 0.6 Ku, Ti = Tu/2 and Td = Tu/8 [41,42]. Åström and Hägglund note that these rules give rather aggressive, poorly damped responses, and recommend more robust methods [41].

3Industrial communication

Industrial networks evolved from point-to-point wiring, through serial fieldbuses in the 1980s and 1990s, to real-time Ethernet today. The fieldbus "wars" of the 1990s ended not with one winner but with a compromise: IEC 61158 specifies many protocol types (communication profile families), and IEC 61784 defines the profiles that select from them [43,44]. Real-time Ethernet variants such as PROFINET, EtherNet/IP and EtherCAT are all profiles within this framework [44]. Above the field level, OPC UA and MQTT carry data to supervisory, enterprise and cloud systems [19,45].

3.1 OPC UA

OPC Unified Architecture is a platform-independent, service-oriented architecture for secure, reliable industrial data exchange, published by the OPC Foundation as the multi-part OPC 10000 series and internationally as IEC 62541 [19,47]. It succeeded "classic" OPC (OPC DA, A&E, HDA), which depended on Microsoft COM/DCOM [19]. Its key ideas are:

  • Information modelling. Data live in an address space of nodes connected by typed references. There are eight node classes: Object, Variable, Method, View, ObjectType, VariableType, ReferenceType and DataType [48]. Every node is identified by a NodeId (namespace index plus identifier, e.g. ns=2;s=Pump01.Speed). Industry groups publish companion specifications that define standard types for robots, machine tools, packaging machines and more [19].
  • Services. Clients use service sets such as Discovery, Session, View (browse), Attribute (read/write), Method (call) and Subscription/MonitoredItem, through which the server pushes data changes and events instead of being polled [19].
  • Security by design. Applications authenticate each other with X.509 application-instance certificates; messages can be signed, or signed and encrypted, under defined security policies; users authenticate separately [49].
  • PubSub. Part 14 adds publish/subscribe, with brokerless UDP multicast transport for low-latency field use and broker-based transport over MQTT or AMQP for cloud integration [50]. Combined with TSN, UADP PubSub is the basis of OPC UA for field-level communication [50,51].

3.2 Modbus

Modbus is an application-layer request/response protocol that has been a de facto industrial standard since Modicon introduced it in 1979; it is now maintained openly by the Modbus Organization [17]. Its data model has four tables: coils (1-bit, read/write), discrete inputs (1-bit, read-only), holding registers (16-bit, read/write) and input registers (16-bit, read-only) [17]. A request carries a protocol data unit (PDU) made of a function code and data; common function codes are 01 Read Coils, 02 Read Discrete Inputs, 03 Read Holding Registers, 04 Read Input Registers, 05 Write Single Coil, 06 Write Single Register, 15 Write Multiple Coils and 16 Write Multiple Registers [17]. A single FC 03 request can read at most 125 registers, and errors are returned as exception responses with the function code's high bit set [17].

On serial lines (Modbus RTU), the PDU is wrapped with a device address and a CRC. On TCP, it is prefixed by the 7-byte MBAP header and sent to port 502; TCP's own checksums replace the CRC [52]. Modbus has no authentication or encryption of its own, so the Modbus Organization published a Modbus/TCP Security specification that wraps the protocol in TLS with X.509 certificates, on port 802 [53].

3.3 PROFINET

PROFINET is the industrial Ethernet standard of PROFIBUS & PROFINET International (PI), specified in IEC 61158 and IEC 61784 [44,54]. A PROFINET IO system consists of IO controllers (usually PLCs), IO devices (distributed I/O, drives, instruments) and IO supervisors (engineering and diagnostic tools); devices are described to engineering tools by GSDML files [54]. Non-time-critical configuration uses standard UDP/IP, while cyclic I/O data use real-time (RT) frames sent directly on Ethernet with EtherType 0x8892 to avoid IP stack delays. Isochronous real-time (IRT) adds hardware-scheduled bandwidth reservation for motion control, supporting cycle times down to 31.25 µs [54]. PI groups these capabilities into conformance classes A, B and C [54].

3.4 EtherNet/IP

EtherNet/IP ("Ethernet Industrial Protocol") is ODVA's adaptation of the Common Industrial Protocol (CIP) to standard Ethernet and TCP/IP [55]. CIP is object-oriented: every device is a collection of objects (Identity, Assembly, Connection Manager, and application objects), with the same model shared by DeviceNet, ControlNet and EtherNet/IP [55,56]. Two kinds of messaging coexist: explicit request/response messaging over TCP port 44818 for configuration and diagnostics, and implicit (I/O) messaging over UDP port 2222, where producers send cyclic data that one or many consumers receive at a requested packet interval (RPI) [55]. Extensions include CIP Safety, CIP Motion and CIP Security [56].

3.5 EtherCAT

EtherCAT, developed by Beckhoff and maintained by the EtherCAT Technology Group, takes a different approach: a single Ethernet frame, sent by the main device, passes through every subdevice in a logical ring. Each subdevice reads the data addressed to it and inserts its own data while the frame passes through, with only nanoseconds of delay per node, so one frame can serve hundreds of I/O points [57,58]. The frame uses EtherType 0x88A4, standard Ethernet controllers suffice in the main device, and subdevices use dedicated EtherCAT controller chips [58]. A distributed-clocks mechanism synchronises all subdevices to well under a microsecond, which is why EtherCAT is popular for multi-axis motion [57,58]. It is type 12 in IEC 61158 [43].

3.6 CANopen

The Controller Area Network was developed by Bosch for automotive use; CAN 2.0 (1991) defined 11-bit standard and 29-bit extended identifiers, and the protocol is standardised in ISO 11898-1, whose 2015 edition also includes CAN FD with payloads up to 64 bytes [59,60]. CAN is a multi-master bus with non-destructive bitwise arbitration: the frame with the lowest identifier wins the bus [59]. CANopen, specified by CAN in Automation (CiA) in CiA 301, adds an application layer [61]:

  • Object dictionary. Every device exposes its parameters and data in a table indexed by a 16-bit index and an 8-bit sub-index, described by an electronic data sheet (EDS) [61].
  • PDOs and SDOs. Process data objects carry up to 8 bytes of real-time data without protocol overhead; service data objects give confirmed access to any dictionary entry for configuration [61].
  • NMT, SYNC, EMCY, heartbeat. Network management controls device states (Initialisation, Pre-operational, Operational, Stopped); SYNC messages trigger synchronous PDOs; emergency and heartbeat messages report faults and liveness [61].

3.7 BACnet

BACnet is the data communication protocol for building automation and control networks defined by ANSI/ASHRAE Standard 135, first published in 1995 and also adopted as ISO 16484-5 [62–64]. It models every device as a collection of standard objects (Device, Analog Input, Analog Output, Binary Value, Schedule, Trend Log and many more), each with properties such as Present_Value, Status_Flags and Units, and defines services for interacting with them: Who-Is/I-Am discovery, ReadProperty and WriteProperty, change-of-value (COV) subscriptions, alarms and events, and scheduling [62,64]. Commandable properties use a 16-level priority array so that, for example, a life-safety override outranks a schedule [62]. BACnet can run over several data links, including BACnet/IP on UDP port 47808 (0xBAC0) and MS/TP over RS-485; BACnet Secure Connect (BACnet/SC) adds TLS-secured WebSocket connections through hubs [62].

3.8 Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN)

TSN is a set of IEEE 802.1 standards that give standard Ethernet bounded latency, low jitter and very low loss, so that control traffic can share a network with best-effort IT traffic [65]. The IEEE 802.1 TSN task group grew out of the Audio Video Bridging (AVB) work [65]. Key building blocks are:

  • Time synchronisation: IEEE 802.1AS, a profile of Precision Time Protocol (generalised PTP), gives every bridge and end station a common time base [66].
  • Scheduling and shaping: the time-aware shaper (originally 802.1Qbv, now part of IEEE 802.1Q) opens and closes per-queue transmission gates according to a cyclic schedule, so scheduled traffic meets a guaranteed time slot; frame preemption lets urgent frames interrupt long best-effort frames [67].
  • Reliability: IEEE 802.1CB frame replication and elimination sends duplicate copies over disjoint paths and discards the extras, giving seamless redundancy [68].
  • Configuration: stream reservation and centralised network configuration models, also in IEEE 802.1Q [67].

Because TSN offers many options, profiles pick the ones each industry needs. IEC/IEEE 60802, the TSN profile for industrial automation developed jointly by IEC SC65C and IEEE 802.1, was published on 29 June 2026 [51]. PROFINET, EtherNet/IP and OPC UA field-level communication are all being mapped onto TSN [50,51].

3.9 MQTT and Sparkplug at the edge

MQTT (covered in IoT Fundamentals) has become a common way to move OT data to IT and cloud systems. Because MQTT itself leaves topic structure and payloads undefined, the Eclipse Sparkplug specification, standardised as ISO/IEC 20237:2023, defines a topic namespace, Protocol Buffers payloads, birth/death certificates for edge nodes and devices, and state management so that SCADA and analytics hosts always know whether data are current [45].

4Securing industrial systems

Connecting OT to IT networks and the cloud exposes systems designed for isolated plants. Many industrial protocols, including Modbus, classic PROFINET and EtherNet/IP implicit messaging, were designed without authentication, so anyone on the network can read or write process values [3,17]. Two incidents illustrate the stakes. Stuxnet, analysed publicly in 2010, reprogrammed Siemens PLCs that controlled uranium-enrichment centrifuges while replaying normal values to operators, showing that malware could cause physical damage through a controller [69]. On 23 December 2015, attackers used compromised remote access to the SCADA systems of three Ukrainian distribution companies to open breakers, cutting power to roughly 225,000 customers [70].

NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 recommends defence in depth: an OT security programme, network segmentation with an industrial DMZ, strict control of remote access, least privilege, monitoring and incident response adapted to safety and availability needs [3]. The IEC 62443 series structures these ideas into zones (groups of assets with common security requirements) connected by conduits, and defines security levels SL 1–4 against increasingly capable attackers [71]. Newer protocol features (OPC UA security policies, CIP Security, Modbus/TCP Security, BACnet/SC) bring authentication and encryption to the field level [49,53,56,62].

Lab rule. Never scan or write to a live industrial network without written permission from its owner. Use simulators (like those in §6) on an isolated network: a single Modbus write or PROFINET command can move real machinery [3].

5Industrial applications

The value of IIoT comes from what the data enable. Surveys group the main application areas into maintenance, production and quality, logistics, energy and process improvement [2,4]. The sections below take each in turn, with the standards that define its vocabulary and metrics.

5.1 Predictive maintenance

EN 13306 distinguishes corrective maintenance (after a fault), preventive maintenance (at predetermined intervals or according to prescribed criteria), condition-based maintenance (based on monitoring of physical condition) and predictive maintenance, which is condition-based maintenance carried out following a forecast derived from repeated analysis or known characteristics and evaluation of the significant parameters of the item's degradation [72]. A condition-monitoring programme has three steps: data acquisition, data processing (feature extraction), and maintenance decision-making through diagnostics and prognostics [73]. ISO 17359 gives general guidelines for setting one up, and ISO 13374 defines a layered data-processing architecture from data acquisition through state detection, health assessment and prognostic assessment to advisory generation [74,75].

For rotating machines, vibration is the most informative signal. Overall vibration severity is assessed against zones defined in the ISO 20816 series [35]. Bearing defects excite characteristic fault frequencies; for a stationary outer race, the ball-pass frequency of the outer race is

BPFO = (n/2) · fr · (1 − (d/D) · cos φ)

where n is the number of rolling elements, fr the shaft rotation frequency, d the rolling-element diameter, D the pitch diameter and φ the contact angle. Because the impacts are weak and excite high-frequency structural resonances, the standard technique is envelope analysis: band-pass filter around the resonance, demodulate, and look for BPFO in the envelope spectrum [77]. Statistical features such as RMS, kurtosis and crest factor track the degradation trend [73,77]. Prognostics then estimates remaining useful life (RUL) using physics-based, statistical or machine-learning models [78]. NASA's C-MAPSS turbofan run-to-failure dataset is a widely used benchmark for RUL algorithms [79]. A complete worked example is in §6.6.

5.2 Production monitoring

Production monitoring turns machine states and counts into key performance indicators. ISO 22400-2 defines KPIs for manufacturing operations management, including availability, effectiveness, quality ratio and the overall equipment effectiveness (OEE) index [80]. OEE, popularised by Nakajima's total productive maintenance, multiplies three ratios [80,81]:

OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality
Availability = run time / planned production time
Performance  = (ideal cycle time × total count) / run time
Quality      = good count / total count

In an IIoT setting, PLC tags for machine state, part counters and reject counters are collected via OPC UA, MQTT/Sparkplug or MTConnect, contextualised by an MES (ISA-95 Level 3) and displayed on dashboards [6,19,31,45].

5.3 Quality control

Statistical process control (SPC), introduced by Walter Shewhart at Bell Telephone Laboratories, separates common-cause variation inherent in a process from assignable-cause variation that should be investigated [82]. A Shewhart X̄–R chart plots subgroup means and ranges; with subgroup size n = 5, the limits are UCL/LCL = X̿ ± A2R̄ with A2 = 0.577, and for the range chart UCL = D4R̄ = 2.114 R̄, LCL = D3R̄ = 0 [83]. Process capability indices such as Cp = (USL − LSL)/6σ and Cpk compare the spread with the specification limits [83]. IIoT adds 100 % in-line measurement, machine vision and the ability to correlate quality data with machine parameters automatically [29,30]. See §6.7 for code.

5.4 Supply chain tracking

Supply chain visibility depends on identifying objects and recording events about them. RFID tags following the EPC UHF Gen2 air interface (ISO/IEC 18000-63) can be read in bulk without line of sight [84]. GS1's EPC Information Services (EPCIS) standard defines how trading partners capture and share event data that answer what (which objects), when, where and why (business step and disposition); EPCIS 2.0 (2022) adds a JSON/JSON-LD syntax, a REST API and sensor data so that, for example, temperature excursions in a cold chain can be attached to shipment events [85].

5.5 Energy management

ISO 50001 specifies requirements for an energy management system using the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle. Organisations establish an energy policy, identify significant energy uses, set an energy baseline (EnB) and energy performance indicators (EnPIs), and demonstrate continual improvement of energy performance [86]. IIoT supplies the measurement layer: sub-metering through Modbus or M-Bus meters, drive and compressor data through fieldbuses, and building systems through BACnet [17,62]. Normalising consumption against production volume and weather, as ISO 50001 requires for EnPIs, depends on combining these data with production data from the MES [6,86].

5.6 Asset monitoring

ISO 55000 defines an asset as an item, thing or entity that has potential or actual value to an organisation, and asset management as the coordinated activity of an organisation to realise value from assets [87]. IIoT asset monitoring tracks location, utilisation, condition and health across fleets of pumps, motors, vehicles, tools or containers, often through wireless sensors (WirelessHART, LPWAN) and gateways [2,38]. The Asset Administration Shell (IEC 63278) gives each asset a standardised digital representation with submodels (nameplate, technical data, documentation, condition) that systems can exchange across company boundaries [9].

5.7 Process optimisation

Above basic PID loops, process plants use advanced process control. Model predictive control (MPC) uses a dynamic process model to predict future outputs and repeatedly solves an optimisation that respects constraints on inputs and outputs, applying only the first move before re-solving; Qin and Badgwell's survey documented thousands of industrial MPC applications, concentrated in refining and petrochemicals [88]. IIoT data, historians and digital twins enable model calibration, soft sensors and real-time optimisation layers that set targets for MPC [30,89].

5.8 Digital manufacturing

Digital manufacturing integrates design, planning, production and service through shared digital models. RAMI 4.0 (DIN SPEC 91345) organises Industrie 4.0 along three axes: the IEC 62264/IEC 61512 hierarchy levels, the product life cycle and value stream (IEC 62890), and six architecture layers from asset to business [8]. ISO 23247 provides a digital twin framework for manufacturing, in which an observable manufacturing element is mirrored by a digital twin that is synchronised through data collection and device control [90]. Tao and colleagues review how digital twins are used across product design, production and prognostics [89].

5.9 Smart grids

A smart grid adds two-way communication, sensing and automated control to the electric power system. NIST's interoperability framework describes it through a conceptual model of interacting domains (generation including distributed energy resources, transmission, distribution, customers, markets, operations and service providers) and catalogues the standards that connect them [91]. Fang and colleagues' survey groups the enabling technologies into a smart infrastructure system (energy, information and communication), smart management and smart protection [92]. Important IIoT-relevant standards include:

  • IEC 61850 for substation and power utility automation, with an object-oriented data model of logical nodes, MMS client/server reporting, and GOOSE and sampled-value messages that carry protection signals directly on Ethernet [93].
  • IEEE C37.118.1 for synchrophasor measurements: phasor measurement units (PMUs) time-stamp voltage and current phasors to a common time reference so that wide-area grid dynamics can be observed [94].
  • DNP3 (IEEE 1815) and IEC 60870-5-104 for SCADA telemetry between control centres and RTUs [15,16].

Advanced metering infrastructure, demand response and the integration of rooftop solar, batteries and electric vehicles all depend on this communication layer; so does the attack surface demonstrated in Ukraine in 2015 [70,91].

6.1 IEC 61131-3 Structured Text: conveyor with seal-in and jam detection

This is Rung 1 of Figure 3, extended with a jam alarm built from the standard on-delay timer (TON) and a part counter (CTU) that can feed the OEE calculation in §6.7. Note the fail-safe convention: the Stop button is wired normally closed, so the input is TRUE when the button is not pressed, and a broken wire stops the machine.

ConveyorControl.st
(* Rung 1 of Figure 3 in Structured Text, plus a jam alarm using a TON timer *)
PROGRAM ConveyorControl
VAR
    Start_PB   AT %IX0.0 : BOOL;   (* momentary, normally open      *)
    Stop_PB    AT %IX0.1 : BOOL;   (* wired NC: TRUE = not pressed  *)
    OL_Ok      AT %IX0.2 : BOOL;   (* overload relay, TRUE = healthy*)
    PartSensor AT %IX0.3 : BOOL;   (* photo-eye at conveyor exit    *)
    Motor      AT %QX0.0 : BOOL;
    JamAlarm   AT %QX0.1 : BOOL;
    JamTimer   : TON;
    PartCount  : CTU;
END_VAR

(* Seal-in: Start latches, Stop or overload unlatches. Fail-safe: a broken
   Stop wire reads FALSE and stops the motor. *)
Motor := (Start_PB OR Motor) AND Stop_PB AND OL_Ok;

(* Jam: motor running but no part seen at the exit for 20 s *)
JamTimer(IN := Motor AND NOT PartSensor, PT := T#20s);
IF JamTimer.Q THEN
    JamAlarm := TRUE;
    Motor    := FALSE;
END_IF;
IF Start_PB AND NOT JamTimer.Q THEN
    JamAlarm := FALSE;             (* operator restart acknowledges *)
END_IF;

(* Production counter for OEE (rising edge counted by CTU) *)
PartCount(CU := PartSensor, R := FALSE, PV := 32767);
END_PROGRAM

6.2 Function block with hysteresis, and analogue scaling

Function blocks are IEC 61131-3's unit of reuse: they keep internal state between calls [12]. This one implements two-point level control with a dead band, and goes to a safe state when the transmitter signals a NAMUR NE 43 fault [37]. The raw count 27648 for full scale is a common vendor convention, not part of the standard.

FB_LevelHysteresis.st
(* Reusable function block: on/off level control with hysteresis *)
FUNCTION_BLOCK FB_LevelHysteresis
VAR_INPUT
    Level_pct : REAL;           (* scaled from 4-20 mA input  *)
    LowSP     : REAL := 30.0;   (* start filling below this   *)
    HighSP    : REAL := 80.0;   (* stop filling above this    *)
    SensorOk  : BOOL;           (* FALSE if signal < 3.6 mA (NAMUR NE 43) *)
END_VAR
VAR_OUTPUT
    FillValve : BOOL;
    Fault     : BOOL;
END_VAR

Fault := NOT SensorOk;
IF Fault THEN
    FillValve := FALSE;                     (* safe state: stop filling *)
ELSIF Level_pct < LowSP THEN
    FillValve := TRUE;
ELSIF Level_pct > HighSP THEN
    FillValve := FALSE;
END_IF;                                     (* between SPs: hold state *)
END_FUNCTION_BLOCK

(* Scaling a raw 16-bit analogue input (0..27648 = 4..20 mA on many PLCs) *)
FUNCTION ScaleAI : REAL
VAR_INPUT Raw : INT; LoEU : REAL; HiEU : REAL; END_VAR
ScaleAI := LoEU + (INT_TO_REAL(Raw) / 27648.0) * (HiEU - LoEU);
END_FUNCTION

6.3 A batch sequence as a state machine

Sequences are often drawn in SFC; the same logic can be written in ST with a CASE statement over an enumerated state. This simplified reactor follows a fill, heat, mix, drain recipe.

BatchReactor.st
(* Batch sequence as a state machine: the textual equivalent of an SFC *)
TYPE E_State : (IDLE, FILL, HEAT, MIX, DRAIN, DONE); END_TYPE

PROGRAM BatchReactor
VAR
    State     : E_State := IDLE;
    StartCmd  : BOOL;
    LevelHigh, LevelLow : BOOL;
    TempC     : REAL;
    InletValve, Heater, Agitator, OutletValve : BOOL;
    MixTimer  : TON;
END_VAR

CASE State OF
    IDLE:  IF StartCmd THEN State := FILL; END_IF;
    FILL:  InletValve := TRUE;
           IF LevelHigh THEN InletValve := FALSE; State := HEAT; END_IF;
    HEAT:  Heater := TempC < 75.0;          (* simple on/off for brevity *)
           Agitator := TRUE;
           IF TempC >= 75.0 THEN State := MIX; END_IF;
    MIX:   Heater := TempC < 75.0;
           MixTimer(IN := TRUE, PT := T#10m);
           IF MixTimer.Q THEN
               MixTimer(IN := FALSE); Heater := FALSE; Agitator := FALSE;
               State := DRAIN;
           END_IF;
    DRAIN: OutletValve := TRUE;
           IF LevelLow THEN OutletValve := FALSE; State := DONE; END_IF;
    DONE:  IF NOT StartCmd THEN State := IDLE; END_IF;
END_CASE;
END_PROGRAM

6.4 Modbus TCP: read holding registers with raw sockets

This client builds the MBAP header and FC 03 PDU by hand (Figure 6), so every byte is visible, and handles exception responses [17,52]. It needs only the Python standard library. The companion simulator listens on port 5020 so it does not need root privileges; real devices use port 502.

modbus_read.py
"""Minimal Modbus TCP client: read holding registers (function code 0x03).
Standard library only. Frame layout per the Modbus Messaging on TCP/IP
Implementation Guide and Modbus Application Protocol Specification V1.1b3."""
import socket, struct, sys

def read_holding_registers(host, start, count, unit=1, port=502, tid=1, timeout=2.0):
    if not 1 <= count <= 125:
        raise ValueError("count must be 1..125 for FC 0x03")
    pdu = struct.pack(">BHH", 0x03, start, count)              # FC, start address, quantity
    mbap = struct.pack(">HHHB", tid, 0, len(pdu) + 1, unit)     # transaction, protocol=0, length, unit
    with socket.create_connection((host, port), timeout=timeout) as s:
        s.sendall(mbap + pdu)
        hdr = _recv_exact(s, 7)
        r_tid, r_pid, r_len, r_unit = struct.unpack(">HHHB", hdr)
        body = _recv_exact(s, r_len - 1)
    if r_tid != tid or r_pid != 0:
        raise IOError("mismatched transaction or protocol id")
    fc = body[0]
    if fc & 0x80:                                               # exception response
        raise IOError(f"Modbus exception code {body[1]:#04x}")
    byte_count = body[1]
    return list(struct.unpack(f">{byte_count // 2}H", body[2:2 + byte_count]))

def _recv_exact(s, n):
    buf = b""
    while len(buf) < n:
        chunk = s.recv(n - len(buf))
        if not chunk:
            raise IOError("connection closed")
        buf += chunk
    return buf

if __name__ == "__main__":
    host = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "127.0.0.1"
    port = int(sys.argv[2]) if len(sys.argv) > 2 else 502
    regs = read_holding_registers(host, start=0, count=4, port=port)
    # Example convention: reg0 = temperature x10, reg1-2 = 32-bit counter (big-endian words)
    temp_c = regs[0] / 10
    counter = (regs[1] << 16) | regs[2]
    print("raw:", regs, "| temperature:", temp_c, "degC | parts counter:", counter)

"""Tiny Modbus TCP server for lab testing: answers FC 0x03 from a fixed register table."""
import socketserver, struct

REGISTERS = [215, 0x0001, 0x86A0, 3]   # 21.5 degC, counter 100000, status 3

class Handler(socketserver.BaseRequestHandler):
    def handle(self):
        while True:
            hdr = self.request.recv(7)
            if len(hdr) < 7:
                return
            tid, pid, length, unit = struct.unpack(">HHHB", hdr)
            pdu = self.request.recv(length - 1)
            fc = pdu[0]
            if fc == 0x03:
                start, qty = struct.unpack(">HH", pdu[1:5])
                if start + qty > len(REGISTERS):
                    resp = struct.pack(">BB", fc | 0x80, 0x02)            # illegal data address
                else:
                    vals = REGISTERS[start:start + qty]
                    resp = struct.pack(f">BB{qty}H", fc, 2 * qty, *vals)
            else:
                resp = struct.pack(">BB", fc | 0x80, 0x01)                # illegal function
            self.request.sendall(struct.pack(">HHHB", tid, 0, len(resp) + 1, unit) + resp)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    with socketserver.TCPServer(("127.0.0.1", 5020), Handler) as srv:
        print("Modbus simulator on 127.0.0.1:5020")
        srv.serve_forever()

6.6 Predictive maintenance: vibration features, anomaly detection and RUL

This script synthesises 60 days of bearing vibration in which an outer-race defect appears on day 35 and grows exponentially. It computes RMS, kurtosis, crest factor and the envelope-spectrum amplitude at BPFO [77], builds a health indicator as the worst z-score against a healthy baseline, smooths it with an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) for alarming [83], and estimates RUL by fitting an exponential trend to RMS and extrapolating to an alarm limit [78]. Real projects replace the synthetic generator with accelerometer data and validate on run-to-failure datasets [78,79].

pdm_analysis.py (requires numpy)
"""Predictive-maintenance analysis of (synthetic) bearing vibration.
Features: RMS, kurtosis, crest factor, envelope-spectrum energy at BPFO.
Detection: z-score against a healthy baseline + EWMA. Prognosis: RUL by
fitting an exponential trend to RMS and extrapolating to an alarm limit."""
import numpy as np

FS = 20_000            # sample rate, Hz
N = 2**14              # samples per snapshot (~0.82 s)
SHAFT_HZ = 29.95       # ~1797 rpm
# Bearing geometry (illustrative): 9 balls, ball dia 7.94 mm, pitch dia 39.04 mm, 0 deg contact
NB, BD, PD, PHI = 9, 7.94, 39.04, 0.0
BPFO = NB / 2 * SHAFT_HZ * (1 - BD / PD * np.cos(PHI))   # outer-race defect frequency

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def snapshot(day, onset=35, fail=60):
    """One vibration record. After &#x27;onset' an outer-race defect grows exponentially."""
    t = np.arange(N) / FS
    x = 0.5 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * SHAFT_HZ * t) + 0.3 * rng.standard_normal(N)
    if day >= onset:
        sev = 0.05 * np.exp(4.0 * (day - onset) / (fail - onset))     # defect severity
        impacts = np.zeros(N)
        impacts[(np.arange(0, t[-1], 1 / BPFO) * FS).astype(int)] = sev * 10
        ring = np.exp(-800 * t[:200]) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 3_000 * t[:200])  # 3 kHz resonance
        x += np.convolve(impacts, ring)[:N]
    return x

def features(x):
    rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(x**2))
    kurt = np.mean((x - x.mean())**4) / np.var(x)**2
    crest = np.max(np.abs(x)) / rms
    # Envelope analysis: band-pass around the resonance via FFT mask, then Hilbert envelope
    X = np.fft.rfft(x); f = np.fft.rfftfreq(N, 1 / FS)
    X[(f < 2_000) | (f > 4_000)] = 0
    analytic = np.fft.ifft(np.concatenate([X, np.zeros(N - len(X))]) * 2)
    env = np.abs(analytic); env -= env.mean()
    E = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(env)) / N
    band = (f > BPFO - 2) & (f < BPFO + 2)
    return rms, kurt, crest, E[band].max()

days = np.arange(0, 60)
F = np.array([features(snapshot(d)) for d in days])
rms, kurt, crest, bpfo_amp = F.T

# Baseline from the first 20 healthy days -> z-scores
mu, sd = F[:20].mean(axis=0), F[:20].std(axis=0)
z = (F - mu) / sd
health = z.max(axis=1)                      # worst-feature health indicator

# EWMA on the health indicator (lambda = 0.3); alarm when it exceeds 3 sigma
lam, ewma = 0.3, np.zeros_like(health)
for i, h in enumerate(health):
    ewma[i] = lam * h + (1 - lam) * (ewma[i - 1] if i else 0.0)
alarm_day = int(days[np.argmax(ewma > 3)])

# RUL: fit log(RMS - baseline) over the last 7 days, extrapolate to 2x baseline RMS
limit = 2 * mu[0]
today = alarm_day + 10
w = slice(today - 7, today + 1)
excess = np.clip(rms[w] - mu[0], 1e-6, None)
b, a = np.polyfit(days[w], np.log(excess), 1)
t_hit = (np.log(limit - mu[0]) - a) / b
print(f"BPFO = {BPFO:.1f} Hz")
print(f"First alarm (EWMA of health index > 3): day {alarm_day}")
print(f"On day {today}: RMS={rms[today]:.3f}, kurtosis={kurt[today]:.1f}, "
      f"BPFO envelope amp={bpfo_amp[today]:.4f} (baseline {mu[3]:.4f})")
print(f"Estimated day RMS reaches {limit:.3f}: {t_hit:.1f} -> RUL ~ {t_hit - today:.1f} days")

6.7 Quality control and production monitoring: control chart and OEE

Phase I limits are computed from in-control subgroups, then later subgroups are checked against them [83]. The OEE section applies the formulas from §5.2 [80,81].

spc_oee.py
"""Quality control and production monitoring: X-bar/R control chart limits and OEE."""
import numpy as np

# Constants for subgroup size n = 5 (standard SPC tables)
A2, D3, D4 = 0.577, 0.0, 2.114

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
subgroups = rng.normal(25.00, 0.02, size=(25, 5))     # 25 subgroups of 5 shaft diameters (mm)
subgroups[22:] += 0.05                                 # a tool-wear shift in the last 3

xbar = subgroups.mean(axis=1)
r = subgroups.max(axis=1) - subgroups.min(axis=1)
# Phase I: limits from the first 20 (in-control) subgroups
xbb, rbar = xbar[:20].mean(), r[:20].mean()
ucl_x, lcl_x = xbb + A2 * rbar, xbb - A2 * rbar
ucl_r, lcl_r = D4 * rbar, D3 * rbar
print(f"X-bar chart: CL={xbb:.4f} UCL={ucl_x:.4f} LCL={lcl_x:.4f}")
print(f"R chart:     CL={rbar:.4f} UCL={ucl_r:.4f} LCL={lcl_r:.4f}")
out = [i + 1 for i, v in enumerate(xbar) if not lcl_x <= v <= ucl_x]
print("Subgroups out of control:", out)

# OEE = Availability x Performance x Quality  (ISO 22400-2 KPI definitions)
planned_min, downtime_min = 480, 47
ideal_cycle_s, total_count, good_count = 30, 760, 741
run_min = planned_min - downtime_min
availability = run_min / planned_min
performance = (ideal_cycle_s * total_count / 60) / run_min
quality = good_count / total_count
print(f"A={availability:.3f} P={performance:.3f} Q={quality:.3f} OEE={availability*performance*quality:.3f}")

8Exercises with solutions

Exercise 1. Assign each system to its ISA-95 / IEC 62264 level: (a) an MES that dispatches work orders, (b) a pressure transmitter, (c) a PLC running a packaging machine, (d) an ERP system, (e) a SCADA HMI.

Solution

(a) Level 3. (b) Level 0/1: the process and its sensing; the transmitter itself is usually counted as Level 1 equipment acting on Level 0. (c) Level 1. (d) Level 4. (e) Level 2 [6].

Exercise 2. A PLC has a 10 ms scan. A push-button changes state just after the input-read phase. What is the worst-case delay before the output responds, ignoring I/O filter and update times?

Solution

About 20 ms. The change is missed in the current scan and read at the start of the next; the output is written at the end of that next scan, so the delay approaches two scan times [10].

Exercise 3. Write the hexadecimal bytes of a Modbus TCP request, transaction ID 0x0007, unit 0x11, to read two input registers starting at address 0x0010.

Solution

00 07 00 00 00 06 11 04 00 10 00 02. FC 04 reads input registers; the length is 6 (unit ID + 5-byte PDU) [17,52].

Exercise 4. A Modbus server returns the PDU 83 02. What happened?

Solution

0x83 is function code 03 with the high bit set, so it is an exception response. Exception code 02 is ILLEGAL DATA ADDRESS: the requested register range does not exist on the server [17].

Exercise 5. A CANopen drive has node ID 5. Give the COB-IDs of its TPDO1, its SDO response (server to client) and its heartbeat.

Solution

TPDO1 = 0x180 + 5 = 0x185; SDO server→client = 0x580 + 5 = 0x585; heartbeat = 0x700 + 5 = 0x705 [61].

Exercise 6. Two CAN nodes start transmitting at the same instant with identifiers 0x185 and 0x205. Which one wins arbitration, and why does the loser not corrupt the frame?

Solution

0x185 wins, because the lower identifier has a dominant (0) bit where the other has recessive (1). A node sending recessive while reading dominant stops transmitting and retries later, so arbitration is non-destructive [59,60].

Exercise 7. A 4–20 mA transmitter is ranged 0–10 bar. (a) What pressure does 13.6 mA represent? (b) The loop reads 2.1 mA. What should the controller conclude?

Solution

(a) (13.6 − 4)/16 × 10 = 6.0 bar. (b) The signal is below the NAMUR NE 43 failure limit of 3.6 mA, so the controller should treat it as a sensor or wiring fault, not as a pressure reading [36,37].

Exercise 8. In a Ziegler–Nichols test, a loop oscillates steadily at Ku = 4.0 with period Tu = 12 s. Give the PID settings and one caveat.

Solution

Kp = 2.4, Ti = 6 s, Td = 1.5 s [42]. Caveat: these settings are typically aggressive and poorly damped, so detune or use a more robust method before production [41].

Exercise 9. A bearing has 8 rolling elements, d/D = 0.2, contact angle 0°, and the shaft turns at 1,500 rpm. Compute BPFO. In which spectrum should you look for it?

Solution

fr = 25 Hz, so BPFO = (8/2) × 25 × (1 − 0.2) = 80 Hz. Look in the envelope spectrum after band-pass filtering around a structural resonance, because the defect impacts modulate a high-frequency carrier [77].

Exercise 10. A shift has 480 min planned time, 60 min of stops, an ideal cycle of 20 s, 1,100 parts made and 1,067 good. Calculate OEE.

Solution

Availability = 420/480 = 0.875. Performance = (20 × 1,100 / 60)/420 = 366.7/420 = 0.873. Quality = 1,067/1,100 = 0.970. OEE = 0.875 × 0.873 × 0.970 ≈ 0.741, or 74 % [80,81].

Exercise 11. You need 64 servo axes synchronised to better than 1 µs with a 250 µs cycle, and a separate requirement to bring plant data to a cloud analytics service. Choose protocols for each and justify.

Solution

For the axes: EtherCAT (distributed clocks, one frame serving all nodes) or PROFINET IRT, both designed for isochronous motion [54,57,58]. For cloud: OPC UA (client/server from an edge gateway, or PubSub over MQTT) or MQTT with Sparkplug, which give modelled or self-describing data and brokered, outbound-only connections [45,50]. TSN under IEC/IEEE 60802 is the route to running both on one converged network [51].

Exercise 12. Using IEC 62443 terminology, sketch a segmentation for a plant whose PLCs must send data to a cloud service.

Solution

Put PLCs and HMIs in a control zone and the historian, edge gateway and remote-access servers in an industrial DMZ zone. Define conduits: control zone to DMZ (OPC UA or Sparkplug, outbound from OT), DMZ to the enterprise/cloud (TLS, outbound only). Block direct enterprise-to-PLC traffic, and assign each zone a target security level [3,71].

Exercise 13. Why does the SPC script in §6.7 compute limits only from the first 20 subgroups?

Solution

Control limits must describe the process when only common-cause variation is present (Phase I). Including the shifted subgroups would widen the limits and could hide the very shift you want to detect [82,83].
