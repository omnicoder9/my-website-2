IoT Hardware and Electronics: from silicon to sensors, actuators and the circuits in between

A practical, referenced guide to the physical layer of the Internet of Things. It covers the processors that run IoT devices, the sensors that measure the world, the actuators that act on it, and the supporting electronics (converters, buses, power and PCB design) that tie them together. Each section includes worked examples and code, and the article closes with exercises, a trivia quiz and a full Vancouver-style reference list.

1. Anatomy of an IoT node

An IoT device is, at its core, an embedded system with a network connection: it senses some physical quantity, processes it locally, communicates with other devices or the cloud, and in many cases acts on its environment [1]. Whatever the application, from a soil-moisture probe to a factory robot, the same functional blocks keep recurring. Figure 1 shows them.

The rest of this article walks through those blocks in the order a signal travels: what does the thinking (Section 2), what does the sensing (Section 3), what does the acting (Section 4), and the electronics that make the three work together reliably on a battery (Section 5).

2. Processing hardware

The processing element sets the device's cost, power budget, software model and security capabilities. The main distinction is between microcontrollers, which integrate CPU, memory and peripherals on one die and usually run bare-metal code or a real-time operating system (RTOS), and microprocessors, which depend on external memory and use a memory management unit (MMU) to run a full operating system such as Linux [2].

2.1 Microcontrollers (MCUs)

MCUs dominate battery-powered IoT end nodes because they combine low cost, low standby current and fast wake-up with a rich set of on-chip peripherals. The families below are the ones most commonly met in IoT products and teaching labs. Table 1 compares representative parts.

ARM Cortex-M

Cortex-M is Arm's microcontroller processor profile, licensed to many silicon vendors (ST, NXP, Microchip, Nordic, Raspberry Pi and others). The Armv7-M architecture (Cortex-M3, M4, M7) executes only the Thumb instruction set, which mixes 16-bit and 32-bit encodings for code density, and defines a nested vectored interrupt controller (NVIC) with a fixed memory map so that interrupt handlers can be ordinary C functions [3]. The newer Armv8-M architecture (Cortex-M23, M33, M55, M85) adds the TrustZone security extension, which partitions the processor into Secure and Non-secure states. This hardware isolation is used to protect keys and firmware-update code in connected devices [4]. Common IoT choices are:

  • Cortex-M0/M0+: the smallest, lowest-power cores, used in cost-sensitive parts such as the dual-core RP2040 [5].
  • Cortex-M3: a mainstream 32-bit core, used in the STM32F1 series [6].
  • Cortex-M4/M7: add DSP instructions and an optional floating-point unit, suitable for sensor fusion and audio [3,7].
  • Cortex-M33/M55: Armv8-M cores with TrustZone. The M55 adds the Helium vector extension (MVE) for on-device machine learning [4].

ESP32

Espressif's original ESP32 integrates a dual-core 32-bit Xtensa LX6 processor at up to 240 MHz, 520 KB of on-chip SRAM, 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi (802.11 b/g/n) and Bluetooth v4.2 (BR/EDR and Bluetooth LE) on one die [8]. Its peripherals include 34 programmable GPIOs, a 12-bit SAR ADC with up to 18 channels, two 8-bit DACs, capacitive touch inputs, UART, SPI, I²C, I²S and a TWAI controller compatible with CAN 2.0 [8,9]. An ultra-low-power (ULP) coprocessor can keep sampling sensors while the main cores are in deep sleep, where the datasheet quotes around 10 µA with the RTC timer and RTC memory running [8]. Later family members such as ESP32-S3 (vector instructions for AI), ESP32-C3/C6 (RISC-V cores) and ESP32-H2 (802.15.4 for Thread and Zigbee) keep the same ESP-IDF software framework.

ESP32 gotchas. GPIO34 to GPIO39 are input-only, the pins are 3.3 V logic, and some pins are strapping pins that select the boot mode [8]. ADC2 cannot be used while Wi-Fi is active, and the ADC is noticeably non-linear near the ends of its range, so use the calibration API [10].

STM32

STMicroelectronics' STM32 is a very broad Cortex-M portfolio, with families from ultra-low-power (L0/L4/U5) through mainstream (F1/G0/G4) to high-performance (F4/F7/H7) and wireless (WB/WL). Two classic examples:

  • STM32F103x8/xB (the "Blue Pill" chip): Cortex-M3 at 72 MHz, 64 or 128 KB flash, 20 KB SRAM, two 12-bit ADCs with 1 µs conversion time, up to three USARTs, two SPI, two I²C, USB and CAN [6].
  • STM32F405/407: Cortex-M4 with FPU at 168 MHz, up to 1 MB flash and 192 KB SRAM (including 64 KB core-coupled memory), three 12-bit 2.4 MSPS ADCs and two 12-bit DACs [7].

ST supplies the free STM32CubeIDE with the CubeMX pin and clock configurator and the HAL/LL driver libraries [11].

AVR

AVR is an 8-bit modified-Harvard RISC architecture, originally from Atmel and now owned by Microchip. The best-known part is the ATmega328P used on the Arduino Uno. It has 32 KB flash, 1 KB EEPROM, 2 KB SRAM, 23 programmable I/O lines, a 10-bit ADC (6 channels in the PDIP package, 8 in TQFP/QFN) and a 1.8 to 5.5 V supply range. Because most of its 131 instructions run in a single clock cycle, it approaches 1 MIPS per MHz, up to 20 MIPS at 20 MHz [12]. Its 5 V tolerance and simple peripherals make it forgiving for beginners, although its 2 KB of RAM limits networking stacks.

PIC

Microchip's PIC family spans 8-, 16- and 32-bit devices. The classic 8-bit PIC16F877A has 8K × 14-bit words of flash program memory, 368 bytes of RAM, 256 bytes of EEPROM, an 8-channel 10-bit ADC and only 35 single-word instructions, running from DC to 20 MHz [13]. PIC parts are common in industrial and automotive designs and are developed with Microchip's MPLAB X IDE and XC compilers [14].

2.2 Microprocessors (MPUs)

A microprocessor (or application processor) is optimised for throughput rather than standby current. It uses caches, external DRAM and an MMU that provides virtual memory and process isolation, which are prerequisites for general-purpose operating systems like Linux [2]. In IoT, MPUs appear in gateways, cameras, HMIs and edge servers that must run containers, databases, TLS-heavy cloud stacks or computer vision. Typical cores are Arm Cortex-A, for example the Cortex-A8 in TI's AM335x [15] or the Cortex-A76 in the Raspberry Pi 5's BCM2712 [16]. The trade-off is power: MPUs draw hundreds of milliwatts to several watts when active and need seconds to boot. That is why many products pair an MPU for networking and UI with a small MCU for real-time control and low-power supervision.

2.3 Single-board computers (SBCs)

An SBC places an application processor, RAM, storage, power regulation and I/O connectors on one ready-to-use board, which makes it ideal for prototyping gateways and edge nodes.

Raspberry Pi

The Raspberry Pi 5 uses the Broadcom BCM2712, a quad-core 64-bit Arm Cortex-A76 at 2.4 GHz with a VideoCore VII GPU, LPDDR4X memory, dual 4Kp60 HDMI outputs, a PCIe 2.0 ×1 interface and the familiar 40-pin GPIO header [16]. On the Pi 5, the GPIO, UART, SPI and I²C lines are served by Raspberry Pi's own RP1 I/O controller rather than by the main SoC [16]. The company also designs the RP2040 microcontroller (Raspberry Pi Pico), whose programmable I/O (PIO) state machines can bit-bang custom protocols in hardware [5]. MIPI CSI-2 camera modules plug directly into the board [17].

BeagleBone

The BeagleBone Black is built around TI's AM3358 Sitara processor (1 GHz Cortex-A8) with 512 MB DDR3 RAM, 4 GB of on-board eMMC and two 46-pin expansion headers [18]. Its distinctive feature for IoT and industrial control is the PRU-ICSS: two 200 MHz programmable real-time units that run independently of Linux and can toggle pins with deterministic, cycle-accurate timing [15,18].

2.4 System-on-Chip (SoC)

A System-on-Chip integrates on one die what would otherwise be several chips: CPU cores, memory controllers, GPU, DSP, radios, security engines and peripherals. The ESP32 is a wireless MCU SoC, combining CPU, RAM, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth basebands and RF front end [8]. The BCM2712 is an application-class SoC [16]. The benefits are lower bill-of-materials cost, smaller boards and lower power, since signals stay on-die. The costs are less flexibility and a heavier dependence on vendor SDKs and long-term supply. Heterogeneous SoCs go further by mixing application cores, real-time cores and accelerators. The AM335x's PRUs are one example [15], and FPGA SoCs (next section) are another.

2.5 FPGA-based systems

A field-programmable gate array is a sea of configurable logic blocks, built from look-up tables (LUTs) and flip-flops, joined by a programmable routing network and surrounded by hard blocks such as block RAM, DSP multipliers, clock managers and high-speed transceivers [19]. AMD's 7 Series, for example, uses 6-input LUTs that can also act as distributed RAM or shift registers, 36 Kb dual-port block RAMs and DSP48E1 slices containing a 25 × 18 multiplier [20]. Designs are described in a hardware description language (VHDL or Verilog) or with high-level synthesis, then synthesised, placed and routed into a configuration bitstream.

In IoT, FPGAs earn their place when a task needs massive parallelism or hard real-time determinism that a CPU cannot provide. Examples are multi-channel high-speed ADC capture, custom industrial Ethernet, motor control at very high loop rates, and low-latency vision pre-processing. FPGA SoCs such as AMD Zynq pair Arm cores with programmable logic, so Linux runs on the CPU while the fabric provides custom accelerators [20]. Kuon, Tessier and Rose survey the architectural trade-offs, including the area and power overhead of programmability compared with fixed ASIC logic [19].

2.6 Embedded GPUs and AI accelerators

Running neural networks at the edge, rather than in the cloud, cuts latency and bandwidth and keeps raw data private. Deep-network inference is dominated by multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations, so accelerators raise throughput with wide parallel MAC arrays and lower-precision arithmetic (8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floats) [21]. Google's first TPU, for example, used a 256 × 256 systolic array of 8-bit MAC units [22]. Accelerator performance is usually quoted in TOPS (tera-operations per second), with TOPS per watt as the efficiency figure.

  • Embedded GPUs. NVIDIA's Jetson Orin Nano Super Developer Kit pairs a 6-core Arm Cortex-A78AE CPU with a 1024-core Ampere GPU (32 Tensor Cores) and quotes up to 67 TOPS of INT8 AI performance within a 7 to 25 W envelope [23].
  • Dedicated NPUs/TPUs. Google's Coral Edge TPU performs 4 TOPS at about 2 W, which is 2 TOPS per watt, and is available as USB, M.2 and PCIe modules [24].
  • Microcontroller ML (TinyML). Quantised models of tens of kilobytes can run keyword spotting or anomaly detection on Cortex-M class MCUs at milliwatt power [25]. Vector extensions like Arm Helium are aimed at exactly this workload [4].

3. Sensors

3.1 Sensor fundamentals

A sensor converts a physical or chemical quantity into an electrical signal. Fraden's handbook defines the vocabulary used throughout datasheets [26]:

  • Transfer function: the relationship between input stimulus and output signal. Sensitivity is its slope (for example 10 mV/°C).
  • Span / full-scale range: the range of inputs the sensor can convert without unacceptable error.
  • Accuracy (closeness to the true value) versus precision or repeatability (closeness of repeated readings to each other). A sensor can be precise but inaccurate, which calibration can fix.
  • Resolution: the smallest input change that produces a detectable output change.
  • Hysteresis, non-linearity, offset, drift and response time: the main error and dynamic terms.
  • Active versus passive: passive (self-generating) sensors such as thermocouples and piezoelectrics need no excitation. Active sensors such as RTDs and strain gauges need an excitation current or voltage.

In practice, IoT designers increasingly choose digital sensors that integrate the transducer, amplifier, ADC and calibration memory and deliver compensated readings over I²C or SPI. Analog sensors are still common where cost, speed or simplicity matter. Table 3 summarises the sensor examples used in this section.

3.2 Temperature

Four technologies cover nearly every IoT temperature measurement [26]:

  • Thermistors (NTC) are cheap ceramic resistors whose resistance falls steeply and non-linearly as temperature rises. The Steinhart–Hart equation, 1/T = A + B·ln R + C·(ln R)³, fits the curve closely over wide temperature ranges using three calibration points [39].
  • RTDs use the near-linear resistance of platinum. A Pt100 has 100 Ω at 0 °C, and IEC 60751 specifies its curve with the Callendar–Van Dusen coefficients A = 3.9083 × 10⁻³ °C⁻¹ and B = −5.775 × 10⁻⁷ °C⁻². That gives a mean coefficient α = 0.00385 °C⁻¹ between 0 and 100 °C. The standard also defines tolerance classes, for example Class A ±(0.15 + 0.002|t|) °C [40].
  • Thermocouples generate a Seebeck voltage of tens of µV/°C at the junction of two dissimilar metals. They reach very high temperatures but need cold-junction compensation and a low-noise amplifier [26].
  • Semiconductor IC sensors use the temperature dependence of a transistor's base-emitter voltage. The analog LM35 outputs 10 mV/°C, specified from −55 to +150 °C, with 0.5 °C guaranteed accuracy at 25 °C for the A grade [27]. The digital DS18B20 is accurate to ±0.5 °C from −10 to +85 °C, offers 9- to 12-bit resolution (up to 750 ms per conversion at 12 bits) and shares a single data wire with other sensors, each identified by a unique 64-bit ROM code [28].
Arduino (Uno): read an LM35 on A0[27,41]
// LM35: Vout = 10 mV per °C. Uno ADC: 10-bit, 5 V reference by default.
const int SENSOR_PIN = A0;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
}

void loop() {
  int raw = analogRead(SENSOR_PIN);           // 0..1023
  float volts = raw * (5.0 / 1023.0);
  float tempC = volts * 100.0;                // 10 mV/°C -> x100
  Serial.print("T = "); Serial.print(tempC, 1); Serial.println(" °C");
  delay(1000);
}

At 5 V full scale, one 10-bit step is about 4.9 mV, which is roughly 0.5 °C for an LM35. Selecting the Uno's internal 1.1 V reference with analogReference(INTERNAL) improves resolution to about 0.11 °C per step, at the cost of limiting the measurable range to about 110 °C [12,41].

3.3 Humidity

Most IoT hygrometers are capacitive. A hygroscopic polymer dielectric between two electrodes absorbs water vapour, and its permittivity, and therefore the capacitance, rises with relative humidity (RH) [26]. Integrated parts like the Bosch BME280 combine the humidity element with temperature compensation, giving ±3 %RH absolute accuracy and a response time of about 1 s [29]. Relative humidity depends strongly on temperature, so applications often derive the dew point using the Magnus approximation with the Alduchov–Eskridge coefficients a = 17.625 and b = 243.04 °C [42]:

γ = ln(RH/100) + a·T/(b + T)   →   Tdew = b·γ / (a − γ)

3.4 Pressure

MEMS barometric sensors etch a thin silicon diaphragm over a sealed reference cavity. Atmospheric pressure deflects the diaphragm, and piezoresistors diffused into it, arranged as a Wheatstone bridge, convert strain into a resistance change [26]. The BME280's pressure channel covers 300 to 1100 hPa with ±1 hPa absolute accuracy. Its relative accuracy of ±0.12 hPa corresponds to roughly ±1 m of altitude, which is fine enough to detect which floor of a building a device is on [29]. Altitude follows from the International Standard Atmosphere [43]:

h ≈ 44330 · [1 − (p / p₀)1/5.255] metres, with p₀ = 1013.25 hPa at sea level
MicroPython (ESP32): detect a BME280 and check its chip ID[29,44]
from machine import Pin, I2C

i2c = I2C(0, scl=Pin(22), sda=Pin(21), freq=400_000)
print("Devices found:", [hex(a) for a in i2c.scan()])   # BME280 is 0x76 or 0x77

ADDR = 0x76
chip_id = i2c.readfrom_mem(ADDR, 0xD0, 1)[0]              # 'id' register
print("Chip ID:", hex(chip_id), "(BME280 expected 0x60)")

Reading compensated values requires applying the calibration constants stored in the sensor's non-volatile memory, as the datasheet's compensation formulas describe [29]. In practice you use a vetted driver library rather than re-implementing them.

3.5 Proximity

Proximity and distance sensing uses several physical principles [26]:

  • Optical time-of-flight (ToF): the ST VL53L0X fires a 940 nm Class 1 VCSEL laser and times the reflected photons, measuring absolute distances up to 2 m largely independent of target colour. It connects over I²C at default 7-bit address 0x29 [30].
  • Ultrasonic: time the echo of a roughly 40 kHz burst. Distance = (speed of sound × echo time)/2, where the speed of sound depends on air temperature (about 343 m/s at 20 °C) [26].
  • Capacitive: a target changes an electrode's capacitance to ground. This is the basis of touch buttons, including the ESP32's touch pins [9].
  • Inductive: eddy currents in a metal target load an oscillator coil. This approach is used for rugged industrial metal detection [26].
  • Infrared reflective / PIR: covered in Section 3.7.

3.6 Motion, acceleration, gyroscopes and magnetometers

Accelerometers

A MEMS accelerometer suspends a tiny proof mass on silicon springs. Acceleration displaces the mass, and interdigitated comb electrodes measure the displacement as a differential capacitance change [26]. Because gravity is an acceleration, a static accelerometer measures tilt. The ADXL345 offers selectable ±2/±4/±8/±16 g ranges with up to 13-bit resolution at a constant 4 mg/LSB over SPI or I²C [32]. The MPU-6050 combines a 3-axis accelerometer (±2, ±4, ±8 or ±16 g) and a 3-axis gyroscope with 16-bit ADCs and an on-chip Digital Motion Processor [31]. The MPU-6050 is a legacy part that is still widely taught, and newer IMUs follow the same principles.

Gyroscopes

MEMS gyroscopes keep a proof mass vibrating along one axis. Rotation produces a Coriolis force perpendicular to that motion, and the resulting secondary vibration is proportional to angular rate [26]. The MPU-6050 gyro offers ±250, ±500, ±1000 and ±2000 °/s full-scale ranges [31]. Integrating rate gives angle, but bias errors accumulate as drift. That is why gyros are fused with accelerometers, which are noisy but drift-free, and magnetometers using complementary or gradient-descent filters [45,46]. A simple complementary filter is:

θk = α·(θk−1 + ω·Δt) + (1 − α)·θaccel,   with α ≈ 0.95 to 0.99

Magnetometers

Magnetometers measure magnetic flux density with Hall-effect, anisotropic magnetoresistive (AMR) or fluxgate elements [26,47]. The Earth's field at the surface is roughly 25 to 65 µT [48], so an electronic compass needs a sensitive part such as the LIS3MDL (±4/±8/±12/±16 gauss full scale, where 1 gauss = 100 µT) [33]. It also needs calibration: hard-iron offsets from nearby magnets shift the measurement sphere, and soft-iron distortions from nearby ferrous metal squash it into an ellipsoid. Heading must also be corrected for magnetic declination, which the World Magnetic Model provides [48].

MicroPython: read raw acceleration from an MPU-6050[31,44]
from machine import Pin, I2C
import struct, time

i2c = I2C(0, scl=Pin(22), sda=Pin(21), freq=400_000)
MPU = 0x68
assert i2c.readfrom_mem(MPU, 0x75, 1)[0] == 0x68    # WHO_AM_I
i2c.writeto_mem(MPU, 0x6B, b'\x00')                  # PWR_MGMT_1: wake from sleep

while True:
    raw = i2c.readfrom_mem(MPU, 0x3B, 6)             # ACCEL_XOUT_H .. ACCEL_ZOUT_L
    ax, ay, az = struct.unpack('>hhh', raw)          # big-endian signed 16-bit
    g = 16384                                        # LSB per g at default ±2 g range
    print("a = %.2f %.2f %.2f g" % (ax/g, ay/g, az/g))
    time.sleep_ms(200)

3.7 Light, optical and infrared sensors

Light sensors. Photodiodes generate a photocurrent proportional to irradiance. Phototransistors add gain, and light-dependent resistors (cadmium-sulfide cells) are slow but cheap [26]. Ambient light sensors like the Vishay VEML7700 integrate a photodiode with a spectral response close to the human eye, an amplifier and a 16-bit converter, and report illuminance in lux over I²C [34].

Infrared sensors fall into three groups [26]:

  • Passive infrared (PIR) motion detectors use a pyroelectric element, which produces charge only when its temperature changes, behind a segmented Fresnel lens. A warm body crossing the lens zones produces an alternating signal, which makes PIR the standard low-power occupancy sensor.
  • Thermopile sensors measure the net IR power exchanged with an object to infer its temperature without contact. The Melexis MLX90614 is factory-calibrated for object temperatures from −70 to +382.2 °C and ambient −40 to +125 °C, with ±0.5 °C accuracy around room temperature, and outputs over SMBus or PWM [35].
  • Active IR: an IR LED plus a photodiode for reflective proximity and line-following, and modulated IR links for remote controls.

3.8 Gas and chemical sensors

Gas sensing is the least "plug-and-play" sensing category, because selectivity, drift and warm-up dominate the error budget. The main technologies are:

  • Metal-oxide semiconductor (MOX): a heated SnO₂ (or similar) film changes resistance when reducing or oxidising gases react with adsorbed oxygen on its surface. Resistance follows a power law of gas concentration [49]. MOX sensors are cheap and sensitive but not very selective. Bosch's BME688 adds programmable heater profiles and machine-learning tools to distinguish gas "fingerprints" [50].
  • Non-dispersive infrared (NDIR): CO₂ absorbs IR strongly near 4.26 µm, so the absorption along an optical path gives concentration. Sensirion's SCD4x uses a photoacoustic variant, detecting the pressure wave produced when pulsed IR is absorbed. This allows a much smaller package, with accuracy specified as a fixed ppm offset plus a percentage of reading [26,36].
  • Electrochemical cells: the target gas (CO, NO₂, O₂ and others) is oxidised or reduced at an electrode, producing a current proportional to concentration. They are selective and low-power but have finite lifetimes [26].

Design tip. MOX heaters draw tens of milliwatts, and NDIR sources pulse at high current. Budget for these in battery designs, and allow for the manufacturer's specified warm-up and burn-in before trusting readings [36,50].

3.9 Acoustic and vibration sensors

Acoustic. MEMS capacitive microphones, a flexible diaphragm over a perforated backplate, have largely replaced electret capsules in connected devices because they are small, reflow-solderable and consistent [51]. Many integrate the ADC and output digital audio as PDM or I²S. The I²S bus carries stereo PCM on three lines: a bit clock (SCK), a word-select line (WS) indicating the left or right channel, and serial data (SD) [52]. The ESP32 has two I²S controllers suitable for microphones and speakers [8].

Vibration. Condition monitoring of motors, pumps and fans is one of the most valuable industrial IoT applications. MEMS or piezoelectric accelerometers are mounted on the machine housing, and the signal is analysed in the frequency domain (via FFT) to spot imbalance, misalignment or bearing faults. ISO 20816-1 sets out general guidelines for evaluating broadband vibration on machine housings, including evaluation zones that classify severity [53]. The sampling rate must be at least twice the highest frequency of interest, and in practice considerably higher, to avoid aliasing (Section 5.1) [54].

3.10 GPS/GNSS

Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou) broadcast precisely timed ranging signals. A receiver measures pseudoranges to the satellites and solves for four unknowns: three position coordinates plus its own clock offset. That is why at least four satellites are needed for a 3D fix [55]. The US government's SPS Performance Standard documents the accuracy commitments for the civilian GPS signal [56]. Under open sky, single-frequency receivers typically achieve a few metres, while multi-constellation, dual-frequency and RTK techniques improve this to centimetres [55].

Most GNSS modules output NMEA 0183 sentences, which are comma-separated ASCII lines such as $GPGGA (fix data) and $GPRMC (recommended minimum data), over a UART [57]. Key IoT concerns are time-to-first-fix, antenna placement and current draw (tens of mA while tracking). Duty-cycling the receiver, or using assisted GNSS, saves battery.

MicroPython: parse latitude and longitude from a GGA sentence[44,57]
from machine import UART, Pin
gps = UART(2, baudrate=9600, tx=Pin(17), rx=Pin(16))   # check your module's default baud

def dm_to_deg(dm, hemi):
    # NMEA gives ddmm.mmmm (lat) or dddmm.mmmm (lon)
    v = float(dm); d = int(v // 100); m = v - d * 100
    deg = d + m / 60
    return -deg if hemi in ('S', 'W') else deg

while True:
    line = gps.readline()
    if not line: continue
    s = line.decode('ascii', 'ignore').strip()
    if s[3:6] == 'GGA':                       # matches $GPGGA, $GNGGA, ...
        f = s.split(',')
        if f[6] != '0' and f[2]:              # fix quality 0 = no fix
            print(dm_to_deg(f[2], f[3]), dm_to_deg(f[4], f[5]), "sats:", f[7])

3.11 Image sensors

Nearly all embedded cameras now use CMOS active-pixel sensors. Each pixel contains a pinned photodiode and transistors for reset, charge transfer and readout. The pinned photodiode lowered dark current and enabled complete charge transfer, which is what lets CMOS image quality rival or surpass CCDs [58]. Important parameters are resolution, pixel size, which drives low-light sensitivity, dynamic range, frame rate and shutter type. Rolling-shutter sensors expose rows sequentially and distort fast motion. Global-shutter sensors expose all pixels at once, which suits machine vision. Embedded cameras connect over MIPI CSI-2. The Raspberry Pi camera documentation lists its modules, for example the 12-megapixel Sony IMX708 in Camera Module 3, and the supporting libcamera/Picamera2 software [17]. Section 2.6 describes the accelerators that process the resulting frames.

3.12 Current and voltage sensors

Measuring current and voltage is essential for energy monitoring, battery fuel-gauging and fault detection.

  • Voltage: scale with a resistor divider into the ADC range, buffered if the source impedance is high (Section 5.14).
  • Shunt-based current sensing: a small series resistor develops a voltage V = I·R that a current-sense amplifier measures. The TI INA219 integrates the amplifier, a 12-bit ADC and a multiplier to report bus voltage (0 to 26 V), shunt current and power over I²C [37].
  • Hall-effect current sensing: the conductor's magnetic field is measured by a Hall element, giving galvanic isolation and very low insertion loss. The Allegro ACS712 routes the current through an internal 1.2 mΩ copper conductor. Its 5 A version outputs VCC/2 at zero current with 185 mV/A sensitivity [38,47].
  • Current transformers (AC only) and Rogowski coils for mains-scale non-invasive metering [26].

3.13 Environmental sensors

"Environmental" sensing combines the quantities above into air-quality, weather and agricultural stations: temperature, humidity and pressure (BME280) [29], volatile organic compounds and gas signatures (BME688) [50], CO₂ (SCD4x) [36], ambient light (VEML7700) [34], and particulate matter, which is measured by optical scattering of a laser beam by airborne particles [26]. Good practice for these stations includes mounting sensors away from the self-heating of the processor and regulator, shielding them from direct sun and rain while allowing airflow, and recording calibration and firmware versions alongside the data.

4. Actuators

Actuators convert electrical energy into motion, force, heat or flow. They usually need far more power than a microcontroller pin can deliver, so an actuator subsystem has three layers: the control signal from the MCU (GPIO, PWM or a bus), a driver stage (transistor, H-bridge or dedicated motor driver IC), and the actuator with its own power supply.

4.1 Driving loads safely

MCU pins source or sink only milliamps. The ATmega328P's absolute maximum is 40 mA per I/O pin [12], and 3.3 V parts are often lower. Use a logic-level MOSFET or BJT as a low-side switch for anything larger. Inductive loads such as relay coils, solenoids and motors store energy in their magnetic field, and when the switch opens the collapsing field produces a large reverse voltage spike (V = L·di/dt). A flyback diode across the coil gives that current a safe path and protects the transistor [59].

4.2 Electric motors, servo motors and stepper motors

Electric motors

In a brushed DC motor, torque is proportional to armature current and speed is roughly proportional to applied voltage minus the IR drop. The spinning motor generates a back-EMF proportional to speed [60]. Speed is usually controlled with PWM, and direction by reversing polarity with an H-bridge (Figure 4). Brushless DC (BLDC) motors replace the brushes with electronic commutation driven by Hall sensors or sensorless back-EMF detection, giving higher efficiency and lifetime, as in drones, fans and pumps. AC induction motors dominate industrial loads and are speed-controlled by variable-frequency drives [60]. Integrated drivers such as the TI DRV8833 contain two H-bridges, each rated at 1.5 A RMS (2 A peak), over a 2.7 to 10.8 V supply [61].

Servo motors

A servo is a motor, gearbox, position sensor and closed-loop controller in one package. Hobby RC servos take a pulse every 20 ms (50 Hz), and the pulse width, nominally about 1 to 2 ms with many models accepting about 0.5 to 2.5 ms, commands the shaft angle. Espressif's MCPWM servo example uses a 50 Hz period with a 500 to 2500 µs pulse range [62]. Industrial servo drives use the same closed-loop principle with encoders and field-oriented control [60].

MicroPython (ESP32): sweep a hobby servo[44,62]
from machine import Pin, PWM
import time

servo = PWM(Pin(18), freq=50)                 # 20 ms period

def angle(deg, min_us=500, max_us=2500):      # adjust to your servo's datasheet
    us = min_us + (max_us - min_us) * deg / 180
    servo.duty_ns(int(us * 1000))

for d in (0, 90, 180, 90):
    angle(d); time.sleep(1)

Stepper motors

Steppers move in discrete angular steps by energising coils in sequence. A common hybrid stepper has a 1.8° step, which is 200 full steps per revolution, and holds position without feedback as long as it does not lose steps under load [60]. Microstepping drivers apply sinusoidally weighted coil currents to subdivide each step for smoother, quieter motion. The TI DRV8825, for example, provides up to 1/32 microstepping, operates from 8.2 to 45 V and delivers up to 2.5 A per bridge (at 24 V, 25 °C), and is controlled through simple STEP and DIR inputs [63].

Arduino: drive a stepper through a DRV8825 (STEP/DIR)[41,63]
const int STEP_PIN = 3, DIR_PIN = 4;
const int STEPS_PER_REV = 200;        // 1.8° motor, full-step mode (M0..M2 low)

void setup() {
  pinMode(STEP_PIN, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(DIR_PIN, OUTPUT);
}

void rotate(int steps, bool cw, unsigned int us_per_step) {
  digitalWrite(DIR_PIN, cw ? HIGH : LOW);
  for (int i = 0; i < steps; i++) {
    digitalWrite(STEP_PIN, HIGH); delayMicroseconds(2);   // > minimum STEP high time
    digitalWrite(STEP_PIN, LOW);  delayMicroseconds(us_per_step);
  }
}

void loop() {
  rotate(STEPS_PER_REV, true, 1000);   // one revolution clockwise
  delay(500);
  rotate(STEPS_PER_REV, false, 1000);
  delay(500);
}

4.3 Relays and solenoids

Electromechanical relays use a coil to pull an armature that opens or closes isolated contacts, so a 3.3 V MCU can switch mains loads. Key ratings are coil voltage and current, contact current and voltage (AC versus DC ratings differ, because DC arcs are harder to extinguish), and mechanical life [59]. The coil is inductive and needs the driver and flyback diode of Figure 3. Solid-state relays (SSRs) use an optocoupled triac or MOSFET output. They are silent and wear-free but dissipate heat and have some leakage current [59].

Solenoids are linear actuators: a coil pulls a ferromagnetic plunger. They are used in door locks, latches and valves. Force falls sharply as the air gap grows. A common energy-saving technique applies full voltage to pull in, then reduces it with PWM to a lower holding current [59].

Mains safety. Switching mains voltage requires proper creepage and clearance on the PCB, fusing, enclosures and compliance with local electrical codes (see Section 5.13) [64]. Prototype with low-voltage loads first.

4.4 Hydraulic and pneumatic actuators, valves and pumps

Fluid power transmits force through a pressurised fluid. Pascal's law gives the force from a cylinder as F = p·A, so 10 MPa (100 bar) acting on a 0.001 m² piston (about 36 mm diameter) produces 10 kN [65].

  • Hydraulic actuators use nearly incompressible oil. They deliver very high force density and stiff, precise positioning, as in excavators, presses and aircraft control surfaces. The costs are pumps, leaks and maintenance [65].
  • Pneumatic actuators use compressed air, which is clean, cheap, fast and inherently compliant. Because air is compressible, precise mid-stroke positioning is harder, so pneumatic cylinders often run end-to-end in pick-and-place and packaging [65].
  • Valves direct and modulate flow. Solenoid valves are on/off devices, either normally open (NO) or normally closed (NC). Directional control valves such as 3/2 and 5/2 types steer air or oil to cylinder ports. Proportional and servo valves vary flow continuously from an analog or PWM command [65]. Motorised ball valves are popular in smart irrigation and leak-shutoff IoT products.
  • Pumps are either positive-displacement (gear, piston, diaphragm, peristaltic), which move a fixed volume per cycle and are ideal for dosing, or dynamic (centrifugal), which suit high flow at lower pressure [65]. In IoT they are typically DC or BLDC motor-driven and controlled by PWM, with flow or pressure sensors closing the loop.

4.5 Heating and cooling elements

Resistive heaters (wire, film or PTC ceramic) convert power P = I²R = V²/R entirely into heat. They are switched by MOSFETs or relays (DC) or by zero-cross SSRs (AC), with a PID loop reading a temperature sensor. PTC heaters self-limit, because their resistance rises sharply at a design temperature [26]. Thermoelectric (Peltier) modules pump heat from one face to the other when current flows, using the Peltier effect in arrays of p- and n-type semiconductor couples. Reversing the current reverses the direction, so the same module can heat or cool. Their coefficient of performance is modest, so the hot side needs a good heat sink [66]. Fans and compressors are motors (Section 4.2) and are controlled with PWM or variable-frequency drives [60].

4.6 Robotic mechanisms

Robotic mechanisms combine actuators with links, joints and transmissions (gears, belts, lead screws, harmonic drives) to produce controlled motion in several degrees of freedom (DOF) [67]. A planar two-link arm with link lengths L₁ and L₂ and joint angles θ₁ and θ₂ places its end-effector at:

x = L₁·cos θ₁ + L₂·cos(θ₁ + θ₂)     y = L₁·sin θ₁ + L₂·sin(θ₁ + θ₂)

This is forward kinematics. Inverse kinematics solves for the joint angles that reach a desired point and is generally harder, with multiple or no solutions [67]. IoT-connected robots, including mobile robots, collaborative arms and grippers, add sensing (encoders, force/torque sensors, cameras), real-time control loops on MCUs or FPGAs, and supervisory planning on an MPU or GPU, which ties together all the hardware in this article.

5. Supporting electronics

Between sensors, processors and actuators sits the electronics that converts signals, moves data and delivers clean power. Most of it is integrated in the MCU, but understanding it is what separates a reliable product from a fragile prototype.

5.1 Analog-to-digital converters (ADC)

An N-bit ADC maps an input range of 0 to Vref onto 2N codes. The step size, or LSB, is Vref/2N, and an ideal converter has a quantisation error of ±½ LSB [68]. For a full-scale sine wave, ideal quantisation noise limits the signal-to-noise ratio to:

SNRideal = 6.02·N + 1.76 dB     ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76) / 6.02

Real converters fall short of this because of thermal noise, clock jitter and non-linearity, so the effective number of bits (ENOB) is the honest figure of merit [68,69]. Sampling must also satisfy the Nyquist–Shannon criterion: a signal containing no frequencies above B is fully determined by samples taken at more than 2B per second [54]. Frequencies above half the sampling rate alias, folding down onto false low frequencies, so an analog anti-aliasing low-pass filter must precede the ADC (Section 5.14).

Architectures. MCUs mostly embed successive-approximation (SAR) ADCs. These perform a binary search with a capacitor DAC and comparator, offering moderate resolution at up to a few MSPS. Sigma-delta converters oversample and noise-shape to reach 16 to 24 bits at lower rates, which suits weighing scales and precision sensors. Flash and pipelined converters serve very high speeds [68]. When the built-in ADC is not good enough, an external part such as the TI ADS1115 (16-bit, up to 860 SPS, programmable-gain amplifier, I²C) is a common upgrade [70].

5.2 Digital-to-analog converters (DAC)

A DAC does the reverse, turning a digital code into a voltage Vout = Vref·code/2N. Common architectures are the R-2R resistor ladder, resistor strings and current-steering arrays. Oversampled sigma-delta DACs are used for audio [68]. IoT uses include setting bias and threshold voltages, generating audio or test waveforms, and driving analog actuators such as proportional valves (4 to 20 mA loops). Options include on-chip DACs (the ESP32 has two 8-bit channels [8], and the STM32F405 has two 12-bit channels [7]), external DACs like the Microchip MCP4725 (12-bit, I²C, with EEPROM that restores the output at power-up) [71], and PWM plus an RC low-pass filter, a cheap approximate DAC whose ripple trades off against settling time (Section 5.4).

from machine import DAC, Pin

table = [int(127.5 + 127.5 * math.sin(2 * math.pi * i / 64)) for i in range(64)]

5.3 General-purpose input/output (GPIO)

A GPIO pin can be configured as an input (floating, with internal pull-up or pull-down), as a push-pull output that actively drives high and low, or as an open-drain output that only pulls low and relies on a pull-up resistor. Open-drain is required for wired-AND buses such as I²C [59,72]. Pins usually also carry an alternate function such as UART TX, a PWM channel or an ADC input, selected through a multiplexer [6,9]. Important electrical limits are the logic thresholds (VIL, VIH), drive current, and whether a pin tolerates 5 V. The ESP32's pins are not 5 V tolerant [8], while the ATmega328P runs natively at 5 V [12]. When connecting 3.3 V and 5 V logic, use a level shifter.

Interrupts and debouncing. Rather than polling, configure a pin to interrupt on a rising or falling edge. Mechanical switches bounce for milliseconds, so filter them with an RC network and a Schmitt-trigger input, or in software by ignoring edges for 10 to 50 ms [59].

5.4 Pulse-width modulation (PWM)

PWM switches an output fully on and off at a fixed frequency and varies the fraction of each period that it is on, called the duty cycle D. The average voltage is D·Vsupply. Because the transistor is either fully on or fully off, it dissipates little power, which is why PWM drives LEDs, motors, heaters, servos and switch-mode power supplies [60,73].

Frequency versus resolution. A timer counting a clock fclk up to 2N gives PWM frequency f = fclk/2N, so higher frequency means fewer duty-cycle steps. The ESP32's LEDC peripheral states the constraint explicitly: the product of frequency and 2resolution cannot exceed the source clock, for example 80 MHz APB [74]. Choose the frequency for the load: above about 20 kHz to be inaudible in motors, around 50 Hz for hobby servos, and high enough to avoid visible flicker for LEDs. On an Arduino Uno, analogWrite() produces about 490 Hz on most PWM pins and about 980 Hz on pins 5 and 6 [41].

5.5 UART

A universal asynchronous receiver/transmitter sends bytes without a shared clock. Each frame starts with a low start bit, carries 5 to 9 data bits (least-significant bit first), an optional parity bit and one or two high stop bits. Both ends must agree on the baud rate [59]. The near-universal format "8N1" uses 10 bit-times per byte, so 115 200 baud carries 11 520 bytes/s. UART is point-to-point (TX to RX, RX to TX, common ground) and is used for debug consoles, GNSS modules, cellular modems and Bluetooth modules. Its physical layer can be extended: RS-232 uses bipolar voltages for short cables [75], and RS-485 uses differential signalling on a multi-drop bus with up to 32 unit loads, the basis of Modbus RTU in industrial IoT [76].

5.6 SPI

The Serial Peripheral Interface, which originated at Motorola, is a synchronous, full-duplex controller/peripheral bus with four signals: SCLK (clock), MOSI/COPI (controller out), MISO/CIPO (controller in) and an active-low chip select per peripheral [77]. Clock polarity (CPOL) and phase (CPHA) define four modes, and controller and peripheral must use the same one. SPI has no addressing, acknowledgement or fixed speed limit, so it routinely runs at tens of MHz for displays, flash memory, SD cards and high-rate sensors [77]. The trade-off is pin count, since each peripheral needs its own chip-select line.

MicroPython: read the ADXL345 device ID over SPI (mode 3)[32,44]
from machine import SPI, Pin
spi = SPI(1, baudrate=1_000_000, polarity=1, phase=1,      # CPOL=1, CPHA=1
          sck=Pin(14), mosi=Pin(13), miso=Pin(12))
cs = Pin(15, Pin.OUT, value=1)

def read_reg(reg, n=1):
    cmd = 0x80 | reg | (0x40 if n > 1 else 0)               # read bit, multi-byte bit
    cs(0); spi.write(bytes([cmd])); data = spi.read(n); cs(1)
    return data

print("DEVID = 0x%02X (expect 0xE5)" % read_reg(0x00)[0])

5.7 I²C

I²C (Inter-Integrated Circuit), which originated at Philips (now NXP), uses just two open-drain lines, SDA (data) and SCL (clock), shared by many devices that are selected by 7-bit (or 10-bit) addresses [72]. A transaction begins with a START condition (SDA falls while SCL is high), sends the address and a read/write bit, and each byte is followed by an acknowledge bit. The bus supports Standard-mode (100 kbit/s), Fast-mode (400 kbit/s), Fast-mode Plus (1 Mbit/s), High-speed mode (3.4 Mbit/s) and a unidirectional Ultra Fast-mode (5 Mbit/s) [72]. Because the drivers only pull low, pull-up resistors set the rising edge. The specification bounds them as follows:

Rp(min) = (VDD − VOL(max)) / IOL     Rp(max) = tr / (0.8473 · Cb)

Here tr is the maximum rise time (1000 ns in Standard-mode, 300 ns in Fast-mode) and Cb is the total bus capacitance, limited to 400 pF in these modes [72]. Address clashes are common, because many sensors offer only one or two selectable addresses. Bus multiplexers solve this.

5.8 CAN bus

The Controller Area Network was developed by Bosch for in-vehicle networking and introduced publicly in 1986 [78]. It is standardised as ISO 11898 [79,80]. CAN is a multi-master broadcast bus on a twisted pair (CAN_H and CAN_L), terminated with 120 Ω at each end to match the cable's characteristic impedance [81]. A logical 0 is dominant (the lines are driven apart) and a logical 1 is recessive (the lines float together). When two nodes transmit at once, they arbitrate bit by bit on the message identifier: the node sending a recessive bit that reads back dominant stops transmitting. The lowest identifier therefore wins without any frame being destroyed [80,81]. Classical CAN runs at up to 1 Mbit/s with 0 to 8 data bytes per frame and 11-bit (standard) or 29-bit (extended) identifiers. Frames carry a CRC, and nodes use error counters that isolate faulty participants [79,80]. CAN FD raises the data phase bit rate and extends payloads to 64 bytes [79]. Beyond cars, CAN (often with CANopen or J1939 on top) is common in industrial machines, agriculture, e-bikes and robotics.

MCUs contain the CAN controller (framing, arbitration, error handling). An external transceiver, such as TI's 3.3 V SN65HVD230 [82], converts between logic levels and the differential bus. The ESP32 calls its controller TWAI, and it is compatible with ISO 11898-1 classical CAN frames but not CAN FD [9,83].

5.9 Power management

For battery IoT, average current is what matters, and it is dominated by how long the device spends asleep. A duty-cycled node's average current is:

Iavg = (Iactive·tactive + Isleep·tsleep) / (tactive + tsleep)     Battery life ≈ Capacity / Iavg

MCUs offer a ladder of low-power modes, each trading wake-up time and retained state for current. On the ESP32 these are modem sleep, light sleep, deep sleep (about 10 µA with the RTC timer and RTC memory) and hibernation [8]. Practical techniques include sleeping between measurements, batching radio transmissions (the radio is usually the biggest consumer), switching off sensors through a load switch or GPIO-powered rail, lowering clock speed, and eliminating leakage paths such as LEDs, pull-ups and resistor dividers left permanently connected.

5.10 Voltage regulation

Linear regulators and LDOs drop the excess voltage across a pass transistor. They are simple, quiet and cheap, but they dissipate P = (Vin − Vout)·I, so efficiency is at best Vout/Vin. Key parameters are dropout voltage, quiescent current (critical in sleep), PSRR and stability with the chosen output capacitor [85]. Switching regulators store energy in an inductor and switch it at hundreds of kHz to MHz, reaching about 85 to 95 % efficiency across large voltage differences [73]. A buck converter steps down, with ideal duty cycle D = Vout/Vin. TI's design note refines this to D = Vout/(Vin·η) and gives the inductor ripple ΔIL = (Vin − Vout)·D/(fs·L) [86]. A boost converter steps up, and buck-boost converters keep a 3.3 V rail alive as a Li-ion cell discharges from 4.2 V to below 3.3 V [73].

Rule of thumb. Use an LDO when Vin is close to Vout and the current is small, or when noise matters (ADC and RF references). Use a buck or buck-boost converter when the voltage drop or the current is large. For µA-sleep designs, compare quiescent currents. A regulator with 50 µA Iq can use more charge than the MCU it powers [73,85].

5.11 Battery management

Lithium-ion and lithium-polymer cells (nominally 3.6 to 3.7 V, fully charged at 4.2 V for standard chemistries) dominate rechargeable IoT. They must be charged with a constant-current/constant-voltage (CC/CV) profile: constant current until the cell reaches the regulation voltage, then constant voltage while the current tapers to a termination threshold [84]. The Microchip MCP73831, for example, is a single-cell linear charger with preset regulation voltages (4.20 V and higher variants), a charge current programmed by one resistor (IREG = 1000 V/RPROG) and a status output [87]. A full battery management system (BMS) also provides protection (over-voltage, under-voltage, over-current, short-circuit, temperature), state-of-charge estimation by coulomb counting (integrating current over time) corrected with voltage-based models, state-of-health tracking, and cell balancing in multi-cell packs [84]. Primary cells such as Li-SOCl₂ and alkaline remain common for 10-year metering devices, because they have very low self-discharge.

5.12 Energy harvesting

Energy harvesting, which scavenges ambient energy, can extend or eliminate batteries [88]. Sources and typical scales:

  • Solar / photovoltaic: the standard test condition for PV is 1000 W/m² [89]. Indoor lighting is orders of magnitude weaker, so indoor harvesters need carefully sized cells [88].
  • Vibration: piezoelectric, electromagnetic or electrostatic transducers tuned to machine vibration frequencies. Roundy, Wright and Rabaey characterised low-level vibration sources and showed that they can power wireless sensor nodes [90].
  • Thermal: thermoelectric generators across temperature gradients, for example a pipe or the human body [66,88].
  • RF: rectified ambient or dedicated RF fields, as used in RFID [88].

Harvesters deliver tiny, intermittent power at awkward voltages, so a dedicated power-management IC is needed. The TI BQ25570 cold-starts from 600 mV, then keeps harvesting from inputs as low as 100 mV. It charges a storage element (supercapacitor or battery) using maximum-power-point tracking and provides a regulated buck output, drawing only 488 nA of quiescent current [91]. Firmware must be energy-aware: measure stored energy and adapt its duty cycle to match.

5.13 PCB design

The printed circuit board is itself a component. Key practices for IoT hardware:

  • Stack-up and ground: use a solid ground plane, ideally on a 4-layer board, to give every signal a low-inductance return path directly beneath it. Avoid splitting the plane under high-speed traces [92].
  • Decoupling: place small ceramic capacitors (typically 100 nF) as close as possible to each IC supply pin, plus bulk capacitance near regulators, to supply transient current locally [59,92].
  • Trace width and current: IPC-2221 relates allowable current to cross-sectional area and temperature rise with I = k·ΔT0.44·A0.725 (A in mil², k ≈ 0.048 for external and 0.024 for internal layers) [64]. The newer IPC-2152 replaces those charts with more accurate, test-derived data [93].
  • Clearance and creepage: IPC-2221 tabulates minimum conductor spacing versus voltage. Mains designs also need safety-standard creepage and isolation slots [64].
  • RF and antennas: follow the module vendor's keep-out area (no copper under or near the antenna) and use 50 Ω controlled-impedance feeds [8,92].
  • EMC: keep switching-regulator hot loops small, route clocks away from board edges, and filter cables leaving the board [92].
  • Design for test and manufacture: add test points, a programming and debug header (SWD, JTAG, UART), fiducials and silkscreen labels.

Open-source KiCad supports the whole flow, from schematic capture and footprint libraries to PCB layout with design-rule checking, 3D view and Gerber output [94].

5.14 Signal conditioning

Raw sensor signals are rarely ready for an ADC. Signal conditioning adapts them [59,95]:

  • Amplification: op-amps in non-inverting (gain 1 + Rf/Rg) or inverting configurations. Instrumentation amplifiers extract the small differential output of Wheatstone bridges (strain gauges, pressure sensors) while rejecting common-mode noise.
  • Filtering: an RC low-pass filter has its cut-off at fc = 1/(2πRC). Placed before the ADC, it serves as the anti-aliasing filter [68].
  • Level shifting and scaling: resistor dividers, offset references and rail-to-rail buffers map a sensor's range onto 0 to Vref. A buffer prevents a high-impedance divider from loading the ADC's sampling capacitor.
  • Protection: series resistors, clamp diodes and TVS devices protect inputs from ESD and over-voltage.
  • Isolation: optocouplers and digital isolators protect the electronics and users when sensing high voltages.
  • Noise awareness: every resistor generates thermal (Johnson) noise vn = √(4kTRΔf), about 4 nV/√Hz for 1 kΩ at room temperature [96]. Keep source impedances low in precision front ends.

7. Exercises with solutions

Try each exercise before opening its solution.

E1. ADC resolution. An ESP32 reads a 0 to 3.3 V signal with its 12-bit ADC. What is the ideal LSB size, and what voltage does code 1500 represent? What is the ideal SNR?

Solution

LSB = 3.3 V / 4096 ≈ 0.806 mV. Code 1500 → 1500 × 0.806 mV ≈ 1.209 V. Ideal SNR = 6.02 × 12 + 1.76 ≈ 74.0 dB [68]. In practice the ESP32 ADC is non-linear, so use the calibration API, which converts raw codes to millivolts [10].

E2. LM35 range and resolution. An LM35 is read by an Arduino Uno using the internal 1.1 V reference. What is the maximum measurable temperature and the temperature resolution?

Solution

The maximum is 1.1 V ÷ 10 mV/°C = 110 °C. Resolution = (1.1 V/1024) ÷ 10 mV/°C ≈ 0.107 °C per count [12,27].

E3. PWM on an ESP32. You need a 20 kHz motor PWM from the LEDC peripheral clocked at 80 MHz. What is the maximum duty resolution in whole bits?

Solution

2N ≤ 80 MHz / 20 kHz = 4000, so N = 11 bits (2048 steps), because 212 = 4096 exceeds 4000 [74].

E4. I²C pull-ups. A 3.3 V Fast-mode (400 kHz) bus has 150 pF of capacitance. Find the allowed pull-up range.

Solution

Rp(min) = (3.3 − 0.4) / 3 mA ≈ 967 Ω. Rp(max) = 300 ns / (0.8473 × 150 pF) ≈ 2.36 kΩ. A standard 2.2 kΩ value fits [72].

E5. UART throughput. A GNSS module outputs about 450 bytes of NMEA data every second at 9600 baud, 8N1. What fraction of the link is used?

Solution

8N1 uses 10 bits per byte, so 9600 baud gives 960 bytes/s. 450/960 ≈ 47 % utilisation. Raising the update rate to 5 Hz (2250 bytes/s) would overflow the link, so the baud rate must increase [57,59].

E6. Battery life. A node sleeps at 10 µA and wakes every 10 minutes for 2 s at 120 mA to measure and transmit. Estimate the life on a 2000 mAh cell.

Solution

Charge per cycle = 120 mA × 2 s + 0.010 mA × 598 s = 240 + 5.98 = 245.98 mA·s. Iavg = 245.98/600 ≈ 0.41 mA. Life ≈ 2000/0.41 ≈ 4880 h ≈ 203 days, before derating. The active burst dominates (98 % of the charge), so shortening the radio-on time pays off most [8,84].

E7. LDO vs buck. A 3.3 V, 200 mA load is powered from a 12 V adapter. Compare the dissipation of an LDO with a buck converter at 90 % efficiency.

Solution

LDO: P = (12 − 3.3) × 0.2 = 1.74 W wasted, and efficiency ≈ 27.5 %. That would overheat a small SOT-23 package. Buck: Pout = 0.66 W, so Pin = 0.733 W and the loss ≈ 0.073 W. The buck converter is clearly the right choice [73,85].

E8. Buck duty cycle and ripple. A buck converter converts 5 V to 3.3 V at fs = 1 MHz with L = 2.2 µH and η = 0.9. Find D and ΔIL.

Solution

D = 3.3 / (5 × 0.9) ≈ 0.733. ΔIL = (5 − 3.3) × 0.733 / (1×10⁶ × 2.2×10⁻⁶) ≈ 0.57 A peak-to-peak [86].

E9. Stepper resolution. A 1.8° stepper drives a lead screw with an 8 mm lead through a DRV8825 at 1/16 microstepping. What is the linear distance per microstep?

Solution

Steps per revolution = 200 × 16 = 3200. Distance = 8 mm / 3200 = 2.5 µm per microstep. Microsteps improve smoothness more than they improve real positional accuracy under load [60,63].

E10. Barometric altitude. A BME280 reads 954.6 hPa. Estimate altitude using the standard-atmosphere formula with p₀ = 1013.25 hPa.

Solution

h = 44330 × [1 − (954.6/1013.25)0.1903] = 44330 × [1 − 0.98872] ≈ 500 m. Local weather changes p₀, so absolute altitude needs a reference reading [29,43].

E11. Anti-aliasing. A vibration sensor is sampled at 4 kHz. Choose an RC anti-alias filter with R = 10 kΩ for fc ≈ 1 kHz.

Solution

C = 1/(2π × 10 kΩ × 1 kHz) ≈ 15.9 nF, so use 15 nF (fc ≈ 1.06 kHz). A first-order filter only attenuates by about 6 dB per octave, so for serious vibration work, oversample or use a higher-order filter [54,68].

E12. Hydraulic force. What pressure does a 50 mm bore cylinder need to push 5 kN?

Solution

A = π × 0.025² ≈ 1.963×10⁻³ m². p = F/A = 5000/1.963×10⁻³ ≈ 2.55 MPa (25.5 bar) [65].

E13. Hall current sensor. An ACS712-05B on a 5 V supply outputs 2.87 V. What is the current?

Solution

Zero-current output = 2.5 V. I = (2.87 − 2.5) / 0.185 V/A ≈ 2.0 A [38].

E14. Design challenge. Sketch the hardware for a solar-powered vineyard node that measures air temperature and humidity, soil moisture and leaf wetness every 15 minutes, and opens a 12 V irrigation valve. Name a part for each block.

One possible answer

MCU/radio: an ESP32-class SoC for Wi-Fi, or a LoRa MCU for long range [8]. Sensors: BME280 on I²C [29], capacitive soil probes on ADC channels with a buffer and RC filter [95]. Actuator: a 12 V NC solenoid valve switched by a logic-level MOSFET with a flyback diode [59]. Power: solar panel → BQ25570 harvester → Li-ion or supercapacitor storage [91], or a CC/CV charger such as MCP73831 [87], then a low-Iq regulator [85]. Firmware: deep sleep between readings, with all sensors powered from a switched rail. PCB: ground plane, antenna keep-out, conformal coating [92].

8. Trivia quiz

Quick-fire questions, each with its answer and source.

  1. Which company created the I²C bus?
    Answer

    Philips (its semiconductor business is now NXP) [72].

  2. At which event was CAN officially introduced in 1986?
    Answer

    The SAE (Society of Automotive Engineers) congress in Detroit, by Bosch [78].

  3. What resistance should you measure between CAN_H and CAN_L on a correctly terminated, unpowered bus?
    Answer

    About 60 Ω, because two 120 Ω terminators are in parallel [81].

  4. Which instruction set do Armv7-M Cortex-M processors execute?
    Answer

    Only Thumb (the mixed 16/32-bit Thumb-2 encoding), not the classic 32-bit Arm instruction set [3].

  5. How many instructions does the PIC16F877A have?
    Answer

    35 single-word instructions [13].

  6. What is the output of an LM35 at 100 °C?
    Answer

    1.00 V (10 mV/°C) [27].

  7. What is the resistance of a Pt100 at 0 °C?
    Answer

    100 Ω, by definition in IEC 60751 [40].

  8. Why does a GNSS receiver need at least four satellites for a 3D fix, not three?
    Answer

    It must also solve for its own clock offset, which is a fourth unknown [55].

  9. What value does the BME280 return from its chip-ID register 0xD0?
    Answer

    0x60 [29].

  10. What makes the BeagleBone Black's AM3358 special for hard real-time I/O?
    Answer

    Its two 200 MHz Programmable Real-time Units (PRU-ICSS), which run independently of the Linux cores [15,18].

  11. How large was the matrix multiply unit in Google's first TPU?
    Answer

    256 × 256 = 65 536 8-bit MAC units arranged as a systolic array [22].

  12. What is unusual about the Raspberry Pi 5's GPIO header compared with earlier Pis?
    Answer

    It is driven by RP1, Raspberry Pi's own I/O controller chip, rather than directly by the Broadcom SoC [16].

  13. In which year did J. B. Johnson publish his measurements of thermal noise in resistors?
    Answer

    1928, in Physical Review [96].

  14. Which company introduced SPI?
    Answer

    Motorola [77].

  15. How many full steps per revolution does a 1.8° stepper motor have?
    Answer

    200 [60].

  16. What is the efficiency of a Coral Edge TPU in TOPS per watt?
    Answer

    About 2 TOPS/W (4 TOPS at 2 W) [24].
