Language survey / Runtimes & ecosystems / July 2026

Ruby, thirty-three years in

A language designed for the pleasure of the person typing it, now carrying two just-in-time compilers, an ISO standard, a package registry with a quarter-trillion downloads, and a popularity chart pointing steadily downward. All four facts are true at once. This is an attempt to hold them together.

First public release0.95 · 21 Dec 1995
Reference implementationCRuby / YARV, written in C

Ruby is the rare language whose founding document is an emotional claim. Its creator set out to build something he would enjoy using, and said so plainly, at a time when language design was overwhelmingly argued in terms of machine efficiency. Three decades later that claim has been tested about as thoroughly as a claim can be: by a framework that reorganised web development, by a decade of scaling pain, by a serious performance-engineering campaign, and now by a slow decline in the share of programmers who reach for it first.

What follows is a technical survey rather than an advocacy piece. It covers the origins and the version history; the design philosophy and the aesthetic commitments that flow from it; the object model, runtime, and concurrency story; the weaknesses, stated without hedging; the current standing against other languages; and the products, open-source projects, libraries, frameworks, and integrations that constitute the working ecosystem. Every factual claim carries a Vancouver-numbered citation.

Conception

A name chosen before a line of code

Ruby has a birthday, and it precedes its first line of code. On 24 February 1993 Yukihiro Matsumoto and his colleague Keiju Ishitsuka settled the name of a language that did not yet exist, in an online chat. Two candidates were on the table, Coral and Ruby; both were gemstones, chosen in deliberate succession to Perl. Matsumoto picked Ruby in a follow-up message, and later noted that a colleague's birthstone had nudged the decision.[1–4]

The motivation is unusually well documented, because Matsumoto wrote it down six years later on the ruby-talk list. He knew Perl 4 and did not like it, finding it had the smell of a toy language. He knew Python and did not consider it genuinely object-oriented, judging that the object features looked bolted on. He wanted a real object-oriented, easy-to-use scripting language, looked for one, could not find it, and decided to build it.[1] He has described the result as a simple Lisp at its core, wearing Smalltalk's object system, with blocks derived from higher-order functions and the practical utility of Perl; the acknowledged influences also include Eiffel, Ada and BASIC.[1]

A language maniac and OO fan for fifteen years wanted a genuine object-oriented, easy-to-use scripting language. He looked for one, could not find it, and so decided to make it.Matsumoto, ruby-talk, 1999 [1]

The naming exchange itself survives: the two developers agreed they wanted a jewel, weighed coral against ruby, and settled it in a few lines.[5] Several months of interpreter writing later,[6] Ruby 0.95 was announced on Japanese domestic newsgroups on 21 December 1995 — three further versions followed within two days, and the release coincided with the launch of ruby-list, the language's first mailing list.[1,3] What is striking about that first public build is how little of the language's character was added later. Object-oriented design, classes with inheritance, mixins, iterators, closures, exception handling and garbage collection were all already present.[1]

Ruby 1.0 arrived on 25 December 1996, beginning a release ritual the project still keeps.[2] In 1997 the first article about Ruby appeared on the web and Matsumoto was hired by netlab.jp to work on the language full-time,[1] which produced a stable 1.2 on Christmas Day 1998.[2] Development began on 4.3BSD-derived Sony NEWS-OS, migrated to SunOS, and settled on Linux.[1]

A note on what "scripting language" meant in 1993

The category Matsumoto was writing into was a narrow one: glue for shell work, text munging and system administration, with object orientation treated as a heavyweight discipline belonging to compiled languages. Ruby's founding argument — that a scripting language could be uncompromisingly object-oriented all the way down to integers and nil — was a genuine position rather than a marketing line.[1]

1995–2011

From Japanese newsgroups to an ISO standard

For its first five years Ruby was a Japanese-language project. The Ruby Application Archive and a simple English homepage appeared in 1998; the English-language ruby-talk list started in 1999, the same year Matsumoto and Ishitsuka published the first book on the language in Japan, followed through the early 2000s by roughly twenty more Japanese titles.[1]

By 2000 Ruby was more popular than Python in Japan. In September of that year the first English-language book, Programming Ruby, was printed and subsequently released free to the public, which materially widened adoption among English speakers. By early 2002 ruby-talk was carrying more traffic than the Japanese ruby-list — a clean marker of the centre of gravity moving.[1]

Ruby 1.8 shipped in August 2003 and stayed stable for an unusually long time, retiring only in June 2013.[1] That longevity made it the dialect that got standardised: language specifications were developed by the Open Standards Promotion Center of Japan's Information-Technology Promotion Agency for submission first to the Japanese Industrial Standards Committee and then to ISO, becoming JIS X 3017 in 2011 and ISO/IEC 30170 in 2012.[1,7] Few dynamic languages of Ruby's generation carry an international standard at all.

The Rails inflection

Around 2005 interest in Ruby surged in tandem with Ruby on Rails, a web framework written in Ruby, and Rails is routinely credited with the rise in awareness of the language itself.[1] This is the single most consequential fact about Ruby's public life, and it cuts both ways: the language acquired an enormous audience it had not sought, and it acquired a reputation as a web-application language that has been difficult to shed.

Rails 3.0, in 2010, folded in the Merb team's priorities — framework agnosticism, slimmer and faster internals, a new router, and an Action Mailer API modelled on Action Controller — and required Ruby 1.8.7 or newer.[8] That merger settled a competitive fork in the ecosystem and is a fair marker for when Rails became infrastructure rather than a movement.

Licensing moved in the same period. From Ruby 1.9.3, released 31 October 2011, the language switched from dual-licensing under the Ruby License and the GPL to the Ruby License and the two-clause BSD licence.[1]

Release history

Three engineering projects in thirty years

Ruby's version history divides cleanly into three engineering projects: get a real virtual machine (1.9), stop breaking things (2.x), and get fast (3.x and 4.0). Each took roughly a decade, and each was more disruptive than the project publicly admitted at the time.

The bytecode break

The standardised 1.8 implementation was written in C as a single-pass interpreter. From 1.9 onward the official interpreter has been YARV, “Yet Another Ruby VM”, which superseded the slower machine used in earlier MRI releases.[1] This was the language's most expensive transition: 1.8 is only partially compatible with 1.9, and adoption was held back because many popular gems required rewriting.[1] The lesson the core team took from it is visible in everything since — Ruby 2.0 shipped with only five known incompatibilities against 1.9.3.[1]

3x3, and what actually delivered it

Ruby 3.0 landed on Christmas Day 2020 under the banner “Ruby 3x3”, a goal announced years earlier: programs should run three times faster than they did on Ruby 2.0.[1,9] The release carried MJIT and later YJIT as just-in-time compilers, both initially experimental and disabled by default, alongside two concurrency mechanisms — the Fiber Scheduler and the experimental Ractor — and RBS, a separate language for describing the types of Ruby programs for static analysis.[1,10]

Ruby 4.0

The version numbered 3.5 through its first preview in April 2025 was renumbered during the preview cycle and shipped on 25 December 2025 as Ruby 4.0.0.[11–13] The headline features are ZJIT, a next-generation JIT compiler, and Ruby Box, an experimental isolation mechanism enabled by setting RUBY_BOX=1. A box isolates the definitions loaded inside it: monkey patches, global and class variable changes, class and module definitions, and loaded native or Ruby libraries stay inside their box. The stated use cases are running tests that monkey-patch without contaminating other tests, running application boxes in parallel for blue-green deployment inside a single process, and evaluating dependency upgrades by diffing responses — with a higher-level package API named as a future possibility that is explicitly not yet designed.[13,14]

Beyond the headliners, 4.0 promoted Set and Pathname from library code to core classes, allowed leading && and || to continue the previous line the way a leading dot already did, bundled RubyGems and Bundler 4, and removed the CGI library from the default gems apart from the escaping helpers. The diff against 3.4.0 runs to 3,889 files changed and 230,769 insertions.[13,15] Unicode moved to version 17.0 and --rjit was removed, with the third-party JIT API relocated to its own repository.[13,16]

Release cadence and support window

A minor version ships each Christmas; the latest stable series then receives a patch release roughly every two months, with the schedule published in advance.[23,24] Each stable branch gets approximately two years of normal maintenance followed by about one year of security-only patches.[25] For teams, this is the most practically useful property of the project: the calendar is boring and the calendar is kept.

Design intent

Least surprise, correctly stated

Matsumoto has stated the goal so consistently for so long that it is easy to stop hearing it: Ruby is designed for programmer productivity and fun, following the principles of good user-interface design.[1] At a Google Tech Talk in 2008 he put it as a hope that Ruby would help every programmer in the world be productive, enjoy programming, and be happy, and named that as the language's primary purpose.[1] The corresponding methodological claim is that systems design should emphasise human rather than machine needs — that engineers habitually optimise for how fast the machine will run and thereby optimise the wrong variable.[1,26]

Least surprise, correctly stated

Ruby is persistently associated with the principle of least astonishment, and the association is a misreading that Matsumoto himself has tried to correct. He has said he did not apply the principle to Ruby's design, and in a 2005 comp.lang.ruby discussion explicitly distanced the language from it: any design choice surprises someone, so he evaluates surprise against a personal standard. If that standard stays consistent, there are few surprises for anyone who has learned it.[1,26]

The principle of least surprise is not for you only. It means the principle of least my surprise. And it means the principle of least surprise after you learn Ruby very well.Matsumoto, interviewed by Bill Venners [26]

This is a more defensible position than the folk version, and it explains features that puzzle newcomers. Ruby is internally consistent to a standard set by one person's taste, rather than externally consistent with the expectations someone brings from C++ or Python. The cost is a learning curve that feels arbitrary until it suddenly does not.

The commitments that follow

Four design decisions do most of the work, and each is a consequence of putting the reader and writer first.

  • Everything is an object. Integers, booleans and nil are objects; so are classes and modules. Every function is a method and every method is called on an object. Methods defined at top level become methods of Object, whose position as ancestor of every class makes them effectively global procedures.[1]
  • Succinct, flexible syntax. The grammar minimises syntactic noise, and that minimisation is what makes Ruby a workable substrate for domain-specific languages — a property noted well outside the Ruby community.[1]
  • State is private by default. Instance variables are completely private to the class and reachable only through accessor methods, which metaprogramming lets you declare in a single line. Because calls need no parentheses, converting a stored attribute into a computed method requires no change at any call site and no refactoring.[1]
  • Multi-paradigm, without apology. Procedural, object-oriented and functional styles are all first class: anonymous functions, closures and continuations are available, statements have values, and methods return their last evaluation.[1]

The official FAQ's own summary is still the sharpest: the syntax resembles Perl's, the semantics resemble Smalltalk's, and the design philosophy differs greatly from Python's.[1] That last clause is not a slight. Ruby and Python answer the same question in opposite directions — Ruby prizes flexibility and expressiveness and accepts that there will be several ways to achieve the same result, where Python's culture treats that plurality as a defect.[2]

The intangibles

What Ruby is trying to feel like

Every language has an aesthetic; Ruby is unusual in having made its aesthetic the explicit design criterion. That makes it fair game for analysis rather than off-limits as mere taste.

Code that reads as prose, and the bill for it

Ruby's most characteristic feature is the block: a chunk of code passed to a method, which is what makes iteration read as a sentence rather than as a loop with bookkeeping. Combined with literal notation for arrays, hashes, regular expressions and symbols, string interpolation, and sigils that encode scope, the result is a language where a well-written method is often legible to someone who does not know Ruby.[1]

# Iteration as a sentence, not as bookkeeping.
releases
  .select { |r| r.major >= 3 }
  .sort_by(&:date)
  .each_with_index do |release, i|
    puts "#{i + 1}. #{release.version} — #{release.headline}"
  end

The bill arrives in two places. First, that same flexibility means a codebase can develop a private dialect: with several ways to express any idea, teams must choose one, which is why a style cop became near-universal infrastructure. Second, expressiveness and inspectability trade against each other — a method that reads beautifully may be assembled at runtime by code that reads far less beautifully.

Domain-specific languages as a first-class outcome

Because parentheses are optional, blocks are cheap, and classes can be reopened and modified at runtime, Ruby lets a library define what looks like new syntax. Rails is the canonical demonstration, but the pattern recurs everywhere: a Gemfile, a Rake task, an RSpec suite and a Chef recipe are all ordinary Ruby that has been dressed as a configuration language. This is the direct product of dynamic reflection and runtime alteration of objects.[1]

# All of this is method calls. None of it is new syntax.
Rails.application.routes.draw do
  resources :invoices, only: %i[index show] do
    member { post :void }
  end
end

Ritual as a design artefact

Ruby ships a minor version on Christmas Day, every year, and has done since 1.0 in 1996.[2,13,19,20] The jewel metaphor runs all the way through the tooling — packages are gems, the registry describes itself as the community gem host, and the manifest is a Gemfile.[27] These are not trivia. A project that sustains a ritual for three decades is signalling something about its intended relationship with the people using it, and the signal has been legible enough to keep a distinctly affectionate community around a language whose usage share is falling.

The affection gap, quantified

In the 2025 Stack Overflow survey, 6.4% of all respondents had done extensive work in Ruby in the past year, but 44.3% of those who used it wanted to keep using it. Rails scored 52% admired against 5.9% usage.[28] Ruby's aesthetic project measurably worked; its distribution project did not.

Semantics

One dispatch rule, and what it buys

Ruby's object model is the part of the language that repays study, because it is both simpler and stranger than it first appears. There are no primitives to carve out, no static dispatch to reason around, and exactly one mechanism — an ordered ancestor list — behind inheritance, mixins, and per-object overrides alike.

One dispatch rule

Ruby supports inheritance with dynamic dispatch, mixins, and singleton methods that belong to a single instance rather than to a class. It has no multiple inheritance; classes import modules as mixins instead.[1] Method resolution is a walk down a single ordered list, and super resumes that walk from wherever the current method sits. The consequence is that a module can wrap a class's own method without the class knowing, which is the mechanism behind an enormous amount of Ruby library design.

Metaprogramming, and the escape hatches

Ruby offers introspection, reflection, metaprogramming and custom dispatch through method_missing and const_missing, and permits dynamic alteration of objects at runtime.[1] Together with operator overloading and Ruby's strict boolean rules — everything is truthy except false and nil — this is what lets libraries behave like language extensions.[1]

class LateBound
  def method_missing(name, *args)
    return super unless name.to_s.start_with?("find_by_")
    field = name.to_s.delete_prefix("find_by_")
    where(field => args.first)
  end

  def respond_to_missing?(name, include_private = false)
    name.to_s.start_with?("find_by_") || super
  end
end

The second method matters as much as the first, and is routinely omitted in hurried code. A method that answers to method_missing but not to respond_to? is invisible to introspection, documentation tools and type checkers alike — a small illustration of a general truth about Ruby, which is that the language will let you build something the rest of the toolchain cannot see.

The rest of the surface

  • Dynamic and duck typing, with parametric polymorphism and strong typing at runtime.[1]
  • Everything is an expression and everything is executed imperatively, including declarations — a class body is code that runs.[1]
  • Four levels of variable scope — global, class, instance and local — distinguished by sigils or their absence.[1]
  • Lexical closures, iterators and generators with block syntax; first-class continuations; built-in rational, complex and arbitrary-precision arithmetic.[1]
  • Unicode and multiple character encodings, a native C plug-in API, and centralised package management through RubyGems.[1]
Execution

A new parser and two just-in-time compilers

The reference implementation, CRuby or MRI, is written in C and runs its own Ruby-specific virtual machine.[1] Since 2023 that stack has been rebuilt from both ends: a new parser at the front, and two compiler tiers at the back.

Prism

Prism shipped as a default gem in Ruby 3.3 as a new parser designed to be error-tolerant, portable, maintainable, fast and efficient, and explicitly intended to replace the several parsers that had accumulated over Ruby's lifetime. It returns a result object carrying the syntax tree together with lists of errors, warnings and comments.[29] By the time the switch was proposed it passed all Ruby tests and specs and Shopify's Core monolith CI, and Matsumoto's position on the ticket was that making it the default in previews was acceptable but that shipping it as the 3.4 default would depend on quality.[30] It shipped, with --parser=parse.y left as an escape hatch.[21,31]

The significance is downstream rather than in the language. One error-tolerant parser, usable as a portable C library, means editors, formatters, type checkers and alternative implementations can share a syntax tree instead of maintaining private parsers. The migration is not free: Prism's AST is not compatible with the long-dominant parser gem, which supports syntax only up to Ruby 3.3, so the Prism team ships translation layers that let existing AST-processing tools keep working.[32]

YJIT

YJIT was built by Maxime Chevalier-Boisvert and Shopify's Ruby infrastructure team. It compiles bytecode to native code lazily, one basic block at a time, using lazy basic-block versioning, which gives it very low warm-up: the application starts fast and accelerates as hot paths compile.[33] Within about a year of starting, the team had roughly 20% speedups on railsbench, after which CRuby's core contributors invited them to upstream it; YJIT became an official part of Ruby 3.1 in December 2021.[34,35]

The numbers since then are worth reading carefully, because they are quoted against different baselines. YJIT 3.2 sped up railsbench by about 38% over the 3.2 interpreter, which was itself faster than 3.1's, making the cumulative improvement 57% against the Ruby 3.1.3 interpreter; the same release added a multi-platform backend and therefore ARM64 support, and Shopify measured 5–10% end-to-end improvements on Storefront Renderer request completion time.[34] YJIT 3.3 was 13% faster than YJIT 3.2 on the same production service.[36] By 3.4, YJIT was around 92% faster than the interpreter on Shopify's x86-64 benchmarking setup.[37]

ZJIT

ZJIT is a second compiler from the same group, merged into CRuby in May 2025 and shipped in Ruby 4.0.[13,40] The rationale is stated plainly: raise the performance ceiling through larger compilation units and an SSA-based intermediate representation, and encourage outside contribution by being a more traditional method compiler than YJIT's basic-block design.[13,41] It requires Rust 1.85 or later to build, is compiled in but not enabled by default, and is switched on with --zjit, the RUBY_ZJIT_ENABLE environment variable, or RubyVM::ZJIT.enable.[41,42]

The team's own assessment is refreshingly unmarketed: ZJIT is faster than the interpreter but not yet as fast as YJIT, users are encouraged to experiment but advised against production deployment, and the goal is to surpass YJIT and become production-ready in Ruby 4.1.[13,41] Between May and December 2025 it gained side exits into the interpreter, the ability to run the full Ruby test suite plus the test suite and shadow traffic of a large Shopify application and GitHub.com's test suite, register spilling for very large methods, and a DOMJIT-style inliner for well-known C methods. It compiles even large methods in under a millisecond.[41]

Three retired JIT tiers

MJIT arrived as an experiment in 2.6 and was reimplemented as a standard library module in 3.2; RJIT, a pure-Ruby JIT, appeared in 3.3; --rjit was removed in 4.0, with the third-party JIT API moved out to its own repository.[1,13,18,19] Three discarded compilers in seven years is not a failure record. It is what a runtime team looks like when it is allowed to abandon approaches.

Garbage collection

The collector has been rebuilt incrementally rather than replaced. Ruby 2.2 introduced an incremental collector and symbol GC; 2.7 added compaction; 3.4 made the GC modular, so alternative implementations can be loaded at runtime via RUBY_GC_LIBRARY when Ruby is configured with --with-modular-gc.[1,31] Ruby 4.0 added independent growth for differently sized heap pools, faster sweeping of large-object pages, a new internal fields object that speeds instance variable access on strings, arrays and typed data, deferred construction of the id2ref table, and write-barrier protection for several more core classes.[13]

Parallelism

The lock, and the four ways around it

Ruby's concurrency story is the single most misrepresented thing about the language, in both directions. It is not true that Ruby cannot do parallelism, and it is not true that threads solve it.

Ruby has native threads and cooperative fibers, the latter a 1.9-era YARV feature.[1] Ruby 3.0 added two more units: a Fiber Scheduler that lets a program supply its own event loop, and Ractor, a lightweight, thread-safe mechanism that achieves safety by exchanging messages rather than sharing objects.[1,10] Ruby 3.3 added an M:N thread scheduler.[1,19]

The lock, precisely

The global VM lock serialises the execution of Ruby code. It does not serialise the process. Ruby 4.0 made the lock per-Ractor and, as one core committer noted while walking through the release notes, rb_thread_call_with_gvl now works whether or not the lock is currently held, so C extensions no longer have to check first — a convenience the same committer argued against on the grounds that authors should stay sensitive to lock state.[13,43] The practical rule is unchanged: threaded servers scale IO-bound web work well, and CPU-bound Ruby needs processes or Ractors.

Ractor in Ruby 4.0

Ractor received its most substantial rework since introduction. A new Ractor::Port class replaced the old yield/take pair to fix long-standing problems with message sending and receiving; Ractor#join and Ractor#value were added to mirror Thread#join and Thread#value; Ractor.select now accepts only Ractors and Ports; and Ractor.shareable_proc and Ractor.shareable_lambda make it practical to share callable objects. Ractor.yield, Ractor#take, Ractor#close_incoming and Ractor#close_outgoing were removed outright.[13,44]

# Ruby 4.0: ports replace the old yield/take pair.
port = Ractor::Port.new

4.times do |i|
  Ractor.new(port, i) do |p, n|
    p << [n, expensive_pure_function(n)]   # no shared mutable state
  end
end

results = 4.times.map { port.receive }.to_h

Underneath, the release removed a great deal of lock contention: frozen strings and the symbol table moved to a lock-free hash set, method cache lookups avoid locking in most cases, class and generic instance variable access is faster and lock-free, object allocation and xmalloc/xfree use per-Ractor and thread-local counters to avoid CPU cache contention, and object_id avoids locking in most cases. Deadlocks between Ractors and Threads, require and autoload inside a Ractor, cross-Ractor encoding issues, GC race conditions and forking after Ractor start were all fixed.[13] The team's stated aim is to remove the experimental label the following year.[13]

Five years experimental

Ractor was introduced in Ruby 3.0 in December 2020 and was still flagged experimental in Ruby 4.0 five years later.[10,13] That is a long time for the language's answer to shared-nothing parallelism to remain provisional, and it is a fair thing to hold against the project. It is also, reading the 4.0 changelog, evidence of how much genuinely hard work the isolation guarantee required.

The debit column

What Ruby costs you, specifically

An honest survey has to be specific about costs. Most Ruby criticism is either a decade out of date or a category error about what the language is for; the criticisms below are neither.

Absolute performance still lags, and the headline numbers hide it

YJIT 3.4 being roughly 92% faster than the interpreter on benchmark hardware is a real achievement measured against the wrong reference point — the baseline is CRuby's own interpreter.[37] Shopify's published band for production Rails applications is a more useful figure, and it is 15–25%, with CPU-heavy workloads gaining more and IO-bound or database-bound endpoints gaining nothing at all, because a JIT cannot accelerate waiting.[33] Where raw single-threaded throughput is the binding constraint, Ruby remains the wrong tool and no amount of compiler work in the next two releases will change that.

Dynamic typing has a maintenance bill, and both answers charge it

Ruby has two gradual typing systems and neither is free. Sorbet's several-year head start over RBS shows in gem coverage and editor intelligence; conversely, adopting RBS on a real project means discovering that only a small fraction of third-party gems ship signatures and then writing them yourself for the dependencies you rely on.[45] Whichever system a team picks, it is signing up to maintain type information for code it does not own.

Open classes are a hazard the language itself is now trying to contain

The clearest evidence that monkey patching is a real production problem is that Ruby 4.0 shipped an experimental isolation mechanism specifically to contain it. Ruby Box exists to isolate monkey patches, global and class variable changes, class and module definitions and loaded libraries from one another, and one of the named use cases is protecting tests from other tests that monkey-patch.[13,14] A language does not build that unless the failure mode is common.

Upgrades are cheap now and were not always

Institutional memory here is long and justified. The 1.8-to-1.9 transition was slowed by gems needing rewrites.[1] GitHub's move to Ruby 2.7 involved coordinating 40 teams, upgrading more than 30 gems and clearing 11,000 deprecation warnings before CI was warning-free, on an application then over 400,000 lines with hundreds of pull requests daily.[46] Current estimates put a Rails 4-to-8 migration at six to twelve months for a large application, against one to two weeks for Rails 7 to 8.[47] The gradient is real, but the entry price for a neglected codebase is steep.

And breaking changes have not stopped. Ruby 4.0 removed four Ractor methods, removed Process::Status#& and #>>, deprecated ObjectSpace._id2ref, dropped the CGI library from default gems apart from escaping helpers, removed SortedSet as an autoloaded constant, and removed Net::HTTP's automatic application/x-www-form-urlencoded Content-Type default — a change that can silently break requests against servers that relied on it.[13]

Domain concentration

Ruby has essentially no presence in numerical computing, data science or machine-learning research, and TIOBE's analysis attributes the language's decline directly to Python's rise, with the blunt gloss that there is not much room left for Ruby.[48] Whatever one thinks of that framing, the underlying observation is correct: the largest growth area in programming over the past decade happened somewhere Ruby was not.

The hiring pipeline is thinner than the installed base

The 2025 Stack Overflow survey shows Ruby at 6.9% among professional developers but only 3.7% among people learning to code — the clearest available signal that the replacement rate is below the retirement rate.[28] Practitioner accounts make the same point about the talent market being thinner than a decade ago while noting that the developer pool is smaller, not absent.[49]

What Ruby is genuinely good at

  • Iteration speed on business logic where readability compounds over years.
  • A coherent, well-maintained standard framework rather than a churning field of contenders[50].
  • Metaprogramming that lets a library define an idiom instead of an API[1].
  • Operational simplicity: Rails 8 can go from a generated app to production on a single rented Linux host with no PaaS and no Redis[51].
  • A predictable release calendar and long support windows[23,25].

What it will cost you

  • Single-thread CPU throughput, permanently, relative to compiled languages.
  • Type information for your dependencies, whichever typing system you adopt[45].
  • Discipline against monkey patching that the language cannot yet enforce for you[13].
  • A hiring funnel that is narrowing at the junior end[28].
  • Concurrency that is still, five years in, officially experimental[13].

CRuby, JRuby and Rubinius have all reached it.[1]

Standing

Three instruments, three different answers

Ruby's standing is genuinely difficult to state in one number, because the available instruments measure different things and currently disagree about the direction of travel. The honest summary is that discourse volume is falling sharply, working usage is falling slowly, and installed base is still growing.

Survey usage

In the 2025 Stack Overflow Developer Survey, 6.4% of all respondents had done extensive development work in Ruby over the past year, against JavaScript at 66%, Python at 57.9% and TypeScript at 43.6%. Among professional developers Ruby rises to 6.9%; among those learning to code it falls to 3.7%. Ruby on Rails was used by 5.9% of all respondents and 6.2% of professionals, in a field where Node.js sits at 49.1% and React at 46.9%.[28]

The same survey's admiration figures cut the other way. Ruby was admired by 44.3% of the people who used it and desired by 5.1% of those who had not; Rails was admired by 52%. For context, Rust led all languages at 72.4% admired and Phoenix led all web frameworks at 79%.[28] Ruby is not a language people are fleeing. It is a language people are not arriving at.

The TIOBE trajectory

TIOBE tells the harshest version of the story. Ruby was TIOBE's Language of the Year in 2006 and peaked at eighth place in May 2016. By March 2026 it had fallen to thirtieth with a rating of 0.55%, down from twenty-fifth the previous month, and TIOBE's chief executive attributed the drop directly to Python's popularity.[48]

Methodology matters here. TIOBE's rating is a percentage of search-engine hits for queries of the form <language> programming across more than twenty-five engines, so it measures how much is written about a language rather than how much is running.[48,52] For scale, the July 2026 index put Python first at 18.94% and admitted Rust to the top ten for the first time at 1.34%, up from eighteenth a year earlier.[52,53] Secondary trackers through 2026 have placed Ruby variously in the mid-twenties and around thirtieth depending on the month, which is itself a signal: at this level, rank is noisy.[54]

The counter-indicator

Registry data points the other way. RubyGems.org hosts 195,468 gems from 245,978 users and has served 259,596,748,988 downloads.[27] The service handles roughly 20,000 requests per second on average — about two billion requests every weekday — with recorded peaks near 225,000 requests per second, serving on the order of 185 terabytes a day at peak, and has had no full outage since 2015.[55]

In production

Who runs Ruby, and at what scale

The strongest argument against reading TIOBE as an obituary is the list of systems that would stop working if Ruby did.

GitHub

GitHub.com has been a Ruby on Rails monolith since the beginning. The application is now approaching two million lines of code, more than a thousand engineers work on it daily, and it deploys as often as twenty times a day.[39] Its upgrade discipline is the part worth copying: a scheduled workflow opens an automated pull request every Monday bumping Rails to the latest commit on the framework's main branch, all builds run against it, and if they pass the change ships the next day. Nearly every week one of those deploys is a Rails upgrade.[39] The same process let GitHub move from Ruby 3.1 to 3.2 within a month of release, and then adopt Ruby 3.2.1 on the day it shipped.[39]

It was not always like this. The earlier move to Ruby 2.7 required dual-booting the application under both 2.6 and 2.7 behind an environment variable, monkey-patching the Warning module to capture deprecations into per-team lists, and coordinating across forty teams to clear eleven thousand warnings.[46] GitHub also open-sourced Trilogy, the database adapter it uses to connect Rails to MySQL-compatible servers.[46]

Shopify

Shopify's Storefront Renderer is the service buyers hit first on any hosted store. It is mostly Ruby, depends on more than 220 gems, renders millions of stores across more than 175 countries, and runs on multiple clusters distributed worldwide.[37] It has run YJIT in production continuously since 2023.[37]

The scale numbers are the ones usually quoted, and they deserve to be. Shopify processed a record $11.5 billion in sales for merchants over the 2024 Black Friday–Cyber Monday weekend, with app servers handling more than 80 million requests per minute on Black Friday while running a prerelease YJIT 3.4.[37] The following year the platform served 81 million customers on Black Friday with a peak of 117 million requests per minute on application servers, against 53 million database reads and 2 million writes per second.[38] Shopify also funds and staffs the compiler work: YJIT, ZJIT and the Packwerk modularity tool all come from the same organisation.[34,41,56]

37signals, Stripe, and the long tail

37signals runs Basecamp and HEY on Rails and is where most of Rails' recent operational direction originates — Kamal 2 was led by Donal McBreen, and Kamal Proxy and Thruster were created by Kevin McConnell, both at 37signals.[51] HEY and Shopify both ran Rails 8.1 in production for months before its release.[57]

Stripe's contribution is different in kind: the company maintains an extremely large and growing Ruby codebase with hundreds of developers and millions of lines, and built Sorbet to keep development scaling on it.[58] That a payments company chose to build a type checker rather than leave Ruby is a data point about switching costs in both directions.

The version-support calendar

Rails 7.0 and 7.1 reached end of life in October 2025, with 7.0.10 and 7.1.6 as their final releases; Rails 8.0's bug-fix support was extended by six months to 7 May 2026 under the policy that support is extended when no new release appears within a year.[59] Teams running Ruby in production are operating on a published, enforced schedule rather than on vibes.

Gems

The libraries that carry the ecosystem

A language's real API is its library ecosystem. Ruby's has an unusual shape: a very large standard library shipped as independently versioned default gems, a small set of near-universal third-party dependencies, and a long tail of single-purpose gems.

Rails 8: the deployment thesis

Rails 8.0, released in November 2024, is best understood as an argument rather than a feature list. The argument is that the accessory services a Rails application used to require — Redis for jobs, caching and WebSockets, a separate job framework, an Nginx in front, a platform-as-a-service underneath — are no longer necessary, because disks have become fast enough that RAM is not needed for as many tasks.[51]

Concretely: Solid Cable replaces Redis as the pub/sub server relaying WebSocket messages between processes, retaining messages in the database for a day by default; Solid Cache replaces Redis or Memcached for HTML fragment caching; and Solid Queue replaces both Redis and a separate job-running framework such as Resque, Delayed Job or Sidekiq.[67] Kamal 2 deploys containers onto any Linux host, with a purpose-built Kamal Proxy replacing the generic Traefik option, zero-downtime deploys, automatic Let's Encrypt certificates, multiple applications on one server, and built-in integration with 1Password and Bitwarden for secrets.[51,75] The generated Dockerfile now includes Thruster, a small proxy sitting in front of Puma providing X-Sendfile acceleration, asset caching and compression, so the default container is ready to take internet traffic with no separate web server.[51,67] Propshaft replaced Sprockets as the asset pipeline, and the framework gained a first-party authentication generator.[75,76]

It is worth being precise about the opt-in question, because the marketing was not: the Solid adapters are the new defaults for freshly generated applications, while existing applications can keep Redis, Sidekiq or any adapter they already use.[76] Kamal is likewise opt-in, and teams with established CI/CD and orchestration can skip it.[76]

Rails 8.1: reliability as the feature

Rails 8.1, released 22 October 2025, represents the work of more than 500 contributors across 2,500 commits.[57] The centrepiece is Active Job Continuations: long-running jobs can be broken into discrete steps with cursors, so that after a restart execution resumes from the last completed step rather than from the beginning — particularly useful under Kamal, which by default gives job containers thirty seconds to shut down.[57] Alongside it came structured event reporting through a new Rails.event API, a local CI runner defined in Ruby, native Markdown rendering and the Lexxy rich-text editor.[57,68,77]

class ProcessImportJob < ApplicationJob
  include ActiveJob::Continuable

  def perform(import_id)
    @import = Import.find(import_id)

    step :initialize do
      @import.initialize
    end

    # The cursor is checkpointed when the job is interrupted.
    step :process do |step|
      @import.records.find_each(start: step.cursor) do |record|
        record.process
        step.advance! from: record.id
      end
    end
  end
end

For everything that is not a full-stack web application, the alternatives are genuinely differentiated rather than merely smaller. Sinatra and Roda boot instantly with minimal dependencies for small services; Roda's routing tree and memory profile make it the throughput choice; Grape handles versioned REST interfaces and mobile back ends; and Hanami is the option for domain-heavy applications expected to stay in service for many years.[50,66,73] The trade-off with Hanami is staffing: Rails remains easier to hire for and easier to support with battle-tested libraries.[50]

Types

Two answers to the same question

Ruby now has two type systems, which is one more than most languages manage and one more than is comfortable. Both are gradual, neither is mandatory, and the split has a straightforward historical explanation.

Sorbet came first, built at Stripe and released in 2019 to catch type errors in very large Ruby applications without blocking deployments.[45] It works with all Ruby, introduces backwards-compatible syntax for method signatures rather than a new language, uses .rbi files to describe gems and metaprogrammed methods, is multithreaded and scales linearly across cores, and offers control-flow-sensitive typing with optional runtime checks.[58,69] RBS arrived with Ruby 3.0 in December 2020 as the language's official type signature format, describing types in separate .rbs files without altering runtime behaviour, with Steep type-checking source against those signatures and TypeProf inferring signatures from untyped code.[1,10,45,70]

# Sorbet: signatures live inline, in ordinary Ruby.
sig { params(id: Integer, force: T::Boolean).returns(T.nilable(Invoice)) }
def find_invoice(id, force: false)
  force ? Invoice.uncached { Invoice.find_by(id: id) } : Invoice.find_by(id: id)
end
# RBS: signatures live beside the code, in sig/invoice.rbs.
class InvoiceRepository
  def find_invoice: (Integer id, ?force: bool) -> Invoice?
  def recent: (?limit: Integer) -> Array[Invoice]
end

Ruby 4.0 bundles rbs 3.10.0 and typeprof 0.31.1, which is the clearest available statement that the official toolchain is maintained rather than ceremonial.[13] The practical difficulty is coverage. A developer adopting RBS on a real application reported that only twelve of their third-party gems shipped signatures, and spent two weeks writing them for widely used libraries; Sorbet's several-year head start shows in gem coverage and in editor intelligence, where autocomplete narrows to the inferred type rather than offering every method in the process.[45]

What most teams actually do

Neither. For the large majority of Ruby in production, the type system is a style analyser plus a test suite plus CI, and this has been true for a decade. That is a legitimate engineering position rather than a failure to adopt, but it does mean the cost of a wrong argument type is paid at call time, in production.

The AI question, answered without enthusiasm

Ruby did not participate in the machine-learning research build-out and will not. What it has instead is a workable client-side ecosystem for consuming models, which arrived late but developed quickly. RubyLLM is a single gem by Carmine Paolino offering one interface across OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini and others with three runtime dependencies — Faraday, Zeitwerk and Marcel — and by late April 2026 stood at version 1.14.1 with roughly 3,900 GitHub stars and over five million downloads.[71] Version 1.10 added extended thinking with streaming and Rails integration plus Ruby 4 compatibility, and 1.11 added first-class xAI support; adjacent gems have appeared for LLM evaluation, for organising prompts as ERB templates in an app/prompts directory, and for monitoring as a Rails engine.[80]

The alternatives are real rather than nominal: langchainrb is the original Ruby LangChain port and remains maintained, Sublayer takes a generator and agent DSL approach, Raix focuses on object-oriented prompt patterns and ships MCP integration, instructor-rb mirrors the Python library for structured output, and both Anthropic and OpenAI publish official Ruby gems.[71] Active Agent takes the most Rails-native position, modelling agents as controllers and integrating with Langchain Ruby, pgvector and the neighbors gem.[78] ruby-openai and langchainrb are the two most commonly cited entry points in practitioner writing.[79]

The claim to treat sceptically

A recurring argument in Ruby circles holds that convention over configuration makes Rails codebases unusually legible to coding agents, and that Ruby is token-efficient enough for agents to stay coherent over longer contexts.[71] Practitioner reports also note that Rails developers find AI tools work well with the framework's conventions.[49,54] This is plausible and it is currently anecdote. No controlled comparison has been published, and the argument should be read as a hypothesis the community likes rather than a finding.

Stewardship

Who decides, and the year that tested it

Ruby's language governance and its packaging governance are separate systems with very different track records, and 2025 made the difference impossible to ignore.

The language

Language decisions run through a public issue tracker with Matsumoto as final arbiter, and the record is legible. On the proposal to switch the default parser to Prism, Matsumoto's posted position was that making it the default in previews was acceptable once it passed all tests, that shipping it as the 3.4 default would depend on quality, and that decisions about later versions and the relationship between Prism, Lrama and parse.y would come later.[30] Formal standardisation runs on a different track again: the specification work was carried out by the Open Standards Promotion Center of Japan's Information-Technology Promotion Agency for submission to the Japanese Industrial Standards Committee and then ISO.[1,7]

The packaging fracture of September 2025

RubyGems and Bundler are sponsored by Ruby Central, a non-profit that also runs RubyConf and RailsConf.[81] In September 2025 control of those projects changed hands in a way that most participants described as a takeover.

The sequence is documented from several directions. On 9 September the RubyGems GitHub enterprise was renamed to Ruby Central, Ruby Central's director of open source Marty Haught was added as an owner, and other maintainers were demoted or removed. Around 15 September most of the change was reversed and described as a mistake, but Haught remained an owner. On 18 September the administrative access of all RubyGems, Bundler and RubyGems.org administrators was revoked without explanation, their organisation and email accounts were deactivated, and ownership of critical gems was taken away — catching André Arko, a veteran contributor, while he was on call for the RubyGems.org service.[81–83] Ruby Central's public justification cited fiduciary responsibility, consultation with legal counsel and a recent security audit, framed as strengthening governance and tightening access to production systems.[81]

Ellen Dash, a maintainer of ten years, resigned, describing the forced removal of people who had maintained RubyGems and Bundler for over a decade as inherently hostile.[81,83] A separate account by developer Joel Drapper alleged that Shopify had applied financial and governance pressure, and that Ruby Central had become financially dependent after a major sponsor withdrew a $250,000-a-year commitment.[82]

Ruby Central published an incident report in March 2026, written by Richard Schneeman, a Salesforce principal engineer who had joined the board a month after the events. It identifies the trigger as Arko launching the rv tool and the Spinel organisation without consulting Ruby Central, quotes an internal message about accelerating his removal, and names the six paid contributors who walked out.[61,84] Mike McQuaid, who leads Homebrew and attempted unsuccessfully to mediate, later observed at FOSDEM that RubyGems is critical internet infrastructure rather than a niche tool, and that both sides had discussed legal action — which he regards as fatal to reconciliation.[61] The board has promised further work on governance, transparency and community participation.[61]

Why this belongs in a technical survey

Because it is a supply-chain risk with a number attached. RubyGems.org serves on the order of two billion requests every weekday and has run without a full outage since 2015, and its maintainers had already named dependency confusion and account takeovers as live threats.[55] An institution that loses six experienced maintainers in a week loses response capacity, and any team with Ruby in production should be tracking the governance outcome the same way it tracks CVEs.

Forward

What 4.1 and after are actually about

Three things are scheduled, one is likely, and one is unresolved.

Scheduled

  • ZJIT reaching production. The stated goal is that ZJIT becomes faster than YJIT and production-ready in Ruby 4.1.[13,41] The named work is concrete: optimising invokeblock and invokesuper, handling instance-variable writes that transition an object's shape, extending optimisation from monomorphic to polymorphic call sites — the team measures monomorphic sends in the high eighties to low nineties as a percentage — a rewritten linear-scan register allocator, deferring writes of frame state until something needs to read it, and a general-purpose method inliner.[41]
  • Ractor losing its experimental label. The 4.0 release notes state the aim directly, on the strength of the stability and performance work already merged.[13]
  • The calendar. A minor release each Christmas, patch releases roughly every two months, and support windows of about three years per branch.[23,25]

Likely

Ruby Box is the most interesting unfinished idea in the language. It currently isolates definitions, monkey patches, global and class variables and loaded libraries behind an environment flag, and the release notes name a higher-level package API as an expected use case while stating plainly that it is not designed yet.[13,14] If that lands, Ruby will have addressed its most durable structural criticism — that any dependency can silently redefine anything — without giving up the open classes that make the language what it is. That is a genuinely difficult design problem and it is worth watching rather than predicting.

Unresolved

Two things. The first is packaging governance, where an incident report and a promise of reform is where matters currently stand, with mediation having failed and legal action discussed on both sides.[61,84] The second is the pipeline: 3.7% of people learning to code report extensive work in Ruby against 6.9% of working professionals, and no compiler improvement addresses that.[28]

A language can be simultaneously unfashionable and load-bearing. Ruby currently is, and the interesting question is not whether the TIOBE line keeps falling — it will — but whether a quarter-trillion gem downloads and a two-million-line monolith deploying twenty times a day are the trailing edge of something or the steady state of it.[27,39,48]

The defensible reading is the second. Ruby's decline is a decline in share of new projects, driven overwhelmingly by Python absorbing the growth in data and machine-learning work.[48] Its production base is large, professionally maintained, on a supported release calendar, and being actively made faster by two of the largest companies that depend on it.[37,39,41] Those are different curves, and conflating them is the most common error in writing about this language.

# Runs on Ruby 3.4; the Ractor section requires 4.0.

Release = Data.define(:version, :date, :codename, :major) do
  include Comparable

  def <=>(other)
    parts <=> other.parts
  end

  def parts = version.split(".").map(&:to_i)
  def to_s  = "#{version} (#{date})"
end

# A module that assumes only Enumerable, so it composes onto anything
# that can iterate. This is the mixin argument in miniature.
module Cohort
  include Enumerable

  def by_major = group_by(&:major).transform_values(&:size)
  def span     = "#{min.version} .. #{max.version}"
end

class ReleaseSet < Array
  include Cohort
end

RELEASES = ReleaseSet[
  Release.new(version: "1.8.0", date: "2003-08-04", codename: :standardised, major: 1),
  Release.new(version: "1.9.0", date: "2007-12-25", codename: :yarv,         major: 1),
  Release.new(version: "2.0.0", date: "2013-02-24", codename: :twenty_years, major: 2),
  Release.new(version: "3.0.0", date: "2020-12-25", codename: :three_by_three, major: 3),
  Release.new(version: "3.4.0", date: "2024-12-25", codename: :prism_default, major: 3),
  Release.new(version: "4.0.0", date: "2025-12-25", codename: :zjit,         major: 4)
].freeze

# Pattern matching against Data#deconstruct_keys, with an alternative pattern.
def era(release)
  case release
  in { codename: :yarv }      then "the bytecode break"
  in { major: 1 }             then "the interpreted years"
  in { major: 2 }             then "consolidation"
  in { codename: :zjit }      then "the compiler era"
  in { major: 3 | 4 }         then "performance as a project"
  end
end

puts "span:     #{RELEASES.span}"
puts "by major: #{RELEASES.by_major}"
RELEASES.sort.each do |release|
  puts format("  %-7s  %-16s  %s", release.version, release.codename, era(release))
end

# Ractors: parallel Ruby with no shared mutable state. Ruby 4.0 replaced
# the old yield/take pair with Ractor::Port.
if defined?(Ractor::Port)
  port = Ractor::Port.new

  RELEASES.each_slice(2) do |slice|
    Ractor.new(port, slice.map(&:major)) do |p, majors|
      p << majors.sum
    end
  end

  checksum = 3.times.sum { port.receive }
  puts "major-number checksum across 3 ractors: #{checksum}"
end

Three details are worth pointing at. Data.define gives value semantics and deconstruct_keys for free, which is what makes the pattern-matching block legible. Cohort depends on Enumerable rather than on Array, so it composes onto anything that iterates — the mixin argument from the object-model section, in four lines. And the Ractor block captures nothing from its enclosing scope: every value it needs is passed as an argument, which is the isolation guarantee the whole mechanism rests on.[13,44]

What this file would have looked like in 2013

No Data, no endless method definitions, no pattern matching, no safe navigation, no Ractor, Set as a library rather than a core class, and Fixnum and Bignum as distinct types.[1,13] Ruby's reputation as a language that stopped evolving after Rails is not supported by its own changelog.
