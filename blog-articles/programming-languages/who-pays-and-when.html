01The question

Every general-purpose programming language computes exactly the same set of functions. This is not a rhetorical flourish; it is a theorem, and it has been one since 1936 12. Any language with sequence, selection and unbounded iteration can express any effectively calculable procedure 3. Whatever your program is, it can be written in any of the languages you are choosing between. The capability question is closed, and it has been closed for ninety years.

So the argument that consumes engineering organisations for weeks at a time is not about capability. It cannot be. What, then, is it about?

The claim of this article is that it is about a schedule. Every difference that survives the Turing equivalence is a decision about when a particular cost is paid and who is holding the bill when it comes due. A static type system does not make errors impossible; it moves a class of them from the operator's pager to the author's editor, and charges the author for the move in annotations and compile time. A garbage collector does not make memory management free; it moves the work from the author's head to the runtime's schedule, and charges the operator in tail latency and footprint. A package registry does not give you libraries; it enrols several hundred strangers into your build, and charges your users if any of them is compromised.

Read this way, the question which language is better? is ill-formed in exactly the way which is the better price? is ill-formed. Prices are not better or worse in the abstract. They are better or worse relative to a budget, and the budgets differ. A team that ships a kernel module to a hundred million devices and a team that writes a script to reconcile two CSV files are not disagreeing about facts when they reach for different languages. They are paying out of different accounts.

What follows tries to make that concrete enough to be wrong. Sixteen decisions are laid out as plates, each with a fixed set of fields, each placed on a six-rung ladder that records when the characteristic failure of the cheap option is discovered. A deterministic lab of ten experiments, run at build time and reproduced verbatim below, supplies every quantity in the text. And a closing section states plainly what the empirical literature will and will not support, because a great deal of what is said confidently about programming languages is not supported by anything at all 4.

02What Turing completeness settles, and what it hides

It is worth being precise about the scope of the equivalence, because the precision is what makes the rest of the argument possible.

Turing completeness is a statement about the extension of a language: the set of partial functions it can compute. It says nothing about the intension — how a program denoting a given function is written, how long it takes to write, how much of it a compiler can check, how it behaves under a schedule the author did not anticipate, or what happens when the person who wrote it leaves. Rice's theorem tells us that non-trivial semantic properties of programs are undecidable in general 5, which is precisely why languages differ: each one carves out a decidable fragment and enforces it, and the choice of fragment is the design.

This is the sense in which a type system is a proof system rather than a convenience. The Curry–Howard correspondence makes the identity explicit: a type is a proposition, a program is its proof, and type checking is proof checking 6. A language with sum types and exhaustive matching is not more powerful than one without; it is a language in which certain propositions about your program can be stated and mechanically discharged. A language without them is one in which the same propositions must be established by testing, by review, or by hope.

Every one of those is a cost. They differ in when they are incurred and who incurs them. That is the whole subject.

Landin's observation that most of the seven hundred languages then in existence differed in surface syntax over a small set of semantic ideas 7 remains largely true, and it is not an argument that the choice does not matter. It is an argument that the choice matters for reasons other than the ones usually given. The interesting variance is not in what can be said but in what must be said, what may be omitted, and what the machine will refuse.

03The discovery ladder

The organising device of this article is a six-rung ladder. Each rung is a moment at which a defect of a given class can be discovered, ordered from the best case to the worst.

Three properties of this ladder do the work.

It is ordinal, not cardinal. D4 is better than D2 in the sense that the discovery happens earlier and in front of fewer people. It is not ten times better, and the article does not claim it is; the multiplier is the most disputed number in the field and section 12 treats it directly.

It is per class, not per language. No language sits on one rung. Rust catches use-after-free at D4 and integer overflow at D1, in release builds by wrapping. Python makes use-after-free unrepresentable at D5 and type mismatches D1. Go catches type mismatches at D4 and, until 2022, made generic containers a D1 problem. A language is a vector of rung assignments across defect classes, and that is the object this article compares.

The rung determines the payer. This is the part usually left implicit. A defect caught at D4 is paid for by the author in seconds. The same defect caught at D1 is paid for by an operator, awake, at a time not of their choosing. Caught at D0, it is paid for by a user who does not know they are paying and cannot report what they cannot see. Moving a class up the ladder does not eliminate cost; it transfers it from a party who cannot control it to a party who can, and usually shrinks it on the way.

The one thing that must not be smuggled in is the assumption that up is always worth it. Standing on a higher rung is bought — with annotations, with compile time, with restricted expressiveness, with a smaller hiring pool. The lab below computes, for one stated cost model, exactly how much a rung move is worth and how much it must not cost. That number turns out to be the whole decision.

04How to read a plate

Each of the sixteen decisions below is presented in the same form, so that they can be compared rather than merely enumerated. The fields are fixed:

  • Decides — the question the language designer had to answer. Not a feature; a fork.
  • Positions — the answers real languages actually give, named. A plate with only two positions is usually a plate that has been described badly.
  • Caught at — the rung, with the reasoning. Where a language family differs, the differences are given.
  • Who pays — the party holding the failure at that rung, and in what currency.
  • Fails as — the characteristic shape of the failure when the cheap position is chosen. Almost never the shape people expect.
  • Evidence — the citations that bear on the claim, including the ones that undercut it.

The gauge on each plate shows the rung and the payer. In the closing ledger all sixteen are collected into a single matrix, and the shape of that matrix is the argument.

05Group I The machine's memory

Four decisions about storage. Together they account for the majority of the exploitable defect corpus of the last thirty years, which is why they are the four that governments have started writing memoranda about.

Storage is where the ladder is easiest to see, because the rungs are so far apart. The same defect — a pointer used after the storage behind it has been reused — is unrepresentable in a tracing-collected language, rejected by the compiler in a language with lifetimes, and undefined behaviour in C. Three positions on one question, three rungs, three different people paying.

This group is also the only one in the article where the empirical case is close to settled, and it is worth being clear about why. The others rest on controlled experiments with small effects, or on models with stated assumptions. This one rests on a decade of vulnerability data from several large organisations who had no incentive to produce the result they produced. Roughly two thirds to three quarters of severe vulnerabilities in large C and C++ codebases are memory-safety defects, reported independently by browser and mobile platform teams over many years 89 10. Android then ran what amounts to a natural experiment: rather than rewriting anything, it wrote new code in memory-safe languages and left the old code alone. The memory-safety share of its vulnerabilities fell year over year and dropped below a fifth, and the vulnerability density of the new Rust code came in around a thousandfold below the C and C++ beside it 1112.

The result that ought to change minds is not that number, though. It is the process data alongside it: the Rust changes had a lower rollback rate and spent less time in code review than the C++ they replaced. The standard objection to moving up this part of the ladder is that safety is bought with velocity. In the largest published dataset on the question, it was not.

What follows is what is actually being decided when a language takes a position here.

Experiment L1 makes the consequence arithmetical. Ten defect classes are injected at identical rates — 810 per 100 KLOC — into four language profiles that differ only in which rung each class is caught at. What reaches a user ranges from 53.8 to 454.0, a spread of 8.44× from identical input.

The headline is not the spread. It is that the C-family and dynamic profiles land within 1.0% of each other on volume and are not remotely alike in kind: 72.51% of what escapes the C-family profile is silent, against 26.71% for the dynamic one — 2.69× more silent escapes. A defect count cannot tell those two apart, and a defect count is what most of the empirical literature measures. The distinction between a language that fails loudly and one that fails quietly does not appear in the metric at all.

The second finding is more uncomfortable. Decomposing the gap between the worst and best profiles by class, the memory-safety classes contribute 0.00% — because both of those profiles already place them above D1 — while type mismatch, null and unchecked error return together carry 72.84%. And the affine language is worse on integer overflow, by 4.06% of the gap, because Python's arbitrary-precision integers make that class unrepresentable while Rust's release-mode wrapping does not. On this model, in this comparison, the classes that dominate the public argument are not the classes that dominate the arithmetic.

That is a claim about a stated model, not about the world, and the model's injection rates are assumptions. What survives the assumptions is the shape: rung moves are not fungible. The best available single-rung move in the C-family profile removes 16.20% of all escapes; the worst effective one removes 1.95%; and one of the ten moves on offer — strengthening type checking from D4 to D5 — removes nothing at all, because there was nothing left to remove.

06Group II What the compiler is allowed to know

Five decisions about how much of the program's meaning is available before the program runs. This is the territory of the type-system argument, and it is also the territory where the empirical evidence is thinnest and most contested.

Here the disagreements get loud, and the evidence gets thin. It is worth stating the shape of the trade before the plates, because it is not the shape the argument usually takes.

A static check is a purchase. You pay in annotation, in compile time, and in programs you cannot write; you receive a class of defect moved from D1 or D2 up to D4. Whether that is a good purchase is an arithmetic question with an answer, and L2 computes it: with an annotation burden of 45 minutes per KLOC and an in-editor fix costing 5 minutes, the check must catch A/(Fearly(R−1)) defects per KLOC to break even, where R is the ratio of a late fix to an early one.

The statically checkable classes in L1's table total 5.60 defects per KLOC. The break-even clears from R ≥ 5 — that is, as soon as a production defect costs more than twenty-five minutes to deal with — and at R = 100 the check need only catch 0.091 per KLOC, 61.6× less than what is on offer. The two ends of that range differ by 24.8× in the threshold and give the same verdict.

The gradual position deserves separate mention, because it is where most large codebases actually live and because its cost is not where people expect. Adding types to a dynamic language incrementally is attractive precisely because it does not require a rewrite. But a sound gradual system has to check values as they cross the boundary between typed and untyped code, and those checks are not free: measured across configurations of a partially typed program, the overheads can be severe enough to make some mixtures impractical 28, and the design space of weaker guarantees trades soundness for speed in ways that are now reasonably well mapped 29 30. The industrial systems most people use — TypeScript, mypy — resolve this by not checking at the boundary at all, which makes them fast and means the types are a lint rather than a proof. That is a defensible choice. It is a different product from the one the word "typed" usually implies, and it lands on a different rung.

This matters because R is the single most disputed quantity in software engineering economics, and the argument about static typing has been conducted for thirty years as though it hinged on it. It does not. It hinges on whether a production defect costs your team more than twenty-five minutes, which is a question your incident tracker can answer this afternoon.

Two of these decisions have consequences sharp enough to compute directly.

Exhaustive matching is usually defended on aesthetic grounds and is better defended on statistical ones. L3 models a variant added to a sum type with fourteen match sites and a 500-test suite in which the new variant appears in 1% of tests, with site reach following a Zipf distribution — because sites are not reached uniformly, and the cold ones are the error paths.

The hottest site is checked with probability 0.9507. The coldest is checked with probability 0.1929. The expected number of unchecked sites is 8.203 of fourteen; ten of the fourteen sit below even odds; and the probability that the suite checks all of them rounds to zero. A 20,000-trial simulation agrees at 8.206. To bring the coldest site alone to the required confidence would take 16,889 tests — 34× the existing suite. The compiler does fourteen of fourteen, in one pass, and its cost does not depend on traffic.

A related point about the dynamic position is that its failure mode is not usually a type error at all. An empirical study of implicit type conversions in JavaScript found that a substantial fraction of them are harmful or confusing, and that the language's coercion rules routinely turn what would have been a type error into a plausible wrong answer 47. That is the difference between D1 and D0 showing up inside a language everyone classifies as memory-safe.

The same asymmetry appears in refactoring. L6 models a change to a core type touching S call sites, found by a search-and-test workflow with per-site recall g.

At g = 0.95, the probability of a clean refactor is 59.87% at ten sites, 7.69% at fifty, and 0.0035% at two hundred; the coin flips at 13.5 sites. To reach 99% confidence on a five-thousand-site change, a manual process would need per-site recall of 0.999998. The compiler's recall is 1 by construction and its cost does not grow with S — which is the actual argument for paying a type system's price on a large, long-lived codebase, and the actual argument against paying it on a small, short-lived one. Both halves follow from the same curve.

The cost of the concurrency decision is not throughput; it is the tail, and the tail is where the shared-resource choice becomes visible. L5 holds mean service time and utilisation exactly equal between two workloads on one server and varies only the variance: one where every request takes 0.6195 ms, one where 99.9% take 0.5 ms and 0.1% take 120 ms.

Same mean. Same load. The p99 is 47.4× worse and the p99.9 is 50.1× worse. One request in a thousand sets the tail for every other request, because a single-threaded reactor cannot be preempted by anything the language offers. A thread pool with an OS scheduler pays for preemption in context switches and buys this back — which is the entire trade, and it is a trade about whose latency is protected rather than about how fast the language is.

Two footnotes belong here. First, the concurrency bugs that actually dominate modern systems are increasingly not within a process at all: a taxonomy of non-deterministic bugs in datacenter distributed systems found timing-dependent failures whose ordering constraints no single language's memory model can express 59, which is the practical content of the observation that ordering in a distributed system is a partial order rather than a total one 60. A language decision cannot fix that. It can only decide whether the intra-process half is also a hazard.

Second, the positions on this plate are not frozen. CPython's global interpreter lock, long the reason its concurrency story was 'use processes', has an accepted proposal to make it optional 61, which moves Python from one column of the table towards another over a period of years — and carries with it every question in plate P10, which the language had previously been able to avoid answering.

The throughput half of the same experiment is worth a sentence because it contradicts the usual story: at every CPU fraction, a sixteen-worker pool sustains exactly sixteen times the reactor's throughput. The reactor never wins on CPU. It wins on memory per connection and on connection count, neither of which appears in the comparison people actually make.

08Group IV The machine and the clock

Three decisions that are invisible in a code review and decisive in an incident review.

These three decisions are invisible in a code review and decisive in an incident review. They are also the three most likely to be settled by a benchmark that does not measure them.

Compile time is the one worth dwelling on, because it is the only cost in this entire article that is paid on a per-keystroke basis and the only one that is routinely dismissed as a matter of taste. It is not. In the Rust compiler performance survey, a majority of respondents reported waiting more than ten seconds for a typical rebuild, and among those who had stopped using the language, roughly forty-five per cent cited long compile times as a reason 62. The annual survey the same community runs found more than a quarter of respondents calling slow compilation a significant problem, in a population that otherwise reported overwhelmingly that adoption had been worth the cost 63. Both things are true simultaneously, and that is the shape of a real trade rather than a complaint.

The reason it belongs on the ladder is that the edit–run loop is the mechanism by which a D4 check gets paid for. A compiler that catches a class of error at D4 has moved the cost to the author; if the author waits two minutes to learn about it, the move has been partly undone. Time-to-feedback is what converts a static guarantee into an actual saving, which is why the comparison that matters is not "static versus dynamic" but "how long until I know".

Pause behaviour deserves the arithmetic because it is routinely dismissed with a mean. L4 takes a runtime that pauses a given request with probability q and asks what happens when one user request touches N services.

At q = 0.01, a single service makes a user wait 1.000% of the time and a hundred services make them wait 63.397% of the time. Inverting: holding P(user waits) under 1% requires q ≤ 0.010000 at N = 1 and q ≤ 0.000020 at N = 500, a tightening of 497.5×. At q = 0.001 with a 10 ms pause, the expected added latency at N = 20 is 0.2 ms while the probability of a user waiting has already crossed 1.98% — the tail crosses the threshold while the mean is still noise.

It is worth saying what compensates when a language sits low on the ladder, because the answer is: a great deal, at a price. Industrial static analysis genuinely finds real defects at scale, as reported from a commercial tool vendor 46, from Google 81 and from Facebook 82; automated test-case reduction makes compiler and runtime bugs tractable to diagnose 83. And where the cost of a D0 escape is catastrophic, entire regimes exist to buy the rungs back by restriction rather than by language: coding standards that forbid the constructs the compiler cannot check 8485, and certification frameworks that make the evidence itself a deliverable 8687. These are the same decision, taken by process instead of by compiler, at considerably greater expense.

This is the point of the tail-at-scale literature 66, restated as a language decision: the runtime's pause distribution is not a property the application can fix, and the architecture that amplifies it was chosen by someone who was not thinking about the collector.

The last two decisions are not language design at all. They are the ones that, in practice, dominate the other fourteen — and they are the two least likely to appear in a language comparison, because neither is a property of the language.

Note in particular that the size of a standard library is a supply-chain decision wearing different clothes. A language that ships batteries reduces the number of times a team reaches for a stranger's package; a language with a small core and a frictionless registry increases it. Neither is obviously right — a large standard library ages badly, accumulates modules nobody should use, and centralises the maintenance burden — but the choice is not about convenience. It is about how many independent parties end up with the ability to execute code on your build machine, which is the quantity L7 counts.

L7 builds a synthetic registry of 6,000 packages and 900 maintainers with preferential attachment and Zipf-distributed ownership, then asks what an application actually trusts.

Across 2,000 sampled applications the median trusts 362 packages from twelve declared dependencies — an amplification of 30.2× — behind which stand 197 distinct humans with publish rights. The registry does not change the hazard per maintainer. It changes how many maintainers there are. This is not hypothetical: a self-replicating npm worm compromised hundreds of packages in September 2025 and again, at larger scale, that November; a variant in May 2026 became the first to span both npm and PyPI; and a further wave in August 2026 reached packages with billions of monthly downloads 919293 9495.

The labour-market decision is usually argued badly, and L8 shows why. Given a team of six at 15% annual attrition over three years, the probability that a given engineer is still present is 0.6141 and the expected number of departures is 2.32 — in every language, identically. Nothing about a language changes the risk.

The reason this matters more than it looks is that software that is used gets changed, continuously and indefinitely — the oldest well-supported empirical regularity in the field 114115. A language decision is therefore not a decision about the code that exists but about the code that will exist, written by people who are not in the room. The accumulating cost of deferred structural work has a substantial literature of its own 116117, and the delivery-performance research consistently finds that the organisations which do well are those with short feedback loops rather than those with particular technologies 118. Ownership concentration and communication structure predict defect outcomes 107119, which is the empirical shadow of Conway's observation 108. None of this is a language property. All of it is priced by the language decision.

What changes is recovery. Under a fixed hiring funnel, the time to fill one seat spans 47.1× between the largest and smallest pools modelled, which over three years is 4,807 extra uncovered engineer-days at the far end. That is the number to weigh against whatever the niche language buys — and the two are not denominated in the same units, which is precisely why this argument never resolves in a meeting.

11The ledger

Collected, the sixteen decisions form a matrix: the rung at which the cheap position's characteristic failure is discovered, against the party holding it when that happens.

Two features of the matrix carry the argument.

The lower-right region is crowded. Six of the sixteen decisions put their failure at D0 with the user paying — reclamation, spatial safety, temporal safety, the memory model, the foreign-function boundary and the registry. These are not a random six. They are the decisions where the language has declined to model something, and where declining to model it means nobody finds out until somebody outside the organisation does.

The D5 column is nearly empty, and it is empty for a reason. Nothing arrives at D5 by accident. Every entry there was bought — with annotation, with restricted expressiveness, with compile time, with a smaller hiring pool. The ladder has an ascent cost, and the plates that sit high are the plates whose languages charge most visibly for the position.

12What the evidence will and will not support

A reader who has got this far is entitled to ask how much of it is known rather than argued. The honest answer is: less than the confidence of the average conference talk would suggest, and the shortfall is itself informative.

The reproduction problem

The most cited empirical claim in this area is that language choice correlates with defect density in open-source repositories 120, republished for a general audience three years later 121. A reproduction study repeated the analysis, found substantial methodological problems — including in the classification of commits as bug-fixing and in the handling of multiple comparisons — and reported that of the original associations, only a small number survived 122. The original authors responded 123; the reproduction authors responded to the response 124. A separate group reanalysed the same data under Bayesian guidelines and concluded that the effects, where present at all, were small enough that practical significance was doubtful 125.

The correct reading is not that languages do not matter. It is that observational data from repositories cannot easily separate the language from the people who choose it, the domain it is used in, and the culture around it. Nothing in this article rests on that literature, which is why it appears here rather than in a plate.

The controlled experiments

There is a body of controlled human-subject work, largely from one research programme, and it is more interesting than its citation count suggests. Early results found that static typing did not reduce development time on small tasks 126. Later and larger studies, with more realistic tasks, found benefits that were real but conditional: static types helped most when the API was undocumented 127, helped with certain maintenance tasks and not others 12839, and interacted with documentation quality 38. Work on syntax found that novices were no more accurate with C-style syntax than with a randomly generated one 129 — a result which, if you believe it, indicts a great deal of received wisdom about readability. The same authors' assessment of the field's epistemic standards is worth reading in full 4. An experimental tradition existed as early as 1977 and was not built upon 130.

The one thing this literature establishes firmly is that the effects are small relative to the variance between individual programmers, which was the finding of the earliest careful cross-language comparison as well 131 and of the Rosetta Code study a decade and a half later 132.

The benchmark problem

Performance comparisons have their own pathology. Measurement bias — layout in memory, environment size, link order — can produce differences large enough to reverse a conclusion, without anything obviously wrong having been done 72. Managed runtimes require statistically rigorous methodology that most published comparisons do not use 71133. Even a careful and well-executed ranking, such as the energy-efficiency work that is widely cited for its league table 134, is a statement about a particular benchmark suite.

Experiment L9 makes the structural point without needing to accuse anyone of sloppiness. Under a generative model in which the language×task interaction has a standard deviation of 0.40 against a language effect of 0.45 — that is, where "it depends on the workload" is a real and comparably sized term — separating the two extreme languages, 2.66× apart, takes three tasks. Separating adjacent languages 1.20× apart takes fifty: seventeen times more evidence for a claim 2.2 times smaller. A twenty-five-task suite recovers the exact ordering of six languages 62.65% of the time, which means that better than one such table in three has at least one adjacent pair the wrong way round.

Two honest researchers can therefore publish contradictory orderings of neighbouring languages, over different task sets, with neither having done anything wrong. This should change how such tables are read. It should not be taken to mean that no performance claim is knowable: the extremes separate quickly, and a claim that a tight numeric loop in CPython is slower than the same loop in C is not in serious doubt.

The cost curve

The 1:10:100 phase-cost curve — the claim that a defect found in production costs a hundred times what it costs in design — is repeated constantly and is much weaker than its reputation. It traces to early data of limited scope 135, has been examined and found unsupported in re-analysis of substantial data sets 136, and has been documented as a case study in how software engineering folklore propagates 137. This article uses the ratio in L2 but treats it as an unknown and reports the break-even across two orders of magnitude of it, precisely because the point value cannot be defended. The reassuring finding is that the verdict does not depend on it: the decision flips only if a production defect costs less than twenty-five minutes to deal with.

The estimation problem underneath

Any argument of the form 'this language will cost us less' inherits the accuracy of software cost estimation generally, which is poor: a systematic review of the estimation literature found persistent, large errors and little evidence that formal models outperform expert judgement 138139. The field's folklore is dense with confidently repeated numbers that do not survive checking 140, which is the motivation for treating claims evidentially rather than by authority 141142. And the framing of the choice matters to the choice: losses loom larger than equivalent gains 143, which is part of why the argument for a safer language is easier to make after an incident than before one. Brooks's warning that no single technique yields an order-of-magnitude improvement 144 is not an argument against choosing carefully; it is an argument against expecting the choice to be the thing that saves the project.

What is actually well established

Three things, at least, are on firm ground.

First, the memory-safety corpus. That a large majority of severe vulnerabilities in large C and C++ codebases are memory-safety defects is reported consistently and independently across vendors and years 1089. Android's data is the strongest public natural experiment available: as new code shifted to memory-safe languages, the memory-safety share of vulnerabilities fell year over year and dropped below twenty per cent, with the reported vulnerability density of the new Rust code around a thousandfold lower than the C and C++ it sat beside — alongside a lower rollback rate and less time in code review, which contradicts the assumption that the safety was bought with velocity 11 12. This is a class of evidence the type-system debate has never had.

Second, the mechanism is understood rather than merely observed. Rust's guarantees have been formalised and machine-checked, including the soundness of its unsafe escape hatches when used to build safe abstractions 2223. Compiler correctness itself has been verified for a realistic C compiler 69, and randomised testing has demonstrated that unverified production compilers contain substantial numbers of wrong-code bugs while the verified middle-end did not 70. Full functional correctness of an operating-system kernel has been proved 145. D5 is not a rhetorical rung; it is reachable, and people have reached it.

Third, adoption is not driven by the merits under discussion. The largest study of language adoption found that prior exposure and library availability dominated intrinsic language features 105. This is worth holding next to any argument in this article, including its own conclusion.

13A procedure, and the number it turns on

Experiment L10 assembles the pieces into a five-year cost of ownership with five terms — building it, changing it, the escapes that reach a user, the compute it burns, and the staffing churn — for four language families across three jobs. The families and their parameters are identical in all three; only the job changes. The defect term is taken directly from L1 rather than assumed independently.

The result is not a ranking. Across the three profiles there are two distinct winners and three distinct dominant cost terms. The kernel module is decided by defects, which account for 66.66% of the winner's own bill. The one-off script is decided by build cost, 64.74% of the winner's bill; the affine language comes in at 1.80× and the C-family at 2.75×, not because either is worse at anything but because nothing in that profile is large enough to amortise a build cost. The fleet is decided by change cost — 41.21% — which is the term that carries the compiler's refactoring recall from L6 and which does not appear in any benchmark anywhere.

A ranking that does not name a profile is therefore a ranking of a cost term the reader may not be paying. That is the specific sense in which "it depends" is not a cop-out but a description of the arithmetic.

The case for staying low

An article organised around a ladder invites the reading that up is good, and that reading is wrong often enough to be worth arguing against explicitly.

The one-off script profile in L10 is the clean case. Its winner is the dynamic language, at 1,986 units against 5,454 for the C-family option — and 64.74% of the winner's bill is simply the cost of writing the thing. Nothing in that profile is large enough to amortise anything. The code will run once, on one machine, watched by the person who wrote it, and a wrong answer costs four hundred units and is immediately visible. Every property that makes a language good at the kernel-module profile is, here, a tax with no corresponding benefit: exhaustive matching protects against a variant that will never be added, refactoring recall protects a refactor that will never happen, and a borrow checker prevents a use-after-free in a process that will exit in nine seconds.

This is not a concession; it is the same arithmetic. The value of standing on a high rung scales with the number of times the code is read, changed and run, and with the blast radius of one wrong answer. When those numbers are small the value goes to zero while the cost does not, and the correct decision is the one that minimises time-to-first-answer. The scripting tradition made exactly this argument, in these terms, before most of the languages in this article existed 146.

The failure mode this creates is worth naming, because it is the most common one in practice: the script that succeeds. Something written under script-profile assumptions acquires users, then a scheduler, then a second author, then a service level objective — and the profile silently changes under code that was correctly optimised for the old one. Almost every large legacy disaster is this, not a wrong decision. The lesson is not to write everything as if it will become a platform. It is to notice when the profile has moved, since the ledger's inputs — lifetime, change frequency, blast radius, whether an operator exists — are exactly the quantities that change first.

Where the answer changes

The more useful question is not which family wins but how far the world has to move before the answer changes. Holding the fleet profile fixed and varying only the cost of one escaped defect produces a single crossover.

Below an escaped-defect cost of about 3,711 currency units, the managed runtime wins; above it, the affine language does. Everything else — the velocity difference, the CPU multiple, the hiring penalty, the refactoring recall — is already priced in and does not move the boundary. The entire argument, for that profile, reduces to a single number that most engineering organisations have never measured and could measure in an afternoon from their own incident record.

This suggests a procedure, which is offered without any claim that it is novel — it is ordinary multi-attribute decision analysis 147, applied to a domain that usually refuses it 148149.

  1. Name the profile before naming any language. Expected lifetime, expected size at end of life, number of times the core data model will change, blast radius of one wrong answer, whether an operator exists at all.
  2. Price one escaped defect. From your own incident log, not from a curve in a book. Order of magnitude is enough; the crossover is insensitive within a factor of three.
  3. Identify the two or three defect classes that actually dominate your history. L1's sensitivity analysis shows the classes are not fungible: the best available single rung move in the C-family profile removes 16.20% of all escapes, the worst effective one removes 1.95%, and one of the ten moves on offer buys literally nothing. Most language arguments are conducted about classes that are not in anyone's top three.
  4. Check which cost term dominates your profile, then ask what each candidate does to that term. If the answer is change cost, compile-time exhaustiveness and refactoring recall are the decisive properties and raw throughput is noise. If it is compute, the reverse.
  5. Price the two terms that are not in the language. The trusted computing base you are joining (L7: twelve declared names, 362 packages, 197 humans) and the replacement time for one departure (L8: identical attrition risk, a 47× spread in recovery).
  6. Write down what would change your mind. If nothing would, the exercise was not a decision.

The obligation that has appeared underneath all of this

One thing has changed since this debate was purely an engineering preference. Language choice has become a matter of published government position: a joint international paper made the case for memory-safe roadmaps 150, a White House report recommended moving to memory-safe languages 151, failing to publish a roadmap has been listed as a product security bad practice 152, and a joint agency information sheet has restated the guidance for developers 153. The European Cyber Resilience Act imposes obligations on products with digital elements 112, and secure development framework guidance has been folded into federal procurement 98113. Meanwhile C++ finished its technical work on the 2026 revision having shipped a hardened standard library and deferred its safety profiles 101102, after declining a more radical borrow-checking proposal 103104; and the Linux kernel dropped the experimental designation from its Rust support 154155, shipping Rust drivers in a tree that reached version 7.0 in April 2026 156.

This does not make the memory-safe languages correct. It makes the D0 rung expensive in a way that is now partly external to the team, which is exactly the kind of change the ledger is built to absorb: a cost that used to be paid by users diffusely is being moved, by policy, onto the vendor concentratedly. The arithmetic does not change. The price does.

A note on what this framework does not cover

Three things sit outside it and should be named rather than quietly omitted. It says nothing about programming as an activity people find pleasant, which is not a soft consideration when it drives who will work on the code. It says nothing about the very large population who write programs without being programmers, for whom the entire calculus is different 157146. And it treats modularity as fixed when in practice the boundaries drawn inside a system — the decision Parnas identified as the real one 158 — dominate many of the sixteen plates above; a badly decomposed program in a good language is worse than the reverse 159. Go's own designers made this argument explicitly, framing the language as a response to engineering scale rather than to expressiveness 42160.

The answer

There is no superior language, and the reason is not diplomatic. It is that "superior" would have to mean "cheaper", cost is a schedule rather than a scalar, and the schedules are not comparable without a profile that names the payer. The best language for a problem is the one whose schedule of prices most closely matches the budget of the team that will own it — including the budget lines nobody writes down, which are the operator's sleep, the reviewer's attention, the maintainer's successor, and the user's silent wrong answer.

Turing settled what can be computed. Everything since has been an argument about the invoice.
