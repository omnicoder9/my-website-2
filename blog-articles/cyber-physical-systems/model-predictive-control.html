Model Predictive Control

Solve the whole problem, apply one move, throw the rest away, and do it again. A method invented in refineries, proved correct two decades later, and now running at a microsecond.

Subject  Receding-horizon control Figures  9, generated programmatically References  39, Vancouver

Almost every feedback controller ever deployed answers a single question: given the error right now, what should the actuator do right now? Model predictive control asks a harder and more useful one. Given everything I know about how this system behaves, what is the best sequence of moves over the next few seconds — and which of those moves am I forbidden from making? It solves that question completely, applies the first move, throws the rest of the answer away, and asks it again at the next sampling instant.

That last step sounds wasteful. It is the entire point. The discarding is what converts an open-loop plan into a feedback law, and the re-solving is what makes the controller robust to the fact that its model is wrong and the world has moved on.[1] Everything else in MPC — the horizon, the cost function, the constraint sets, the quadratic program solved in microseconds on an embedded chip — is machinery in service of that loop.

MPC is now the dominant advanced control technology in the process industries, the standard tool for constrained multivariable problems in automotive and aerospace, and increasingly the method of choice in legged robotics and power electronics. It is also, unusually for a control methodology, a technique that industry invented and academia spent two decades catching up to.[2, 3]

01The receding horizon

Fix a sampling instant and call it k. The controller holds an explicit dynamic model of the plant. It takes the current state — measured, or more usually reconstructed by an observer — and uses the model to predict how the plant would respond to any candidate sequence of future inputs over a finite window of N steps. Among all sequences that respect the system's physical limits, it selects the one that minimises a scalar cost: typically the accumulated tracking error plus a penalty on control effort.

The optimiser returns a full plan, N moves deep. The controller applies only the first, u(k). At the next instant the window slides forward one step, the state is measured afresh, and the whole problem is solved again from scratch. The horizon recedes ahead of the controller like a carrot on a stick, never reached, always the same length. Hence the name: receding-horizon control.[1, 4]

Why discard a perfectly good plan? Because the plan was optimal for a model, and the model is a fiction. Real plants drift, loads change, disturbances arrive unannounced, and the identification data was collected on a Tuesday two years ago. An open-loop plan executed to completion accumulates model error with no mechanism to correct it. Re-solving from the measured state at every instant injects the one thing the plan lacks — feedback — and does so without the controller ever needing an explicit feedback gain. The optimiser is the feedback law; it just happens to be defined implicitly, as the solution to a problem rather than the output of a formula.[5]

Three things MPC needs that a PID loop does not

  1. A model. Explicit, dynamic, and good enough over the horizon length — not over all time.
  2. An optimiser. Run to convergence, in real time, every sampling instant, on the target hardware.
  3. A state estimate. The prediction has to start somewhere, and that somewhere is rarely measured in full.

The cost of those three requirements is why MPC did not begin life on fast systems. Its natural first home was the chemical plant, where sampling periods are measured in minutes and a minute is a comfortable budget for an optimisation. Its subsequent history is largely the story of that budget collapsing by nine orders of magnitude.

02Born in industry

The intellectual ingredients of MPC — dynamic models, quadratic costs, optimal trajectories over a horizon — were assembled by the optimal control theory of the late 1950s and 1960s. But the linear-quadratic regulator that theory produced has a structural flaw from an industrial point of view: it is optimal only in the absence of constraints, and process plants are defined by their constraints. Valves saturate, vessels have pressure ratings, and product purity has a contractual floor. A control law that cannot represent those facts is a control law that operators will detune into uselessness.[3, 1]

So the method was invented, more or less independently of the academic literature, by people running refineries. In 1978 Richalet and colleagues published model predictive heuristic control, implemented as the IDCOM package and demonstrated on industrial plants; it used an impulse-response model and a reference trajectory approaching the setpoint.[6] In 1980 Cutler and Ramaker of Shell Oil described dynamic matrix control, built on a step-response model and least-squares solution of the prediction equations.[7] Neither paper contained a stability proof. Both worked in the field, which mattered more.

The decisive move came in 1986, when García and Morshedi reformulated DMC as a quadratic program.[8] That single change made constraints first-class citizens: instead of clipping the computed move at a limit after the fact, the optimiser was told about the limit in advance and planned around it. Almost everything distinctive about modern MPC descends from that reformulation. In parallel, Clarke and co-workers developed generalised predictive control from the adaptive-control tradition, using transfer-function models and drawing an explicit connection to self-tuning regulators.[9]

By the late 1990s the gap between practice and theory had become embarrassing: thousands of successful installations governed by algorithms with no guarantee of stability. The consolidation arrived in 2000, when Mayne, Rawlings, Rao and Scokaert published a synthesis showing that a broad family of constrained MPC formulations could be proved stabilising by equipping them with a terminal cost and a terminal constraint set, with the optimal value function serving as a Lyapunov function.[5] It is difficult to overstate how much of the subsequent literature is downstream of that paper.

Qin and Badgwell's industrial survey, published in 2003, documented well over four thousand deployed applications across vendors, heavily concentrated in refining and petrochemicals, and made the scale of the installed base legible to the academic community for the first time.[2] Two years earlier, Bemporad and colleagues had shown that for linear systems with quadratic costs and linear constraints the optimal MPC law is piecewise affine over a polytopic partition of the state space, and can therefore be computed offline.[10] That result opened the door to sampling rates that online optimisation could not then reach. By 2014 Mayne could survey a field that had acquired robust, stochastic, economic and distributed variants, and identify the remaining open problems as ones of scale and computation.[11]

03The problem being solved

Write the plant as a discrete-time model with state \(x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\) and input \(u \in \mathbb{R}^{m}\). At instant k the controller has an estimate \(\hat{x}(k)\), and it reasons about predicted quantities \(x_{j|k}\) — the state predicted j steps ahead, given information at k. The finite-horizon optimal control problem is then:

$$ \begin{aligned} \min_{\;u_{0|k},\,\dots,\,u_{N-1|k}}\quad & V_f\big(x_{N|k}\big) \;+\; \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\ \ell\big(x_{j|k},\,u_{j|k}\big)\\[4pt] \text{subject to}\quad & x_{j+1|k} = f\big(x_{j|k},\,u_{j|k}\big),\qquad x_{0|k}=\hat{x}(k)\\[2pt] & x_{j|k}\in\mathcal{X},\qquad u_{j|k}\in\mathcal{U},\qquad j=0,\dots,N-1\\[2pt] & x_{N|k}\in\mathcal{X}_f \end{aligned} $$

Four objects carry all the design freedom. The stage cost \(\ell\) prices deviation and effort at every step. The terminal cost \(V_f\) prices the state left over when the horizon runs out. The constraint sets \(\mathcal{X}\) and \(\mathcal{U}\) encode what the hardware and the process will tolerate. The terminal set \(\mathcal{X}_f\) is the theoretical keystone, and we return to it below. In the common linear-quadratic case these specialise to

$$\ell(x,u)=\lVert x-r\rVert_{Q}^{2}+\lVert u\rVert_{R}^{2}, \qquad V_f(x)=\lVert x-r\rVert_{P}^{2},$$

with \(Q\succeq 0\) and \(R\succ 0\) chosen by the designer and \(P\) usually taken as the solution of the discrete algebraic Riccati equation, so that the tail of the horizon is priced as though the unconstrained optimal policy took over at step N.[1, 4]

Horizon length is the first real design decision, and it is a trade. Too short and the controller becomes myopic: it will happily drive the state into a corner from which the constraints admit no escape, because the trap lies beyond the window. Too long and the problem grows, the solve time grows with it, and the predictions at the far end are dominated by model error anyway. The terminal cost exists precisely to buy back some of the foresight that a short horizon gives up.[12]

Two ways of handing the problem to a solver dominate practice. The condensed form eliminates the predicted states by substituting the dynamics recursively, leaving only the input sequence as a decision variable. Stacking the predictions gives \(\mathbf{X}=S_x\hat{x}(k)+S_u\mathbf{U}\) and the problem collapses to a dense quadratic program:

$$\min_{\mathbf{U}}\ \tfrac12\,\mathbf{U}^{\!\top} H\,\mathbf{U} + \big(F\hat{x}(k)\big)^{\!\top}\mathbf{U} \qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad G\,\mathbf{U}\;\le\;w+E\,\hat{x}(k)$$

The Hessian \(H\) is small — its dimension is Nm, not N(n+m) — but dense. The sparse form keeps the states as variables and the dynamics as equality constraints, producing a much larger problem with a banded, block-diagonal structure that a structure-exploiting solver can factor in time linear in the horizon rather than cubic. For long horizons or high-dimensional states the sparse form wins decisively, which is why fast MPC implementations almost universally adopt it.[13]

04Constraints, and why they are the point

Strip the constraints out of the problem above and, for a linear plant with a quadratic cost and an infinite horizon, the solution collapses to the linear-quadratic regulator: a constant gain matrix, computable once, evaluated in nanoseconds. If that were the operating regime, nobody would tolerate the cost of an online optimiser. MPC earns its keep exactly in the region where the LQR answer is inadmissible.[1]

Constraints come in kinds that behave very differently. Input constraints — actuator saturation, slew-rate limits, valve travel — are structurally benign: the set \(\mathcal{U}\) is under the controller's direct authority, so a feasible input can always be found. State and output constraints are the dangerous ones. A temperature ceiling or a minimum clearance is a restriction on where the plant may go, and whether it can be honoured depends on where the plant already is and how much control authority remains. A state constraint can be violated by a disturbance before the controller has any say in the matter.

This is why hard state constraints are usually softened in deployment: the constraint is relaxed by a non-negative slack variable which is then heavily penalised in the cost, often with an exact-penalty term that keeps the slack at zero whenever the hard problem is feasible. The result is a controller that respects the limit when it can and degrades gracefully rather than failing to return an answer when it cannot. An optimiser that reports infeasibility to a plant that still needs a valve position in eighty milliseconds has not solved anybody's problem.[4, 1]

The deeper issue is recursive feasibility. It is not enough that the problem be solvable now; it must remain solvable at every future instant. Nothing in the naive formulation guarantees this, and a controller can walk itself into a state from which no admissible input sequence satisfies the constraints. The standard remedy is to require the predicted state to land, at the end of the horizon, inside a terminal set \(\mathcal{X}_f\) that is control invariant: a set from which some admissible input keeps the state inside the set forever. Once the state reaches such a region the controller has a guaranteed escape route in perpetuity, and the feasible solution at the current step can be shifted forward to construct a feasible solution at the next.[14, 5]

Constraints are not a nuisance bolted onto the control problem. They are the reason the control problem is interesting.

There is an economic argument here too, and in the process industries it is the one that pays for the installation. Plants are most profitable near their limits — higher throughput, tighter product specification, less energy per unit output. A controller with large variance must sit well back from every limit to avoid violating it. A controller that handles constraints explicitly can operate closer to the boundary and, in many installations, ride along it. The constraint becomes an operating point rather than a hazard, and the difference between those two regimes is measured in margin.[2]

05Stability, feasibility, robustness

An MPC controller is defined implicitly, as whatever the optimiser returns. Proving that such an object stabilises anything is not obvious. The now-standard argument makes the optimal value function \(V^{\star}\) do the work of a Lyapunov function. Equip the problem with a terminal cost that is a local control-Lyapunov function on a control-invariant terminal set, together with a local controller that keeps the state inside that set. Then the optimal plan computed at the current step can be shifted one step forward and completed by the local controller, producing a feasible — though generally suboptimal — plan for the next step. Comparing its cost to the current optimum yields the descent inequality

$$V^{\star}\big(x^{+}\big)-V^{\star}(x)\ \le\ -\,\ell\big(x,\kappa(x)\big),$$

which drives \(V^{\star}\) monotonically down and the state to the origin. The same shifted-plan construction simultaneously delivers recursive feasibility, which is why the two properties are almost always proved together.[5]

Stability, however, is not robustness. The argument above assumes the model is exact. A controller can be nominally stabilising and still lose feasibility under a disturbance that pushes the state outside the region where the shifted plan works. Three families of response have developed.

Tube MPC separates the problem into a nominal trajectory and an error. A nominal MPC plans for the disturbance-free system, while an ancillary feedback law keeps the true state inside a bounded set — the tube — centred on the nominal path. The nominal problem is then solved with tightened constraints, shrunk by exactly the tube's cross-section, so that every realisation within the tube satisfies the original constraints. The result is robust constraint satisfaction with a nominal problem no harder than before.[15] The construction extends to nonlinear dynamics, where the tube is generated by a local controller rather than an explicit set recursion.[16]

Stochastic MPC takes a different view: if the disturbance has a known distribution, insisting on satisfaction for every realisation is needlessly conservative, since worst cases may be vanishingly improbable. Constraints are instead imposed in probability — the chance of violation must stay below a stated level — which trades a small, quantified risk for a substantial gain in performance. The price is that chance constraints are generally non-convex and must be approximated, by scenario sampling, distributionally robust reformulation, or analytic tightening.[17]

Min–max MPC optimises against the worst admissible disturbance directly. It is the most conservative approach and, in its closed-loop form, the most expensive; it appears more often in the literature than on plants.[18]

06Making it fast enough

Every sampling instant, an optimisation problem must be solved to sufficient accuracy, on the available hardware, within the sampling period, every single time. A control law that is usually fast enough is not a control law. This hard real-time requirement shapes the algorithms far more than raw throughput does: what matters is a tight and provable bound on the worst case, not a good average.

For linear MPC the problem is a convex quadratic program, and three solver families dominate. Active-set methods guess which constraints are tight and update that guess; because consecutive MPC problems differ only slightly, an active-set solver can be warm-started from the previous solution and often converges in a handful of iterations. qpOASES exploits this by treating the QP as a parametric problem traversed along a homotopy from the previous solve to the current one.[19] Interior-point methods follow a central path and offer far more predictable iteration counts; combined with the banded structure of the sparse formulation, they give per-iteration cost linear in the horizon, and this combination is what first made online MPC viable at kilohertz rates.[13] First-order operator-splitting methods trade asymptotic accuracy for extremely cheap iterations and a division-free inner loop; OSQP, built on the alternating direction method of multipliers, factors its matrix once and reuses it across iterations and across solves, is division-free after setup, and detects infeasibility reliably — properties that suit embedded targets well.[20]

A fourth strategy removes the online solver altogether. Because the linear MPC problem depends on the current state only through the right-hand side of its constraints, it is a multiparametric quadratic program, and its solution is a piecewise-affine function of the state defined over a polytopic partition of the feasible region.[10] Solve it once offline, store the regions and their affine gains, and the online task reduces to locating the region containing the current state and evaluating one small matrix–vector product.[21]

Explicit MPC delivers microsecond execution and a worst-case time that can be certified, which is why it appears in automotive and power-electronics applications where the sampling period is short and the processor is small. Its limitation is combinatorial: the number of critical regions grows sharply with horizon length, state dimension and constraint count, and beyond a handful of states the lookup table exceeds any plausible memory budget. Explicit MPC is a technique for small problems that must run very fast, not a general replacement for online optimisation.[4]

Nonlinear MPC adds another layer, since each solve is now a nonconvex program. The pivotal insight is that the controller does not need the exact optimum — it needs a good input, quickly, and it will get another chance in a few milliseconds. The real-time iteration scheme exploits this by performing a single Newton-type step per sampling instant, splitting the work into a preparation phase that runs before the measurement arrives and a short feedback phase that runs after, so the latency between measuring and actuating is a small fraction of the total computation.[22] Combined with automatic differentiation and code generation, it brought nonlinear MPC onto systems sampling at hundreds of hertz.[23, 24]

07A family, not an algorithm

“MPC” names a design pattern — predict, optimise, apply the first move, repeat — instantiated very differently depending on what the model looks like and what the cost is asked to represent.

08The working toolchain

The practical barrier to MPC was never the concept; it was the engineering between a formulation on paper and a solver running deterministically on a microcontroller. Over roughly fifteen years that gap has been closed by a small set of open frameworks, most of them from the same academic lineage that produced the theory.

A workable division of labour has settled out. Prototype the formulation in a high-level environment where the model can be written symbolically and the derivatives come for free; profile against realistic sampling periods; then generate self-contained C for the target, with a solver whose worst-case iteration count you can bound and defend to whoever signs off on the system.

09A worked example

The smallest problem that shows what MPC actually does is a double integrator: a unit mass on a frictionless line, driven by a bounded force, asked to move one metre and stop. Two hard limits apply. The force saturates at \(\pm 1\). And velocity may never exceed \(0.6\) — a constraint on a state, not an input, which is the case a saturation block downstream of a PID cannot handle at all.

Everything the controller needs is in the code below: the model, the weights, the terminal cost from the Riccati recursion, the constraints, and the loop that solves, applies one move, and discards the rest.

mpc_double_integrator.py
"""Constrained linear MPC on a double integrator, solved in closed loop.

The plant is a unit mass sliding on a frictionless line: the state is
(position, velocity) and the input is a bounded force. A ceiling on velocity
makes a *state* constraint go active during the transient -- the situation a
fixed-gain law cannot honour and MPC handles by construction.
"""
import numpy as np
import cvxpy as cp

dt, N = 0.1, 20                        # sample period (s), prediction horizon
A = np.array([[1.0, dt], [0.0, 1.0]])
B = np.array([[0.5 * dt ** 2], [dt]])
Q = np.diag([12.0, 1.0])               # state cost: mostly position error
R = np.array([[0.5]])                  # input cost: penalise effort
u_max, v_max = 1.0, 0.6                # hard input limit, hard state limit
x_ref = np.array([1.0, 0.0])           # move one metre, then hold still

# Terminal weight = the LQR cost-to-go, so the tail beyond the horizon is
# priced as if the unconstrained optimal policy took over at step N.
P = Q.copy()
for _ in range(500):
    K = np.linalg.solve(R + B.T @ P @ B, B.T @ P @ A)
    P = Q + A.T @ P @ (A - B @ K)


def mpc_step(x0):
    """Solve the finite-horizon OCP from x0; return only the first input."""
    x = cp.Variable((2, N + 1))
    u = cp.Variable((1, N))
    cost, cons = 0, [x[:, 0] == x0]
    for k in range(N):
        cost += cp.quad_form(x[:, k] - x_ref, Q) + cp.quad_form(u[:, k], R)
        cons += [x[:, k + 1] == A @ x[:, k] + B @ u[:, k],   # dynamics
                 cp.abs(u[:, k]) <= u_max,                   # input limit
                 cp.abs(x[1, k + 1]) <= v_max]               # state limit
    cost += cp.quad_form(x[:, N] - x_ref, P)                 # terminal cost
    cp.Problem(cp.Minimize(cost), cons).solve(solver=cp.OSQP)
    return float(u[:, 0].value[0])


x, log = np.zeros(2), []
for k in range(55):
    uk = mpc_step(x)                   # re-solve from the measured state
    log.append((k * dt, x[0], x[1], uk))
    x = A @ x + B.flatten() * uk       # apply the first move, discard the rest

t, pos, vel, u = map(np.array, zip(*log))
print(f"settles at {pos[-1]:.3f} m   peak velocity {vel.max():.3f} m/s "
      f"(limit {v_max})   peak |force| {np.abs(u).max():.3f} (limit {u_max})")

Running it produces the closed-loop response in Figure 8, and the behaviour is worth reading carefully, because both constraints become active and the controller handles each differently.

The force saturates immediately and stays pinned at its limit while the mass accelerates. Velocity climbs to exactly \(0.6\) and then tracks that ceiling — the state constraint is active, and the optimiser has found the input that holds the trajectory precisely on the boundary rather than backing away from it. Then, while still short of the target and with no error signal yet suggesting trouble, the controller reverses the force and begins braking. It does this because the horizon is twenty steps long and already contains the stop; the plan that ignores it is more expensive. No reactive controller produces that behaviour, because the information that motivates it does not exist in the present error.

The final position settles at 1.000 m with peak velocity 0.600 and peak force 1.000 — both limits reached, neither breached. That is the whole argument for the method in one plot: constraints treated as part of the problem, and the future treated as available information.

10Where it actually runs

MPC's migration across engineering can be read as a single number falling: the sampling period at which the optimisation can be completed reliably. Each time that number dropped by an order of magnitude, a new domain came into range.

Process industries. The original and still the largest installed base. Refining, petrochemicals and chemicals account for the great majority of documented deployments, on multivariable problems with strong interaction and hard quality and safety limits, where operating nearer a constraint translates directly into margin.[2]

Automotive. Powertrain, chassis, and driver-assistance functions are natural MPC problems — multivariable, heavily constrained, with preview information available from route and sensor data. The industrial challenge has been less formulation than certification and cost: the algorithm must run on an inexpensive automotive-grade processor with a worst-case execution time that can be bounded and defended, which is one reason explicit and short-horizon formulations have been attractive here.[32]

Legged robotics. Balance is a constrained optimisation problem: contact forces must lie inside friction cones, feet may only push, and the centre of mass must remain dynamically supportable. The MIT Cheetah 3 team formulated locomotion as a convex MPC over simplified centroidal dynamics and solved it fast enough to run in the control loop, producing gaits and recovery behaviours that were not designed by hand but fell out of the optimisation.[33]

Aerospace. Attitude control, rendezvous and docking, entry and landing are all constrained trajectory-optimisation problems with hard safety limits and, often, a fuel budget as the natural cost. Spacecraft rendezvous adds line-of-sight and approach-cone constraints that MPC represents directly.[34, 35] The most celebrated result in the area is the convexification of powered-descent guidance: what appears to be a nonconvex problem — a thrust magnitude bounded both below and above, plus pointing limits — can be transformed into an equivalent convex program whose global optimum is computable in bounded time, a property that matters when the solve happens during descent with no opportunity to retry.[36, 37] That line of work, developed at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, underpins the modern approach to precision landing.

Power electronics. At the other end of the timescale, finite-control-set MPC exploits the fact that a converter's inputs are not continuous at all but a short list of switch configurations. The optimisation becomes an enumeration over that list, cheap enough to run every switching period, which allows the cost function to price things that are awkward to express in a cascaded linear design — torque ripple, switching losses, common-mode voltage — as explicit terms.[28, 38]

Buildings and energy. Building thermal dynamics are slow, constrained by comfort bands, and driven by weather and occupancy that can be forecast — close to an ideal MPC problem, with reported energy savings that are substantial and consistent across studies. The obstacle is economic rather than technical: every building needs its own model, and the cost of obtaining one has been the main barrier to deployment at scale.[39] Related arguments apply on the grid, where distributed MPC has been used to coordinate generation across interconnected control areas,[27] and in data centres, where learning-based predictive control of cooling plant has been demonstrated on operating facilities.[29]

11What it costs, and what is unsolved

MPC's weaknesses follow directly from its strengths. It needs a model, and the model is usually the expensive part: identification effort dominates commissioning cost, and performance degrades as the plant drifts away from the model that was fitted to it. It needs an optimiser that converges within the sampling period every time, which constrains hardware and complicates certification in regulated domains. And it has more tuning knobs than a PID loop — horizon lengths, two or more weighting matrices, terminal conditions, softening penalties — that interact in ways which are not always intuitive.

Several frontiers are active. Learning-based MPC attempts to use data where first-principles models are unavailable or too costly, whether by learning the dynamics, learning the residual between a nominal model and reality, or learning to approximate the control law itself; the open question is how to retain constraint satisfaction and stability guarantees when part of the controller is fitted rather than derived.[11] Economic formulations optimise profit or energy directly rather than tracking a setpoint computed by a separate layer, which collapses two levels of the control hierarchy into one but forfeits the natural Lyapunov structure that tracking costs provide, requiring dissipativity conditions to recover stability guarantees.[26] Scale remains hard: distributed formulations that recover centralised performance without centralised communication are still an area of active work.[27, 18]

The oldest tension in the field has not gone away either. MPC was invented by practitioners, and the most-deployed formulations are still not the ones with the strongest guarantees; terminal sets and terminal costs, which do most of the theoretical work, are frequently omitted in industrial implementations in favour of a long horizon and operational experience. The gap has narrowed, but the field remains one where the theory explains the practice more often than it directs it.[11, 3]

12Coda

What makes model predictive control durable is not any particular algorithm but a reframing. Classical control asks for a function from error to action, to be designed once and evaluated forever. MPC asks instead for a well-posed optimisation problem — a model, a cost, a set of things that must not happen — and delegates the design of the control law to a solver, at every instant, forever. The engineer's work moves from tuning gains to stating the problem correctly.

That is a heavier computational contract and a stricter modelling one. In exchange it buys something no fixed gain can offer: a controller that knows what it is not allowed to do, and that plans around the future instead of reacting to the present. Nearly fifty years after the first refinery installations, the horizon is still receding, and the method keeps arriving in domains that could not previously afford it.
