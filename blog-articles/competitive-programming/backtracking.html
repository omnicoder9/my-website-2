Part I
The Skeleton

§1The Three Lines

Two functions, one token apart, that enumerate different universes

Here are two functions. They are the same length, they use the same variables, and they differ in one place.

A
walk(start):
    record(path)
    for i := start; i < n; i++:
        path.push(a[i])
        walk(i + 1)
        path.pop()
B
walk():
    if len(path) == n: record(path)
    for i := 0; i < n; i++ if !used[i]:
        used[i] = true; path.push(a[i])
        walk()
        path.pop(); used[i] = false

A enumerates the 2n subsets of the array. B enumerates its n! permutations. At n = 12 that is four thousand answers against four hundred and seventy-nine million: a factor of a hundred and seventeen thousand, produced by a change to the loop header and nothing else. Neither function is cleverer than the other. Neither is better optimised. They walk different trees because they were given different answers to a single question: at this node, what am I allowed to choose next?

This is the claim the rest of this article defends. The recursion in a backtracking solution is not the hard part and is not where the variation lives. Choose, explore, un-choose is three lines, and those three lines are identical in the subset enumerator, the permutation enumerator, the N-Queens solver, the Sudoku solver, and the grid word search. Everything that distinguishes one classic problem from another is contained in three decisions made around the recursion:

  1. The choice set. Where do the candidates at this node come from — an index into an array, the complement of a used-set, or the legal values of a fixed position?
  2. The record point. When is a partial candidate also an answer — at every node, only at a leaf, or when a predicate fires?
  3. The kill rule. What makes a branch dead — a violated bound, a constraint conflict, or the fact that a sibling has already covered it?

Get all three right and the code writes itself, in any language, in about fifteen lines. Get the first one wrong and you enumerate the wrong universe. Get the second wrong and you solve a different problem with the same tree. Get the third wrong and you either produce duplicates, lose answers, or wait forever. Almost every bug in almost every backtracking solution is one of those four failures, and almost every one of them is visible in the loop header.

A word on what this article is and is not. It is not an argument that backtracking is underrated. In competitive programming it is the tool you reach for when the input bound is small enough to admit it and there is no polynomial structure to exploit, and the last section argues at some length for the cases where reaching for it is a mistake. It is an argument that the family of problems it covers — all subsets, all permutations, all combinations, any valid configuration — is a single problem wearing a dozen costumes, and that seeing the costume for what it is turns a dozen memorised templates into one template and three questions.

§2What the Word Means

Lehmer's coinage, Walker's formulation, and a 1965 paper that named a technique everyone was already using

The technique is older than its name, and much older than the recursion that now expresses it. Enumerating configurations by extending a partial one and abandoning it when it cannot be completed is what anyone does by hand with a pencil and an eraser, and it is what nineteenth-century recreational mathematicians did when they worked out the eight solutions families of the eight-queens puzzle. Gauss corresponded about the problem in 1850 and got the count wrong twice before settling on 9212.

The word backtrack is generally attributed to D. H. Lehmer in the 1950s. The first general formulation in print is Robert Walker's 1960 contribution to a combinatorial analysis symposium, which describes an enumerative procedure for a class of problems in terms that are recognisably the modern algorithm3. The paper that made it a named, teachable technique is Golomb and Baumert's Backtrack Programming in the Journal of the ACM in 1965, which sets out the method in full generality and — importantly — spends most of its length on the question that still matters, which is how to make the search cheap rather than how to make it correct4. Bitner and Reingold's 1975 follow-up in Communications of the ACM collected the practical techniques: preclusion, reordering, the use of bit vectors for constraint sets5. Fletcher's pentomino solver in 1965 and Wells's Elements of Combinatorial Computing in 1971 are the era's working examples67.

The technique also left a mark on language design: Lindstrom's 1979 work on backtracking as a generalised control construct is part of a line of attempts to make the choose/explore/un-choose discipline a first-class feature rather than a coding convention8. That line largely failed, and the reason is instructive — the interesting part of a backtracking program is never the control flow.

The backdrop to all of it is complexity. Backtracking is the method of choice for problems with no known polynomial algorithm, and the problems in this article are, in their general forms, exactly the ones Cook and Karp identified: satisfiability, graph colouring, Hamiltonian path, subset sum, exact cover91011. When a problem is NP-hard, "walk the tree intelligently" is not a stopgap awaiting a better idea; under standard assumptions it is the shape of the best available idea, and the engineering question becomes how much of the tree you can avoid rather than whether you can avoid all of it.

Knuth devotes the whole of Volume 4B of The Art of Computer Programming to backtracking and its descendants, and it is the single best source on the subject12. His 1975 paper on estimating the cost of a backtrack program before running it is the most useful thing ever written about the technique and is almost unknown outside the research literature; we implement it in §2213.

Two lineages descend from that root and are worth naming, because a competitive programmer who writes backtracking solutions is doing, by hand and at small scale, what both fields automated. The first is constraint satisfaction: Mackworth's arc consistency14, Haralick and Elliott's forward checking and minimum-remaining-values heuristics15, Gaschnig's and Prosser's backjumping1617, and the synthesis in Dechter's Constraint Processing18 and the Handbook of Constraint Programming19. The second is satisfiability solving: the Davis–Putnam procedure20, its 1962 backtracking reformulation as DPLL21, and the conflict-driven clause-learning solvers that grew out of GRASP and Chaff and now decide industrial instances with millions of variables22232425. A modern SAT solver is a backtracking search with about forty years of bookkeeping bolted onto it. Every piece of that bookkeeping has a recognisable ancestor in the loop headers we are about to look at.

§3The State You Carry and the State You Restore

Why the un-choose line exists, and what it must undo

A backtracking search is a depth-first traversal of a tree that is never materialised2627. The nodes are partial candidates; the edges are choices; the leaves are either complete candidates or dead ends. Because the tree is not materialised, the only representation of the current node is whatever the program is holding at the moment: a path array, some auxiliary flags, possibly a mutated copy of the input. That state is shared across the entire subtree below the current node, and the entire correctness of the algorithm rests on a single invariant:

When walk() returns, every piece of mutable state it touched is in exactly the condition it was in when walk() was called.

Everything that a node modifies on the way down, it restores on the way up. That is what the third line is for. It is also why the state is usually a stack: the last thing pushed is the first thing popped, and the discipline is easy to verify by eye because the push and the pop are adjacent in the source, separated only by the recursive call.

The state divides into three kinds, and it is worth separating them because they have different restore costs:

  • The path. The sequence of choices made to reach this node. Almost always an array with push and pop. This is what gets copied into the result.
  • Derived indices. Structures that make the kill rule cheap: the used[] array in a permutation, the three conflict sets in N-Queens, the row, column and box masks in Sudoku, the running remainder in combination sum. These are redundant — they can always be recomputed from the path — and they exist purely so that the legality test is O(1) instead of O(depth).
  • The input itself. Sometimes the cheapest place to record a choice is in the input: word search marks a visited cell in the board rather than carrying a separate visited array. This is legitimate and fast, and it is also the easiest kind of state to forget to restore, because the mutation does not look like state.

Three restore idioms cover almost everything. The first is push and pop, used for the path and for any array-shaped index. The second is save and write back, used when a slot is overwritten rather than appended to: keep the old value in a local, write the new one, restore the local afterwards. Word search does this with a board cell; Sudoku does it with a grid cell. The third is pass by value, which is not a restore at all: if the state the child needs is small enough to be a function argument, the parent's copy is untouched by construction and there is nothing to undo. The bitmask N-Queens solver in §19 has no un-choose line for exactly this reason, and it is the cleanest illustration in the article of why the restore exists — it is the price of sharing, and if you do not share, you do not pay it.

The failure mode that this discipline is designed to prevent is subtle and worth naming because it produces wrong answers rather than crashes. If a node fails to restore something, the sibling branches explored after it see a state that no legitimate partial candidate could produce. The search then explores a subtree of a tree that does not exist. It will usually still terminate, and it will usually still produce output, and the output will be missing answers or contain impossible ones. There is no exception, no assertion, and no obvious symptom. The only defence is that the push and the pop are written adjacent to each other and reviewed as a pair.

§4The Copy You Must Remember to Make

One array, reused for the whole search, and the one place it must not be

The path is a single array that lives for the duration of the search and is mutated millions of times. The results list holds answers that must survive the search. Those two facts are in direct conflict, and reconciling them is the most common bug in backtracking code across every language that has reference semantics.

If you append the path itself to the results list, you have appended a reference to a buffer that the search is about to change. By the time the search finishes, every entry in the results list points at the same array, and that array is empty, because the last thing the search did was pop everything off it. The symptom is unmistakable once seen: the right number of answers, all of them identical, all of them empty.

Every language expresses the fix differently, and the differences are instructive:

Goappend(res, path) append(res, append([]int(nil), path...)) A slice is a header over a shared backing array28
Javares.add(path) res.add(new ArrayList<>(path)) ArrayList is a reference type29
C++res.push_back(path) — already correct push_back copies the vector30
Rustwill not compile res.push(path.clone()) The borrow checker rejects the bug outright31
C#res.Add(path) res.Add(new List<int>(path)) List<T> is a reference type32
TypeScriptres.push(path) res.push([...path]) Arrays are objects33

Two of these deserve comment. C++ is correct by default because push_back takes the vector by value and copies it; the C++ programmer's equivalent mistake is storing a reference or a pointer into the path, or moving from it. Rust is the interesting case: the naive version does not compile, because path is behind a mutable borrow and cannot also be moved into the results vector. The borrow checker turns a silent, data-dependent wrong answer into a compile error, which is close to the ideal outcome for a bug of this shape.

The cost is not nothing. Copying the path at every record point makes the total output work O(answers × average answer length) rather than O(answers), which for subsets is O(n · 2n) rather than O(2n). That is unavoidable if the caller wants a list of independent answers. If the caller only wants to consume each answer — print it, sum it, test it — then passing a callback that reads the live path and returns is strictly better, and eliminates the copy entirely. This is a real optimisation in a contest setting and worth knowing about, and it is also the reason the standard library of no language ships a "give me all the subsets" function: the right interface depends on what you are going to do with them.

Part II
The Three Axes

§5Axis One: Where the Next Choice Comes From

Three shapes of choice set, and the problems each one generates

Every backtracking loop iterates over a choice set. There are exactly three shapes it takes in practice, and the shape determines the size and structure of the tree before any pruning happens.

The start index. for i := start; i < n; i++, recursing with i+1. Each element is considered at most once, and only in a fixed left-to- right order, so a candidate can never contain element 5 followed by element 2. This generates combinations: order does not matter and each set appears once. The fan-out shrinks with depth — n at the root, n−1 below the first child, and so on — and the total node count is 2n. Subsets, combinations, combination sum, partitions of a string, and subset sum are all this shape.

The used set. for i := 0; i < n; i++, skipping used elements. Every unused element is a candidate at every depth, so order does matter and the same set appears in every order. This generates permutations. The fan-out is n − depth, and the total node count is the sum of n!/k! over k, which is approximately e · n!. Permutations, arrangements, Hamiltonian paths, and the assignment problem are this shape.

The position domain. The depth of the recursion fixes a position — a row of the board, a cell of the grid, a segment of the output — and the loop ranges over the values that position may legally take. Nothing is chosen "from" an input array at all. This is the constraint-satisfaction shape, and it is the one where the interesting literature lives, because the choice of which position to fix at each depth is itself a decision with enormous consequences (§20). N-Queens, Sudoku, graph colouring, and grid word search are this shape.

The lab confirms the arithmetic and, more usefully, shows that the interior overhead differs sharply between the families. At n = 10, subset enumeration visits 1,024 nodes and produces 1,024 answers — the tree is the answer set, and there is not a single wasted node. Permutation enumeration at the same n visits 9,864,101 nodes to produce 3,628,800 answers: nearly two thirds of the work exists only to carry state down to a leaf. That ratio is not a defect of the implementation. It is a property of the question, and it is decided by the loop header.

A useful diagnostic follows from this. If your problem's answers are sets and your implementation emits the same set more than once in different orders, you have written a permutation enumerator by accident: the loop starts at 0 when it should start at start. If your problem's answers are sequences and your implementation is missing some, you have written a combination enumerator by accident. The bug and its fix are both in the loop header, and the size of the output tells you which one you have.

§6Axis Two: When You Record

The same tree solves three different problems depending on one line

The second axis is the smallest of the three and the easiest to overlook, because in most implementations it is a single statement. It determines which nodes of the tree are answers.

At every node. Every partial candidate is a complete answer. This is subsets, and it is the only case where the tree and the answer set coincide. The record statement sits at the top of the function, before the loop, and there is no base case at all — the recursion terminates because the loop range empties.

At a fixed depth. Only candidates of length k are answers. This is combinations, permutations, and every fixed-length problem. The record statement sits inside a guard at the top, and the guard returns immediately afterwards, because nothing below a complete candidate can be an answer.

On a predicate. The candidate is an answer when some property of it holds — the remainder has reached zero, the string is exhausted, the board is full. This is combination sum, palindrome partitioning, and Sudoku. The predicate is usually a cheap test on the derived state rather than on the path, which is exactly why the derived state exists.

There is a fourth case worth distinguishing because it changes the shape of the whole function: no record at all. Word search returns a boolean. N-Queens is often asked for a count rather than a list. In these problems the recursion returns a value up the stack instead of appending to a shared list, and the first success short-circuits the remaining siblings. That short-circuit is a real algorithmic difference and not just a coding style: a search for any solution and a search for all solutions have different expected costs on the same tree, and the gap between them can be enormous. The lab's Sudoku experiment finds a solution to the classic puzzle in 52 nodes under a good variable ordering; proving that the solution is unique would require exhausting the tree.

§7Axis Three: What Kills a Branch

Four families of pruning, and the one that costs nothing

Pruning is what separates a backtracking search from a generate-and-test loop, and it is where all the engineering is. Four families cover the classics.

Bound pruning. The partial candidate has already violated a numeric constraint, or provably cannot satisfy one. The remainder has gone negative; the path is too long; the best achievable value is worse than a solution already found. Bound pruning is the mechanism of branch and bound, and it is two-sided far more often than people write it: there is usually a bound saying you have overspent and a second bound saying you can no longer catch up. The lab measures both on subset sum, and they are startlingly unequal — the overshoot bound alone removes 73.6% of the tree, the cannot-catch-up bound alone removes 3.4%, and together they remove 77.1%.

Conflict pruning. The choice violates a hard constraint of the problem — two queens on a diagonal, two nines in a Sudoku row, a grid cell already on the current path. This is the constraint-satisfaction case, and the whole art is making the test O(1) by maintaining derived state, and then making the ordering good so that conflicts are discovered near the root rather than near the leaves.

Sibling pruning. A different branch at the same node has already covered every candidate this branch would produce. This is the duplicate-collapse case, and it is peculiar in that the pruned subtree is not invalid — it is redundant. It gets its own section (§9) because the predicate is subtle and the two ways of getting it wrong have opposite symptoms.

Symmetry pruning. The problem has an automorphism group, and exploring one representative of each orbit suffices. Fixing the first queen to the left half of the board halves the N-Queens tree for four lines of code; fixing vertex 0 to colour 0 in graph colouring removes a factor of m!. Symmetry breaking is free when the canonical-form test is trivial and prohibitive when it is not, and the boundary between the two is where this technique stops being a beginner's trick.

There is a fifth thing people call pruning that is better thought of as a different axis entirely: folding the constraint into the choice set. Instead of offering a candidate and rejecting it, do not offer it. Generate Parentheses is the canonical illustration (§16): the constraint "you may not close more brackets than you have opened" can be a test applied to a generated string, in which case the search walks 4n strings and validates each, or it can be a condition on which of two moves is available, in which case the search walks Catalan(n) nodes and every leaf is valid. Same constraint, same answers, and the difference at n = 12 is a factor of about eighty. When you can express a constraint as a restriction on the choice set rather than as a predicate on the partial candidate, do; the dead branches then cost nothing because they are never created.

The Enumeration Family

§8Subsets, and the Alternative That Is Not Backtracking

The simplest member of the family, and when to write a for-loop instead

Subsets is the reference implementation of the whole technique, and §4 has already given it in six languages. Two things about it are worth drawing out.

The first is that there are two natural formulations and they produce different orderings. The start-index loop given above produces subsets in a lexicographic-by-index order: {}, {1}, {1,2}, {1,2,3}, {1,3}, {2}, {2,3}, {3}. The alternative is a binary recursion — at each depth, either include element i or do not, then recurse on i+1 — which produces the same 2n subsets in a different order and, notably, has 2n+1−1 nodes rather than 2n, because the "exclude" decisions are themselves nodes. Both are correct. The start-index form is preferable when the answers should be ordered by size-then-content and when the loop is going to acquire a bound later (as in combinations); the binary form is preferable when the decision at each element is genuinely binary and the two branches will do different things, which is the case in subset sum.

The second is that for subsets specifically, you should usually not write a recursion at all. The subsets of an n-element array are in bijection with the integers 0 to 2n−1, and the iterative form is three lines with no stack, no restore discipline, and no copy bug:

for mask := 0; mask < (1 << n); mask++ {
    for i := 0; i < n; i++ {
        if mask&(1<<i) != 0 { /* element i is in this subset */ }
    }
}

This is faster, shorter, and — more importantly in a contest — composes with the subset-sum and submask-enumeration tricks that make bitmask dynamic programming work. The recursion earns its place the moment you need to prune, because a bound cannot skip a range of masks the way it can skip a subtree, and it earns its place when the elements are not an array of n things but a stream of choices whose availability depends on earlier ones. Absent those two conditions, the loop wins.

That is a general point and not a remark about subsets. Backtracking is a way of imposing structure on an enumeration so that structure can be exploited. If you are not exploiting it — if no branch is ever pruned and every leaf is reached — then you have paid for a tree and used a list, and an iterative enumeration will be faster and less error-prone every time.

§9Duplicates: One Predicate, Two Ways to Get It Wrong

Why the guard is i > start and not i > 0

Give the subset enumerator a multiset — an array with repeated values — and it produces duplicates. [1,2,2] yields eight answers, of which six are distinct; {1,2} appears twice because there are two 2s and the enumerator distinguishes them by index.

The fix is two lines: sort the array, and at each node skip a candidate whose value equals the previous candidate's value within this loop.

for i := start; i < n; i++ {
    if i > start && a[i] == a[i-1] { continue }
    ...
}

The sort is what makes equal values adjacent, so that "have I already tried this value at this node" reduces to a comparison with the immediate predecessor. The guard is what enforces one branch per distinct value per node. Both are necessary; neither is sufficient.

The interesting part is the i > start, which is the single most commonly mistyped condition in this entire family of problems. It is easy to write i > 0, and i > 0 is catastrophically wrong in a way that is not obvious from reading it. i > start means "this value has already been tried as a sibling at this node". i > 0 means "this value appears earlier in the array", which is true of the second copy of every repeated value regardless of where the current node is in the tree — and so it cuts branches whose parent never had the chance to try the value at all.

The lab makes the magnitudes concrete. On [1,1,2,2,2,3,3], which has 36 distinct sub-multisets: no guard emits 128 answers, 92 of them duplicates. The guard i > start emits exactly 36, all distinct — it is not an approximation, it is exact, and it visits exactly 36 nodes to do it. The guard i > 0 emits 8, missing 28 of the 36 genuine subsets. Both wrong versions "work" on small hand-checked inputs; only one of them is loud about it.

A note on the alternative approach, which is to collect the results in a hash set and deduplicate at the end. It produces the right answer and it is a bad habit, for three reasons. It requires hashing the whole answer, which is O(length) per answer rather than O(1) per node. It does no work to reduce the tree — the search still visits all 128 nodes above, it just discards afterwards. And it needs a canonical form for the answer, which for a subset means sorting it, which means the sort you avoided at the top is now inside the loop. The sibling guard costs one comparison per candidate and prunes the tree at the source.

§10Combinations, and a Bound That Costs Nothing

The count-remaining prune, which is pure profit and almost always omitted

C(n,k) — every k-element subset of 1..n — is the start-index shape with the record point moved to depth k. The naive version differs from subsets in two lines:

if len(path) == k { record(path); return }
for i := start; i <= n; i++ { ... }

and it is correct, and it wastes a great deal of time near the bottom of the tree. At depth d with k−d elements still needed, any starting index above n−(k−d)+1 leads to a subtree that cannot possibly reach depth k: there simply are not enough numbers left. The fix is to move that arithmetic into the loop bound:

need := k - len(path)
for i := start; i <= n-need+1; i++ { ... }

This bound has a property that makes it unusually attractive: it can never remove a leaf. Every node it prunes is provably barren, so the answer set is untouched by construction, and no correctness argument is needed beyond the counting. The lab measures it at n = 10, k = 5: the unpruned search visits 638 nodes, the pruned one 462, a 27.6% reduction with the same 252 answers. The saving grows as k approaches n, where the unpruned version spends most of its time in subtrees that are one element short.

Two variants are worth having in your fingers. If the problem asks for combinations of an array rather than of 1..n, the bound becomes i <= len(a)-need and everything else is identical. If the problem asks for combinations of a multiset, add the sibling guard from §9; the two prunes compose without interacting, because one constrains the top of the loop range and the other skips individual values within it.

§11Permutations: Two Formulations With Different Costs

The used-set, the swap-in-place, and why the difference matters for duplicates

Permutations are the used-set shape. The standard formulation carries a boolean array, marks an element used on the way down, and clears it on the way up. It produces permutations in lexicographic order if the input is sorted, which is often what the problem wants, and it generalises cleanly to the multiset case.

The alternative is swap-in-place: at depth d, swap each element of a[d..n) into position d, recurse, and swap back. It carries no auxiliary array, does no allocation, and touches the array O(1) times per node instead of O(1) times per candidate. It is meaningfully faster in tight code. It has two costs. The output is not in lexicographic order — it is in an order determined by the swap sequence, which is rarely what a problem statement asks for. And it does not admit the multiset deduplication guard, because after a swap the array is no longer sorted and adjacency no longer implies equality of value; the swap version needs a per-level hash set of values already placed, which gives back the allocation you saved.

Two further alternatives are worth knowing about and are not backtracking at all. Heap's algorithm generates all n! permutations with a single swap between consecutive outputs, which is the minimum possible34; the Steinhaus–Johnson–Trotter algorithm does the same with adjacent transpositions3536; Ehrlich's loopless variants generate each successor in O(1) worst case37; the broader family of minimal-change enumerations is surveyed under the heading of combinatorial Gray codes38. Sedgewick's 1977 survey compares the whole family and is still the reference39. And in C++, std::next_permutation gives you lexicographic successor in amortised O(1), which turns "enumerate all permutations" into a do { ... } while (next_permutation(...)) loop with no recursion at all40. If you want all permutations with no pruning, use one of these. Backtracking earns its place when you want to abandon a prefix — which is to say, when the problem is not really "enumerate permutations" but "search over orderings".

That distinction matters more than it sounds. Travelling-salesman-by-brute-force, Hamiltonian path, and the assignment problem are all "search over orderings", and all of them prune. Ordinary "print all permutations" does not, and should not be recursive.

§12Permutations of a Multiset

Two correct guards that keep different copies, and one that keeps none

The multiset case for permutations has the same shape as the multiset case for subsets and a different predicate, because the choice set is different. Sort the input, then:

if used[i] { continue }
if i > 0 && a[i] == a[i-1] && !used[i-1] { continue }

Note that here the guard is i > 0, which is exactly the condition that was wrong for subsets. The reason is that the choice set is the whole array at every node, not a suffix of it, so "already tried at this node" and "appears earlier in the array" now coincide — with one correction, which is the !used[i-1] clause.

That clause is the part worth understanding. Among a run of identical values, we want to fix a canonical order in which they get placed, so that the search never places the second copy before the first. !used[i-1] says: if my identical left neighbour is not currently placed, then placing me now would put me before it, and some other branch will produce that same permutation with the copies in the other order — so skip. The effect is to force identical values to be consumed left to right.

Reverse it to used[i-1] and you get a canonical order too — the opposite one — and the algorithm is still correct. It emits each distinct permutation exactly once. But it is slower, because it nominates as canonical the branch that is discovered later, so the search descends further before the cut fires. The lab measures both on [1,1,2,2,3]: the unpruned tree is 326 nodes, !used[i-1] visits 90, used[i-1] visits 150. Identical output, 1.67× the work.

This is a small result and a generalisable one. When a symmetry-breaking rule has a free choice of representative, the cheap representative is the one the search reaches first.

§13Combination Sum: Reuse, Exhaustion, and What break Actually Buys

A one-character difference in the recursive call, and a widely repeated optimisation that does not do what people think

Combination sum is the start-index shape with a predicate record point and a bound. There are three members of the family and they differ only in the recursive call:

Unbounded reuse — each candidate may be used any number of times — recurses with i, so the same index is offered again in the child. Each element once recurses with i+1. Take-or-leave, used for subset sum and k-sum, makes both branches explicit rather than looping. In all three the record point is remain == 0 and the bound is c[i] > remain.

Now the part that is usually taught wrong. The standard advice is: sort the candidates, and replace continue with break when a candidate overshoots, because with a sorted array the first overshoot proves that every later candidate also overshoots. This is true, and the reason given for it — "it prunes the search" — is false.

continue and break produce exactly the same set of children. Both skip the overshooting candidate; neither recurses into it. The tree is identical. What break removes is the remainder of the loop scan at that node — a constant-factor saving on the loop, not a smaller search. The lab measures it: on nine candidates with target 40, all three variants visit 8,796 nodes and find 1,092 solutions. The candidate-test counts are 18,116 (unsorted, continue), 31,098 (sorted, continue), and 15,860 (sorted, break).

Two things in those numbers are worth pausing on. First, sorting on its own makes things worse — 31,098 tests against 18,116 — because putting small values first means more nodes have low start indices and therefore longer loops. Sorting is not an optimisation; it is a precondition that makes break sound, and break is what pays. Second, the actual tree reduction happened earlier and invisibly, when someone wrote if c[i] > remain at all rather than recursing and testing remain < 0 in the child. Testing at the parent instead of the child is the prune; it is worth an entire level of the tree, and nobody ever mentions it because it looks like ordinary code.

The general lesson generalises past this problem. When you evaluate a pruning technique, count nodes, not lines saved. A change that removes work within a node is a constant factor; a change that removes a child is a factor in the exponent. Both are worth having and they are not the same kind of thing, and conflating them is how folklore optimisations acquire reputations they have not earned.

§14Partitioning: When the Choice Is Where to Cut

Palindrome partitioning, word break, and restore-IP-addresses are one problem

A family of problems asks you to split a sequence into contiguous pieces, each of which satisfies some property. Palindrome partitioning wants palindromic pieces; word break wants dictionary words; restore-IP-addresses wants four pieces each in 0..255 with no leading zeros; expression-add-operators wants pieces that are numbers with operators between them.

These are all the start-index shape. The insight is that the "element" chosen at each node is not a value from an array — it is a prefix of the remaining suffix. The choice set is the set of legal prefixes; the record point is "the cut position reached the end"; the kill rule is the legality test on the prefix.

The engineering point specific to this family is that the legality test sits inside the innermost loop and is therefore worth precomputing. A naive palindrome partitioner tests isPalindrome(s[start:end]) for every candidate cut, which is O(n) per test and O(n2) tests per path. The two-dimensional table pal[i][j], filled bottom-up in O(n2) total from the recurrence pal[i][j] = s[i]==s[j] && (j-i < 2 || pal[i+1][j-1]), makes the test a single array read. This is not a micro-optimisation; it changes the asymptotic cost of the whole search by a factor of n, and it is the standard reason a correct palindrome partitioner times out.

Word break is the same shape and is the standard illustration of a different point entirely, which is the boundary between backtracking and dynamic programming. If the question is "can this string be segmented", the answer at position i does not depend on how you reached position i, and one memo array collapses the search to linear. If the question is "list every segmentation", no memo helps, because the output itself is exponential. That distinction gets §22 to itself; it is the single most useful thing to internalise about when to stop writing backtracking.

§15Folding the Constraint Into the Choice Set

Generate Parentheses, and the cheapest pruning there is

Generate Parentheses asks for every balanced string of n bracket pairs. There is an obvious solution: enumerate all 22n strings over {(, )} and keep the balanced ones. It is correct, and at n = 12 it walks sixteen million strings to find 208,012.

The better solution does not generate a candidate list at all. It carries two counters and offers at most two moves:

if open < n     { buf.push('('); walk(open+1, closed); buf.pop() }
if closed < open { buf.push(')'); walk(open, closed+1); buf.pop() }

Every leaf reached is a valid balanced string. Not one dead branch is ever created, because the constraint is not a predicate applied to a partial candidate — it is a condition on which choices exist. The number of leaves is the Catalan number, and the number of nodes is proportional to it.

This is the third axis at its cheapest, and it generalises further than the parentheses problem. Ask, of any constraint in your problem: can this be expressed as a restriction on which candidates the loop offers, rather than as a test applied after one is chosen? The count-remaining bound on combinations (§10) is the same move — the constraint "the path must reach length k" is folded into the loop's upper bound. The i > start sibling guard is the same move. The N-Queens avail = full & ~(cols|diag|anti) mask is the same move at its logical extreme: the choice set is computed as a set of legal values, and the loop iterates over exactly the survivors.

The difference between "offer and reject" and "do not offer" is invisible in the answer set and can be several orders of magnitude in the tree. It is worth checking for deliberately, every time.

§16Word Search: Marking a Path, Not a Graph

The distinction between visited-forever and visited-on-this-path, and the board that destroys you

Word search is the position-domain shape on a grid. From each cell, the choice set is the four neighbours; the depth is the index into the word; the kill rule is bounds, letter mismatch, and "this cell is already on the current path".

That third condition is where the problem earns its place in this article, because it is the one place in the classic canon where backtracking differs visibly from ordinary depth-first search, and getting the difference wrong produces a plausible-looking program that returns wrong answers.

Ordinary graph DFS marks a node visited and never unmarks it, because it is answering a question about reachability: if a node has been seen, there is no information to be gained by seeing it again. Word search is answering a question about paths: whether a cell can be part of a solution depends entirely on which other cells are already in use. A cell rejected on one path must be available on the next. So the mark goes in on the way down and comes out on the way up, and the natural place to put it is the board itself — overwrite the letter with a sentinel, recurse, write the letter back.

A global visited array that is allocated once and never cleared will find the word if the first starting cell happens to work and will report failure for boards where it does not. It passes the sample tests. This is the single most common wrong answer in grid backtracking.

The second thing worth knowing about word search is that its worst case is not adversarial input in the usual sense — it is uninformative input. On a board where every cell holds the same letter, every one of the four moves matches at every step, so the branching factor is three (the search cannot step back onto its parent) all the way to the length of the word. The lab measures a 6×6 board of a single repeated letter against a thirteen-letter word that is not present: 2,502,164 DFS calls, for a board of thirty-six cells.

Two prechecks defuse this, both O(cells) and both trivial to write. First, count the letters on the board and compare against the letters in the word; if the board does not contain enough of some letter, the answer is no and the search never starts. On both uniform boards above this reduces the cost from millions of calls to zero. Second, if the board contains more of the word's first letter than of its last, search for the reversed word — the search starts from fewer cells and prunes earlier. The lab's Q-field case goes from 183 calls to 23. Neither of these changes the algorithm; both change which searches you have to run at all, and cheap tests that fire rarely are worth writing when the tail is this heavy.

§17Word Search II, and Searching for Many Things at Once

Driving the walk from a trie instead of from a word

Given a board and a list of words, find which words appear. The naive approach runs the single-word search once per word, which repeats the same board traversal for every word sharing a prefix. The standard improvement builds a trie of the word list4142 and runs one traversal in which the trie node advances alongside the board position: the choice set at each cell becomes the four neighbours intersected with the children of the current trie node, and a word is recorded when a node carrying an end-of-word mark is reached.

This is the same restructuring that made Appel and Jacobson's Scrabble move generator fast in 1988: drive the search from the dictionary structure rather than from the board, so that a prefix shared by many words is walked once43. The gain is real on natural-language dictionaries with heavy prefix sharing.

Two cautions, because this is one of those techniques that is repeated as universally good and is not. First, the win depends entirely on prefix sharing; on a word list with none, the trie adds indirection and memory and buys nothing. Second, and more seriously, the trie-driven search is worse than the naive one on a uniform board unless you also prune the trie — deleting matched words and collapsing exhausted subtrees — because the board's own branching is what dominates and the trie no longer constrains it. The right instinct is to reach for the trie when the word list is large and the board is ordinary, and to reach for the letter-count prechecks of §16 when the board is degenerate. They defend against different failure modes.

§18N-Queens

Three conflict sets, a bitmask, and a tree that is 98% dead

The eight-queens puzzle appeared in a German chess magazine in 1848; the generalisation to n×n boards followed by 1869, and it has been the standard demonstration of backtracking ever since2. It deserves the status. It is small enough to trace by hand, it has a natural formulation in which the position is fixed by depth, and its conflict test is the cleanest illustration in the canon of turning an O(depth) check into an O(1) one.

The formulation that matters is: one queen per row. This is not a constraint imposed on the search, it is an observation about the problem — any solution has exactly one queen in each row — and building it into the shape of the recursion removes a factor of C(n2,n)/nn from the search space before a single line of pruning is written. The recursion's depth is the row index; the choice set is the columns; the kill rule is the three conflict sets.

The index arithmetic is worth stating plainly because it is the reusable part. r − c is constant along a down-right diagonal and r + c is constant along a down-left one. The first ranges over −(n−1)..(n−1), so shift it by n−1 to get an array index; the second already ranges over 0..2n−2. Three boolean arrays of sizes n, 2n−1, 2n−1 then answer "is this square attacked" in three reads.

The lab confirms what this does and — more interestingly — what it does not. The naive O(r) rescan of already-placed queens and the three-array version visit exactly the same nodes at every n. The representation is not pruning; it is bookkeeping. What it buys is a constant, and the constant is not constant: the rescan's test count runs from 1.40× the array version at n = 4 to 4.11× at n = 11, and keeps growing, because the rescan is O(r) at row r.

The bitmask form is the same algorithm with the three arrays collapsed into three integers, and it is the most elegant thing in the classical repertoire. A diagonal, viewed one row later, is the same set of columns shifted by one — so the recursion can pass cols|p, ((diag|p) << 1) & full, and (anti|p) >> 1 by value, and the choice set is computed in one operation as avail = full & ~(cols | diag | anti). Iterating the set bits with p = avail & -avail; avail -= p visits exactly the legal columns in increasing order5.

That last property deserves emphasis, because it closes the loop with §3. The bitmask solver has no restore line. Not because someone optimised it away, but because the state it shares is nothing: each child receives its own copy of three integers, and the parent's copies are sitting untouched in the parent's stack frame. The un-choose step is the price of sharing mutable state, and if the state is small enough to copy, the price is not paid.

The number that should stay with you from that figure is 98.3%. At n = 12 the search spends more than ninety-eight per cent of its time in branches that produce nothing. That is not a sign of a bad implementation. A backtracking search is a machine for discovering which branches are dead, and the dead branches are necessarily where it spends its time. The only question worth asking about an implementation is whether it discovers them near the root or near the leaves.

Symmetry breaking is the cheapest available improvement and the lab measures it: the board has a reflection symmetry, so restricting the first row's queen to the left half and doubling the count (plus the centre column when n is odd) removes very close to half the tree for four lines of code. The full dihedral group of order 8 would buy more, and the canonical-form test stops being free, which is the general boundary for this technique.

On the mathematics, briefly, because it bears on what is and is not computable by search. The exact counts Q(n) are known only up to n = 27, computed on an FPGA cluster in 20164445; the asymptotics resisted for over a century. Rivin, Vardi and Zimmermann conjectured the growth rate in 199446; Luria proved an upper bound47; Bowtell and Keevash, and independently Luria and Simkin, established the matching lower bound in 20214849; and Simkin sharpened the constant, showing Q(n) = ((1 ± o(1))n e−α)n with α = 1.942 ± 0.003, which is approximately (0.143n)n50; subsequent work tightened the numerical bounds on α by convex optimisation51. The related completion problem — extend a given partial placement — is NP-complete5253, which is a useful reminder that the puzzle's tractability for a solver is a property of the specific question asked, not of the board. Finding one solution, by contrast, admits a polynomial construction and does not require search at all54.

§19Sudoku: Choosing Which Variable to Assign

The one decision that matters more than every other optimisation combined

Sudoku is a constraint satisfaction problem with 81 variables, domains of size 9, and 27 all-different constraints55. Deciding whether a partially filled n2×n2 grid has a solution is NP-complete56. The 9×9 case is small enough that a naive solver works and large enough that a naive solver can be made to look foolish.

The naive solver fills the first empty cell in reading order, tries each legal digit, and recurses. It is correct. On a newspaper puzzle it finishes instantly. The lab runs it on four puzzles of increasing difficulty and the result is the most dramatic single finding in this article:

On the classic 30-clue puzzle, reading order takes 4,209 nodes and minimum-remaining-values takes 52 — a factor of 81. On a 17-clue puzzle, which is the proven minimum number of clues for a uniquely solvable grid57, reading order blows a three-million-node cap and MRV finishes in 6,037. On the puzzle Nicolas Juillerat constructed specifically to defeat left-to-right solvers, reading order blows the cap again and MRV finishes in 58,234 nodes and a third of a second.

The heuristic itself is one sentence: fill the cell with the fewest legal candidates first. It goes back to Haralick and Elliott, who called it the minimum-remaining-values or most-constrained-variable ordering and showed it dominant across a range of CSPs15; it is in every textbook treatment of constraint search1858; Norvig's 1,200-word Python solver is built on it and on constraint propagation59.

The reason it wins is worth stating precisely, because it is counter-intuitive on its face. MRV does strictly more work per node — it scans every open cell and counts candidates before choosing one, where reading order takes the first. It wins anyway because it changes the branching factor near the root, and the size of a tree is the product of its branching factors. A cell with one candidate is not a choice at all; taking it multiplies the subtree by one instead of by six. Deferring the six-way branch until after the forced moves have propagated means many of those six options have been eliminated by the time you get there — or that the branch is never reached, because a contradiction surfaced first.

Two related ideas complete the picture and are worth having in the toolkit. The first is forward checking: after assigning a value, remove it from the domains of the constrained neighbours, and fail immediately if any domain empties15. The candidate masks in the implementation below do this implicitly — a cell whose mask is zero fails the moment MRV looks at it — which is why the two techniques are usually seen together. The second is arc consistency, Mackworth's AC-3, which propagates domain reductions transitively until a fixed point14; for Sudoku this subsumes the "naked single" and "hidden single" techniques that human solvers use, and a solver that propagates to a fixed point before branching solves most published puzzles with no search at all.

Two facts about the problem space, for calibration. There are 6,670,903,752,021,072,936,960 valid completed grids60, reducing to 5,472,730,538 under the symmetry group of the puzzle61. And no 16-clue puzzle with a unique solution exists — established in 2012 by an exhaustive computation over all grids, itself an enormous backtracking search made feasible by a novel hitting-set enumeration57. That result is a good illustration of the technique's ceiling: the search was affordable only because the authors reformulated the problem so that its tree was smaller, not because they made their solver faster.

§20The Classical Enhancements

Look-ahead, look-back, and the road from a contest template to a SAT solver

Everything in the previous section belongs to a body of technique developed for constraint satisfaction between roughly 1965 and 1995 and then absorbed into SAT solving. It divides cleanly into two directions, and knowing the names is worth more than knowing the implementations, because the names tell you what to look for when a search is too slow.

Look-ahead improves the search by doing work before descending. Variable ordering — which position to fix next — is the highest-value decision available, and minimum-remaining-values with a degree tiebreak is the standard answer15. Value ordering — which candidate to try first — matters when you want one solution rather than all, and the least-constraining-value rule (prefer the value that removes fewest options from neighbours) is the standard heuristic58. Forward checking and arc consistency propagate the consequences of an assignment before recursing1514. Freuder's work characterises when enough propagation makes search backtrack-free entirely62. Dynamic reordering — recomputing the variable order at every node rather than fixing it in advance — is Purdom, Brown and Robertson's contribution, and Brélaz's DSATUR is the same idea specialised to graph colouring, where the next vertex chosen is the one with the most distinct colours already among its neighbours63; dynamic reordering and is what the Sudoku solver below actually does64.

Look-back improves the search by using information from failures. Backjumping notices that when a subtree fails, the culprit assignment may be several levels above the immediate parent, and jumps there rather than unwinding one level at a time16. Conflict-directed backjumping tracks which earlier assignments contributed to each failure17. Nogood learning records the failed combination so that it is never re-derived elsewhere in the tree65. Kondrak and van Beek's theoretical comparison sorts the family into a partial order of dominance66, and Bitner and Reingold had already described the essential idea of preclusion two decades earlier5.

Follow the look-back direction far enough and you arrive at conflict-driven clause learning, which is what every industrial SAT solver does: search, and on each conflict, derive a new clause that explains the failure, add it permanently, and backjump to the level where it becomes unit2223. The result decides instances with millions of variables, and it is still, structurally, the 1962 DPLL backtracking loop2167. Follow the look-ahead direction and you arrive at constraint programming systems and at Knuth's Dancing Links, which is not a heuristic at all but a data structure — a doubly linked representation of an exact cover matrix in which removing and restoring a row or column is O(1) and perfectly reversible68. Algorithm X with dancing links solves N-Queens, Sudoku, pentomino packing, and every other exact cover problem with one solver, and it is the best demonstration in print that the un-choose step can be made free rather than merely cheap.

For a competitive programmer, the practical ranking is: variable ordering first, because it is usually the difference between finishing and not; then folding constraints into the choice set; then symmetry breaking; then everything else. Backjumping and learning are rarely worth implementing by hand under contest conditions — but knowing they exist tells you that when your search is unwinding one level at a time through thousands of doomed siblings, there is a named technique for that.

§21Deciding, Counting, Enumerating

Where memoisation applies, where it cannot, and how to tell before you write the code

The most valuable thing to know about backtracking is when to stop writing it. The boundary is sharp, it is decidable by inspection, and it is not about the recursion — it is about the question.

Take one recursion: segment a string into dictionary words. Three questions can be asked of the identical tree.

Decide — is there any segmentation? Count — how many are there? Enumerate — list them all. The lab runs all three. Deciding an unsatisfiable 23-character input takes 3,147,216 calls without a memo and 101 with one. Counting is the same: the number of segmentations of a suffix is a function of that suffix alone, so one integer per position suffices. Enumerating is not: at |s| = 24 there are 5,976,577 segmentations and no memo can make listing them cheaper than listing them.

The test, in one sentence: does the answer at this node depend on how the walk reached this node?

If it does not — if the state that determines the sub-answer is summarisable into a key, and the sub-answer is summarisable into a value — then memoise, and you are writing dynamic programming. The path becomes irrelevant, the un-choose step becomes unnecessary because there is no shared mutable state, and the complexity collapses from exponential to (states × transitions).

If it does, one of two things is true. Either the state genuinely includes the path — as in Hamiltonian path, where which vertices you have used matters, in which case the right move is usually to encode the path as a bitmask and memoise on it anyway, giving the O(2n·n2) Held–Karp algorithm6970 — or the output itself is the path, in which case there is nothing to compress and backtracking is not a slow algorithm you should improve. It is the only shape the answer has.

That last case has a formal theory worth knowing about. Enumeration algorithms are analysed in terms of the delay between consecutive outputs rather than total runtime, and an algorithm with polynomial delay is considered efficient even though its total runtime is exponential, because its output is71. A well-written backtracking enumerator has polynomial delay by construction: the work between two consecutive leaves is bounded by the depth times the branching factor. Read and Tarjan established these bounds for listing cycles, paths and spanning trees in 197572; Avis and Fukuda's reverse search generalises the construction73. If you are enumerating, "exponential" is not an indictment — it is the size of the answer, and the thing to measure is the overhead per answer.

The corollary for contest work is a decision procedure. Read the problem. Ask whether the output is a count, a boolean, an optimum, or a list. If it is a list, backtracking, and the input bound will be tiny. If it is anything else, ask whether the recursion's return value depends on the path; if not, you have a DP and the input bound will be large. If it does depend on the path but the path is a subset, you have bitmask DP and the bound will be around 20. The input bound in the problem statement is telling you which of these three you have been given permission to write.

§22Estimating the Tree Before You Walk It

Knuth's 1975 random probe, and the only question worth asking before you press run

The question a backtracking search cannot answer about itself is how long it will take. Its cost is the size of a tree defined implicitly by the pruning, and there is no formula for that size in terms of the input. This is the practical reason backtracking is frightening: you write it, you run it, and you do not know whether to wait.

Knuth solved this in 1975, and the solution is three lines long13. Take a single random walk from the root. At each node, count the children; multiply a running weight by that count; add the weight to a running estimate; pick a child uniformly at random and descend. Stop at a leaf. The resulting number is an unbiased estimator of the total number of nodes in the tree.

estimate, weight := 1.0, 1.0
for state has children {
    d := len(children(state))
    weight *= float64(d)
    estimate += weight
    state = children(state)[rand(d)]
}
return estimate

The intuition is that a node at depth k reached by branching factors d1..dk is one of Π di nodes that a random walk was equally likely to have reached, so counting it Π di times gives an unbiased count of that level. Average over many walks and the variance falls.

The lab measures it against ground truth on N-Queens for n = 8 to 14. With 100 probes the estimates land within about 10%; with 10,000 probes they land within about 1%, and the whole 10,000-probe run costs 200 milliseconds at n = 14 against a true tree of 27 million nodes. At n = 20 — where the true tree is far beyond what the interpreter can walk — 20,000 probes take 0.75 seconds and report 3.8 × 1012 nodes, which at the interpreter's observed rate is roughly 879 hours of enumeration. That is the answer you wanted before you started, and it cost less than a second.

The estimator has a well-known weakness and the lab measures that too. It is unbiased but heavy-tailed: a typical random descent misses the rare dense subtrees that hold most of the mass, so it under-reports more often than it over-reports. Over eight independent batches of 200 probes at n = 12, six were low, and the spread ran from −6% to +10%. Treat a small-sample estimate as a lower bound with a long right tail, not as a measurement. The subsequent literature addresses this directly: Purdom's partial backtracking uses a stratified sample rather than a single path74; Chen's heuristic sampling stratifies by node type75; Kilby, Slaney, Thiébaux and Walsh give online estimators that improve as the real search proceeds76; Lelis, Otten and Dechter extend the family to branch and bound77.

For contest purposes there is a cruder version of the same idea that costs nothing: instrument the real search with a node counter, run it on the largest legal input, and print the count every million nodes. If the count is still climbing after a second, the approach is wrong and no constant-factor tuning will save it. The estimator is the principled version of the same instinct, and the instinct is the important part — measure the tree, not the code.

§23What It Costs

Stack, allocation, and the growth curves you should know by heart

Three practical costs, in decreasing order of how often they bite.

The tree. This is the only one that matters asymptotically, and the numbers are worth memorising because problem setters choose input bounds to signal which of them you are being invited to walk.

Against a budget of about 108 nodes: permutations run out at n = 11, subsets at n = 26, and the middle binomial at n = 29. In practice the useful heuristics are n ≤ 8 means "you may enumerate orderings", n ≤ 20 means "you may enumerate subsets", n ≤ 25 means "you may enumerate subsets if you prune", and anything larger is a refusal — go and find the dynamic programming, the greedy argument, or the polynomial algorithm.

Allocation. The copy at the record point is unavoidable if the caller wants independent answers, and it makes output work O(answers × length). What is avoidable is allocation per node: a fresh path array per recursive call, a fresh candidate list per node, a substring per legality test. These do not change the asymptotics and they routinely cost a factor of five to twenty in wall clock, which is exactly the range that separates a passing submission from a timing out one. The rule is that the search should allocate at record points and nowhere else.

Parallelism is worth a note because it is the one dimension where backtracking is unusually friendly. The subtrees below distinct siblings are independent — they share no mutable state once the state is copied at the split — so a search can be cut into tasks at any node, and work stealing handles the fact that the subtrees are wildly unequal in size78. The literature contains the delightful result that parallel backtracking can exhibit superlinear speedup when the search is for a single solution rather than all of them, because a parallel search may find a solution in a branch a sequential search would have visited last79. This is not available to enumeration, where the whole tree must be walked regardless.

Stack depth. Backtracking recursion is depth-bounded by the length of a candidate, which for these problems is small — n for permutations, 81 for Sudoku, the word length for grid search. It is essentially never a problem. The exceptions are worth knowing: deep string partitioning on a long input, path enumeration on a large graph, and any recursion over 105 elements. Default thread stacks are around 1 MB on the JVM (adjustable with -Xss)80, 8 MB on typical Linux for the main thread, and JavaScript engines will throw a range error at roughly 104 frames33; Python defaults to a 1,000-frame limit that must be raised explicitly81. No mainstream implementation of any of the six languages here guarantees tail-call elimination, and backtracking recursion is not tail-recursive anyway, since work follows the recursive call. If depth is genuinely a problem, convert to an explicit stack of frames — the transformation is mechanical, and it makes the state you were carrying implicitly into state you carry explicitly, which is occasionally clarifying in its own right.

§25When Not to Use Backtracking

The cases where reaching for it is the mistake

Backtracking is a general method, and general methods are dangerous because they always apply. Six cases where it applies and is wrong.

When the answer is a count, an optimum, or a boolean, and the state is summarisable. This is §21 and it is by far the most common error. If the sub-answer at a position does not depend on the path taken to it, you are writing dynamic programming with the memo deleted. The symptom is an exponential runtime on an input bound that is clearly meant to be polynomial. The lab's word-break experiment shows a factor of 31,161 at |s| = 23, and it grows.

When the path matters but is a subset of a small universe. Hamiltonian path, travelling salesman, assignment, and set cover over n ≤ 20 all "need" the path — and the path is a subset, so encode it as a bitmask and memoise on (mask, last). Held–Karp turns O(n!) into O(2nn2)69, which at n = 18 is the difference between six thousand years and a second. There is a whole discipline of doing this systematically — designing exponential algorithms with the best achievable base rather than settling for the naive one — and it is worth knowing that it exists before you write O(n!) by reflex9293.

When there is a polynomial algorithm. Bipartite matching, shortest path, maximum flow, and topological ordering all have plausible-looking backtracking formulations, and all of them have polynomial algorithms that a backtracking solution will lose to by an unbounded margin. Any problem whose constraints form a matroid or a network is in this category. Finding one N-Queens solution is a small instance of the same lesson: there is a direct construction and no search is needed54.

When the enumeration has no pruning at all. §8's argument: if no branch is ever cut, you have paid for a tree and used a list. Iterate over bitmasks, or use next_permutation, or use a nested loop. It will be faster, shorter, and it cannot have the restore bug.

When meet-in-the-middle applies. Subset problems at n = 40 are out of reach for 2n enumeration and comfortable for 2n/2 — split the set, enumerate both halves, sort one, and binary search. Horowitz and Sahni's 1974 construction is the canonical form and it is worth recognising, because n = 40 in a subset problem is a deliberate signal94.

When an off-the-shelf solver is available and the instance is hard. If you are outside a contest and the problem is genuinely a constraint satisfaction or satisfiability instance of real size, a hand-written backtracker will lose to a modern SAT or CP solver by orders of magnitude, and the gap is entirely made of the look-back machinery in §202519. Encoding your problem and calling MiniSat is not a failure of nerve24.

What is left after those exclusions is a genuinely large and important class: problems that ask for every witness, problems with constraints too irregular to admit structure, and problems small enough that the exponent does not matter. That is where backtracking is not merely acceptable but optimal, and it is where every problem in this article lives.

§26The Question in the Loop Header

Return to the two functions at the top. They differ in a loop header, and the difference between them is the difference between four thousand answers and four hundred and seventy-nine million. Nothing else in either function carries any information about the problem being solved. The recursion is scaffolding; the record statement is one line; the un-choose is bookkeeping that follows mechanically from what the choose did.

The eighteen plates in the ledger below describe eighteen problems, and they share a single implementation. What varies between them fits in four fields: where the candidates come from, when a candidate is also an answer, what makes a branch dead, and what it looks like when you get it wrong. That is the entire content of the technique, and it is why learning eighteen templates is the wrong way to learn it — the templates are the same template, and the memorisation obscures the one place where the thinking has to happen.

Three habits follow, and they are worth more than any individual template. Before you write the loop, say out loud what the choice set is; if you cannot, you do not yet understand the problem well enough to code it. Before you optimise, count nodes, because a change that saves work inside a node is a constant and a change that removes a child is an exponent, and they are not comparable. And before you press run on anything with an exponential shape, spend a second estimating the tree — Knuth gave us the tool in 1975 and it answers the only question that matters, which is not whether the code is correct but whether you are going to be here tomorrow.

  is a function of that suffix alone, so one integer per position suffices.

  ENUMERATE cannot be compressed by any memo, because the output is
  exponential in the input; there is nothing to cache that is smaller than
  what you must emit. This is the boundary. If your recursion returns a
  summarisable value, you are writing dynamic programming and should stop
  backtracking. If it must emit every witness, backtracking is not a slow
  algorithm to be improved -- it is the only shape the answer has.

  |s|=24: deciding takes 25 memoised calls. Counting takes 25.
  Listing takes 5,976,577 outputs. No implementation trick closes that gap.

def count_subsets(n):
    """start-index choice set; record at EVERY node."""
    nodes = emitted = 0

    def walk(start, depth):
        nonlocal nodes, emitted
        nodes += 1
        emitted += 1
        for i in range(start, n):
            walk(i + 1, depth + 1)

    walk(0, 0)
    return nodes, emitted


def count_combinations(n, k, prune):
    """start-index choice set; record at LEAVES of depth k."""
    nodes = emitted = 0

    def walk(start, depth):
        nonlocal nodes, emitted
        nodes += 1
        if depth == k:
            emitted += 1
            return
        need = k - depth
        last = (n - need) if prune else (n - 1)
        for i in range(start, last + 1):
            walk(i + 1, depth + 1)

    walk(0, 0)
    return nodes, emitted


def count_permutations(n):
    """used-set choice set; record at LEAVES of depth n."""
    nodes = emitted = 0
    used = [False] * n

    def walk(depth):
        nonlocal nodes, emitted
        nodes += 1
        if depth == n:
            emitted += 1
            return
        for i in range(n):
            if used[i]:
                continue
            used[i] = True
            walk(depth + 1)
            used[i] = False

    walk(0)
    return nodes, emitted

def subsets_multiset(nums, mode):
    """mode: 'none' (no skip), 'sibling' (i > start), 'global' (i > 0, WRONG)."""
    nums = sorted(nums)
    n = len(nums)
    out = []
    nodes = 0
    path = []

    def walk(start):
        nonlocal nodes
        nodes += 1
        out.append(tuple(path))
        for i in range(start, n):
            if mode == "sibling" and i > start and nums[i] == nums[i - 1]:
                continue
            if mode == "global" and i > 0 and nums[i] == nums[i - 1]:
                continue
            path.append(nums[i])
            walk(i + 1)
            path.pop()

    walk(0)
    return out, nodes


def permutations_multiset(nums, mode):
    """mode: 'none', 'correct' (!used[i-1]), 'inverted' (used[i-1], WRONG)."""
    nums = sorted(nums)
    n = len(nums)
    used = [False] * n
    out = []
    nodes = 0
    path = []

    def walk():
        nonlocal nodes
        nodes += 1
        if len(path) == n:
            out.append(tuple(path))
            return
        for i in range(n):
            if used[i]:
                continue
            if mode == "correct" and i > 0 and nums[i] == nums[i - 1] and not used[i - 1]:
                continue
            if mode == "inverted" and i > 0 and nums[i] == nums[i - 1] and used[i - 1]:
                continue
            used[i] = True
            path.append(nums[i])
            walk()
            path.pop()
            used[i] = False

    walk()
    return out, nodes

# Bound pruning on combination sum.

def subset_sum_nodes(vals, target, prune_over, prune_under):
    """Each value used at most once. Two bounds: overshoot, and can-never-reach."""
    n = len(vals)
    suffix = [0] * (n + 1)
    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        suffix[i] = suffix[i + 1] + vals[i]
    nodes = 0
    sols = 0

    def walk(i, remain):
        nonlocal nodes, sols
        nodes += 1
        if remain == 0:
            sols += 1
            return
        if i == n:
            return
        if prune_over and remain < 0:
            return
        if prune_under and remain > suffix[i]:
            return
        walk(i + 1, remain - vals[i])   # take vals[i]
        walk(i + 1, remain)             # skip vals[i]

    walk(0, target)
    return nodes, sols


def combination_sum_tests(cands, target, sort_it, brk):
    """Unbounded reuse. Counts nodes AND candidate tests (loop-body entries)."""
    c = sorted(cands) if sort_it else list(cands)
    nodes = 0
    tests = 0
    sols = 0

    def walk(start, remain):
        nonlocal nodes, tests, sols
        nodes += 1
        if remain == 0:
            sols += 1
            return
        for i in range(start, len(c)):
            tests += 1
            if c[i] > remain:
                if brk:
                    break
                continue
            walk(i, remain - c[i])

    walk(0, target)
    return nodes, tests, sols

# N-Queens: four conflict representations, and how dead the tree is.

def nqueens_naive(n):
    """O(r) rescan of placed queens at each candidate square."""
    nodes = 0
    tests = 0
    sols = 0
    q = []

    def walk(r):
        nonlocal nodes, tests, sols
        nodes += 1
        if r == n:
            sols += 1
            return
        for c in range(n):
            ok = True
            for pr in range(r):
                tests += 1
                pc = q[pr]
                if pc == c or pr - pc == r - c or pr + pc == r + c:
                    ok = False
                    break
            if not ok:
                continue
            q.append(c)
            walk(r + 1)
            q.pop()

    walk(0)
    return nodes, tests, sols


def nqueens_sets(n):
    """O(1) conflict test with three boolean arrays."""
    nodes = 0
    tests = 0
    sols = 0
    col = [False] * n
    diag = [False] * (2 * n - 1)
    anti = [False] * (2 * n - 1)

    def walk(r):
        nonlocal nodes, tests, sols
        nodes += 1
        if r == n:
            sols += 1
            return
        for c in range(n):
            tests += 1
            d = r - c + n - 1
            a = r + c
            if col[c] or diag[d] or anti[a]:
                continue
            col[c] = diag[d] = anti[a] = True
            walk(r + 1)
            col[c] = diag[d] = anti[a] = False

    walk(0)
    return nodes, tests, sols


def nqueens_bits(n, symmetry=False):
    """Bitmask conflict sets; optionally break the mirror symmetry of row 0."""
    full = (1 << n) - 1
    nodes = 0
    sols = 0

    def walk(cols, diag, anti):
        nonlocal nodes, sols
        nodes += 1
        if cols == full:
            sols += 1
            return
        avail = full & ~(cols | diag | anti)
        while avail:
            p = avail & -avail
            avail -= p
            walk(cols | p, ((diag | p) << 1) & full, (anti | p) >> 1)

    if not symmetry:
        walk(0, 0, 0)
        return nodes, sols

    # row 0 restricted to the left half; double the count, add the centre column
    half = 0
    for c in range(n // 2):
        p = 1 << c
        before = sols
        walk(p, (p << 1) & full, p >> 1)
        half += sols - before
    total = 2 * half
    if n % 2 == 1:
        p = 1 << (n // 2)
        before = sols
        walk(p, (p << 1) & full, p >> 1)
        total += sols - before
    return nodes, total

# Word search: the worst case is not the average case.

def word_search(board, word, letter_precheck=False, allow_reverse=False):
    rows, cols = len(board), len(board[0])
    calls = 0

    if letter_precheck:
        from collections import Counter
        have = Counter(ch for row in board for ch in row)
        need = Counter(word)
        for ch, k in need.items():
            if have[ch] < k:
                return 0, False, "rejected by letter count"

    w = word
    reversed_used = False
    if allow_reverse:
        from collections import Counter
        have = Counter(ch for row in board for ch in row)
        if have[word[-1]] < have[word[0]]:
            w = word[::-1]
            reversed_used = True

    def walk(r, c, k):
        nonlocal calls
        calls += 1
        if r < 0 or r >= rows or c < 0 or c >= cols or board[r][c] != w[k]:
            return False
        if k == len(w) - 1:
            return True
        saved = board[r][c]
        board[r][c] = "\0"
        found = (walk(r + 1, c, k + 1) or walk(r - 1, c, k + 1)
                 or walk(r, c + 1, k + 1) or walk(r, c - 1, k + 1))
        board[r][c] = saved
        return found

    for r in range(rows):
        for c in range(cols):
            if walk(r, c, 0):
                return calls, True, ("reversed" if reversed_used else "found")
    return calls, False, ("reversed" if reversed_used else "exhausted")

# Sudoku: variable ordering is not a micro-optimisation.

def sudoku_solve(puzzle, mrv, cap=3_000_000):
    grid = [list(row) for row in puzzle]
    rows = [0] * 9
    cols = [0] * 9
    boxes = [0] * 9
    for r in range(9):
        for c in range(9):
            ch = grid[r][c]
            if ch != ".":
                b = 1 << (int(ch) - 1)
                rows[r] |= b
                cols[c] |= b
                boxes[(r // 3) * 3 + c // 3] |= b

    nodes = 0
    assignments = 0
    hit_cap = False

    def pick():
        best = None
        best_count = 10
        for r in range(9):
            for c in range(9):
                if grid[r][c] != ".":
                    continue
                mask = 0x1FF & ~(rows[r] | cols[c] | boxes[(r // 3) * 3 + c // 3])
                if not mrv:
                    return r, c, mask
                k = bin(mask).count("1")
                if k < best_count:
                    best = (r, c, mask)
                    best_count = k
                    if k <= 1:
                        return best
        return best

    def walk():
        nonlocal nodes, assignments, hit_cap
        nodes += 1
        if nodes > cap:
            hit_cap = True
            return False
        cell = pick()
        if cell is None:
            return True
        r, c, mask = cell
        if mask == 0:
            return False
        b = (r // 3) * 3 + c // 3
        m = mask
        while m:
            p = m & -m
            m -= p
            assignments += 1
            grid[r][c] = str(p.bit_length())
            rows[r] |= p
            cols[c] |= p
            boxes[b] |= p
            if walk():
                return True
            if hit_cap:
                return False
            rows[r] &= ~p
            cols[c] &= ~p
            boxes[b] &= ~p
            grid[r][c] = "."
        return False

    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    ok = walk()
    dt = time.perf_counter() - t0
    return nodes, assignments, ok, hit_cap, dt, ["".join(row) for row in grid]

# Knuth 1975: estimate the size of a tree you have not walked.

def nqueens_children(state, n):
    """Return the list of child states of an n-queens partial assignment."""
    cols, diag, anti = state
    full = (1 << n) - 1
    if cols == full:
        return []
    out = []
    avail = full & ~(cols | diag | anti)
    while avail:
        p = avail & -avail
        avail -= p
        out.append((cols | p, ((diag | p) << 1) & full, (anti | p) >> 1))
    return out


def knuth_probe(n, rng):
    """One random root-to-leaf descent. Returns the estimate of total nodes."""
    state = (0, 0, 0)
    estimate = 1.0
    weight = 1.0
    while True:
        kids = nqueens_children(state, n)
        d = len(kids)
        if d == 0:
            return estimate
        weight *= d
        estimate += weight
        state = kids[rng.randrange(d)]

# The memoisation boundary: deciding vs counting vs enumerating.

WORDS = {"a", "aa", "aaa", "aaaa", "aaaaa"}


def word_break_decide(s, words, memo=False):
    calls = 0
    seen = {} if memo else None

    def walk(i):
        nonlocal calls
        calls += 1
        if i == len(s):
            return True
        if seen is not None and i in seen:
            return seen[i]
        ans = False
        for j in range(i + 1, min(i + 6, len(s)) + 1):
            if s[i:j] in words and walk(j):
                ans = True
                break
        if seen is not None:
            seen[i] = ans
        return ans

    return walk(0), calls


def word_break_count(s, words, memo=False):
    calls = 0
    seen = {} if memo else None

    def walk(i):
        nonlocal calls
        calls += 1
        if i == len(s):
            return 1
        if seen is not None and i in seen:
            return seen[i]
        total = 0
        for j in range(i + 1, min(i + 6, len(s)) + 1):
            if s[i:j] in words:
                total += walk(j)
        if seen is not None:
            seen[i] = total
        return total

    return walk(0), calls


def word_break_enumerate(s, words):
    calls = 0
    out = 0
    path = []

    def walk(i):
        nonlocal calls, out
        calls += 1
        if i == len(s):
            out += 1
            return
        for j in range(i + 1, min(i + 6, len(s)) + 1):
            if s[i:j] in words:
                path.append(s[i:j])
                walk(j)
                path.pop()

    walk(0)
    return out, calls


def exp8():
    head(8, "where memoisation applies, and where it cannot")
    print()
    print(f"  dictionary = {sorted(WORDS)}   (runs of 'a' of length 1..5)")
    print()
    print("  (A) DECIDE on an unsatisfiable input: s = 'a'*n + 'b'.")
    print("      No segmentation exists, so the search cannot short-circuit.")
    print()
    print(f"  {'|s|':>5} {'calls, no memo':>16} {'calls, memo':>13} {'ratio':>12}")
    print("  " + THIN)
    for n in range(6, 23, 2):
        s = "a" * n + "b"
        ok, plain = word_break_decide(s, WORDS)
        ok2, memoed = word_break_decide(s, WORDS, memo=True)
        assert ok is False and ok2 is False
        print(f"  {len(s):>5} {plain:>16,} {memoed:>13,} {plain/memoed:>11,.0f}x")
    print()
    print("  (B) COUNT and ENUMERATE on a satisfiable input: s = 'a'*n.")
    print()
    print(f"  {'|s|':>5} {'segmentations':>15} {'count calls':>13} {'+memo':>8}"
          f" {'enumerate calls':>17}")
    print("  " + THIN)
    for n in range(6, 25, 2):
        s = "a" * n
        total, plain = word_break_count(s, WORDS)
        _, memoed = word_break_count(s, WORDS, memo=True)
        if n <= 20:
            outs, ecalls = word_break_enumerate(s, WORDS)
            assert outs == total
            ec = f"{ecalls:,}"
        else:
            ec = "-"
        print(f"  {len(s):>5} {total:>15,} {plain:>13,} {memoed:>8,} {ec:>17}")
    print()
    print("  Three questions over one identical recursion tree.")
    print()
    print("  DECIDE is linear with a memo because the answer at position i does not")
    print("  depend on how the walk reached i; the prefix is irrelevant once you know")
    print("  where you are. COUNT is the same: the number of segmentations of a suffix")
    print("  is a function of that suffix alone, so one integer per position suffices.")
    print()
    print("  ENUMERATE cannot be compressed by any memo, because the output is")
    print("  exponential in the input; there is nothing to cache that is smaller than")
    print("  what you must emit. This is the boundary. If your recursion returns a")
    print("  summarisable value, you are writing dynamic programming and should stop")
    print("  backtracking. If it must emit every witness, backtracking is not a slow")
    print("  algorithm to be improved -- it is the only shape the answer has.")
    s = "a" * 24
    total, _ = word_break_count(s, WORDS, memo=True)
    _, dm = word_break_decide(s, WORDS, memo=True)
    print()
    print(f"  |s|=24: deciding takes {dm} memoised calls. Counting takes {dm}.")
    print(f"  Listing takes {total:,} outputs. No implementation trick closes that gap.")
