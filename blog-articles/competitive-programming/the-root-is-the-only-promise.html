Part I · The promise

§1Two Programs, One Comparator Apart

Here are two solutions to two different problems. The first finds the fourth largest number in an array. The second finds the four points closest to the origin. One is about integers and the other about geometry; one wants big things and the other wants small ones. Written out, they differ in a single expression: the function that decides which of two elements loses.

That is not a cute observation about code reuse. It is the whole subject. A binary heap is an array plus one comparison function, and every problem in this article — kth largest1, k closest points2, merge k sorted lists3, median from a data stream4, task scheduling5, Dijkstra6 — is that same array with a different answer to three questions. What goes in. What is kept out. And what you do when the element at the root turns out to be stale.

The structure itself is sixty years old and has not changed. J. W. J. Williams published it in June 1964 as Algorithm 232, "Heapsort", in Communications of the ACM7; six months later, in the December issue, Robert Floyd published Algorithm 245, "Treesort 3", which added the bottom-up construction that makes building a heap linear rather than linearithmic8. Between them they wrote roughly forty lines of ALGOL that no one has improved on for the general case. Everything since has been either a constant-factor refinement or a different data structure wearing the same interface.

What has changed is the surrounding vocabulary. A heap is now something you mostly do not write: you reach for PriorityQueue, priority_queue, BinaryHeap, container/heap — each with different defaults, different failure modes, and in one case no existence at all. This article treats the six languages as six dialects of the same idea, gives every listing in all of them, and is honest about where the dialects diverge in ways that will cost you a submission.

The organising claim: a heap maintains a purely local invariant — each node beats its children — and that local rule buys exactly one global fact, the identity of the extreme element. Every technique in the field is an answer to what to do with a structure that knows one thing and refuses to know anything else.

The article proceeds in four parts. Part I builds the structure and measures it. Part II works through the pattern families, with implementations in Go, Java, C++, Rust, C# and TypeScript that were compiled, executed and diffed against each other as a condition of publishing. Part III covers the variations — arity, mergeability, indexing — and the several ways a comparator can quietly destroy your correctness. Part IV argues, at some length, about when you should not use a heap at all, which turns out to be more often than the problem lists suggest.

§2What a Heap Actually Is

A binary heap is a complete binary tree in which every node beats its children under some ordering. "Complete" means every level is full except possibly the last, which fills left to right. That completeness is not an aesthetic preference; it is what allows the tree to be stored with no pointers at all.

Put the root at index 0. Then the children of index i live at 2i+1 and 2i+2, and the parent of i lives at (i−1)/2 with integer division. That arithmetic is the data structure9. There is no allocation per node, no pointer chasing, and — the part that matters on modern hardware — the top of the tree occupies a handful of contiguous cache lines that stay resident under any workload10.

Two clarifications that eliminate most beginner confusion. First, the heap property is local and one-directional. In a min-heap, a[3] is smaller than a[7] and a[8], and that is all. It says nothing about a[4], nothing about whether a[3] is the second-smallest element, and nothing about the order of any two siblings. The second-smallest element of a min-heap is one of the root's two children — but which one is unknown without a comparison, and the third-smallest could be anywhere in the top three levels.

Second, "heap" here has nothing whatsoever to do with the heap that malloc allocates from. The collision is historical and unfortunate; the data structure came first, in 19647, and the memory region borrowed the word later.

Min-heap, max-heap, and the sign confusion

There is no such thing as a max-heap implementation. There is one implementation and two comparators. A max-heap is a min-heap whose comparison function has been reversed, and every mainstream library expresses it that way: greater<> in C++, Comparator.reverseOrder() in Java, Reverse(x) in Rust, a Less method that returns greater-than in Go.

Two habits will save you: negate the key rather than the comparator when the key is a number you control, and never negate at all when the key can reach the minimum representable integer, because −INT_MIN is INT_MIN11. Where a language lets you pass a comparator, pass a comparator. Where it does not — Rust, and .NET when you use the tuple form — the sign lives in the key and you must write a comment saying so.

§3Sift Up, Sift Down

The entire mutable behaviour of a heap is two procedures, and they are not symmetric. Understanding why they differ explains most of the performance results later in this article.

Sift up restores the invariant after an element is added at the end. Compare with the parent; if it beats the parent, swap; repeat. One comparison per level, and it stops the moment the element loses. On random input a new element is very likely already in the right place: roughly half of all insertions stop after a single comparison, because half of all positions in a complete tree are leaves whose parents are randomly ordered relative to them.

Sift down restores the invariant after the root is removed and the last element is moved into its place. Now you must find the better of two children and then decide whether the parent beats it: two comparisons per level, not one. Worse, the element you moved into the root came from the bottom of the tree, where the losers live, so it will usually sink all the way back down. Sift down almost always runs the full depth.

This asymmetry is worth a specific number. A push costs about 1 comparison per level it climbs and climbs very little; a pop costs about 2 comparisons per level and descends nearly all of them. In a heap of n elements a pop is therefore roughly 2·log₂n comparisons where a push is closer to 2 in total. That is why the standard advice — "insertion and deletion are both O(log n)" — is technically true and practically misleading, and why the lab in this article counts comparisons rather than operations.

The fused operation nobody teaches

There is a third primitive, and it is the single most useful thing in this article for competitive work. If you are about to pop the root and then immediately push a replacement, do not do both. Overwrite the root with the new element and sift down once. You have saved an entire sift up plus the bookkeeping of a length change, and more importantly you have avoided the case where the new element climbs back to the top and is popped again next iteration.

Python exposes this as heapreplace12; .NET calls it EnqueueDequeue13; Rust reaches it through peek_mut, which returns a guard that sifts down when it is dropped14; the Go generic-heap proposal now working through the standard library process calls it ChangeMin and names top-k as its motivating use case15. Java and C++ have no such operation, so you write pop(); push(x); and pay for it. The listings in this article implement it under the name pushPop wherever the standard library does not supply one.

§4The Linear Build

Building a heap from n elements by pushing them one at a time costs O(n log n). Building it by Floyd's method — start at the last internal node, sift down, walk backwards to the root — costs O(n)8. The proof is a single observation that is worth internalising because it recurs throughout the subject: almost every node is a leaf, and a leaf cannot fall.

In a complete tree of n nodes, about n/2 sit at height 0, n/4 at height 1, n/8 at height 2. A node at height h can sink at most h levels. So the total work is bounded by n·∑(h/2^(h+1)), and that sum converges to 1 regardless of n, giving at most 2n swaps. The expensive nodes are precisely the rare ones16.

The lab measures this at four sizes and finds the ratio holds flat: at n = 1,000,000 the push loop costs 2.285 comparisons and 3.569 array writes per element, while bottom-up heapify costs 1.882 and 2.489. That is a 2.10× advantage in comparisons and about 1.43× in writes — real, worth taking for free, and considerably less dramatic than "O(n) versus O(n log n)" makes it sound.

The honest readingThe asymptotic gap is real but the constant hides it. log₂(10⁶) is 20, yet the push loop costs only 2.29 comparisons per element, not 20, because sift up almost always stops immediately. Asymptotic notation describes the worst case of a single operation; it is a poor predictor of the average cost of a million of them.

Every language in this article gives you the linear build somewhere, and it is not always where you would look. C++ gets it from the iterator-range constructor of priority_queue, which calls make_heap17. Rust gets it from BinaryHeap::from(vec) and from collect(), both of which rebuild rather than push18. Go's heap.Init19 and Java's collection-taking PriorityQueue constructor do the same. .NET exposes it as EnqueueRange on an empty queue13. If you find yourself writing a loop of pushes over a slice you already own, you are leaving this on the table.

§5What a Heap Refuses to Know

Most explanations of heaps enumerate what they can do. The more useful exercise is the inverse, because it is the refusals that determine which problem you are allowed to point one at.

A heap cannot tell you whether an element is present without scanning all of it. It cannot find the second-smallest element without comparing two candidates, nor the kth without effectively doing k extractions. It cannot give you a predecessor, a successor, or a range. It cannot iterate in sorted order without destroying itself. It cannot merge with another heap in less than linear time. And — the refusal that generates an entire section of this article — it cannot find an arbitrary element in order to change its key.

Java is unusually candid about this. PriorityQueue.remove(Object) exists, and it is a linear scan followed by a repair20. It compiles, it passes small tests, and it turns your O(m log n) algorithm into O(m·n) on the large case. C++ does not offer the operation at all, which is arguably the more honest design21. .NET deliberately shipped PriorityQueue in .NET 6 without priority updates on the grounds that supporting them would slow down everything else, then added a linear-scan Remove in .NET 9 specifically so that people could write Dijkstra with it2213. Rust has no removal API beyond the root at all.

These refusals are not oversights. They are the price of the array. A structure that supported search would need either a second index (see §15) or a different shape entirely (see §21). The design says: I will give you the extreme element in constant time and maintain it in logarithmic time, and in exchange I will know nothing else about my contents. The entire skill of using a heap well is recognising when that trade is the right one — and, as Part IV argues, noticing the surprisingly common cases where it is not.

§6Six Standard Libraries, Six Different Defaults

Before any algorithm, a survey, because a startling number of wrong answers in contests are simply sign errors inherited from a library's default direction.

C++ is the trap. std::priority_queue is a max-heap by default, because its comparator defaults to std::less and the comparator answers "does a come out after b"2123. Almost every other language defaults to minimum. If you write priority_queue<int> pq expecting Dijkstra's frontier, you will get the longest path to nowhere. The idiom to memorise is priority_queue<T, vector<T>, greater<T>>.

Rust's BinaryHeap is also a max-heap, and takes no comparator at all: ordering comes from Ord18. You control it by choosing the key — wrap in std::cmp::Reverse to flip, put the tiebreak in a tuple. This is more rigid than a comparator and considerably harder to get subtly wrong, with one glaring exception: f64 is not Ord, so a heap of floats requires OrderedFloat, a manual wrapper, or a scaled integer key. That is not pedantry; it is the standard library refusing to let you build a heap whose comparator is non-transitive on NaN24.

Go asks the most of you. container/heap is an interface, not a container: you supply the storage and implement five methods, two of which are confusingly named Push and Pop and are not the functions you call19. It also predates generics, so elements come back as any. A proposal for container/heap/v2 was filed in February 2026 and is open as of this writing; it lists exactly these complaints, and adds an Init for the linear build, a ChangeMin for the fused replace, and a NewIndexed constructor that maintains position indices for you1525. Until it lands — Go 1.27 is in release-candidate as this is written and does not contain it26 — the practical answer in Go is the thirty-line generic heap in Listing 2.

Java is the most conventional: a min-heap over a Comparator, with an O(n) collection constructor and the linear remove(Object) already noted20. .NET separates element from priority, which is genuinely better design — the key is computed once at enqueue instead of on every comparison13. TypeScript has nothing. There is no priority queue in JavaScript, in Node, or in the browser; the ecosystem answer is a package27 and the contest answer is to paste in twenty lines.

One point of style visible in all six panes: the tiebreak is written down. None of these libraries is stable, none promises to be, and §18 shows experimentally how many different correct answers that permits.

Part II · The patterns

§7The Heap You Write Yourself

Two of the six languages here have no usable generic priority queue, and in all six there are contests where you want the array itself — to seed it in linear time, to fuse a pop and a push, to store the keys unboxed. So the first listing is the structure, written out, with the three operations that matter and nothing else.

Three details in that listing repay attention.

The constructor takes an optional slice and heapifies it, so the linear build is the default path rather than an optimisation you remember later. The sift-down loop compares the left child against the current best and then the right child against the result, which is one comparison more than strictly necessary at the last level but keeps the code branch-simple; the variant that sifts to the bottom and then sifts back up saves roughly half the comparisons and is what a serious implementation does2829. And pushPop is written to return the argument unchanged when the argument would win, which is the case that makes bounded top-k cheap.

You should not write this in a contest if a standard library will do. You should write it when the library's ordering fights you (Rust with floats), when there is no library (TypeScript), when you need the fused operation (Java, C++), or when you are about to need the indexed version in §15. The published implementations of all six were compiled with warnings enabled and their outputs diffed byte-for-byte against each other as a condition of publishing: 6/6 byte-identical, 651 bytes, sha256 4e972678b3a894c0.

§8Bounded: Keep k, Discard the Rest

The first and most common pattern. You are asked for the kth largest element, or the k largest, or the k most frequent. The answer is a heap of size k — and, counter-intuitively for almost everyone the first time, a min-heap.

Hold the k largest elements seen so far. The root of that min-heap is the smallest of the k, which means it is simultaneously the answer to "what is the kth largest" and the doorman: any incoming element that cannot beat the root cannot possibly belong in the top k, and is discarded after exactly one comparison. That single guard comparison is the algorithm. Everything else is bookkeeping.

The lab offers 100,000 elements to a size-10 heap in three orders. On random input 95 elements are ever admitted — 0.10% of the stream — against a theoretical expectation of k·H(n) ≈ 120.9, which is to say O(k log n) admissions rather than O(n)30. On descending input exactly k are admitted, because the first ten are the largest ten. And on ascending input every single element is admitted, because each new arrival beats the incumbent.

That last case deserves alarm. Sorted-ascending input is not adversarial cleverness; it is what you get when the array arrives from a database with an ORDER BY, or is a prefix-sum, or is a timestamp column. The Rust documentation flags the same hazard for its own push: expected cost is O(1) averaged over random orderings, degrading to amortised O(log n) when elements arrive in ascending sorted order18.

And here is the result that the problem lists never mention. At k =  1,000 on already-ascending input the size-k heap costs 1,877,504 comparisons against 815,024 for a full merge sort: the heap is 2.3× worse than sorting the entire array. O(n log k) is an upper bound, not a discount, and when k approaches n it is no bound at all.

When to use quickselect instead

For the specific question "what is the kth largest element of an array I already hold in memory", the heap is not the best answer and never was. Quickselect — Hoare's FIND31 — is O(n) expected, with median-of-medians giving O(n) worst case32. The heap wins in exactly three circumstances, and you should be able to say which one applies before you reach for it:

  • The input is a stream. You cannot partition what you cannot hold. A bounded heap needs O(k) memory for an input of unbounded length30, which is the whole of §8's claim to existence33.
  • You need all k, not just the kth. Quickselect gives you a partition, not an order; if the answer must be sorted you are sorting k elements anyway.
  • k is genuinely small and n is genuinely large. Then the guard comparison dominates and the heap is barely more expensive than a scan.

§9The Derived Key

The second pattern is the first one with the key computed rather than given. K closest points to the origin2 is the canonical instance: the elements are points, the ordering is by distance, and the distance is not stored anywhere.

Note the inversion relative to Listing 3. There we kept the k largest with a min-heap; here we keep the k smallest with a max-heap. The rule underneath both: the heap is ordered opposite to the thing you want, so that the root is the worst survivor and therefore the one to evict. If you can state which element you want to throw away, you have determined the comparator.

Never take the square root

Comparing √(x²+y²) and comparing x²+y² give identical orderings, because the square root is monotone on non-negative reals. Taking it costs a transcendental operation per comparison and introduces floating-point error into what was exact integer arithmetic. This generalises well past distance: any strictly monotone transformation of the key can be dropped. Do not divide to compare ratios, cross-multiply. Do not take logarithms to compare products unless overflow forces you to.

Overflow, preciselyWith coordinates bounded by 10⁵, x²+y² reaches 2×10¹⁰, which overflows a 32-bit signed integer at 2.1×10⁹. Use 64-bit keys. Separately: never write a comparator as a - b. It is the most common integer-overflow bug in contest code, it produces a comparator that is not transitive, and in Java a non-transitive comparator will eventually make TimSort throw "Comparison method violates its general contract" from inside the standard library — a failure mode that took a formal-methods paper to characterise properly3536. Write Integer.compare(a, b), a < b, or cmp::Ordering.

The same discipline applies to the eviction test itself. In Listing 4 the guard is dist2(p) < dist2(h.peek()), which recomputes the root's key on every element. For a cheap key that is fine; for an expensive one — a string comparison, a geometric predicate, a database lookup — store the key alongside the element and compare the stored copy. This is precisely the design .NET chose for its whole API by separating TElement from TPriority13, and it is why the Rust listing carries the distance as the first tuple field rather than recomputing from the coordinates.

§10Frontier: One Cursor per Source

The third pattern is where heaps stop being a convenience and start being structural. You have k sorted sequences and want them merged3. Put the head of each sequence into a heap. Pop the smallest, emit it, and push only the element immediately behind it. The heap never holds more than k elements no matter how long the sequences are.

Two properties make this the load-bearing pattern of the whole family. Cost is O(N log k) for N elements total, and memory is O(k) regardless of N. That second property is why the same listing, with the arrays replaced by file handles, is an external merge sort — the classical answer to sorting more data than you have memory for9. It is also why it works on inputs you cannot rewind: generators, sockets, database cursors.

The problems in that band share a discipline: never push a candidate you can generate later. The k-pairs problem37 has n×m possible pairs; pushing all of them is O(nm log nm) and fails on the large case. Pushing only (0,0) and, on popping (i,j), pushing (i+1,j) and (i,j+1) with a visited set keeps the heap at O(k). The kth-smallest-matrix problem38 has the same structure, and both are strictly dominated for large k by binary search on the answer value — which is worth saying out loud, because both are usually taught as heap problems and neither is optimal.

The lab measures the heap against two pairwise alternatives on 120,000 elements. Merging sequentially — accumulate into one list, merge the next in — is quadratic in k and collapses as expected: at k =  512 it costs 17.0× the heap. But merging in a balanced tournament is also O(N log k), and it costs 1,077,273 comparisons against the heap's 1,801,838 — 0.60×, that is, the heap loses by 40%. It loses at every k above 2.

The heap still wins the argument, but for reasons that are not comparison counts: it holds O(k) memory rather than O(N), it streams, and it does not need to know the sequences' lengths in advance. Choose it for those reasons and not because it is theoretically faster, because it is not.

§11Paired: Two Heaps Across a Boundary

A single heap knows one extreme. Two heaps, arranged back to back, know a boundary — and a boundary is what a median is4.

Keep the smaller half of the data in a max-heap and the larger half in a min-heap. The two roots are the two elements adjacent to the median. Maintain the sizes within one of each other, and the median is either the larger heap's root or the mean of the two roots. Every insertion costs one push, at most one transfer between the heaps, and no scanning of anything.

Whenever you see a set that becomes eligible monotonically and a greedy choice among the eligible, this is the shape: one heap gates admission, one heap makes the choice.

§12The Clock: Heaps That Schedule

§13Re-entrant: When the Output Goes Back In

A small band, but it contains the oldest application of the structure. Pop two elements, combine them, push the result. Repeat until one remains. The heap's contents change identity as the algorithm runs.

Connecting sticks44 is Huffman coding45 with the labels removed. Huffman's 1952 construction — repeatedly merge the two least frequent symbols, and the resulting tree is an optimal prefix code — is the earliest important algorithm that is fundamentally a loop over a priority queue, and its correctness proof is the exchange argument that underwrites this entire band: if an optimal solution does not join the two smallest first, you can swap to make it do so without increasing cost.

One implementation note with real consequences. If the inputs are already sorted, Huffman needs no heap at all: keep two FIFO queues, one of original leaves and one of merged nodes, and take the smaller head of the two. Both queues stay sorted for free, and the whole algorithm becomes O(n) after the sort. This is standard in production encoders and almost never mentioned in the problem statements.

Part III · Variations and hazards

§14Lazy Deletion: When the Root Is a Lie

Dijkstra's algorithm6 needs an operation the heap refuses to provide. When a shorter path to a vertex is discovered, that vertex's key must decrease, and the vertex is somewhere in the middle of the array where the heap cannot find it.

The pragmatic answer, and the one used in essentially all competitive code, is to give up on updating47. Push the improved distance as a new entry and leave the old one to rot. When a stale entry surfaces at the root, its key disagrees with the current best distance for that vertex, and you drop it and continue. One comparison per pop buys the right to never maintain a position map at all4849.

The lab runs both variants on 20,000-vertex graphs at five densities, with an assertion that the distance arrays agree. On a tree the lazy heap is exactly the indexed heap: no vertex is ever improved twice, and the stale rate is 0.00%. By m/n = 16 it holds 61,154 entries of which 67.3% are dead on arrival, its peak size is 46,547 against the indexed heap's 17,231, and it performs 3.04× the comparisons.

Those are the true costs, and they are worth stating plainly because lazy deletion is usually presented as free. It is not free: it is O(m) memory instead of O(n), a constant factor more comparisons, and a heap that grows with the number of edges relaxed. It is cheaper anyway on sparse graphs, because the position map costs four writes per swap on every operation forever, while the stale entries cost one comparison each on the way out. The crossover is around the point where the graph stops being sparse — and for contest input sizes, graphs are essentially always sparse.

Where lazy deletion generalises

The technique is not about graphs. It is the general answer to "I need to delete something that is not the root", and it comes in two forms:

  • Validity-checked entries. Store enough in each entry to test on extraction whether it is still current — a distance against dist[], a version counter, an index against the window bound. This is what §14 and the sliding-window heap both do.
  • The two-heap delete. Keep a second heap of elements that have been logically removed. Before reading the root of the main heap, while the two roots are equal, pop both. This supports arbitrary deletion of known values with no index at all, at the cost of a heap that never shrinks below the number of deletions.

Both are correct only if the extraction check is cheap and total. If you have to search to know whether an entry is stale, you have reinvented the problem you were avoiding.

§15The Indexed Heap

The alternative to tolerating stale entries is to make the heap addressable. Maintain, alongside the array, a map from element identity to array position, and update it on every swap. Now decreaseKey is a lookup followed by a sift up — and only ever a sift up, because a key that has decreased can never need to sink.

Historically this is the point where the theory diverges from the practice. Fibonacci heaps51 were designed to make decreaseKey O(1) amortised, which drops Dijkstra to O(m + n log n) and is one of the celebrated results of the field. In practice they are almost never worth it. Their per-node overhead, pointer chasing and poor cache behaviour cost more than the asymptotic saving on any input you will meet: the empirical literature has said so consistently since the early nineties525354, and the most careful modern study — Larkin, Sen and Tarjan's deliberately plain reimplementation of ten heap variants — found that implicit d-ary heaps win at small sizes, pairing heaps win at large ones, and the L1 cache miss rate predicts wall-clock time better than the operation counts do5556.

Which to write, in practice

Use lazy deletion by default. Use an indexed heap when any of the following holds: the graph is dense enough that duplicate entries would dominate memory; you need to query a vertex's current key, not just pop the best; you need deletion of specific elements rather than expiry; or the elements are large enough that duplicating them is worse than indexing them. Note that four of the six standard libraries here cannot do it at all — Java and .NET only via an O(n) removal2022, C++ and Rust not at all — so an indexed heap is nearly always something you write. The Go proposal for container/heap/v2 is the sole mainstream design that takes it seriously, offering a NewIndexed constructor precisely because a survey of real Go code found that everyone who needs it has already hand-rolled an index field into their element struct15.

§16When the Deque Wins

Sliding window maximum57 is the standard cautionary tale, and it is usually told wrongly. It is offered as a heap problem, solved with a heap and lazy deletion, and then the monotonic deque is introduced as a clever trick. The better framing is that the deque encodes a fact the heap has no way to express.

The fact: if element a arrives before element b and a ≤ b, then a can never again be the answer, because any window containing a also contains b. It may be deleted immediately and permanently. A deque can act on that; a heap has no operation for "delete this element I am looking at".

The measurements are stark. On 200,000 random elements with a window of 1,000, the lazy heap peaks at 198,627 live entries — 99% of the entire input, because an element is only ever removed when it reaches the root, and most never do. The deque peaks at 19 entries. The heap performs 2.55 comparisons per element; the deque performs 2.00 operations per element in every order tested, because each element is pushed once and popped at most once.

Ascending input is the worst case for the heap (15.69 comparisons per element, and it never sheds an entry until the very end) and the best case for the deque, which holds exactly one. Descending input inverts the deque's size to the full window but does not change its operation count.

§17Arity: Why Four Children

Nothing about a heap requires two children. A d-ary heap gives each node d children, at indices d·i+1 through d·i+d, with parent (i−1)/d59. The tree becomes shallower by a factor of log d, which makes sift up cheaper, and each sift-down step must examine d children instead of 2, which makes pop dearer.

That trade has a clean statement. A push costs one comparison per level and the number of levels is log_d n, so pushes get cheaper monotonically with d. A pop costs d−1 comparisons per level to select the best child, so pop cost goes as (d−1)·log_d n, which is minimised at small d and grows without bound. Whether increasing d helps depends entirely on the ratio of pushes to pops in your workload.

The lab measures both extremes on 200,000 elements. Building and then draining the whole heap — heapsort, the pop-heaviest workload there is — makes d = 2 the winner and d = 16 cost 2.10× as many comparisons. A Dijkstra-shaped workload of eight pushes per pop inverts it: d = 4 is best at 0.84× the binary heap's comparisons.

And the comparison counts understate the case for wider heaps, because they ignore the reason d-ary heaps are actually used. Data movement falls sharply with arity — and, more importantly, the d children of a node are contiguous. At d = 4 with 8-byte keys, all four candidate children usually occupy one cache line, so the d−1 extra comparisons are free in memory terms while the saved level is a whole cache miss avoided. This is why the cache-conscious literature has recommended 4-ary heaps for three decades1060, why Larkin, Sen and Tarjan found L1 miss rate to be the best predictor of running time55, and why .NET's PriorityQueue ships as a 4-ary heap internally1361.

The practical ruleUse a binary heap unless you are push-heavy and performance-bound, in which case try d = 4 and measure. The change is three lines: the parent formula, the child range, and the loop that picks the best child. The indexed heap in Listing 9 takes d as a constructor parameter for exactly this reason.

§18Ties, Determinism, and the Comparator

No mainstream priority queue is stable. Java's documentation says its iteration order is unspecified and makes no promise about equal elements20; C++ specifies only the heap property23; Rust's BinaryHeap documents nothing about ties18; .NET's is explicitly not stable13. This is not an oversight — stability would require either a sequence field or a stable sift, both of which cost something for a guarantee most callers do not need.

The practical consequence is that a specification like "always process a task of highest priority" does not determine an output. The lab makes this concrete by exhaustively enumerating all 720 insertion orders of six tasks with priorities 5, 5, 5, 3, 3, 1, and collecting the distinct drain sequences.

Comparing on priority alone yields 12 distinct outputs, every one of which satisfies the specification. Adding the arrival index to the key yields 1. If a judge diffs your output, the tiebreak is not an implementation detail — it is part of the key, and you must write it down.

The rule, stated once

Make the key a total order. If two distinct elements can compare equal, add a field until they cannot: an arrival counter, an id, a coordinate. Every listing in this article does it — the Dijkstra entry is (distance, vertex) rather than distance, the merge cursor is (value, list index), the scheduler task is (count, name). That is what allows six independent implementations, over four different internal heap layouts including a 4-ary one, to print byte-identical output. Without total keys, the diff would fail and so would the article's central claim that the six programs are the same program.

There is a second reason beyond reproducibility. A stable, total key removes an entire class of Heisenbug in which your solution passes locally and fails on the judge because their standard library breaks ties differently — or because your own library changed version. It costs one field.

§19Six Ways to Break a Comparator

A heap is only as correct as the ordering you hand it, and an ordering can be wrong in ways that produce plausible output rather than an error. Each of the following has cost a submission somewhere, and four of the six are language-specific.

1. Subtraction

Writing return a - b in an integer comparator is the most common of these bugs. With values near the representable limits it overflows and inverts the sign, producing a relation that is not transitive. A heap built on a non-transitive comparator does not crash; it silently violates its own invariant, and the root is simply no longer the minimum. In Java the same comparator handed to List.sort will eventually throw "Comparison method violates its general contract" from TimSort — a detection that only exists because a formal verification of the JDK's sort found the underlying bug3536. Write Integer.compare, a < b, or cmp::Ordering.

2. Floating-point keys

NaN compares false against everything, including itself, so a comparator using < on doubles is not a strict weak ordering the moment a NaN enters24. Rust simply refuses: f64 does not implement Ord, so BinaryHeap<f64> does not compile18. The other five will happily build a corrupt heap. Where you control the data, scale to integers; where you do not, sort NaN to one end explicitly and document it.

3. Comparing against a mutable field

If the key is computed from a field that changes while the element is in the heap, the heap is now ordered by a value that no longer exists. Nothing detects this. It is the single most common cause of "my Dijkstra returns the wrong distance sometimes", and it is why the indexed heap in Listing 9 requires you to call decreaseKey rather than assigning the key directly.

4. The C++ direction

C++'s comparator answers "does a come out after b", which is the reverse of the intuition most people carry from other languages21. Every C++ programmer has written priority_queue<pair<int,int>> for a Dijkstra frontier and got a max-heap. The mitigation is mechanical: write greater<> for min-heaps, always, and never rely on the default.

5. Negating for a max-heap

Negating the key to invert a min-heap works until the key can be the minimum representable integer, at which point −x = x and the ordering inverts for exactly one value11. It also fails outright for non-numeric keys. Where the language offers a comparator, use it; where it does not — Rust, and .NET's tuple form — prefer Reverse/Comparer.Create to arithmetic.

6. Assuming ties are broken the way they were last time

Covered at length in §18: none of the six libraries is stable and none promises to be. If two elements can compare equal and the output distinguishes them, the key is incomplete.

A checklist worth thirty secondsIs the comparator transitive under the actual value range? Can two distinct elements compare equal? Does the key depend on anything mutable? Is the direction the one I think? If all four answers are good, the heap will be correct; if any is not, it will be wrong in a way that no test on small input will reveal.

§20Beyond the Binary Heap

The implicit binary heap is one point in a large design space. The rest of the space exists because of two operations it cannot do: merging two heaps in sublinear time, and lowering a key without a position map.

Mergeable heaps

Leftist heaps62 and skew heaps63 are pointer-based binary trees whose merge is a walk down the right spines, O(log n) worst case and amortised respectively. Every other operation is defined in terms of merge: insertion is a merge with a singleton, extraction is a merge of the root's two subtrees. Skew heaps are the self-adjusting variant — no balance information stored at all — and are the heap analogue of splay trees64.

Binomial queues6566 represent a heap of n elements as a forest of binomial trees mirroring the binary representation of n, which makes merging an exercise in binary addition with carries. Fibonacci heaps51 relax the structure further to get O(1) amortised insert and decrease-key at the cost of a genuinely intricate implementation, and relaxed heaps67 restore those bounds in a form suited to parallel machines. Strict Fibonacci heaps achieve the same bounds in the worst case rather than amortised68, and Brodal queues reached worst-case optimality earlier6970.

Pairing heaps56 are the practical member of this family: a multiway tree, no stored balance information, a merge that is a single link, and a delete-min that pairs up the children in two passes. Their precise amortised bound resisted analysis for two decades71, but they are easy to write and consistently fast, and they are the structure Larkin, Sen and Tarjan found to dominate at large sizes55.

Double-ended heaps

If you need both extremes, a single heap is the wrong shape. Min-max heaps72 alternate the ordering by level, so even levels are min-ordered and odd levels max-ordered, giving both extremes in O(1) from the same array. Interval heaps73 store two elements per node representing a nested interval. Both keep the implicit array representation. Both are more than most contests need — two heaps and lazy deletion is usually simpler — but they exist and they are the right answer when memory is genuinely tight.

Approximate and specialised

Soft heaps74 allow a controlled fraction of keys to be corrupted upward in exchange for constant amortised time per operation, and are a component of the fastest known minimum-spanning-tree algorithms rather than something you would use directly. At the other end of the abstraction, the average-case analyses of the plain binary heap — how far a random element sinks, how many comparisons heapsort really needs — are a small literature of their own7576.

What to actually writeIn a contest: the implicit binary heap, or the standard library's. If you need merge: a pairing heap, in about forty lines. If you need worst-case guarantees for a proof rather than a program: cite one of the above. The gap between the best structure in theory and the best structure on a machine has been measured repeatedly and it consistently favours the simple ones5554.

§21The Things That Are Not Heaps

Four structures answer questions that look like heap questions and answer them better. Recognising them is worth more than any refinement of the heap itself.

The balanced search tree

C++'s std::multiset, Java's TreeMap, Rust's BTreeMap: all give you the minimum and the maximum, plus predecessor, successor, range queries, and — the decisive one — erasure of a specific element in O(log n)7778. The cost is a constant factor of two to four on the operations a heap does support, and considerably more memory. Whenever a problem needs deletion of arbitrary known elements, sliding-window medians, or "the smallest element greater than x", the tree is the correct structure and the heap is a workaround.

The monotonic deque

Covered in §1679. When the expiry rule is by position rather than by value, and dominated elements can be discarded on arrival, the deque is O(1) amortised against the heap's O(log n) — and, as the lab shows, orders of magnitude better in memory.

Bucket and radix queues

If the keys are small integers, sorting them is not necessary; indexing them is. Dial's implementation of Dijkstra80 keeps an array of buckets indexed by distance and a pointer that only moves forward, giving O(1) per operation and O(m + nC) total for maximum edge weight C. Radix heaps refine this to O(m + n log C)81, and the theoretical line continues to integer priority queues with constant-time decrease-key82. If your priorities are bounded small integers — and in scheduling problems they very often are — a heap is strictly worse than an array of lists.

Sorting, and selection

The most commonly missed alternative is the simplest. If the collection does not change — if every element is known before the first query — sort it once and read off the answers. §8 measured a case where a bounded heap costs 2.3× a full sort. And for a single order statistic, quickselect is linear expected3132. The heap earns its place when the collection changes: when elements arrive after queries have begun, or when the algorithm's own output re-enters the input.

It is worth noting where the boundary blurs: introsort, the algorithm behind most standard-library sorts, falls back to heapsort when quicksort's recursion gets too deep, precisely because heapsort's O(n log n) is a guarantee rather than an average83. The heap's worst case being equal to its average is a real virtue — it is just not usually the virtue you are shopping for.

Six findings are worth restating because they contradict something commonly taught.

  1. The linear build is worth 1.21×, not 10×. The asymptotic gap between O(n) and O(n log n) construction is real; the measured gap at a million elements is 2.29 comparisons per element against 1.88, because sift up almost never climbs.
  2. A bounded top-k heap can be slower than sorting. At k =  1,000 on ascending input it costs 2.3× a full merge sort. The O(n log k) bound is not a discount.
  3. Input order changes admissions by four orders of magnitude — 10 against 100,000 for the same k and n — without changing the complexity class at all.
  4. Lazy deletion at m/n = 16 discards 67.3% of everything it pops and holds 2.7× the peak entries of an indexed heap, and is still the right default on sparse graphs.
  5. A balanced pairwise merge beats a k-way heap on comparisons at every k > 2, by 40% at k = 512. The heap's advantage is memory and streaming, not speed.
  6. Six tasks with tied priorities admit twelve distinct correct outputs. The tiebreak is part of the key or the answer is not determined.

§23When Not to Use a Heap

The problem lists teach recognition: see "kth", reach for a heap. That heuristic is good enough to pass and bad enough to leave a factor of two or a whole complexity class on the table. Here is the inverse list, in rough order of how often it applies.

The collection does not change. If every element is known before the first query, sort once. Sorting gives you every order statistic, every range, and every rank, for one O(n log n) payment. A heap gives you one extreme and then destroys itself producing the second.

You want one order statistic from an array in memory. Quickselect is O(n) expected31, or O(n) worst case with a linear-time median routine32. The heap is only better when the input streams or when you need all k in order.

The keys are small bounded integers. Bucket queues make every operation O(1)80. Counting sort makes top-k frequent linear. A heap here is paying log n for an ordering you could have indexed.

Elements expire by position rather than by value. Monotonic deque, as measured in §16.

You need to delete, search, or query rank. A balanced tree does all three and the heap does none, and the workarounds — linear removal, lazy deletion, a parallel index — are each a small tax that a tree charges you once, up front, in a constant factor8485.

k approaches n. Then O(n log k) is O(n log n) with a worse constant and worse locality than a sort.

The problem has a closed form. Task scheduler's answer is arithmetic after a counting pass. Meeting-rooms is a sweep over two sorted arrays. Both are routinely solved with heaps that are not needed.

Conversely, the situations where a heap is not merely acceptable but correct are narrower than they look and easy to state: the set changes while you are querying it, the queries are always for an extreme, and the keys are not small integers. Streaming top-k. K-way merging of sequences you cannot rewind. Best-first graph search. Discrete event simulation, where the entire program is a loop over "what happens next" and the priority queue is the clock86. Greedy algorithms whose output re-enters their input. In those cases nothing else is close, and the sixty-year-old array of Williams and Floyd remains, as far as anyone has been able to demonstrate on real hardware, the best available answer.

§26Resources

The original papers are short and worth the twenty minutes. Williams' Algorithm 232 is two pages7; Floyd's Algorithm 245 is one8. Between them is the entire structure.

For the theory: Knuth volume 3 remains the reference for heaps, heapsort and external merging9; CLRS chapter 6 is the standard teaching treatment84; Mehlhorn and Sanders is the best short account of why the implicit representation matters78. Sedgewick and Wayne is the most readable on priority queues as an abstract type77, and Skiena is the best at telling you when not to use one85.

For the practice: Larkin, Sen and Tarjan's empirical study is the one paper to read if you read only one, because it reimplements ten heap variants plainly and measures them honestly55. Chen and colleagues do the same specifically for Dijkstra54, and Cherkassky, Goldberg and Radzik remain the standard reference for shortest-path implementations at scale53.

For contests: Laaksonen's Competitive Programmer's Handbook is free and covers priority queues in the context of the problems that need them87; Competitive Programming 4 is the comprehensive option88; cp-algorithms has the cleanest write-ups of sparse Dijkstra and of the monotonic-queue trick that replaces a heap4879; the USACO Guide sequences the material by difficulty49.

For the problems: work the plates in §24 in band order rather than by difficulty. The bands are the pattern families, and the point of doing them in order is that the fifth problem in a band should feel like the first one with different nouns. If it does not, the plate's "what the root means" line is where to look. The two problems most worth doing carefully because they teach against the grain are the smallest-range problem89, where the heap gives you one end of the interval and a scalar gives you the other, and ugly numbers90, where every output is an input to three future candidates.

For the language details: read your standard library's source, not its documentation. The Go proposal thread for container/heap/v215 is unusually good on why the API decisions were made — including a survey of how real Go code maintains heap indices by hand — and .NET's decision to ship without priority updates and then add a linear removal three versions later22 is a well-documented worked example of the tension this whole article is about: a structure that keeps one promise, and an ecosystem that keeps wanting a second one.
