1. Scope and method

This article covers the IT Governance and Management family, the fifth subgroup of Enterprise IT and Information Systems in the series taxonomy. Parts 1.1 to 1.3 dealt with people who support, administer and engineer technology. This part deals with the people who decide what technology the organisation should invest in, make sure it is controlled and compliant, independently check that the controls work, and deliver change through projects and programmes. In SFIA 9 terms the centre of gravity moves from operational and engineering skills to governance (GOVN), risk management (BURM), audit (AUDT), portfolio, programme and project management (POMG, PGMG, PRMG), strategic planning (ITSP) and investment appraisal (INVA).[1]

Each role is described along the series' five independent dimensions:

DimensionQuestion it answersHow it applies to this family

Work functionWhat kind of work?Dominated by govern (direct, decide, assure) and analyze (assess risk, test controls, evaluate investments). The delivery roles add a coordinating form of build, and the risk and controls roles share territory with protect.
SpecializationWhich narrow expertise?A framework (COBIT, NIST RMF, ISO/IEC 27001), a regulation (SOX, DORA, HIPAA, the EU AI Act), a discipline (internal audit, enterprise risk, portfolio management) or a delivery method (predictive, agile, hybrid).
Organizational levelHow much responsibility?Analyst roles start at intermediate level and are rarely first jobs; manager, director and CIO roles are management and executive positions. SFIA defines its governance skill only at levels 6 and 7.[2]
IndustryWhich sector context?Every sector, but regulation shapes the work most strongly in finance, healthcare, government, defense, payments and listed companies.

A caution on job titles. Only one of the eleven titles maps cleanly onto a government occupational code. BLS lists "chief information officer (CIO)" as an alternative title for computer and information systems managers (SOC 11-3021), and O*NET lists "Information Technology Director" and "IT Manager" as reported titles for the same occupation.[3,4] O*NET's information technology project managers occupation (15-1299.09) reports "IT Program Manager" and "IT Project Manager" among its titles but has no wage data of its own; it borrows the figures for the broad "computer occupations, all other" group.[5] BLS explicitly places IT auditors within accountants and auditors (13-2011).[6] The governance, risk, compliance and controls analyst titles have no code at all. The nearest proxies are compliance officers (13-1041), compliance managers (11-9199.02), financial risk specialists (13-2054) and information security analysts (15-1212), and the compliance officers profile is dominated by licensing examiners rather than technology staff.[7–10] Where this article quotes pay or growth for a role, it names the code used as the nearest proxy.

Author's analysis: what makes this family different. Three differences from the earlier parts recur in the sources. First, the object of work changes from systems to decisions: SFIA defines governance as "defining and operating frameworks for decision-making, risk management, stakeholder relationships and" compliance, and portfolio management as applying a management framework to "a portfolio of programmes, projects and/or ongoing services".[2,17] Second, independence becomes a structural feature of some careers: the Three Lines Model requires internal audit to stay independent of management, which is why the IT auditor's career ladder runs separately from the risk and compliance roles even though the work looks similar.[11] Third, the family draws heavily from outside IT. The DoD's IT program auditor pathway reports that more incumbents align with the 511 Auditing series (39%) than with 2210 Information Technology (36%), and BLS notes that project management specialists may come from business analyst or training roles.[16,18] Accountants, lawyers, business analysts and engineers therefore compete with technologists for these roles.

Five points stand out. First, the leadership end of the family is the best paid and among the fastest growing. Computer and information systems managers earn a median $175,140 and are projected to grow 16%, much faster than average, with about 53,500 openings a year; BLS expects five or more years of related experience, and notes that chief technology officers and IT directors "usually need many years and extensive experience in the IT field".[3] Second, project management is a large and growing occupation in its own right. BLS counts about 1.1 million project management specialists, projects 7% growth, and attributes part of the demand to "the growing volume and complexity of information technology (IT) projects".[18] PMI's own modelling estimated in 2021 that the global economy would need 25 million new project professionals by 2030.[20] Third, the risk-adjacent occupations are growing fastest: information security analysts are projected to grow 21% and financial risk specialists by 7% or more.[9,10] Fourth, the audit route pays less at entry but is very large: accountants and auditors hold about 1.6 million jobs, with about 115,300 openings a year, and BLS says IT auditors "review controls for their organization's IT systems".[6] Fifth, the industry concentration differs by route. Computer and information systems managers are concentrated in computer systems design (19%), finance and insurance (13%), information (13%) and management of companies (10%), whereas the largest employers of accountants and auditors are accounting firms (21%), finance and insurance (8%) and government (8%).[3,6]

Education expectations are high across the family. In O*NET's surveys, 48% of computer and information systems manager respondents said a new hire needs a bachelor's degree and 14% a master's degree; 64% of project management specialist respondents and 69% of accountant and auditor respondents cited a bachelor's degree.[4,21,22] For IT project managers the answers were more varied: 38% bachelor's degree, 24% associate's degree and 19% post-baccalaureate certificate.[5] BLS adds that some organisations require or prefer a graduate degree in computer science, information technology or business administration for IT managers.[3]

Employer demand surveys point the same way. In ISACA's October 2025 poll of 2,963 digital trust professionals, 62% of organisations planned to hire for roles such as audit, risk and cybersecurity in 2026, and 44% of those expected difficulty filling them; 66% named regulatory compliance as their top focus area for 2026.[23]

4. Frameworks, standards and processes

More than any other family in the series, these roles are defined by the frameworks they apply. This section groups them by purpose. The service management practices covered in Part 1.1 (ITIL, ISO/IEC 20000) and the security baselines in Parts 1.2 and 1.3 remain relevant as the controls that governance, risk and audit roles evaluate.

4.1 Governance of enterprise IT: ISO/IEC 38500 and COBIT

ISO/IEC 38500:2024, now in its third edition, gives guiding principles for governing bodies, and those who support them, on the effective, efficient and acceptable use of IT in their organisations. It treats IT governance as a domain of organisational governance and applies to public and private companies, government entities and not-for-profit organisations of any size.[24] COBIT 2019 is the most widely used operational framework. It has 40 governance and management objectives. The governance objectives sit in a single domain, Evaluate, Direct and Monitor (EDM), and the management objectives in four: Align, Plan and Organize (APO); Build, Acquire and Implement (BAI); Deliver, Service and Support (DSS); and Monitor, Evaluate and Assess (MEA).[25,26] COBIT 2019 introduced design factors and focus areas for tailoring, and seven components of a governance system: processes; organisational structures; information flows and items; people, skills and competencies; culture, ethics and behaviour; policies and procedures; and services, infrastructure and applications.[26] COBIT is about to change: ISACA has announced COBIT 7 for November 2026, with a digital platform, three-tiered assessments and an improved design guide, and the COBIT 2019 Foundation certificate is due to be replaced by a COBIT 7 Foundation certificate on 19 November 2026.[27,28] For governance analysts and managers, the practical processes are a governance system design, a policy framework, decision rights (often recorded in a RACI chart), performance metrics and periodic capability assessments.

4.2 Risk management: ISO 31000, NIST and ERM

ISO 31000:2018 provides generic risk management guidelines applicable to any organisation.[29] For technology risk specifically, NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 sets out how to conduct risk assessments, and NIST SP 800-37 Rev. 2, the Risk Management Framework (RMF), defines seven steps: Prepare, Categorize, Select, Implement, Assess, Authorize and Monitor.[30,31] In the RMF, an authorising official formally accepts a system's security and privacy risks when granting an authorisation to operate.[31] The control catalogue most often used with the RMF is NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5.[32] NIST CSF 2.0, published in February 2024, added a sixth function, Govern, to Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover, which places cybersecurity risk strategy, roles and oversight explicitly within governance.[33] Linking IT risk to the enterprise view is the subject of NIST IR 8286 Rev. 1 (December 2025), which explains how organisations record cyber risk in risk registers and roll measures up from system to enterprise level.[34] SFIA's risk management skill (BURM) spans levels 2 to 7, from helping to collect risk data to setting the organisation's risk strategy and risk appetite.[35]

4.3 Internal control, compliance and the three lines

The COSO Internal Control—Integrated Framework, updated in May 2013, organises internal control into five components (control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information and communication, and monitoring activities) and articulates 17 principles.[36] In the IIA's Three Lines Model, first-line roles are "most directly aligned with the delivery of products and/or services to clients", second-line roles "assist with managing risk" by providing expertise, monitoring and challenge on matters such as compliance, controls and security, and internal audit provides independent assurance to the governing body.[11] ISO 37301:2021 specifies requirements for a compliance management system, and ISO/IEC 27001:2022 does the same for an information security management system, giving compliance analysts certifiable reference models.[37,38]

For listed companies in the United States, Section 404 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act requires each annual report to state management's responsibility for "establishing and maintaining an adequate internal control structure and procedures for financial reporting" and to assess their effectiveness; for larger filers the external auditor must attest to that assessment.[39] PCAOB AS 2201 governs that audit. It directs the auditor to "understand how IT affects the company's flow of transactions", states that identifying IT risks and controls "is not a separate evaluation", and names information technology general controls as a factor in assessing the risk of other controls.[40] This is the main source of demand for technology controls analysts and SOX IT auditors, whose core work is testing IT general controls over access, change management and computer operations.

4.4 Assurance: IIA Standards, ITAF and SOC reports

The IIA's Global Internal Audit Standards were released in January 2024 and took effect on 9 January 2025.[41,42] They are organised into five domains (purpose of internal auditing; ethics and professionalism; governing the internal audit function; managing the internal audit function; and performing internal audit services) and 15 principles.[43] The IIA also issues topical requirements. The first, on cybersecurity, was issued on 5 February 2025 and became effective on 5 February 2026; it sets a baseline for assessing cybersecurity governance, risk management and control processes.[44] ISACA's IT Audit Framework (ITAF), in its fourth edition since October 2020, defines the roles, ethics, conduct and required knowledge of IT audit and assurance practitioners and gives guidance on planning, performing and reporting engagements.[45] SFIA's audit skill (AUDT) is defined from level 2 to level 7: at level 3 a practitioner carries out structured fieldwork, at level 5 plans and conducts audits of complex cross-functional areas, and at level 7 directs the organisation's audit function.[46] Service organisations demonstrate their controls to customers through SOC 2 examinations against the AICPA Trust Services Criteria for security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy. On 28 October 2026 the AICPA exposed a proposed restructuring of those criteria that adds points of focus on AI governance, model monitoring, prompt manipulation and data poisoning, with comments due by 15 January 2027.[47]

4.5 Portfolio, programme and project management

ISO 21504:2022 gives guidance on the principles of project and programme portfolio management for organisations of any type or size, and ISO 21502:2020 does the same for project management.[48,49] PMI unveiled the eighth edition of its PMBOK Guide at its Global Summit in November 2025, and at the same event announced a refresh of the PMP examination, scheduled for July 2026, with more emphasis on AI, sustainability and delivering value.[50] PRINCE2 7, launched by PeopleCert in October 2023, added a stronger focus on people, made sustainability a project performance indicator, and added guidance on digital and data management.[51] For agile delivery, the 2020 Scrum Guide, published on 18 November 2020, replaced the separate development team with a single Scrum Team accountable for a valuable increment every sprint, and added the Product Goal as a commitment.[52] SFIA's portfolio management skill (POMG) is defined only at levels 5 to 7: at level 5 the portfolio manager ensures that programme, project and service leads follow portfolio and governance frameworks; at level 7 the role authorises the portfolio structure and its alignment with strategy and sets rules for prioritising resources.[17]

4.6 Technology finance and value

Portfolio managers, IT directors and CIOs need a common language for technology spending. The TBM Council's Technology Business Management taxonomy, at version 5.0.1 since July 2025, classifies technology costs in four layers (cost pools, resource towers, solutions and consumers) to support showback, chargeback, total cost of ownership and benchmarking.[53] The FinOps Foundation's 2026 framework extends cloud financial management to SaaS, data centre, data cloud and AI spending, adds an executive strategy alignment capability, and describes FinOps as working alongside IT asset management, IT financial management and enterprise architecture rather than replacing them.[54]

4.7 Public sector, AI and workforce frameworks

In U.S. federal agencies the CIO role is statutory. Under 40 U.S.C. 11315 the agency CIO advises the agency head, develops and maintains "a sound, secure, and integrated" IT architecture, monitors the performance of IT programmes and advises whether to continue, modify or terminate them, and annually assesses the knowledge and skill requirements of agency personnel for information resources management.[55] AI governance is emerging as a distinct responsibility. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, January 2023) and ISO/IEC 42001:2023, the AI management system standard, provide voluntary structures.[56,57] The EU AI Act imposes binding obligations: prohibitions and AI literacy duties applied from 2 February 2025 and general-purpose AI obligations from 2 August 2025, while the Digital Omnibus amendment, in force from 27 July 2026, postponed obligations for high-risk AI systems from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and to 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products.[58] Finally, the NICE Framework's Oversight and Governance category includes the Program Management (OG-WRL-010), Secure Project Management (OG-WRL-011), Technology Portfolio Management (OG-WRL-015) and Technology Program Auditing (OG-WRL-016) work roles, all of which include the task "conduct technology program and project audits".[12,59] DoD Manual 8140.03 links the corresponding DCWF roles to qualification requirements for defense personnel.[60]

5. Role profiles

Each profile gives a five-dimension summary, then responsibilities, skills and core competencies, tools, processes, qualifications and credentials, and career moves. "Typical" statements about level and industry are the author's synthesis of the cited sources. Each profile ends with the pay proxy used.

5.1 IT Governance Analyst

  • Domain Enterprise IT as a whole: policies, decision rights, metrics
  • Function Govern, analyze
  • Specialization COBIT, policy management, IT performance measurement
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All; most common in finance, government and large regulated firms

The governance analyst keeps the machinery of IT governance running: the policy library, the committee calendar, the metrics that tell leaders whether IT is meeting its objectives, and the evidence that governance requirements are met. Because SFIA defines its governance skill only at levels 6 and 7, the analyst's own work is better described by supporting skills such as measurement, quality assurance and risk management, applied in service of a governance framework that someone more senior owns.[1,2]

Responsibilities

  • Maintain the IT policy and standards framework and track exceptions, using COBIT's policies and procedures component as a reference.[26]
  • Collect and report performance and conformance metrics for governance bodies, in line with COBIT's Monitor, Evaluate and Assess domain.[25,26]
  • Support capability and maturity assessments of COBIT processes, each of whose activities is associated with a capability level.[26]
  • Map governance obligations to frameworks such as NIST CSF 2.0's Govern function.[33]

Skills and core competencies

  • Working knowledge of COBIT 2019 and ISO/IEC 38500.[24,25]
  • Policy writing, metrics design and stakeholder communication.
  • SFIA: MEAS, QUAS, BURM, supporting GOVN.[1]

Tools

  • GRC and policy-management platforms (O*NET lists MetricStream and Thomson Reuters Paisley for compliance managers), Microsoft SharePoint, Excel and Visio.[8]
  • Business intelligence dashboards such as Tableau or Power BI.[4,9]

Processes

  • Governance system design and tailoring with COBIT design factors; policy lifecycle; committee secretariat; performance reporting.[26]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Education: a bachelor's degree in information systems, business or a related field is the norm; see section 8.
  • COBIT 2019 Foundation (ISACA); ITIL Foundation; later CGEIT once five years of governance experience are accrued.[61,62]

Career moves

  • Up: senior governance analyst, IT governance manager, head of IT governance.
  • Across: IT risk, compliance, enterprise architecture or PMO roles.

Pay proxy: none exact. Compliance officers (13-1041) had a median of $80,730 in 2025 and information security analysts (15-1212) $129,180; analysts in technology-heavy governance teams are likely closer to the second figure, but this is the author's inference.[7,10]

5.2 IT Governance Manager

  • Domain Enterprise IT governance system
  • Function Govern
  • Specialization Governance frameworks, IT risk appetite, regulatory liaison
  • Level Management
  • Industry All; mandated in effect by board-accountability rules in finance and critical sectors

The governance manager owns the framework the analyst operates. SFIA's description of governance at level 6 fits the role closely: putting the governance framework into practice, setting governance requirements that reflect the organisation's values, ethics and risk appetite, leading independent reviews of governance practice and acting as the main contact with regulators and external stakeholders.[2] Regulation increasingly makes this work visible to boards. DORA makes the management body of a financial entity responsible for ICT risk management, NIS2 requires management bodies of essential and important entities to approve cybersecurity risk-management measures and undergo training, and the SEC requires listed companies to describe board oversight and management's role and expertise in managing cybersecurity risk.[63–65]

Responsibilities

  • Design and maintain the IT governance system, committees and decision rights.[24,25]
  • Set governance requirements reflecting risk appetite; lead governance reviews; liaise with regulators.[2]
  • Prepare board and committee reporting on IT and cyber risk, including material required for SEC Regulation S-K Item 106 disclosures.[65]
  • Coordinate AI governance policy against NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001 and, in the EU, the AI Act.[56–58]

Skills and core competencies

  • Framework design, regulatory interpretation, board-level communication and negotiation.
  • Integration of cyber risk with enterprise risk management.[34]
  • SFIA: GOVN (level 6), ITSP, BURM, MEAS.[1,2]

Tools

  • Enterprise GRC platforms, policy repositories and risk registers.[8,34]

Processes

  • Governance system design; policy approval; risk appetite setting; governance assurance cycle with internal audit.[11,25]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Bachelor's degree plus several years in IT management, risk or audit; a master's degree is common at this level.[4]
  • CGEIT: five years' experience in managing, advisory or oversight roles supporting the governance of IT, across at least three of four domains.[61]
  • CISM or CRISC where governance is security- or risk-led.[66,67]

Career moves

  • Up: head of IT governance, chief risk officer for technology, CIO or CISO office leadership.
  • Across: IT audit management, enterprise risk management.

Pay proxy: computer and information systems managers (11-3021), median $175,140 in 2025; compliance managers (11-9199.02) fall within managers, all other, median $141,900.[3,8]

5.3 IT Risk Analyst

  • Domain Technology, cyber and third-party risk
  • Function Analyze, protect
  • Specialization Risk assessment methods, risk quantification, RMF, vendor risk
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry Concentrated in finance, insurance, government and healthcare

The IT risk analyst identifies and evaluates technology risks and turns them into decisions: accept, mitigate, transfer or avoid. The analyst usually sits in a second-line risk function, providing expertise, monitoring and challenge to first-line IT teams.[11] SFIA's risk management skill at level 4, managing risk within a function or technical area by identifying and assessing risks and developing mitigations, is a good description of the established analyst.[35]

Responsibilities

  • Conduct risk assessments following NIST SP 800-30 or ISO 31000, and maintain the IT risk register.[29,30,34]
  • Analyse areas of potential risk to the organisation's assets and the impact of new legislation on risk exposure, as O*NET records for financial risk specialists.[9]
  • Support RMF categorisation, control selection and authorisation packages in government settings.[31]
  • Assess third-party and ICT supplier risk, a specific obligation under DORA for financial entities.[63]

Skills and core competencies

  • Qualitative and quantitative risk analysis; statistical tools (O*NET lists SAS, MATLAB, Python and R for financial risk specialists).[9]
  • Security control knowledge (NIST SP 800-53) and threat awareness.[32]
  • SFIA: BURM, SCTY, THIN.[1]

Tools

  • GRC risk modules, risk registers, Excel, Power BI or Tableau.[9,34]

Processes

  • Risk identification, assessment, treatment and monitoring; key risk indicators; risk acceptance and exception handling; roll-up to enterprise risk.[29,34]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Bachelor's degree in IT, information systems, finance or a quantitative field.
  • CRISC: three years' IS audit, control or security experience across at least two of four domains.[67]
  • ISC2 CGRC for RMF-focused roles; CISSP for security-heavy roles.[68,69]

Career moves

  • Up: senior risk analyst, IT risk manager, head of technology risk, chief risk officer (technology).
  • Across: IT audit, security governance, third-party risk management.

Pay proxy: information security analysts (15-1212), median $129,180 in 2025; financial risk specialists (13-2054), median $117,330.[9,10]

5.4 IT Compliance Analyst

  • Domain Technology regulation, standards and contractual obligations
  • Function Govern, analyze
  • Specialization A regime: SOX, PCI DSS, HIPAA, ISO/IEC 27001, CMMC, DORA, privacy
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry Shaped strongly by sector; see section 10

The compliance analyst translates external obligations into internal requirements, monitors whether they are met and maintains the evidence. O*NET's compliance managers occupation lists the core tasks: identifying compliance issues that need follow-up, maintaining documentation of compliance activities, conducting periodic internal reviews or audits to ensure procedures are followed, and monitoring compliance systems for effectiveness.[8] In technology the obligations come from many regimes at once. PCI DSS v4.0 introduced 64 new requirements, 51 of them future-dated to 31 March 2025, and New York's cybersecurity regulation (23 NYCRR 500) requires an annual certification of compliance signed by the highest-ranking executive and the CISO.[70,71]

Responsibilities

  • Maintain an obligations register and map obligations to controls and owners.[37]
  • Monitor compliance, run self-assessments, and coordinate external assessments and certification audits.[8,38]
  • Prepare regulatory submissions and attestations, such as the NYDFS annual certification or CMMC assessments.[71,72]
  • Deliver compliance training on policies and procedures.[8]

Skills and core competencies

  • Regulatory reading and interpretation; control mapping across frameworks; evidence management; report writing.
  • SFIA: QUAS, BURM, supporting AUDT.[1]

Tools

  • Compliance software such as MetricStream and Thomson Reuters Paisley, LexisNexis, SharePoint and Microsoft Project.[8]

Processes

5.5 IT Auditor

  • Domain Information systems, IT controls and technology governance
  • Function Analyze, govern (independent assurance)
  • Specialization SOX IT audit, cyber audit, application audit, data analytics, AI audit
  • Level Entry (graduate programmes) to senior, then audit management
  • Industry Public accounting firms, financial services, government, large corporates

The IT auditor provides independent assurance that technology controls are designed and operating effectively. BLS describes IT auditors as reviewing "controls for their organization's IT systems" to check that financial and non-financial data come from a reliable source, and O*NET records auditor tasks that include examining and evaluating financial and information systems and recommending controls, and conducting pre-implementation audits of systems under development.[6,22] The DCWF IT program auditor role "conducts evaluations of an IT program or its individual components to determine compliance with published standards".[16] Internal IT auditors work in the third line and report primarily to the governing body; external IT auditors work for audit firms on financial statement and SOC engagements.[11,47]

Responsibilities

  • Plan and perform risk-based audits of processes, systems and services; document risks and root causes; report findings on control effectiveness.[46]
  • Test IT general controls supporting financial reporting under PCAOB AS 2201.[40]
  • Review or conduct audits of IT programmes and projects; develop methods to monitor risk, compliance and assurance efforts.[16]
  • Assess cybersecurity governance, risk management and controls against the IIA Cybersecurity Topical Requirement.[44]

Skills and core competencies

  • Audit methodology, sampling, evidence evaluation and report writing (ITAF; IIA Standards).[43,45]
  • Control frameworks: COSO, COBIT, NIST SP 800-53.[25,32,36]
  • Data analytics: SQL Server, Oracle, Alteryx, Tableau, Excel and SAS appear in O*NET technology data for auditors.[22]
  • SFIA: AUDT, BURM, SCTY.[1]

Tools

  • Audit management and GRC platforms, data analytics and BI tools, and system-native reports (access listings, change logs, configuration exports).[22]

Processes

  • Annual risk-based audit planning; engagement planning, fieldwork, reporting and follow-up; quality assurance and improvement programme.[43,46]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Bachelor's degree in accounting, information systems or computer science.[6]
  • CISA: five years' IS audit, control or security experience, 120 CPE hours per three-year cycle.[73]
  • CIA (IIA); CPA for those in public accounting; AAIA for AI audit specialists.[6,74,75]

Career moves

  • Up: senior IT auditor, IT audit manager, director of IT audit, chief audit executive.
  • Across: IT risk, technology controls, compliance, security governance, consulting.

Pay proxy: accountants and auditors (13-2011), median $83,680 in 2025; this code covers all auditors and probably understates IT audit pay (author's inference).[6]

5.6 Technology Controls Analyst

  • Domain IT general and application controls
  • Function Analyze, protect
  • Specialization SOX ITGCs, control design, continuous control monitoring, SOC report review
  • Level Intermediate
  • Industry Banking, insurance and listed companies

The technology controls analyst designs, documents and tests the controls that management relies on, most often in first-line or second-line "controls office" teams in banks and listed companies. The role exists largely because SOX Section 404 makes management responsible for internal control over financial reporting, and PCAOB AS 2201 makes IT general controls a factor in how auditors judge other controls.[39,40]

Responsibilities

  • Document control objectives, risks and control activities using COSO's control activities component.[36]
  • Test design and operating effectiveness of access, change and operations controls; track deficiencies to remediation.[40]
  • Review SOC 2 reports from service organisations that support in-scope processes.[47]
  • Prepare evidence for internal and external auditors.

Skills and core competencies

  • Control design and testing, deficiency evaluation (including whether a deficiency could be a material weakness under AS 2201).[40]
  • Technical literacy in identity and access, change management and job scheduling.
  • SFIA: AUDT, BURM, QUAS, SCTY.[1]

Tools

  • GRC control libraries, ITSM change records, identity governance reports, Excel and data analytics tools.[22]

Processes

  • Risk and control self-assessment; SOX scoping and testing cycles; issue management.[36,39]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Bachelor's degree in information systems, accounting or computer science.
  • CISA or CRISC; CIA for those heading towards audit.[67,73,74]

Career moves

  • Up: senior controls analyst, technology controls lead, head of technology controls.
  • Across: IT audit, IT risk, compliance.
Author's analysis: one title, three homes. "Technology controls analyst" is used for at least three placements: a first-line role embedded in IT that owns controls; a second-line role that challenges and monitors them; and an internal-audit role that tests them independently. The Three Lines Model treats these as different responsibilities with different independence requirements.[11] Job seekers should check the reporting line, because it decides whether the work is designing controls, overseeing them or auditing them, and which credentials will count most.

Pay proxy: accountants and auditors (13-2011), median $83,680; information security analysts (15-1212), median $129,180.[6,10]

5.7 IT Portfolio Manager

  • Domain The organisation's whole set of technology investments
  • Function Govern
  • Specialization Investment appraisal, prioritisation, benefits realisation, technology finance
  • Level Senior to management
  • Industry Large enterprises and government agencies with formal investment governance

The portfolio manager decides, or prepares the decisions on, which technology initiatives the organisation funds, pauses or stops. The DCWF IT investment/portfolio manager "manages a portfolio of IT investments that align with the overall needs of mission and enterprise priorities", and the NICE Framework's Technology Portfolio Management role carries the same definition.[15,76] In federal agencies this work supports the CIO's statutory duty to monitor IT programme performance and advise whether to continue, modify or terminate programmes.[55]

Responsibilities

  • Lead portfolio planning and monitoring; check each component supports overall goals; report portfolio measures and governance compliance.[17]
  • Ensure programme, project and service leads follow portfolio and governance frameworks, and recommend portfolio changes.[17]
  • Ensure acquisitions and outsourcing address security and supply chain requirements, the core tasks in the DCWF 804 pathway.[15]
  • Allocate and report technology costs using the TBM taxonomy; work with FinOps on cloud and AI spending.[53,54]

Skills and core competencies

  • Investment appraisal, business case evaluation, benefits management, demand management and financial literacy.
  • Portfolio governance (ISO 21504).[48]
  • SFIA: POMG, INVA, BENM, DEMM.[1]

Tools

  • Strategic portfolio management modules in project tools; Oracle Primavera, Jira and Confluence appear in O*NET data for IT project managers.[5]
  • Cost transparency tooling aligned to the TBM taxonomy.[53]

Processes

  • Demand intake, scoring and prioritisation; stage-gate funding; quarterly portfolio review; benefits tracking.[17,48]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Bachelor's degree, often plus an MBA or finance qualification (author's observation).
  • PfMP: 96 months' professional business experience and 36 to 84 months' portfolio management experience depending on education; 170 questions in 240 minutes.[77]

Career moves

  • Up: head of portfolio or enterprise PMO, IT director, CIO.
  • Across: IT finance, strategy, enterprise architecture.

Pay proxy: computer and information systems managers (11-3021), median $175,140 in 2025.[3]

5.8 IT Program Manager

  • Domain Groups of related technology projects delivering a shared outcome
  • Function Govern, build
  • Specialization Transformation, ERP and cloud programmes, vendor-delivered programmes
  • Level Senior
  • Industry All; very common in consulting, government and financial services

The program manager coordinates several projects so that together they deliver benefits that none would achieve alone. The DCWF program manager "leads, coordinates, communicates, integrates, and is accountable for the overall success of the program", "ensuring alignment with agency or enterprise priorities"; its core tasks begin with developing and maintaining strategic plans and performing needs analysis for new business process solutions.[13] O*NET lists "IT Program Manager" as a reported title for IT project managers, which shows how often the two roles share a ladder.[5]

Responsibilities

  • Define the programme roadmap, governance and benefits; integrate projects and manage dependencies.[13]
  • Ensure acquisitions and outsourcing address security requirements; provide supply chain risk guidance for continuity planning.[13]
  • Manage programme risk, budget and stakeholder communication.[1]

Skills and core competencies

  • Benefits management, stakeholder and supplier management, risk management and executive communication.
  • Hybrid delivery: combining predictive governance with agile teams (PMBOK Guide; Scrum Guide).[50,52]
  • SFIA: PGMG, BENM, SUPP, BURM.[1]

Tools

  • Jira, Confluence, Microsoft Teams and SharePoint, Oracle Primavera, Excel and PowerPoint.[5]

Processes

  • Programme definition, tranche planning, benefits realisation reviews, integrated risk and issue management, programme boards.

Qualifications and credentials

  • Bachelor's degree; several years as a project manager.
  • PgMP: 36 to 48 months' project management experience (or a PMP) plus 36 to 84 months' programme management experience; panel review of experience before a 170-question, four-hour exam.[78]
  • PMP as a common baseline.[79]

Career moves

  • Up: senior programme manager, portfolio manager, head of PMO, IT director.
  • Across: transformation leadership, consulting.

Pay proxy: computer occupations, all other (15-1299), median $116,580 in 2025, the group O*NET uses for 15-1299.09; senior programme leaders may fall within 11-3021 (median $175,140).[3,5]

5.9 IT Project Manager

  • Domain Individual technology projects: implementations, upgrades, migrations
  • Function Build, govern
  • Specialization Infrastructure, application, cloud or cybersecurity projects; agile or predictive methods
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All

The IT project manager is the most common entry point into this family for technologists. O*NET describes the role as planning, initiating and managing IT projects, leading technical staff and acting as liaison between business and technical teams, and lists among its top tasks managing execution "to ensure adherence to budget, schedule, and scope", monitoring milestones and deliverables, and performing risk assessments to develop response strategies.[5] The DCWF role "directly manages information technology projects", with core tasks including advising on project costs, design concepts or design changes.[14]

Responsibilities

  • Plan scope, schedule, budget and resources; track milestones and deliverables; report status.[5]
  • Confer with project staff to identify and resolve problems; initiate, review or approve changes to project plans.[5]
  • Assess risks and develop response strategies.[5]
  • Close out projects and hand over to service operations.[18]

Skills and core competencies

  • Planning and estimation, communication, problem solving, time management and leadership; BLS lists analytical, communication, critical-thinking, interpersonal, organisational, problem-solving and time-management skills.[18]
  • Enough technical knowledge to judge estimates and risks.
  • SFIA: PRMG, PROF, BURM, COMG.[1]

Tools

  • Jira, Confluence, Oracle Primavera, Microsoft Teams, SharePoint, Slack, Wrike, Excel and Visio.[5]

Processes

  • Initiation, planning, execution, monitoring and closure (ISO 21502); sprint planning and reviews in Scrum; change requests through the IT change process described in Part 1.1.[49,52]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Bachelor's degree in business, project management or a technical field.[18]
  • Entry: CompTIA Project+ (PK0-005, up to 90 questions in 90 minutes, 6 to 12 months' experience recommended) or CAPM.[79,80]
  • Established: PMP (36 months leading projects with a four-year degree, or 60 months without, plus 35 hours of training or CAPM; 180 questions); PRINCE2 7; Scrum certifications.[51,52,79]

Career moves

  • Up: senior project manager, programme manager, PMO manager.

5.10 IT Director

  • Domain An IT function, division or major service area
  • Function Govern
  • Specialization Infrastructure and operations, applications, service delivery or a business-unit IT function
  • Level Management (senior)
  • Industry All; in smaller organisations the IT director is the most senior technology leader

The IT director runs a substantial part of the IT organisation. BLS describes the duties of computer and information systems managers as analysing IT needs and recommending upgrades, planning and directing installation and maintenance, establishing processes to detect, prevent and mitigate security threats, assessing costs and benefits of projects, determining staffing needs, leading IT staff and negotiating with and monitoring vendors.[3] O*NET's top tasks for the occupation include assigning and reviewing the work of systems analysts and programmers; developing computer information resources "providing for data security and control, strategic computing, and disaster recovery"; and developing and interpreting organisational goals, policies and procedures.[4]

Responsibilities

  • Set direction and budgets for the IT area; justify funding through cost-benefit analysis.[3]
  • Recruit, hire, train and supervise staff; determine staffing needs.[3,4]
  • Own service performance, security processes and vendor relationships.[3]
  • Present proposals to top executives; BLS notes that IT directors sometimes present new ideas to a firm's top executives.[3]

Skills and core competencies

  • Analytical, communication, computer, decision-making, leadership and problem-solving skills, as listed by BLS.[3]
  • Budgeting, supplier management, workforce planning and service management.
  • SFIA: ITMG, ITSP, BUDF, SUPP, OCDV.[1]

Tools

  • Microsoft Excel, Project and Visio, SQL Server, Tableau, Splunk Enterprise and AWS appear in O*NET technology data for the occupation.[4]
  • ITSM and portfolio platforms; cost transparency tooling.[53]

Processes

  • Annual planning and budgeting; service reviews; vendor governance; risk and audit response; workforce planning.

Qualifications and credentials

  • Bachelor's degree plus five or more years of related experience; some employers require or prefer a graduate degree in computer science, IT or business administration.[3]
  • Certification is typically optional at this level, though some employers prefer it.[3] ITIL, PMP, CGEIT or CISM are common supplements.[61,62,66,79]

Career moves

  • Up: vice president of IT, CIO, CTO.
  • Across: director of a different IT area; business operations leadership.

Pay proxy: computer and information systems managers (11-3021), median $175,140 in 2025.[3]

5.11 Chief Information Officer (CIO)

  • Domain Enterprise information and technology strategy
  • Function Govern
  • Specialization Digital transformation, AI adoption, operating model, technology investment
  • Level Executive
  • Industry All; statutory role in U.S. federal agencies

The CIO is the executive accountable for the organisation's information and technology. BLS lists CIO alongside chief technology officer and MIS director as titles within computer and information systems managers.[3] In federal agencies the role is defined in statute: the CIO advises the agency head, maintains a sound, secure and integrated IT architecture, promotes effective information resources management processes, monitors IT programme performance, and assesses whether agency staff have the IT knowledge and skills required.[55] In listed companies, SEC rules require disclosure of management's role and expertise in assessing and managing material cybersecurity risks, which brings the CIO's and CISO's qualifications into public filings.[65] SFIA's level 7, "set strategy, inspire, mobilise", describes the scope: directing governance, setting risk appetite and authorising the portfolio's alignment with strategy.[2,17,35]

Responsibilities

  • Set technology strategy and architecture aligned with business strategy.[55]
  • Direct the technology portfolio and budget; decide on continuing, modifying or terminating programmes.[17,55]
  • Account to the board for technology and cyber risk, alongside the CISO.[63–65]
  • Lead workforce strategy, including assessing skill requirements and closing gaps through hiring and training.[55]
  • Lead AI adoption and governance, in line with obligations such as the EU AI Act's AI literacy duty.[58]

Skills and core competencies

  • Strategy, financial management, executive influence, organisational change and talent leadership.
  • Enough technical depth to judge architecture, security and AI risk.
  • SFIA (level 7): ITSP, GOVN, STPL, INVA, CIPM.[1]

Tools

  • Board reporting and enterprise dashboards; TBM and FinOps reporting for technology spend.[53,54]

Processes

  • Strategic planning; investment governance; enterprise risk reporting; architecture governance; regulatory and disclosure processes.[34,55,65]

Qualifications and credentials

  • No licence or mandatory credential. A bachelor's degree is typical and graduate degrees in computer science, IT or business administration are often preferred.[3]
  • Executive education and governance credentials such as CGEIT are more common than technical certifications at this level.[61]

Career moves

  • Across: chief digital officer, chief technology officer, chief operating officer.
  • Beyond: board and advisory roles.

Pay proxy: computer and information systems managers (11-3021), median $175,140 in 2025; BLS median pay is unlikely to reflect executive compensation at large organisations (author's inference).[3]

The labour data support the direction of travel. BLS notes that experienced public accountants may move into executive roles in internal auditing, that information security analysts can advance to chief security officer or other IT management roles, and that project management specialists typically progress from small to large, complex projects.[6,10,18] Median pay rises from $83,680 for accountants and auditors and $102,320 for project management specialists to $129,180 for information security analysts and $175,140 for computer and information systems managers.[3,6,10,18]

Author's analysis: three entry doors and a crossing point. The family has three entry doors. The accounting door leads from a business or accounting degree, often through a public accounting firm's IT audit practice, and is credential-driven (CPA, CIA, CISA). The technology door leads from support, administration or security roles (Parts 1.1 to 1.3) into risk, controls or project management. The business door leads from business analysis or operations into project and portfolio management, a route BLS describes for project management specialists.[18] The tracks meet at the management level: IT directors and CIOs need to understand delivery, risk and assurance, and the ability to move between first, second and third line roles is valuable for people aiming at the top of the family. A deliberate move across lines, for example from IT audit into IT risk or from project management into portfolio management, is a common way to build that breadth.

11. Trends shaping the field

  • AI governance becomes a job requirement. The EU AI Act's AI literacy duties have applied since February 2025, and its high-risk obligations, postponed by the Digital Omnibus, now apply from December 2027.[58] ISO/IEC 42001 and the NIST AI RMF give organisations management-system and risk frameworks for AI.[56,57] The assurance profession is following: ISACA launched the AAIA in 2025, and the AICPA's proposed SOC 2 revisions add AI governance and model-monitoring points of focus.[47,75] Yet in ISACA's 2025 poll only 13% of professionals said their organisation was very prepared for generative AI risks.[23]
  • Cyber and digital disruption dominate the audit agenda. In the IIA's 2026 Risk in Focus survey of 3,285 chief audit executives in 132 countries, 80% ranked cybersecurity among their top five risks, and digital disruption including AI rose 10 points to 58%.[86] The IIA's Cybersecurity Topical Requirement, effective since February 2026, makes cyber assessment a defined part of internal audit's work.[44]
  • Governance moves into the boardroom. NIST CSF 2.0's Govern function, the SEC's disclosure rules, DORA and NIS2 all make senior leaders and boards explicitly accountable for technology and cyber risk.[33,63–65] In the author's view, this is raising demand for governance managers who can write for boards, not only for technical teams.
  • New editions of the core frameworks. The IIA's Global Internal Audit Standards took effect in January 2025, PMI unveiled the PMBOK Guide's eighth edition in November 2025 and scheduled a PMP exam refresh for July 2026, ITIL (Version 5) is being released in phases during 2026, and ISACA has announced COBIT 7 for November 2026.[27,41,50,62] Practitioners will spend the next two years re-certifying and re-mapping their frameworks.
  • Technology value management converges. The TBM taxonomy's 5.0 release and the FinOps Foundation's 2026 framework, which now covers SaaS, data centre and AI spending and adds an executive strategy capability, are drawing portfolio management, IT finance and cloud cost management closer together.[53,54]
  • Continuing demand for project talent. BLS projects 7% growth for project management specialists and links part of the demand to the volume and complexity of IT projects; PMI estimated a need for 25 million new project professionals worldwide by 2030.[18,20]
  • Hiring pressure in audit and risk. Among organisations planning to hire for audit, risk and cybersecurity roles in 2026, 44% expected difficulty filling them, and regulatory compliance was the most-cited organisational focus area.[23]

12. Limitations

Labour statistics are U.S.-specific and occupation-based, so they approximate rather than measure the titles in this family. Several proxies are poor fits: the compliance officers code is dominated by licensing examiners, the accountants and auditors code covers all auditors, and the 15-1299 figures used for IT project managers describe a much broader group.[5–7] BLS median pay is unlikely to reflect executive compensation for CIOs at large organisations. Survey results from ISACA and the IIA reflect their respondents and are not population estimates.[23,86] Credential and framework details change frequently. This article reflects issuer pages as of 3 November 2026, including the transition to COBIT 7, an updated CISM exam outline taking effect on that date, the PMP refresh and the rollout of ITIL (Version 5).[27,50,62,66] Several standards (ISO/IEC 38500, ISO 31000, ISO 37301, ISO/IEC 27001, ISO/IEC 42001, ISO 21502, ISO 21504, COBIT 2019 and the PMBOK Guide) are paywalled and are described here from publishers' summaries. The structure of the IIA Standards is reported from the IIA's own page, while the release date is corroborated by a KPMG summary.[42,43] SFIA level mappings, cluster groupings, NICE work role assignments, career paths, framework crosswalks and the boxes marked Author's analysis are the author's synthesis and are labelled as such.
