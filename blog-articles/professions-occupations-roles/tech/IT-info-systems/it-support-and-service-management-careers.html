1. Scope and method

This article covers the IT Support and Service Management family of roles, the first subgroup of Enterprise IT and Information Systems in the series taxonomy. These are the people who answer users, restore service when it fails, control change, keep the inventory of technology assets and configurations accurate, and run the processes and platforms that make IT accountable to the organisation it serves.

Each role is described along the series' five independent dimensions:

DimensionQuestion it answersHow it applies to this family
Technical domainWhich body of technology?Enterprise IT: end-user computing, infrastructure services, business applications and the service-management platforms that coordinate them.
Work functionWhat kind of work?Mostly operate (support, restore, fulfil), with govern (change, asset, configuration and service-level control), analyze (process and operations analysis) and some build (configuring ITSM platforms).
SpecializationWhich narrow expertise?For example, endpoint management, Windows and macOS, a specific ITSM platform, software licensing, major-incident command or change risk.
Organizational levelHow much responsibility?Entry through management. Several roles in this family are defined by seniority (a "manager" title often denotes process ownership rather than line management).
IndustryWhich sector context?Every sector employs these roles, but regulation in finance, healthcare, government and defense, and life-sciences manufacturing changes the shape of the work.

Method. Facts about employment, pay and education come from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Occupational Outlook Handbook and the O*NET database. Process definitions come from ITIL 4 and ITIL (Version 5), ISO/IEC 20000-1, ISO/IEC 19770-1 and COBIT 2019. Skill taxonomies come from SFIA 9 and the NICE Framework. Credential details come from the issuing bodies, and tool descriptions from vendor documentation. Statements that are the author's analytical synthesis, rather than facts reported by a source, are labelled as such (for example, "indicative" level mappings).

A caution on job titles. Government occupational classifications do not have a separate code for most titles in this family. BLS and O*NET group help desk, desktop support and IT support titles under computer user support specialists (15-1232), and process-owner and service-manager titles are distributed across systems analyst, project manager and IT manager codes.[1–4] Where this article quotes pay or growth for a role, it names the occupational code used as the nearest proxy.

Three points stand out. First, the frontline support occupation is large but projected to shrink slightly: BLS projects a 3% decline for computer user support specialists over 2025–35, yet still expects about 48,700 openings a year across computer support specialists, all of them from replacement of workers who change occupations or leave the labour force.[7] Entry-level hiring therefore continues even in a flat market. Second, the management end of the family sits inside an occupation projected to grow 16%, much faster than average, but BLS notes that it typically requires five or more years of experience in a related occupation.[10] Third, education requirements rise with level. BLS says user support specialists typically need some college coursework and network support specialists an associate's degree, while candidates for either may qualify with a high school diploma plus relevant IT certifications.[7] O*NET places user support in Job Zone Three (medium preparation) and network support, systems analysts and IT managers in Job Zone Four (considerable preparation, usually a bachelor's degree).[1–3,6]

4. Frameworks, standards and processes

Most job descriptions in this family are written in the vocabulary of a small number of frameworks. Understanding them is a core competency in its own right.

4.1 ITIL 4 and ITIL (Version 5)

ITIL 4, published in 2019, describes a service value system and 34 management practices, grouped into general management, service management and technical management practices.[11,12] The practices most relevant to this family include service desk, incident management, problem management, change enablement, service configuration management, IT asset management, service level management, service request management, monitoring and event management, knowledge management and continual improvement.[11]

PeopleCert, which owns ITIL, began a phased release of ITIL (Version 5) in 2026. PeopleCert describes it as covering an end-to-end lifecycle for digital products and services, as "AI-native" in design, and as having a simpler qualification structure.[13] The new Foundation introduces an eight-stage product and service lifecycle (Discover, Design, Acquire, Build, Transition, Operate, Deliver, Support), keeps ITIL 4's guiding principles, value system, practices and continual improvement, and gives clearer definitions of practices such as incident, problem and change management.[12] PeopleCert states that existing ITIL 4 certifications keep their value and that the ITIL 4 path remains available.[13] For job seekers in late 2026, ITIL 4 Foundation and ITIL Foundation (Version 5) are both in circulation; training providers such as HDI list both.[14]

4.2 ISO/IEC 20000-1

ISO/IEC 20000-1:2018 is the certifiable international standard for a service management system. It sets requirements for planning, designing, transitioning, delivering and improving services.[15] Its operational clauses cover, among others, service catalogue, asset and configuration management (8.2), service level and supplier management (8.3), change management and release and deployment management (8.5), incident, service request and problem management (8.6), and availability, continuity and information security management (8.7).[15] An amendment on climate action was issued in 2024.[15] In organisations certified to ISO/IEC 20000-1, process managers in this family are often the named owners of the evidence an auditor will examine.

4.3 ISO/IEC 19770 for IT asset management

ISO/IEC 19770-1:2017 specifies requirements for an IT asset management system, following the common ISO management-system structure.[16] ISO/IEC TS 19770-10:2025 adds implementation guidance.[17]

4.4 COBIT 2019

COBIT 2019, from ISACA, is a governance framework. Its management objectives map closely onto this family: APO09 Managed Service Agreements, BAI06 Managed IT Changes, BAI09 Managed Assets, BAI10 Managed Configuration, DSS02 Managed Service Requests and Incidents and DSS03 Managed Problems.[18] Auditors and risk functions often use COBIT, so senior process owners benefit from being able to translate between ITIL practice language and COBIT control language.

4.5 Knowledge-Centered Service (KCS)

KCS is a methodology for capturing and improving knowledge as a by-product of solving issues, maintained by the Consortium for Service Innovation. It is organised around a Solve Loop and an Evolve Loop, with defined roles, measures and content standards.[19,20] KCS is widely referenced in service desk and technical support roles.

4.6 Site reliability engineering and DevOps evidence

Two bodies of work outside ITIL strongly influence modern incident and change practice. Google's Site Reliability Engineering describes structured incident command and a blameless postmortem culture.[21] Vendor practice documentation such as PagerDuty's public incident response guide sets out incident roles (incident commander, scribe, subject matter expert) and severity levels.[22] On change control, Forsgren, Humble and Kim's research for Accelerate found that approval of changes by an external body, such as a change advisory board, was associated with lower software delivery performance and was not associated with lower change failure rates. They recommended lightweight, peer-review-based approval instead.[23] ITIL 4's renaming of "change management" to "change enablement", and its emphasis on delegating change authority, reflect the same direction.[11]

4.7 Security frameworks that touch service management

Service management and security overlap at incidents, configurations and inventories. NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 (April 2025) places incident response within cybersecurity risk management, aligned to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, and supersedes Rev. 2.[24] NIST SP 800-128 covers security-focused configuration management.[25] NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 contains the controls auditors cite for baseline configuration (CM-2), configuration change control (CM-3), system component inventory (CM-8) and incident handling (IR-4).[26] The first two CIS Critical Security Controls are inventory and control of enterprise assets and of software assets.[27] This makes asset and configuration management a security discipline as much as a service-management one.

5. Role profiles

Each profile gives a five-dimension summary, then responsibilities, skills and core competencies, tools, processes, qualifications and credentials, and career moves. "Typical" statements about level and industry are the author's synthesis of the cited sources.

5.1 Help Desk Technician

  • Domain Enterprise IT, end-user computing
  • Function Operate
  • Specialization First-contact troubleshooting, accounts and access
  • Level Entry
  • Industry All sectors; largest employers are IT services and education[7]

The help desk technician is the first point of contact for users with a technology problem. BLS uses "help-desk technicians" as an alternative name for computer user support specialists, who diagnose problems and guide non-IT users through solutions.[7] O*NET lists "Help Desk Technician" as a reported title for occupation 15-1232.[1]

Responsibilities

  • Answer user inquiries about hardware and software and resolve them, by phone, chat, portal or in person.[1]
  • Investigate problems by reading technical manuals, conferring with users or running diagnostics.[1]
  • Log, categorise and prioritise every contact as an incident or service request, and escalate according to defined procedures.[11,28]
  • Handle password resets, account unlocks and access requests within delegated authority.[29]
  • Search and contribute to the knowledge base.[19]

Skills and core competencies

  • Structured troubleshooting across PC hardware, operating systems, networking basics and common business applications.[30,31]
  • Customer communication: active listening, empathy and managing difficult conversations.[28]
  • Accurate ticket documentation and prioritisation by impact and urgency.[11]
  • SFIA: Customer service support (CSMG) and Incident management (USUP) at the lower levels of responsibility.[5]

Tools

  • ITSM ticketing platforms. O*NET lists ServiceNow and Atlassian JIRA as hot technologies for this occupation.[32]
  • Directory services (Microsoft Active Directory), remote-control software and operating systems including Windows, iOS and Android.[32]

Processes

  • Service desk, incident management, service request management and knowledge management.[11]
  • KCS Solve Loop (capture, structure, reuse, improve).[19]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Education: BLS says user support specialists typically need some college coursework, and a high school diploma plus certifications can qualify.[7]
  • CompTIA A+ (Core 1, 220-1201, and Core 2, 220-1202; V15 launched March 2025; CompTIA recommends 12 months' hands-on experience).[30,31]
  • HDI Customer Service Representative or HDI Support Center Analyst; ITIL 4 Foundation or ITIL Foundation (Version 5).[14]

Career moves

  • Up: service desk analyst (senior), desktop support, team lead.
  • Across: towards systems administration, networking or security, often by adding CompTIA Network+ or Security+.[33,34]

Pay proxy: computer user support specialists, median $61,860 (May 2025).[7]

5.2 IT Support Specialist

  • Domain Enterprise IT, end-user and small-site infrastructure
  • Function Operate
  • Specialization Generalist support, often with light administration
  • Level Entry to intermediate
  • Industry All; prominent in small and mid-sized organisations and schools

"IT Support Specialist" is a reported O*NET title for computer user support specialists.[1] Compared with a help desk technician, the title usually implies broader scope: setup, installation, minor repairs and some system oversight, as well as fielding requests. The DoD's equivalent work role, Technical Support Specialist (DCWF code 411), "provides technical support to customers who need assistance utilizing client-level hardware and software".[29]

Responsibilities

  • Set up equipment for employees, including cabling, operating systems and software.[1]
  • Install and perform minor repairs to hardware, software and peripherals.[1]
  • Oversee the daily performance of computer systems.[1]
  • Manage user accounts, network rights and system access. Monitor and report client-level system performance.[29]
  • Develop and deliver technical training to users.[29]
  • Confer with staff, users and management on requirements for new systems or changes.[1]

Skills and core competencies

  • Hardware, networking, mobile devices, virtualisation and cloud basics (A+ Core 1 domains). Operating systems, security, software troubleshooting and operational procedures (A+ Core 2 domains).[30,31]
  • Scripting for routine tasks (PowerShell is an O*NET hot technology for the occupation).[32]
  • SFIA: CSMG, USUP, System software administration (SYSP).[5]

Tools

  • Ticketing (ServiceNow, Jira Service Management), Active Directory, Windows Server, endpoint management such as Microsoft Intune.[32,35,36]
  • Network diagnostics such as Wireshark and Nagios.[32]

Processes

  • Incident, request, access and basic change processes.[11]
  • Asset tagging and record updates at install and disposal.[16]

Qualifications and credentials

  • CompTIA A+, then Network+ (N10-009, launched June 2024).[30,33]
  • Microsoft 365 Certified: Endpoint Administrator Associate (exam MD-102) for Microsoft-centric environments.[37]
  • ITIL Foundation; HDI Support Center Analyst.[14]

Career moves

  • Up: senior support, team lead, IT support manager.
  • Across: systems administrator, network support specialist (higher median pay, $76,220).[7]

5.3 Desktop Support Technician

  • Domain Enterprise IT, end-user computing
  • Function Operate
  • Specialization Endpoint hardware, imaging and deployment, peripherals, deskside service
  • Level Entry to intermediate (second line)
  • Industry All; campus-heavy sectors such as healthcare, education and government

Desktop support technicians resolve problems that cannot be fixed remotely or that need hands-on work with devices. "Desktop Support Technician" is a reported O*NET title for 15-1232,[1] and HDI runs a specific Desktop Advanced Support Technician course and certification.[14]

Responsibilities

  • Install, configure and repair workstations, laptops, printers and peripherals to organisational standards.[1,29]
  • Deploy and refresh devices, apply patches and manage device compliance with endpoint management tools.[36,37]
  • Take escalations from the service desk and fulfil deskside requests.[11]
  • Keep asset records accurate when devices are moved, reassigned or retired.[16,27]

Skills and core competencies

  • Hardware and network troubleshooting (the largest A+ Core 1 domain, 28%).[30]
  • Operating system and security configuration (A+ Core 2: 28% each).[31]
  • Device lifecycle management: preparing infrastructure for devices, managing, protecting and automating endpoints (MD-102 skill areas).[37]
  • SFIA: CSMG, USUP, Asset management (ASMG).[5]

Tools

  • Endpoint management (Microsoft Intune), configuration tools such as patch management software, remote-control software.[32,36]
  • ITSM platform with asset or CMDB records.[35,38]

Processes

  • Request fulfilment, incident escalation, standard changes for routine device work, asset lifecycle.[11,15]

Qualifications and credentials

  • CompTIA A+; Microsoft MD-102; HDI Desktop Advanced Support Technician.[14,30,37]
  • IAITAM Certified Hardware Asset Management Professional (CHAMP) for those moving towards asset work.[39]

Career moves

  • Up: senior desktop or end-user computing engineer, desktop support manager (HDI offers a Desktop Support Manager certification).[14]
  • Across: IT asset management, endpoint or Microsoft 365 administration.

5.4 Technical Support Engineer

  • Domain Enterprise IT or vendor products (software, SaaS, infrastructure)
  • Function Operate, analyze
  • Specialization A specific product or platform; complex, often third-line, troubleshooting
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry Concentrated in software vendors and IT services; also internal enterprise teams

Technical support engineers handle escalated technical cases, often for a single product, either inside an enterprise or at a vendor supporting customers. O*NET's 15-1232 includes "Technical Support Specialist" among reported titles,[1] while network-heavy versions overlap with 15-1231, whose tasks include identifying causes of networking problems using diagnostic software and documenting support activities.[6] The title's "engineer" usually signals that the role reproduces defects, reads logs and code, and works with engineering on fixes. This is the author's interpretation of common usage, not a classification.

Responsibilities

  • Diagnose and resolve escalated incidents, problems and events.[29]
  • Reproduce issues, analyse logs and network traces, and identify root causes.[6]
  • Raise and track known errors and defects with engineering, and publish workarounds.[11]
  • Write and improve knowledge articles as part of the KCS Evolve Loop.[19]
  • Act as subject matter expert during major incidents.[22]

Skills and core competencies

  • Deep product knowledge plus networking, operating systems, and scripting.[6,32]
  • Analytical problem solving and written communication with both customers and engineers.[14]
  • SFIA: USUP, Problem management (PBMG), Infrastructure operations (ITOP).[5]

Tools

  • Case management (ServiceNow, Jira Service Management), log and monitoring tools, packet capture (Wireshark), remote access.[6,32,35]

Processes

  • Incident and problem management, knowledge management, and swarming models such as Intelligent Swarming (for which the Consortium for Service Innovation offers a fundamentals course).[11,20]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Often an associate's or bachelor's degree in a computing field (BLS: associate's typical for network support).[7]
  • Vendor product certifications; CompTIA Network+ or Security+; KCS v6 Practices; HDI Troubleshooting and Problem-Solving.[14,20,33,34]

Career moves

  • Up: senior or principal support engineer, escalation manager, support engineering manager.
  • Across: site reliability engineering, solutions engineering, QA, or software engineering.

5.5 IT Operations Analyst

  • Domain Enterprise IT infrastructure and operations
  • Function Operate, analyze
  • Specialization Monitoring, event management, job scheduling, availability and capacity analysis
  • Level Intermediate
  • Industry Large, always-on enterprises such as banking, telecoms and government

IT operations analysts watch the health of systems and services, act on events and alerts, and analyse operational data. The role has no dedicated SOC code. Its tasks overlap with computer systems analysts (troubleshooting program and system problems to restore normal operation; testing, maintaining and monitoring systems)[3] and with network support specialists (backing up data, diagnosing network problems).[6]

Responsibilities

  • Monitor infrastructure and applications, triage events, and open incidents.[11]
  • Run and verify batch jobs, backups and routine operational procedures.[6]
  • Produce availability, capacity and performance reports.[15]
  • Carry out standard changes and runbook automation.[11]
  • Support continuity and recovery testing.[15]

Skills and core competencies

  • Operating systems (Windows Server, Linux), networking, and monitoring concepts.[6]
  • Data analysis and reporting; scripting and automation.
  • Understanding of service level objectives and error budgets from SRE.[21]
  • SFIA: ITOP, Availability management (AVMT), Capacity management (CPMG), Measurement (MEAS).[5]

Tools

  • Monitoring and network management tools (O*NET lists SolarWinds, Nagios and HP Network Node Manager among network-support technologies), backup software such as Veritas NetBackup, virtualisation (VMware).[6,32]
  • ITSM event and incident modules.[35,38]

Processes

  • Monitoring and event management, incident, availability, capacity and performance management, service continuity.[11,15]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Associate's or bachelor's degree is common (Job Zone Four for systems analysts).[3]
  • CompTIA Network+; ITIL Foundation; cloud or monitoring vendor certifications.[11,33]

Career moves

  • Up: senior operations analyst, operations or NOC manager, availability or capacity manager.
  • Across: systems administration, SRE, observability engineering, incident management.

5.6 IT Service Desk Analyst

  • Domain Enterprise IT
  • Function Operate
  • Specialization First-line resolution, request fulfilment, knowledge capture
  • Level Entry
  • Industry All; very common in outsourced service providers

The service desk analyst is functionally close to the help desk technician. The difference is mainly one of framing: "service desk" is the ITIL practice name, and implies a single point of contact for all incidents and service requests, not only break-fix work.[11] "Help Desk Analyst" is a reported O*NET title for 15-1232.[1] HDI's Support Center Analyst certification targets this role and covers incident management from detection to closure, request fulfilment, access management, knowledge management, contact handling, customer management and metrics.[28]

Responsibilities

  • Log, classify, prioritise and resolve contacts at first line, within agreed service levels.[11,28]
  • Fulfil standard service requests from the service catalogue.[11]
  • Escalate functionally and hierarchically, and keep users informed.[28]
  • Capture and reuse knowledge in the workflow.[19]

Skills and core competencies

  • Communication, including active listening and cross-cultural communication; emotional intelligence and empathy.[28]
  • Time and stress management, and working to metrics and quality assurance.[28]
  • SFIA: CSMG, USUP, Knowledge management (KNOW).[5]

Tools

  • ITSM platform with portal, knowledge base, virtual agent and telephony or chat.[32,35]

Processes

  • Service desk, incident, request, access and knowledge practices.[11]

Qualifications and credentials

  • HDI Support Center Analyst: 65 multiple-choice questions in 75 minutes, 80% pass mark; HDI states its certifications do not expire.[28]
  • ITIL Foundation; CompTIA A+; KCS v6 Fundamentals.[14,20,30]

Career moves

  • Up: senior analyst, team lead (HDI Support Center Team Lead), service desk manager (HDI Support Center Manager).[14]
  • Across: incident manager, knowledge manager, ITSM administrator.

5.7 IT Service Manager

  • Domain Enterprise IT
  • Function Govern
  • Specialization Service management system ownership, service portfolio and performance
  • Level Senior to management
  • Industry All; mandatory function in ISO/IEC 20000-1-certified providers

The IT service manager owns how IT services are designed, delivered, measured and improved, and is often accountable for the whole service management system. O*NET's computer and information systems managers occupation lists "Technical Services Manager" and "IT Manager" among its titles, with tasks such as directing daily operations, setting priorities and standards, and resolving problems with department heads and vendors.[2]

Responsibilities

  • Establish and maintain the service management system, policies and process ownership.[15]
  • Own the service catalogue and service level framework, and report service performance to the business.[11,15]
  • Direct daily operations, set priorities and standards, and manage budgets and staff.[2]
  • Manage suppliers that deliver parts of the service.[15]
  • Lead continual improvement and prepare for audits.[15,18]

Skills and core competencies

  • Leadership, financial and supplier management, stakeholder communication.[10]
  • Working command of ITIL, ISO/IEC 20000 and COBIT.[11,15,18]
  • SFIA: Technology service management (ITMG), Service level management (SLMO), Supplier management (SUPP) at the upper levels of responsibility.[5]

Tools

  • ITSM platform reporting and dashboards, CMDB and service maps, spreadsheet and BI tools.[38]

Processes

  • All of the practices in the crosswalk in section 4.8, with emphasis on service level, supplier and continual improvement.[11]

Qualifications and credentials

  • BLS: a bachelor's degree, usually in computer science, IT or a related field, plus five or more years of related experience; some employers prefer a graduate degree.[10]
  • Advanced ITIL designations (ITIL 4 Managing Professional or Strategic Leader, or ITIL Version 5 advanced modules); ISO/IEC 20000 practitioner or auditor training; HDI Support Center Manager or Director.[11,13,14]

Career moves

  • Up: head of service management, director of IT operations, CIO.
  • Pay proxy: computer and information systems managers, median $175,140 (May 2025).[10]

5.8 IT Asset Manager

  • Domain Enterprise IT (hardware, software, cloud and SaaS assets)
  • Function Govern
  • Specialization Software asset management (licensing), hardware asset management, disposition
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All; acute where software spend and audit exposure are high

IT asset managers run the lifecycle of technology assets from request and procurement through deployment, maintenance and disposal, balancing cost, compliance and risk. The discipline has its own management-system standard, ISO/IEC 19770-1.[16] Because an accurate asset inventory is the first CIS Critical Security Control, the role also supports security.[27]

Responsibilities

  • Establish and run the IT asset management system, policies and processes.[16,17]
  • Maintain the hardware and software inventory and reconcile it against discovery data.[26,27]
  • Manage software licence entitlements and compliance, and support vendor audits.
  • Optimise spend through harvesting, renewal management and contract input.[18]
  • Govern secure disposition of retired assets.[39]

Skills and core competencies

  • Licensing models, contract and commercial literacy, financial analysis.
  • Data quality management and reconciliation.
  • SFIA: Asset management (ASMG), Supplier management (SUPP).[5]

Tools

  • ITSM platform asset modules (ServiceNow, Jira Service Management Assets), discovery and endpoint management (Intune).[35,36,38]

Processes

  • IT asset management, supplier management, financial management, and links to configuration and change.[11,15]
  • COBIT BAI09 Managed Assets.[18]

Qualifications and credentials

  • IAITAM: Certified IT Asset Manager (CITAM), Certified Asset Management Professional (CAMP), Certified Software Asset Manager (CSAM), CHAMP, Certified IT Asset Disposition (CITAD), and newer certifications for mobile, security and AI asset management.[39]
  • ITIL Foundation; a degree in business, finance or IT is common.

Career moves

  • Up: ITAM or SAM lead, head of IT asset management, IT procurement or vendor management leader.
  • Across: FinOps or cloud cost management, IT finance, configuration management.

5.9 IT Configuration Manager

  • Domain Enterprise IT
  • Function Govern
  • Specialization CMDB design and data quality, service mapping, security baselines
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All; especially regulated sectors that must evidence baselines

The configuration manager makes sure that accurate information about services and the configuration items (CIs) that support them is available when needed. This is the purpose ITIL 4 gives the service configuration management practice.[11] ISO/IEC 20000-1 requires configuration information to be recorded and controlled.[15] In vendor platforms this centres on the configuration management database (CMDB). ServiceNow describes its Common Service Data Model as a standardised framework that defines service-related terms and relationships, and treats the CMDB as the best practice for modelling it.[38]

Responsibilities

  • Define the configuration model, CI classes, relationships and data ownership.[11,38]
  • Run discovery, reconciliation and data-quality audits.[15]
  • Maintain baseline configurations and support configuration change control.[25,26]
  • Provide impact analysis to change and incident management.[11]

Skills and core competencies

  • Data modelling and data governance; infrastructure and application architecture literacy.
  • Security-focused configuration management concepts.[25]
  • SFIA: Configuration management (CFMG), Change control (CHMG).[5]

Tools

  • CMDB and discovery tools; configuration automation tools such as Chef and Puppet (O*NET hot technologies).[32,38]

Processes

  • Service configuration management, change enablement, release and deployment.[11,15]
  • COBIT BAI10 Managed Configuration; NIST CM-2, CM-3 and CM-8.[18,26]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Bachelor's degree is common (systems analyst proxy is Job Zone Four).[3]
  • ITIL Foundation and practice-level modules; ServiceNow CSA and implementation-specialist credentials where ServiceNow is used.[11,40,41]

Career moves

  • Up: CMDB architect, head of configuration and asset management.
  • Across: ITSM platform architecture, enterprise architecture, security compliance.
  • Pay proxy: computer systems analysts, median $105,850 (May 2025).[8]

5.10 Incident Manager

  • Domain Enterprise IT
  • Function Govern and operate
  • Specialization Major-incident command, communications, post-incident review
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All; heightened in finance (regulatory incident reporting) and healthcare

Incident managers own the process for restoring normal service as quickly as possible and minimising impact, and personally command major incidents. ITIL 4 defines incident management in those terms.[11] ISO/IEC 20000-1 sets requirements for incident management, including handling major incidents.[15] Modern practice borrows the incident command model: PagerDuty's documentation defines incident commander, scribe and subject matter expert roles and severity levels,[22] and Google's SRE practice emphasises clear command roles and blameless postmortems.[21]

Responsibilities

  • Own the incident process, priority model and escalation paths.[11,15]
  • Command major incidents: assemble responders, coordinate technical work, manage stakeholder communications.[21,22]
  • Run post-incident reviews and hand off to problem management.[11,21]
  • Coordinate with security incident response where incidents have a security cause.[24]
  • In regulated firms, support regulatory classification and reporting of major ICT incidents (for example, under the EU DORA).[42]

Skills and core competencies

  • Calm decision-making under pressure, facilitation and concise communication.
  • Broad technical literacy across infrastructure and applications.
  • SFIA: Incident management (USUP) at higher levels; Continuity management (COPL).[5]

Tools

  • ITSM incident and major-incident modules, on-call and alerting platforms, status pages, collaboration tools.[22,35]

Processes

  • Incident management, problem management, service continuity, and security incident response.[11,15,24]
  • COBIT DSS02.[18]

Qualifications and credentials

  • ITIL Foundation and practice-level training; HDI and KCS credentials for service-desk-originated managers.[11,14]
  • Experience in a support or operations role is typically the main qualification.

Career moves

  • Up: major incident lead, head of service operations, IT service manager.
  • Across: SRE, business continuity, security incident response.

5.11 Problem Manager

  • Domain Enterprise IT
  • Function Govern and analyze
  • Specialization Root-cause analysis, known-error management, trend analysis
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All; especially large estates with recurring incident volume

Problem managers reduce the likelihood and impact of incidents by identifying their causes and managing workarounds and known errors. That is the purpose ITIL 4 gives the practice.[11] ISO/IEC 20000-1 requires problems to be identified, analysed and resolved, and known errors recorded.[15] HDI offers a dedicated Problem Management Principles certification.[14]

Responsibilities

  • Identify problems through trend analysis of incidents and through major-incident reviews.[11]
  • Facilitate root-cause analysis and track corrective actions to closure via change.[11,15]
  • Maintain the known-error database and publish workarounds to the service desk.[11,19]
  • Report on problem backlog, recurrence and avoided incidents.[18]

Skills and core competencies

  • Analytical methods (for example, timeline analysis, fault-tree and "five whys" style techniques), data analysis.
  • Facilitation across technical teams without line authority.
  • SFIA: Problem management (PBMG), Knowledge management (KNOW).[5]

Tools

  • ITSM problem modules, analytics and BI tools, knowledge base.[35]

Processes

  • Problem, incident, change and knowledge management; postmortem practice.[11,21]
  • COBIT DSS03 Managed Problems.[18]

Qualifications and credentials

  • HDI Problem Management Principles; ITIL Foundation and practice modules; KCS v6 Practices.[14,20]

Career moves

  • Up: head of problem management, service operations manager, IT service manager.
  • Across: reliability engineering, quality management, continual improvement lead.

5.12 Change Manager

  • Domain Enterprise IT
  • Function Govern
  • Specialization Change risk assessment, change authority design, release coordination
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All; heavily scrutinised in finance, healthcare and life sciences

Change managers maximise the number of successful changes by making sure risks are assessed, changes are authorised and the change schedule is managed. This is how ITIL 4 frames change enablement, which distinguishes standard, normal and emergency changes.[11] ISO/IEC 20000-1 requires a change management policy and controlled assessment, approval and review of changes.[15] The role is changing. Accelerate's evidence that external approval bodies did not improve change stability, while slowing delivery, has pushed organisations towards automated risk assessment, peer review and pre-approved standard changes.[23]

Responsibilities

  • Own the change policy, change models and change authorities, including any change advisory board.[11,15]
  • Assess risk and impact using configuration data, and maintain the change schedule.[11,26]
  • Review failed and emergency changes and drive improvement.[15]
  • Provide audit evidence of change control (for example, for revision and change control in FDA-regulated computerised systems).[43,44]

Skills and core competencies

  • Risk assessment, negotiation and stakeholder management.
  • Understanding of release, deployment and CI/CD pipelines.[23]
  • SFIA: Change control (CHMG), Release management (RELM).[5]

Tools

  • ITSM change modules with change calendars and change windows; integration with deployment pipelines.[35]

Processes

  • Change enablement, release management, deployment management, configuration management.[11]
  • COBIT BAI06 Managed IT Changes; NIST CM-3.[18,26]

Qualifications and credentials

  • ITIL Foundation and practice modules; COBIT or audit-oriented training in regulated sectors.[11,18]

Career moves

  • Up: release and change lead, head of service transition, IT service manager.
  • Across: release engineering, DevOps enablement, IT risk and compliance.

5.13 Service Delivery Manager

  • Domain Enterprise IT and IT services
  • Function Govern
  • Specialization Client and contract management, service level reporting
  • Level Senior to management
  • Industry Concentrated in managed service providers and outsourcers; computer systems design is the largest employer of support staff[7]

Service delivery managers are accountable for one or more services, or one client account, meeting agreed service levels. They are commonly found at managed service providers, where the computer systems design industry employs about a fifth of user support specialists.[7] O*NET's IT project managers occupation, which includes "Transition Program Manager" and "IT Program Manager", captures the budget, schedule and deliverables side of the work,[4] while the manager-level accountability sits in 11-3021.[2]

Responsibilities

  • Agree, monitor and report service levels with customers, and run service reviews.[11,15]
  • Manage the commercial relationship, service credits and improvement plans.[18]
  • Coordinate internal teams and subcontractors to deliver against the contract.[15]
  • Manage transitions of new services or clients into operation.[4]

Skills and core competencies

  • Relationship and commercial management, reporting and presentation.
  • Contract and SLA literacy; budget and margin management.
  • SFIA: SLMO, SUPP, ITMG.[5]

Tools

  • ITSM SLA and reporting modules, BI dashboards, contract management systems.[35,38]

Processes

  • Service level, relationship, supplier, and continual improvement practices.[11,15]
  • COBIT APO09 Managed Service Agreements.[18]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Bachelor's degree common; advanced ITIL; project or programme management credentials; ISO/IEC 20000 familiarity for certified providers.[10,11,15]
  • HDI offers Experience Management and XLA certifications that reflect a shift from SLA to experience-level measures.[14]

Career moves

  • Up: account director, head of service delivery, director of managed services.
  • Across: IT service manager, vendor management, customer success.

5.14 ITSM Administrator

  • Domain Enterprise IT, business applications
  • Function Build and operate
  • Specialization A specific ITSM platform (for example, ServiceNow or Jira Service Management)
  • Level Intermediate
  • Industry All mid-sized and large organisations; consultancies and platform partners

The ITSM administrator configures and maintains the platform on which every other role in this family works. O*NET lists ServiceNow as an "in-demand hot technology" for user support specialists,[32] which reflects how central these platforms have become. Jira Service Management, for example, supports request, incident, problem and change management, change calendars and windows, and links an asset repository to incidents and changes.[35] The role overlaps with computer systems analysts.[3]

Responsibilities

  • Configure forms, workflows, SLAs, notifications, catalogue items and access controls.
  • Maintain users, groups and integrations, and apply platform upgrades.
  • Maintain CMDB data structures in line with the platform's data model.[38]
  • Build reports and dashboards for process owners.
  • Implement process changes defined by process owners and analysts.[11]

Skills and core competencies

  • Platform configuration and low-code development; scripting (on ServiceNow, JavaScript); REST integrations.
  • Understanding of the ITIL practices the platform implements.[11]
  • SFIA: System software administration (SYSP), Methods and tools (METL), CFMG.[5]

Tools

  • ServiceNow, Jira Service Management, and comparable ITSM suites; identity and directory integration (Active Directory).[32,35]

Processes

  • Platform release and change management; all ITSM processes as a configurer.[11]

Qualifications and credentials

  • ServiceNow Certified System Administrator (CSA), then Certified Implementation Specialist in IT Service Management (CIS-ITSM).[40,41]
  • Atlassian certifications for Jira-based shops; ITIL Foundation.[11]

Career moves

  • Up: platform developer, technical consultant, platform architect, platform owner.
  • Across: business analysis, ITIL process analyst, CMDB architect.

5.15 ITIL Process Analyst

  • Domain Enterprise IT
  • Function Analyze
  • Specialization Process design, measurement, maturity assessment
  • Level Intermediate
  • Industry Large enterprises, public sector, consultancies

The ITIL process analyst designs, documents, measures and improves service management processes. The closest occupational anchor is computer systems analysts, which O*NET defines in terms of applying computers to analyse and solve business problems,[3] and BLS projects the occupation to grow 8% over 2025–35, much faster than average.[8] ISO/IEC 20000-1's requirements for performance evaluation and improvement, and COBIT's monitoring objectives, provide the analytical frame.[15,18]

Responsibilities

  • Map current-state processes and design target processes, RACI matrices and procedures.[11]
  • Define KPIs and build measurement and reporting.[15]
  • Assess process maturity and capability against frameworks.[18]
  • Write platform requirements for ITSM administrators and support adoption.[35]
  • Run continual improvement initiatives.[11]

Skills and core competencies

  • Process modelling, requirements analysis, data analysis.
  • Fluency in ITIL 4 and ITIL (Version 5), ISO/IEC 20000 and COBIT.[11,12,15,18]
  • SFIA: Measurement (MEAS), Methods and tools (METL), ITMG.[5]

Tools

  • Process modelling tools, BI and analytics, ITSM platform reporting.[38]

Processes

  • Continual improvement, measurement and reporting, and every practice under review.[11]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Bachelor's degree typical (BLS for systems analysts).[8]
  • ITIL Foundation and advanced modules; ISO/IEC 20000 practitioner training; business analysis credentials.[11,13]

Career moves

  • Up: process owner, ITSM consultant, service management office lead.
  • Across: business analyst, ITSM administrator, IT governance and audit.

6. Cross-cutting competencies and organizational levels

Three competency families recur in every profile above. The grouping is the author's, drawn from the sources cited.

Technical

Hardware, operating systems, networking, security and cloud fundamentals (CompTIA A+ and Network+ domains), endpoint management, scripting, and platform configuration.[30,31,33,37]

Process and governance

ITIL practices, ISO/IEC 20000-1 requirements, COBIT objectives, and security controls for inventory, configuration and incidents.[11,15,18,26]

Human

Communication, empathy, managing difficult conversations, stress and time management, facilitation, and stakeholder management.[28]

6.2 The NICE Framework and government work roles

The NICE Framework, NIST SP 800-181 Rev. 1, gives a common language for describing cybersecurity work as tasks, knowledge and skills organised into work roles.[45] Its components are updated separately from the special publication; version 2.2.0 is current.[46] Its "Technical Support" work role, and the DoD's corresponding Technical Support Specialist role (DCWF 411), describe frontline support tasks such as diagnosing and resolving customer-reported incidents, installing and configuring hardware and software, managing user accounts and access, and monitoring client-level performance.[29] In defense settings, DoD Manual 8140.03 sets qualification requirements for personnel in these work roles.[47]

9. Career paths

The diagram shows four common progressions. They are the author's synthesis of the role profiles above, not a published standard. Lateral moves between tracks are common, especially from frontline support into a process role.

Technical track

  1. Help desk technician / service desk analyst
  2. IT support specialist / desktop support
  3. Technical support engineer / operations analyst
  4. Systems administrator, SRE, cloud or security engineer (other series articles)

Process track

  1. Service desk analyst
  2. Incident, problem or change manager; configuration or asset manager
  3. Process owner / service management office lead
  4. IT service manager → head of service management

Platform track

  1. Service desk analyst / IT support
  2. ITSM administrator
  3. Platform developer or ITIL process analyst
  4. Platform architect / platform owner

Delivery track

  1. Team lead (service desk)
  2. Service desk or support manager
  3. Service delivery manager
  4. Director of service delivery / IT operations → CIO

The labour market data favour moving along these tracks. The entry occupation is projected to decline 3%, while the systems-analyst and IT-manager occupations that sit at the end of the process, platform and delivery tracks are projected to grow 8% and 16%.[7,8,10] Median pay roughly triples from the frontline ($61,860) to IT management ($175,140), although management requires five or more years of related experience.[7,10]

10. Industry variation

The largest employers of computer user support specialists are computer systems design and related services (21%), educational services (12%), information (10%), administrative and support services (7%) and government (7%).[7] Regulation changes the work most in the following sectors.

11. Trends shaping the field

  • AI in service management. ITIL (Version 5) is described as "AI-native", designed with AI and AI governance in mind.[12] HDI now offers an AI in the Support Organization certification,[14] and Atlassian documents AI-assisted incident handling in Jira Service Management.[35] A plausible effect, consistent with BLS's projected decline in user support employment,[7] is fewer purely transactional frontline roles and more emphasis on knowledge, automation and complex cases. That inference is the author's, not a BLS finding.
  • From services to products. Version 5's product and service lifecycle reframes service management around digital products, from discovery to support.[12]
  • Lighter change control. Evidence that external approval boards do not improve stability is shifting change managers towards automation and risk-based, delegated authority.[11,23]
  • Experience-level measures. Experience Management and XLA certifications from HDI reflect a move from purely technical SLAs to experience-based targets.[14]
  • Convergence with security. Updated incident response guidance (NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3) and the prominence of asset inventory in the CIS Controls tie ITSM roles more tightly to security.[24,27] IAITAM's newer security and AI asset certifications point the same way.[39]

12. Limitations

Labour statistics are U.S.-specific and occupation-based, so they approximate rather than measure most titles in this family.[7] Credential details change frequently. This article reflects issuer pages as of 2 November 2026, including a pending CompTIA Security+ version change and the ongoing ITIL (Version 5) rollout.[13,34] Some ITIL 4, ISO/IEC 20000-1 and COBIT content is available only in paid publications, and descriptions here paraphrase those documents. SFIA level mappings, cluster groupings, career paths and framework crosswalks are the author's analytical synthesis and are labelled as such.
