1. Scope and method

This article covers the Enterprise Infrastructure family, the third subgroup of Enterprise IT and Information Systems in the series taxonomy. Where Part 1.2 (Careers in Systems Administration) dealt with the people who run individual servers, directories and platforms, this part deals with the people who design and engineer the infrastructure as a whole: the data centre facility, the network, the storage and virtualisation layers, the integrated platform that ties them together, and the teams that operate and lead it. In SFIA 9 terms the centre of gravity moves from system software administration (SYSP) towards infrastructure design (IFDN), systems integration and build (SINT), network design (NTDS), facilities management (DCMA) and solution architecture (ARCH).[1]

Each role is described along the series' five independent dimensions:

Author's analysis: how this family differs from systems administration. Three differences recur in the sources. First, the work function shifts from operate to build: SFIA's infrastructure design and integration skills, BLS's description of architects who "create plans and layouts", and the DCWF network operations role that "plans, implements, and operates" all describe design work that administrators usually execute rather than originate.[1,10,11] Second, the unit of responsibility grows from a system to an estate, which is why capacity, availability and continuity skills appear in almost every profile below. Third, the family reaches below the operating system into the physical facility, a layer with its own standards bodies, trades and safety regime (section 4.3). The practical consequence for job seekers is that most titles here are second or third jobs, reached after time as an administrator, network support specialist or technician.

Five points stand out. First, the design-oriented occupations are growing while the administration occupation shrinks. BLS projects network architects to grow 8%, much faster than the 3% average, because firms adopting artificial intelligence and cloud computing need their networks and IT infrastructure upgraded,[10] whereas administrators are projected to decline 4%.[12] Second, architects are rarely hired straight from education: BLS says they typically need several years of IT experience, often as network and computer systems administrators, and that experienced architects may move into management.[10] Third, management pays most and grows fastest: computer and information systems managers earn a median $175,140 and are projected to grow 16%, and BLS notes that some managers oversee a specific area such as infrastructure.[14] Fourth, the entry points into data centre work are lower-paid and slower-growing. The largest employers of network architects are computer systems design and related services (25%), finance and insurance (11%), telecommunications (9%) and management of companies (9%).[10] In O*NET's survey of network architects, 55% of respondents said new hires need a bachelor's degree, with 15% each citing a postsecondary certificate or an associate's degree.[4] For network support specialists the figures were 47% bachelor's, 22% associate's and 14% some college.[5]

4. Frameworks, standards and processes

Enterprise infrastructure has an unusually wide set of governing documents, because it spans buildings as well as IT. The service management processes described in Part 1.1 (incident, change, configuration) and the hardening, patching and backup practices described in Part 1.2 still apply. This section covers what is distinctive to this family.

4.1 Workforce frameworks: SFIA and the DCWF

SFIA 9 describes infrastructure work through skills including infrastructure design (IFDN), infrastructure operations (ITOP), network design (NTDS), storage management (STMG), availability management (AVMT), capacity management (CPMG) and solution architecture (ARCH).[1] Its facilities management skill (DCMA), defined as "planning, designing and managing the buildings, space and facilities which, collectively, make up the IT estate", is the one most specific to data centres. At level 4 it involves using data centre management tools to report on power, cooling and space and supporting mandatory electrical safety testing; at level 5, developing data centre standards and planning installed infrastructure; and at level 6, setting policy for the IT estate and planning future data centre space.[15] The DoD Cyber Workforce Framework provides two work roles that map onto this family. Role 651, Enterprise Architect, "develops and maintains business, systems, and information processes to support enterprise mission needs" and "develops information technology (IT) rules and requirements that describe baseline and target architectures".[16] Role 441, Network Operations Specialist, "plans, implements, and operates network services / systems, to include hardware and virtual environments".[11] Both pathways report that the roles most commonly align with OPM occupational series 2210 (Information Technology), at 72% and 70% respectively.[11,16]

4.2 Data centre design standards and tiers

Three documents dominate facility design. ANSI/TIA-942-C, published in May 2024, applies to all data centre types from hyperscale and colocation to enterprise facilities, and covers architecture, telecommunications, power, cooling, fire protection, physical security, safety and monitoring; Revision C added edge data centre content, a new cable media type, and requirements for floor loading and minimum cabinet width.[17] ANSI/BICSI 002-2024 is a 575-page best-practice standard covering site selection, electrical and mechanical systems (including immersion and liquid cooling), security, DCIM, telecommunications, commissioning and energy efficiency, and it is the reference for BICSI's Data Center Design Consultant credential.[18] The Uptime Institute Tier Standard defines four topology levels: Tier I (basic capacity), Tier II (redundant capacity components), Tier III (concurrently maintainable, so any component can be maintained without shutting down IT) and Tier IV (fault tolerant, so individual equipment failures or distribution path interruptions do not affect IT). A separate volume covers operational sustainability, which Uptime argues strongly affects real-world performance.[19]

4.3 Environment, energy and safety

ASHRAE TC 9.9's 2021 thermal guidelines set a recommended inlet envelope of 18–27 °C dry-bulb for equipment classes A1 to A4, and a narrower 18–22 °C for the high-density class H1.[20] Energy performance is measured with power usage effectiveness (PUE), standardised in ISO/IEC 30134-2, whose second edition was published in January 2026.[21] In the European Union, Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1364 establishes the first phase of a common rating scheme for data centres.[22] Operators of data centres with IT energy demand of at least 500 kW had to report 24 key performance indicators by 15 September 2024, and must report annually by 15 May from 2025.[23] Safety is a distinct competency in the facility roles. NFPA 75 sets minimum requirements for protecting IT equipment and areas from fire, smoke, corrosion, heat and water damage.[24] NFPA 70E addresses workplace electrical hazards, specifically electric shock and arc flash,[25] and NFPA notes that OSHA requires almost all employers to protect employees from those hazards.[26] OSHA's lockout/tagout standard, 29 CFR 1910.147, governs the control of hazardous energy during servicing and maintenance.[27]

4.4 Network architecture and zero trust

NIST SP 800-207 defines zero trust as a set of principles that move defences from static, network-based perimeters to users, assets and resources, and assumes that no implicit trust is granted on the basis of network location.[28] For network infrastructure engineers this shifts design effort from perimeter firewalls towards segmentation, identity-aware access and continuous monitoring. NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 supplies the corresponding controls, including boundary protection (SC-7) and baseline configuration (CM-2).[29] The DCWF's network operations tasks remain highly practical: configuring and optimising routers and switches, developing network backup and recovery procedures, installing and maintaining network device operating systems and firmware, and monitoring network capacity and performance.[11]

4.5 Storage, virtualisation and data protection

NIST SP 800-209 covers the security of storage infrastructure, noting that networked, software-defined and cloud storage increase management complexity and the risk of configuration errors.[30] ISO/IEC 27040:2024 gives requirements and guidance for storage security.[31] The NVM Express Base Specification (Revision 2.3, July 2025) defines both memory-based (PCIe) and message-based (fabrics) transports, the basis of NVMe over Fabrics storage networks.[32] NIST SP 800-125 addresses security for full virtualisation.[33] The joint #StopRansomware Guide advises offline, encrypted backups, regular restore testing, golden images of critical systems and offline copies of infrastructure-as-code templates,[34] which places backup architecture squarely in the engineering roles as well as the administration roles covered in Part 1.2. NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1 and ISO 22301 frame contingency and continuity planning.[35,36]

4.6 Architecture practice

ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022 sets requirements for architecture descriptions of software, systems and enterprises, and separates the architecture of an entity from the description that documents it.[37] The TOGAF Standard, 10th Edition, is the basis for The Open Group's current Enterprise Architecture Foundation and Practitioner certifications.[38] Cloud providers publish their own architecture frameworks; the AWS Well-Architected Framework, for example, is organised around six pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimisation and sustainability.[39]

4.7 Operations: service levels, toil and procedures

Google's site reliability engineering practice defines a service level indicator as "a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the level of service", a service level objective as "a target value or range of values for a service level that is measured by an SLI", and a service level agreement as a contract that includes consequences of meeting or missing the SLOs it contains.[40] The same book defines toil as manual, repetitive, automatable operational work and aims to keep it below half of each engineer's time.[41] Procedure discipline matters at the facility layer too: Uptime Institute's 2025 outage analysis reported that among organisations with a major human-error outage in the previous three years, 85% of incidents stemmed from staff failing to follow procedures or from flawed procedures, and that IT and networking issues caused 23% of impactful outages in 2024.[42] Patch management (NIST SP 800-40 Rev. 4) and ITIL practices, including the phased 2026 release of ITIL (Version 5), complete the operational toolkit.[43–45]

5. Role profiles

Each profile gives a five-dimension summary, then responsibilities, skills and core competencies, tools, processes, qualifications and credentials, and career moves. "Typical" statements about level and industry are the author's synthesis of the cited sources. Each profile ends with the pay proxy used.

5.1 Infrastructure Engineer

  • Domain Enterprise IT infrastructure, compute, network, storage and cloud
  • Function Build, operate
  • Specialization Infrastructure as code, automation, hybrid platforms
  • Level Intermediate to principal
  • Industry All; heavy in IT services, finance and cloud-native firms

The infrastructure engineer is the generalist builder of this family: the person who turns an architecture into working, repeatable infrastructure. O*NET's computer systems engineers/architects occupation, the closest proxy, "designs and develops solutions for complex application problems, system administration issues, or network concerns".[2] In current practice the role is defined by automation: infrastructure is increasingly described in code and deployed through pipelines, and CISA's ransomware guidance even recommends keeping offline copies of infrastructure-as-code templates so that environments can be rebuilt.[34]

Responsibilities

  • Communicate with staff or clients to understand system requirements, and evaluate existing systems to recommend improvements.[2]
  • Configure servers and maintain hardware and software; design and run hardware and software tests.[2]
  • Define infrastructure as code and automate provisioning across on-premises and cloud estates.[4,46]
  • Build for monitoring, reliability, business continuity, deployment and automation (each 22% of AWS's CloudOps Engineer exam).[47]

Skills and core competencies

  • Infrastructure design, integration and configuration management.[1]
  • Scripting and configuration automation: Ansible, Terraform, PowerShell, Chef and Puppet appear in O*NET's technology data for network architects.[4]
  • Containers and orchestration (Kubernetes, as tested by the CKA).[48]
  • SFIA: IFDN, SINT, ITOP, CFMG.[1]

Tools

  • Terraform (now an IBM product following IBM's acquisition of HashiCorp on 27 February 2025), Ansible, Git, Docker and Red Hat OpenShift.[3,4,49]
  • Public cloud consoles and APIs (AWS, Azure), Windows Admin Center and Azure Arc for hybrid estates.[4,50]

Processes

  • Change and release through version control, design review, capacity and availability planning, toil reduction.[1,41]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Education: a computing bachelor's degree is common, but the role is usually reached from administration; see section 8.
  • HashiCorp Certified Terraform Associate (one-hour multiple-choice exam, currently testing Terraform 1.12) and the newer Terraform Infrastructure Engineer Professional.[46]
  • Platform credentials: AWS CloudOps Engineer – Associate, Azure Administrator Associate, RHCE, CKA.[47,48,51,52]

Career moves

  • Up: senior or principal infrastructure engineer, infrastructure architect.
  • Across: platform engineering, site reliability engineering, cloud engineering (later parts of the series).

Pay proxy: computer occupations, all other (15-1299), median $116,580 in 2025, the group O*NET uses for 15-1299.08.[2]

5.2 Infrastructure Architect

  • Domain Enterprise IT infrastructure, hybrid and multi-cloud
  • Function Govern, build
  • Specialization Target-state design, standards, technology selection
  • Level Senior to principal
  • Industry All; largest in IT services, finance and telecoms[10]

Infrastructure architects decide what the infrastructure should become and set the rules engineers build to. BLS's network architects "create plans and layouts for data communication networks", present designs to management and customers, and research and recommend new technologies,[10] while the DCWF's Enterprise Architect "develops information technology (IT) rules and requirements that describe baseline and target architectures".[16] The infrastructure architect combines these, usually across compute, storage, network, facility and cloud.

Responsibilities

  • Analyse user needs and requirements to plan architecture; develop architecture or system components to meet them.[16]
  • Identify and prioritise critical business functions with stakeholders; document and update architecture activities.[16]
  • Present designs to management, customers and staff; research and recommend new technologies.[10]
  • Develop disaster recovery plans and recommend security measures.[4]

Skills and core competencies

  • Analytical, interpersonal, leadership, organisational and problem-solving skills, and attention to detail (BLS's important qualities for architects).[10]
  • Architecture description, viewpoints and models (ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010).[37]
  • Designing secure, resilient, high-performing and cost-optimised architectures (the four SAA-C03 domains, weighted 30%, 26%, 24% and 20%).[53]
  • SFIA: ARCH, IFDN, STPL, EMRG.[1]

Tools

  • Architecture frameworks and reviews (TOGAF, AWS Well-Architected), modelling and diagramming tools, cost and capacity analytics such as those built into VMware Cloud Foundation 9.0.[38,39,54]

Processes

  • Architecture governance and design authority; technology roadmaps; standards and patterns; risk and continuity design.[16,35]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Experience: BLS reports that architects typically need several years of IT experience, often as administrators.[10]
  • TOGAF Enterprise Architecture Foundation and Practitioner (TOGAF 10th Edition).[38]
  • Microsoft Azure Solutions Architect Expert (exam AZ-305; requires Azure Administrator Associate first); AWS Certified Solutions Architect – Associate (SAA-C03; one year's design experience recommended).[53,55]
  • VCP-VCF Architect and VCAP-VCF Architect for VMware private cloud.[56]
  • BICSI DCDC where the scope includes facilities.[57]

Career moves

  • Up: chief architect, head of infrastructure, CTO (BLS notes architects may move into management).[10]
  • Across: enterprise, solutions, security or cloud architecture.

Pay proxy: computer network architects (15-1241), median $134,050 in May 2025.[10]

5.3 Enterprise Infrastructure Engineer

  • Domain Large-scale enterprise IT, hybrid estates
  • Function Build, operate
  • Specialization Resilience, capacity, multi-site and hybrid integration
  • Level Intermediate to principal
  • Industry Large enterprises: finance, healthcare, government, manufacturing

The enterprise infrastructure engineer does the infrastructure engineer's job at enterprise scale, where the estate spans several sites, a legacy core and one or more clouds, and where regulators take an interest in how it is built. The FFIEC's examination booklet on architecture, infrastructure and operations is an example of the scrutiny such estates receive in U.S. banking.[58]

Author's analysis: infrastructure engineer or enterprise infrastructure engineer? The sources do not distinguish the two titles, and many employers use them interchangeably. Where a distinction is drawn in practice, the "enterprise" variant tends to signal three things: a large, heterogeneous, regulated estate; heavier emphasis on availability, capacity and continuity (SFIA AVMT, CPMG, COPL) than on greenfield automation; and closer work with identity tiering and Windows-centric hybrid platforms. Job seekers should read the responsibilities, not the title.

Responsibilities

  • Engineer hybrid infrastructure spanning on-premises Windows Server and Azure, including identity, compute, storage, networking, high availability and disaster recovery.[50]
  • Plan capacity and availability across sites; design and test continuity arrangements.[1,35]
  • Protect management planes in line with Microsoft's tier model, which treats systems that control identity as Tier 0.[59]
  • Produce evidence for regulators and auditors on configuration, recovery and change.[58,60]

Skills and core competencies

  • Design of identity, governance, monitoring, data storage, business continuity and infrastructure solutions (the AZ-305 skill areas).[55]
  • Continuity management and business impact analysis.[35,36]
  • SFIA: IFDN, SINT, AVMT, CPMG.[1]

Tools

  • Windows Server 2025, Azure Arc, Azure Monitor; VMware Cloud Foundation or Nutanix; enterprise backup platforms such as Veeam.[50,54,61–63]

Processes

  • Change advisory and release, capacity and availability management, disaster recovery testing, lifecycle and refresh planning.[1,35]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Windows Server Administrator Associate, Azure Administrator Associate and Azure Solutions Architect Expert.[50,51,55]
  • VCP-VCF Administrator or NCP-MCI for the private cloud layer; ITIL Foundation for process.[44,56,62]

Career moves

  • Up: lead or principal engineer, infrastructure architect, technical infrastructure manager.
  • Across: resilience engineering, cloud platform engineering.

Pay proxy: computer occupations, all other (15-1299), median $116,580 in 2025.[2]

5.4 Data Center Engineer

  • Domain Data centre facilities and the IT hardware housed in them
  • Function Build, operate
  • Specialization Critical power, cooling, space, cabling; or rack-level IT engineering
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry Colocation, hyperscale and cloud providers; large enterprises

"Data center engineer" has two meanings. In colocation and hyperscale operators it often means a critical facilities engineer responsible for the electrical, mechanical and fire systems that keep the data hall running, work that overlaps with the electrical and HVAC trades.[7,8] In enterprises it more often means an IT engineer who designs and maintains the racks, servers, cabling and network inside the hall. SFIA's facilities management skill spans both, from reporting on power, cooling and space to developing data centre standards and planning installed infrastructure.[15] Both meanings are growing: BLS links electrician demand to data centre construction, and Uptime Institute's 2025 survey found nearly two-thirds of operators struggling to retain staff, find qualified candidates, or both.[7,64]

Responsibilities

  • Use data centre management tools to report on power, cooling and space; carry out audits; support mandatory electrical safety testing.[15]
  • Develop data centre standards, processes and documentation; optimise space; plan and manage installed infrastructure; track sustainability targets.[15]
  • Maintain the thermal environment within the ASHRAE recommended envelope, and report PUE.[20,21]
  • Maintain redundancy and concurrent maintainability appropriate to the site's Tier.[19]
  • In the EU, compile the data needed for rating-scheme reporting at sites of 500 kW IT demand or more.[23]

Skills and core competencies

  • Electrical distribution (UPS, generators, switchgear), mechanical systems and airflow, increasingly including liquid and immersion cooling.[18]
  • Telecommunications cabling, floor loading and cabinet design (TIA-942-C).[17]
  • Electrical safety (NFPA 70E), fire protection (NFPA 75) and lockout/tagout.[24,25,27]
  • SFIA: DCMA, IFDN, AVMT, SUST.[1]

Tools

  • Data centre infrastructure management (DCIM) and IP-enabled building systems, which BICSI 002-2024 covers as part of facility and building systems.[18]
  • Power and environmental monitoring; cable and asset management systems.[15]

Processes

  • Method of procedure and change control for maintenance; commissioning; capacity planning; incident response. Uptime's outage research shows that failure to follow procedures, or flawed procedures, lies behind most major human-error outages.[18,42]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Facility track: electrical or mechanical engineering degree or a trade apprenticeship; electricians typically complete a four- or five-year apprenticeship and most states require a licence.[7]
  • EPI Certified Data Centre Professional, Specialist and Expert (CDCE requires CDCS; five-day course; valid three years).[65]
  • CNet Training's Certified Data Centre Technician, Management and Design Professional programmes.[66]
  • Uptime Institute Accredited Operations Specialist (for critical facility operators) and Accredited Tier Designer (for licensed professional engineers).[67,68]

Career moves

  • Up: senior or lead critical facilities engineer, data centre manager, data centre design consultant (BICSI DCDC).[57]
  • Across: infrastructure architect (facility scope), sustainability or energy roles.

Pay proxy: no single code. IT-side roles fit 15-1244 (median $99,130); facility-side roles overlap with electricians ($63,190) and HVAC mechanics ($61,010), although senior critical facilities engineers typically earn more than trade medians.[7,8,12] The last point is the author's observation, not a BLS figure.

5.5 Data Center Technician

  • Domain Data centre floor operations
  • Function Operate
  • Specialization Racking, cabling, hardware replacement, smart hands
  • Level Entry to intermediate
  • Industry Colocation, hyperscale and cloud providers; large enterprises

Data centre technicians are the hands on the floor: they install and remove equipment, run and test cabling, replace failed parts, and follow access and safety procedures. It is one of the few genuinely entry-level roles in this family. SFIA's facilities management skill at level 2 describes exactly this work: helping with physical access tasks under routine supervision, following procedures for the physical environment, and maintaining documentation.[15] Uptime Institute's 2025 staffing survey found that junior facilities staff are in the shortest supply and have the highest turnover, particularly in colocation.[69]

Responsibilities

  • Install, rack and remove servers, storage and network hardware; replace failed components and reassemble equipment after repair.[1,9]
  • Configure LAN and WAN equipment; identify the causes of networking problems with diagnostic equipment; document support activities.[5]
  • Process access requests and monitor compliance with physical procedures.[15]
  • Maintain parts inventories and asset records.[9]

Skills and core competencies

  • Hardware, networking and troubleshooting fundamentals (CompTIA A+ and Server+ domains).[70,71]
  • Fibre and copper termination, splicing and testing (the CFOT body of knowledge).[72]
  • Safe working: electrical hazard awareness and lockout/tagout.[25,27]
  • SFIA: HSIN, DCMA, ITOP, ASMG.[1]

Tools

  • Cable testers, optical loss test sets and OTDRs; ticketing systems such as ServiceNow; DCIM and asset tools.[5,18,72]

Processes

  • Work orders and remote hands requests; change and access control; documented methods of procedure.[15,42]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Education: an associate's degree is typical for network support specialists, and O*NET reports high school or postsecondary certificates for computer repairers.[9,13]
  • CompTIA A+ (V15, exams 220-1201 and 220-1202), Network+ (N10-009; A+ and 9–12 months' experience recommended) and Server+.[70,71,73,74]
  • FOA CFOT (100-question exam, 70 to pass; via an approved course or two years' documented field experience).[72]
  • CNet CDCTP or EPI CDCP for data centre fundamentals.[65,66]

Career moves

  • Up: senior or lead technician, data center engineer, data centre operations manager.
  • Across: network infrastructure engineer, systems administration (Part 1.2), critical facilities trades.

5.6 Network Infrastructure Engineer

  • Domain Enterprise networks: LAN, WAN, data centre fabric, wireless
  • Function Build, operate
  • Specialization Routing and switching, SD-WAN, wireless, automation, zero trust segmentation
  • Level Intermediate to principal
  • Industry All; largest in IT services, finance and telecoms[10]

Network infrastructure engineers design, build and maintain the networks the rest of the estate runs on. They sit between the network administrator described in Part 1.2 and the network architect. The DCWF's Network Operations Specialist captures the operating half of the job,[11] and BLS's network architect profile the design half.[10]

Responsibilities

  • Configure and optimise routers and switches; install and maintain network device operating systems and firmware.[11]
  • Monitor network capacity and performance; diagnose connectivity problems.[11]
  • Develop network backup and recovery procedures and recommend security measures such as firewalls.[4,11]
  • Deploy and test new network designs; upgrade hardware and software.[10]

Skills and core competencies

  • Network infrastructure and connectivity, switching and access, IP routing, services and security, and AI and network operations (CCNA v2.0 exam topics, weighted 25/25/20/20/10%).[75]
  • Zero trust principles and segmentation.[28]
  • BGP, Ansible and Terraform, all in O*NET's technology data for network architects.[4]
  • SFIA: NTDS, NTAS, IFDN.[1]

Tools

  • Cisco, HPE (which completed its acquisition of Juniper Networks on 2 July 2025) and other vendor platforms; SD-WAN controllers; network automation with Ansible and Python.[4,5,76,77]
  • Monitoring with tools such as Splunk.[4]

Processes

  • Change control for network changes, capacity planning, configuration backup, incident and problem management.[1,11]

Qualifications and credentials

  • CompTIA Network+ (N10-009).[74]
  • Cisco CCNA (200-301, 120 minutes).[78] Cisco has published v2.0 exam topics that add a domain on AI and network operations.[75]
  • Cisco CCNP Enterprise: core exam 350-401 ENCOR plus one concentration (advanced routing, SD-WAN, design, wireless or automation); ENCOR also qualifies candidates for the CCIE Enterprise lab.[77]
  • BICSI RCDD for communications distribution design.[79]

Career moves

  • Up: senior network engineer, network or infrastructure architect.[10,12]
  • Across: network security engineering, cloud networking.

Pay proxy: computer network architects (15-1241), median $134,050, for design-heavy roles; 15-1244, median $99,130, for operations-heavy roles.[10,12]

5.7 Storage Engineer

  • Domain Enterprise data storage and data protection
  • Function Build, operate
  • Specialization Block, file and object storage; SAN and NVMe fabrics; replication and backup architecture
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All; data-heavy sectors such as finance, healthcare, media and research

Storage engineers design the storage platforms that Part 1.2's storage administrators run. The engineering emphasis is on architecture (tiers, protocols, replication, capacity growth) and resilience. NIST warns that networked, software-defined and cloud storage increase management complexity and the risk of configuration errors,[30] and ransomware has made immutable and offline copies a design requirement rather than an operational nicety.[34]

Responsibilities

  • Design storage architecture across block, file and object tiers and storage networks, including NVMe over Fabrics.[32]
  • Implement storage security controls for data protection, isolation, restoration assurance and encryption.[30,31]
  • Design replication, backup and recovery architecture, including offline, encrypted and tested copies.[34]
  • Forecast and manage capacity and performance.[1]

Skills and core competencies

  • Storage protocols, data services and performance analysis.[32,80]
  • Continuity and recovery design.[35,36]
  • SFIA: STMG, IFDN, CPMG, COPL.[1]

Tools

  • NetApp ONTAP; VMware Cloud Foundation storage (the focus of Broadcom's VCAP VCF Storage track); backup platforms such as Veeam and Veritas NetBackup.[3,56,63,80]

Processes

  • Capacity management, data lifecycle and retention, restore testing, change control for storage migrations.[1,81]

Qualifications and credentials

  • NetApp Certified Data Administrator, ONTAP (six to twelve months' ONTAP experience recommended).[80]
  • Broadcom VCAP VCF Storage (Architect, Implement or Support), based on VCF 9.0.[56]
  • Veeam VMCE+ or VMCSE for data protection.[63]

Career moves

  • Up: senior storage engineer, storage or data protection architect.
  • Across: backup and recovery, cloud storage engineering, data platform engineering.

Pay proxy: 15-1299 (median $116,580) for design roles; 15-1244 (median $99,130) for operations roles.[2,12]

5.8 Virtualization Engineer

  • Domain Compute virtualisation and private cloud
  • Function Build, operate
  • Specialization VMware Cloud Foundation, Nutanix, Hyper-V, KVM-based and Kubernetes-integrated platforms
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All organisations with data centres; managed service providers

Virtualization engineers design and build the private cloud platform on which most enterprise workloads run, and increasingly lead migrations between platforms. The market changed after Broadcom completed its acquisition of VMware on 22 November 2023.[82] VMware Cloud Foundation 9.0, released in June 2025, runs virtual machines and Kubernetes containers side by side through an embedded vSphere Kubernetes Service and adds GPU-as-a-service for private AI workloads.[54] Some organisations have responded by evaluating alternatives such as Nutanix and Hyper-V; the author's view is that platform evaluation and migration have become core skills for this role.

Responsibilities

  • Design hypervisor clusters, virtual networking and storage policies; build templates and automation.[54,62]
  • Plan capacity, availability and live migration across hosts and sites.[1,61]
  • Secure the hypervisor and management plane.[33,59]
  • Provide self-service infrastructure to developers with role-based access and governance policies.[54]

Skills and core competencies

  • Compute, storage and network virtualisation; Kubernetes integration (CKA-level knowledge).[48,54]
  • Deploying, configuring, migrating, troubleshooting and managing a Nutanix environment, as measured by NCP-MCI.[62]
  • SFIA: IFDN, SYSP, CPMG, AVMT.[1]

Tools

  • VMware Cloud Foundation with the vSphere Kubernetes Service, Nutanix Prism Central, Microsoft Hyper-V on Windows Server 2025.[54,61,62]

Processes

  • Capacity management, host lifecycle and patching, cluster-wide change control, platform migration planning.[1,43]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Broadcom VCP-VCF Administrator or Architect; VCAP by domain (VKS, Automation, Operations, Storage, Networking) or by role, all based on VCF 9.0.[56]
  • Nutanix NCP-MCI 7.5 (available from June 2026, remote or test centre).[62]
  • Microsoft Windows Server Administrator Associate for Hyper-V estates.[50]

Career moves

  • Up: senior virtualisation engineer, private cloud or infrastructure architect.
  • Across: Kubernetes platform engineering, cloud engineering. See Part 1.2 for the virtualization administrator role that commonly precedes this one.

Pay proxy: 15-1299 (median $116,580); 15-1244 (median $99,130) for operations-weighted roles.[2,12]

5.9 Enterprise Systems Engineer

  • Domain Enterprise IT systems and their integration
  • Function Build
  • Specialization Systems design, integration, requirements and testing across platforms
  • Level Intermediate to principal
  • Industry All; also defense contractors and IT services

The enterprise systems engineer designs and integrates the systems that make up an organisation's IT, ensuring they work together. It is the engineering counterpart of the systems engineer covered in Part 1.2, which treated the title from the senior administrator's angle. O*NET's computer systems engineers/architects occupation describes the core: understanding requirements, evaluating existing systems, configuring servers, and designing and running hardware and software tests.[2] The title has a second, distinct meaning in aerospace, defense and manufacturing, where systems engineering is a lifecycle discipline certified by INCOSE.[83]

Author's analysis: three uses of the title. Job adverts use "enterprise systems engineer" in at least three ways: (1) a senior infrastructure engineer responsible for integrating server, storage, identity and network platforms; (2) an engineer who runs and integrates enterprise application platforms such as ERP; and (3) a systems engineer in the INCOSE sense working on large enterprise programmes. This article profiles the first, which fits the taxonomy subgroup. The other two are covered in later parts of the series.

Responsibilities

  • Communicate with staff or clients to understand requirements; evaluate existing systems and recommend improvements.[2]
  • Design and run hardware and software tests; document designs and train users.[2]
  • Integrate platforms across identity, compute, storage and network, and manage change across them.[1]

Skills and core competencies

  • Systems design, integration and change control.[1]
  • Automation and configuration management (Ansible, Chef, Puppet, Git) and cloud deployment tooling.[3]
  • SFIA: DESN, SINT, IFDN, CHMG.[1]

Tools

  • Configuration management and version control, AWS CloudFormation, Docker and Red Hat OpenShift, as listed in O*NET technology data.[3]

Processes

  • Requirements, design review, integration testing, change and release, documentation.[2,37]

Qualifications and credentials

  • RHCE, Azure Administrator Associate, AWS CloudOps Engineer – Associate, CKA.[47,48,51,52]
  • INCOSE CSEP (at least 60 months' systems engineering experience) for the defense and aerospace sense.[83]

Career moves

  • Up: principal engineer, infrastructure or solutions architect.
  • Across: enterprise architecture, technical programme management.

Pay proxy: 15-1299 (median $116,580), the group O*NET uses for 15-1299.08.[2]

5.10 Infrastructure Operations Engineer

  • Domain Enterprise IT infrastructure in production
  • Function Operate
  • Specialization Monitoring, incident and problem management, reliability, automation of toil
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All; 24×7 environments in finance, healthcare, telecoms and service providers

Infrastructure operations engineers keep the estate running: they watch it, respond when it breaks, find out why, and engineer away repeat failures. The role sits between traditional operations (network operations centres, on-call administrators) and site reliability engineering, and borrows from both. Its distinctive competencies are measurement and procedure: defining service levels in terms of SLIs and SLOs, keeping toil under control, and following and improving runbooks.[40–42]

Responsibilities

  • Monitor network and system capacity and performance; diagnose connectivity and service problems.[11]
  • Run incident response and problem management; write and maintain runbooks and procedures.[42,44]
  • Define SLIs and SLOs and report against them.[40]
  • Coordinate patching across the estate.[43]
  • Automate repetitive operational work.[41]

Skills and core competencies

  • Monitoring, logging and remediation; reliability and business continuity; deployment and automation (AWS CloudOps exam domains).[47]
  • Scripting in PowerShell and Bash.[3]
  • SFIA: ITOP, USUP, PBMG, AVMT.[1]

Tools

  • Splunk Enterprise, Nagios and ServiceNow (in O*NET technology data for 15-1244); cloud-native monitoring such as Azure Monitor.[3,50]

Processes

  • Incident, problem, change and event management (ITIL); blameless post-incident review; on-call and escalation.[40,44,45]

Qualifications and credentials

  • ITIL 4 Foundation or ITIL Foundation (Version 5).[44]
  • AWS CloudOps Engineer – Associate (SOA-C03), Azure Administrator Associate.[47,51]
  • CompTIA Server+ or Linux+ for operating system depth.[71,84]

Career moves

  • Up: senior operations engineer, operations or service delivery manager, technical infrastructure manager.
  • Across: site reliability engineering, platform engineering, security operations.

Pay proxy: network and computer systems administrators (15-1244), median $99,130.[12]

5.11 Technical Infrastructure Manager

  • Domain Enterprise IT infrastructure as a managed service
  • Function Govern
  • Specialization People, budget, vendors, risk and roadmap for infrastructure
  • Level Management (first-line to head of infrastructure)
  • Industry All

The technical infrastructure manager leads the teams in this article and answers for the estate's cost, reliability and risk. BLS notes that some computer and information systems managers oversee the entire IT department while others handle a specific area such as infrastructure.[14] O*NET lists "Technical Services Manager" among the reported titles for the occupation.[6]

Responsibilities

  • Analyse IT needs and recommend upgrades; plan and direct the installation and maintenance of hardware and software.[14]
  • Assess costs and benefits and justify funding; determine staffing needs.[14]
  • Negotiate with vendors and monitor their performance.[14]
  • Develop computer information resources providing for data security and control, strategic computing and disaster recovery.[6]
  • Recruit, hire, train and supervise staff.[6]

Skills and core competencies

  • Technology service management, strategic planning, budgeting and forecasting, supplier management and resourcing.[1]
  • Workforce retention in a tight market: Uptime reported that in 2025, for the first time, recruiting and retaining senior staff was harder than early-career staff.[64]
  • SFIA: ITMG, ITSP, BUDF, SUPP, RESC.[1]

Tools

  • IT service management, financial and portfolio tools; cost analytics and showback/chargeback, such as those in VCF 9.0.[54]

Processes

  • Budgeting, vendor and contract management, risk and compliance reporting, continuity planning, ITIL service management.[14,35,44]

Qualifications and credentials

  • BLS: typically a bachelor's degree and several years of related experience; some employers prefer a graduate degree; certification is usually optional.[14]
  • PMI PMP (36 months' project leadership with a four-year degree, or 60 months without, plus 35 hours' training; 180-question exam).[85]
  • Advanced ITIL designations; Uptime AOS for managers of critical facilities.[45,67]

Career moves

  • Up: head of infrastructure, IT director, CTO or CIO.[14]
  • Across: service delivery, programme management, enterprise architecture.

Pay proxy: computer and information systems managers (11-3021), median $175,140 in May 2025.[14]

9. Career paths

The diagram shows four common progressions. They are the author's synthesis of the role profiles above, not a published standard.

Facility track

  1. Data centre technician
  2. Senior technician / critical facilities technician
  3. Data center engineer
  4. Data centre manager or design consultant

Network track

  1. Network support specialist or technician
  2. Network administrator (Part 1.2)
  3. Network infrastructure engineer
  4. Network or infrastructure architect

Platform track

  1. Systems, storage or virtualisation administrator (Part 1.2)
  2. Storage, virtualisation or infrastructure engineer
  3. Enterprise infrastructure or systems engineer
  4. Infrastructure architect

Operations and management track

  1. Operations or NOC engineer
  2. Infrastructure operations engineer
  3. Technical infrastructure manager
  4. Head of infrastructure → CTO / CIO

The labour market data support moving along these tracks. Administrators (15-1244) are projected to decline 4%, while architects (15-1241) are projected to grow 8% and IT managers 16%.[10,12,14] Median pay rises from $76,220 for network support specialists and $99,130 for administrators to $134,050 for architects and $175,140 for managers.[10,12–14] BLS itself describes the steps: network administrators may become architects, and architects may move into management.[10,12]

Author's analysis: two entry doors. The family has two distinct entry doors. The IT door leads from support or administration into engineering, and is mostly credential- and experience-driven. The facilities door leads from the electrical and mechanical trades, or from data centre technician roles, into critical facilities engineering, and is licence- and safety-driven. The doors meet at the data center engineer and infrastructure architect roles, where people who understand both the IT load and the facility that supports it are scarce. Uptime's staffing surveys, which report persistent difficulty filling both junior and senior roles, suggest that crossing between the two doors is a realistic route for career changers.[64,69]

11. Trends shaping the field

  • AI is driving physical build-out. U.S. data centres used about 4.4% of the nation's electricity in 2023 (176 TWh), and the Department of Energy's Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory projects 6.7% to 12% by 2028.[95,96] The IEA estimates global data centre consumption at about 485 TWh in 2025, rising to about 950 TWh in 2030, and reports that data centre demand grew 17% in 2025 amid bottlenecks in grid connections, transformers, gas turbines and chips.[97,98] In the author's view this is the single biggest source of new demand for data centre engineers, technicians and the electrical and mechanical trades.
  • Higher densities and liquid cooling. Uptime's 2025 survey found average rack densities rising, with more operators adopting 10–30 kW racks, and about a third of operators running some AI training or inference.[64] ASHRAE's H1 class for high-density equipment and BICSI 002-2024's expanded immersion and liquid-cooling content reflect the same shift.[18,20]
  • A persistent staffing gap. Nearly two-thirds of operators report difficulty retaining staff, finding qualified candidates, or both, and in 2025 senior roles became harder to fill than junior ones for the first time.[64] Uptime's staffing survey found 37% of respondents naming hiring as a top challenge.[69]
  • Sustainability becomes a reporting duty. EU operators of 500 kW and above now report energy and sustainability KPIs annually, and PUE measurement was updated in ISO/IEC 30134-2:2026.[21,23] Uptime reports that average PUE has barely changed for six years.[64]
  • Virtualisation market realignment. Broadcom's acquisition of VMware and the VCF 9.0 platform, which merges VMs, Kubernetes and private AI, have reshaped the largest private cloud platform and its certifications.[54,56,82]
  • Vendor consolidation in automation and networking. IBM acquired HashiCorp, maker of Terraform, in February 2025, and HPE acquired Juniper Networks in July 2025.[49,76] Cisco's v2.0 CCNA topics add AI and network operations.[75]
  • Procedures and resilience over heroics. Uptime's outage research links most major human-error outages to procedural failures and shows IT and networking causes rising.[42] Combined with SRE practice and ITIL (Version 5),[40,44] this is pushing operations roles towards measurable service levels and automation.
  • Data centres as critical infrastructure. The UK designated data centres as Critical National Infrastructure in 2024, and the EU's NIS2 Directive brings data centre service providers into its scope.[88,89]

12. Limitations

Labour statistics are U.S.-specific and occupation-based, so they approximate rather than measure the titles in this family. The 15-1299 figures used for engineers cover a much broader group, and no government code distinguishes critical facilities engineers from tradespeople or IT engineers.[2,7] Uptime Institute survey results reflect self-selected respondents.[64,69] Credential details change frequently. This article reflects issuer pages as of 2 November 2026, including the rollout of ITIL (Version 5), the CCNA v2.0 exam topics and Nutanix NCP-MCI 7.5.[44,62,75] Several standards (TIA-942-C, BICSI 002, NFPA 75, NFPA 70E, ISO/IEC 30134-2) are paywalled and are described here from publishers' summaries.
