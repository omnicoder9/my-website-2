1. Scope and method

This article covers the Enterprise Applications family, the fourth subgroup of Enterprise IT and Information Systems in the series taxonomy. Part 1.2 (Careers in Systems Administration) and Part 1.3 (Careers in Enterprise Infrastructure) dealt with the servers, networks and platforms underneath business software. This part moves up the stack to the business software itself: enterprise resource planning (ERP), customer relationship management (CRM), human capital management (HCM) and IT service management (ITSM) suites, and the integrations that join them. The central skill is not writing software from scratch but shaping a vendor's product to fit an organisation. SFIA 9 names this directly. Its software configuration skill (PORT), "designing and deploying software product configurations into software environments or platforms", applies typically to "large, complex software, such as ERP and CRM systems", SaaS products and integration platforms.[1]

Author's analysis: what makes this family distinct. Three features recur in the sources. First, the product sets the boundaries. A Salesforce developer works inside governor limits that exist because the platform is multitenant,[9] an SAP developer is steered towards released interfaces to keep the core "clean, standard, and upgrade-stable",[10] and Davenport observed as early as 1998 that an enterprise system "imposes its own logic on a company's strategy, organization, and culture".[11] Second, business process knowledge carries as much weight as technical skill: BLS lists business skills among the important qualities of systems analysts, and SAP's financial accounting certification tests configuration of payables, receivables and the general ledger rather than programming.[12,13] Third, careers are strongly tied to vendor ecosystems, whose credentials, release cycles and acquisitions change faster than the underlying occupations (section 11).

Five points stand out. First, the core analyst occupation is growing much faster than average. BLS projects computer systems analysts to grow 8% against a 3% average for all occupations, and its description of the work (consulting managers on the role of IT, analysing costs and benefits, designing systems "by configuring hardware and software", overseeing configuration, testing, and training end users) reads as a description of packaged-application work.[12] Second, development is splitting. Software developers are projected to grow 10%, partly because of software development for artificial intelligence, while computer programmers are projected to decline 7% because programming work, including through AI, is increasingly automated and some higher-skilled tasks shift to developers.[4,14] In the author's reading this favours platform developers who design solutions over those who only code to specification. Third, consulting is large and growing: management analysts number about 1.1 million, are projected to grow 10%, and BLS expects strong growth at smaller consulting firms that specialise in areas such as information technology. O*NET flags SAP software and Oracle PeopleSoft as "Hot Technologies", which it defines as "requirements frequently included in employer job postings".[15]

The largest employers of systems analysts are computer systems design and related services (23%), finance and insurance (13%), management of companies (10%), government (7%) and healthcare and social assistance (7%).[12] BLS adds that some analysts work as consultants, either directly for an organisation or as contractors, often for IT firms, and that some employers prefer an MBA with an information systems concentration.[12] For software developers the largest employer is again computer systems design and related services (29%), followed by finance and insurance (11%) and software publishers (10%).[4]

4. Frameworks, standards and processes

The service management processes described in Part 1.1 (incident, change, configuration) and the security practices described in Part 1.2 still apply to enterprise applications. This section covers what is distinctive to this family: what an enterprise system is, how it is implemented, how it is extended, how it is integrated, and how it is controlled.

4.1 What counts as an enterprise application

There is no single accepted definition of ERP. Klaus, Rosemann and Gable's review of the literature and survey of experts found "dissenting views on the phenomenon" and concluded that a broadly agreed definition was unlikely.[16] The common thread is integration: a single system, or tightly coupled suite, that supports many business functions on shared data. Davenport's influential 1998 analysis argued that the main cause of failed implementations is business-related rather than technical, because firms fail to reconcile the system's requirements with their actual business needs.[11] That insight explains why so many roles in this family are about analysis and fit rather than code.

4.2 Workforce framework: SFIA 9

SFIA 9 has no "ERP" or "CRM" skill, but several of its skills describe the work precisely. Software configuration (PORT) covers porting configurations across environments, refactoring overlapping configurations and automating deployment, and is defined from level 2 (assisting with configuration tasks) to level 6 (developing organisational policies and leading large deployments).[1] Systems integration and build (SINT) covers integrating "system elements, subsystems and interfaces" into operational systems, including continuous integration and validation against requirements.[17] Application support (ASUP) is "delivering management, technical and administrative services to support and maintain live applications".[18] Business situation analysis (BUSA), which replaced the older single "business analysis" skill, together with requirements definition and management (REQM) and business process improvement (BPRE), covers the analytical work.[8,19]

4.3 Implementation methodology: fit-to-standard

Vendors publish their own implementation methodologies, and consultants are expected to know them. SAP Activate, for example, structures a project in six phases (discover, prepare, explore, realize, deploy and run) supported by preconfigured best-practice processes, quality gates and tools such as SAP Cloud ALM. Its explore phase is built around "fit-to-standard workshops" in which the business validates SAP's standard processes before any extension is designed.[20] The approach reverses the older practice of gathering requirements first and customising the product to match them, and it changes the consultant's job from specifying custom code to steering the business towards the standard.

4.4 Extending the product: clean core and platform limits

Every major platform constrains how it may be extended. SAP's "clean core" guidance sorts extensions into four levels, from level A (built only on publicly released, stable SAP interfaces) to level D (modifications or direct write access to SAP tables, which SAP does not recommend), and positions ABAP Cloud and SAP Business Technology Platform (BTP) as the routes for upgrade-safe extension.[10] Salesforce enforces limits in code: because Apex "runs in a multitenant environment", each synchronous transaction is limited, for example, to 100 SOQL queries, 150 DML statements and 10 seconds of CPU time.[9] ServiceNow offers low-code tools (App Engine Studio, Flow Designer) alongside a web-based development environment.[21] For practitioners the lesson is the same across vendors: good work stays inside the supported extension points, and poor work becomes technical debt at the next upgrade.

4.5 Integration

Hohpe and Woolf's Enterprise Integration Patterns remains the standard vocabulary for connecting applications.[22] It distinguishes four integration styles: file transfer, shared database, remote procedure invocation and messaging.[23] Modern platforms expose APIs described with the OpenAPI Specification, whose version 3.2 was announced in September 2025 with streaming media types and support for the OAuth 2.0 device authorization flow,[24] and they secure those APIs with OAuth 2.0 (RFC 6749).[25] Business-to-business exchange still relies heavily on electronic data interchange (EDI) standards: ASC X12, chartered by ANSI, maintains transaction sets such as the 810 invoice,[26] and UN/EDIFACT, maintained by UN/CEFACT, is the international EDI standard, with directories updated twice a year.[27] Platform-specific tooling sits on top: Workday's no-code Enterprise Interface Builder (EIB) and its Studio design environment, backed by "hundreds of SOAP- and REST-based web service APIs",[28] and MuleSoft's Anypoint Platform with DataWeave transformation and API-led connectivity.[29]

4.6 Controls, audit and data protection

ERP systems hold the books, so they sit directly inside financial controls. Section 404 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act requires public companies' annual reports to contain management's assessment of "the effectiveness of the internal control structure and procedures of the issuer for financial reporting".[30] The PCAOB's AS 2201 requires auditors to understand "how IT affects the company's flow of transactions", treats IT general controls over program changes, access to programs and computer operations as conditions for relying on automated application controls, and notes that benchmarking automated controls "can be especially effective for companies using purchased software".[31] In practice this makes separation of duties, least privilege and configuration change control (NIST SP 800-53 controls AC-5, AC-6 and CM-3) daily concerns for administrators and developers.[32] CRM and HCM systems hold personal data, so the GDPR's requirements for data protection by design and by default (Article 25) and security of processing (Article 32) apply to their configuration.[33]

4.7 Service management on the platforms

ServiceNow is both an enterprise application and the tool many IT teams use to run ITIL processes, so its administrators and developers need service management knowledge as well as platform skills. ServiceNow's Common Service Data Model (CSDM) provides the reference data model for services in the platform,[34] and ITIL (Version 5), released in phases during 2026, is the current version of the ITIL framework.[35]

5. Role profiles

Each profile gives a five-dimension summary, then responsibilities, skills and core competencies, tools, processes, qualifications and credentials, and career moves. "Typical" statements about level and industry are the author's synthesis of the cited sources. Each profile ends with the pay proxy used.

5.1 Enterprise Applications Administrator

  • Domain Enterprise business applications (ERP, CRM, HCM, ITSM)
  • Function Operate
  • Specialization Platform administration, security roles, release and support
  • Level Entry to senior
  • Industry All; largest in IT services, finance and government[12]

The enterprise applications administrator keeps live business systems available, secure and current, often across more than one vendor platform. SFIA's application support skill describes the core of the job: "delivering management, technical and administrative services to support and maintain live applications".[18] Unlike a systems administrator (Part 1.2), the administrator works mostly inside the application: users and security roles, configuration, scheduled jobs, interfaces and vendor releases.

Responsibilities

  • Troubleshoot program and system malfunctions and help users solve application problems.[3]
  • Test, maintain and monitor applications and their interfaces.[3]
  • Provision users and security roles, enforcing separation of duties and least privilege.[32]
  • Apply vendor patches and releases through controlled change, and move configuration between environments.[1,18]
  • Support audit evidence for IT general controls over program changes and access.[31]

Skills and core competencies

  • Platform configuration and environment management.[1]
  • Access control and change control (NIST SP 800-53 AC-5, AC-6, CM-3).[32]
  • Service management: incident, problem and change practice.[35]
  • SFIA: ASUP, PORT, CHMG, IAMT.[8]

Tools

  • SAP, Oracle, Microsoft Dynamics, Salesforce and ServiceNow, all listed among systems analysts' technology skills in O*NET.[3]
  • Platform administration consoles, transport or deployment tools, job schedulers and ITSM tooling.[36,37]

Processes

  • Change and release management, access reviews, incident and problem management, vendor upgrade cycles.[31,35]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Education: BLS gives a bachelor's degree as typical for systems analysts, though not always in computing.[12]
  • Platform administration credentials: Salesforce Certified Platform Administrator, ServiceNow CSA, SAP's system administration track.[36–38]
  • ITIL Foundation.[35]

Career moves

  • Up: senior administrator, platform owner, enterprise applications engineer.
  • Across: business applications analyst, applications security, ITSM.

Pay proxy: computer systems analysts (15-1211), median $105,850 in May 2025.[12]

5.2 Enterprise Applications Engineer

  • Domain Enterprise business applications and their integration and extension layers
  • Function Build
  • Specialization Solution design, extensions, automation, release engineering
  • Level Intermediate to principal
  • Industry All; common in technology firms and large enterprises

The enterprise applications engineer is the technical builder of the business application estate. Where an administrator runs the platforms, the engineer designs and builds what goes on them: extensions, integrations, automated deployments and data flows. The O*NET proxy, computer systems engineers/architects, communicates with clients "to understand specific system requirements", investigates component suitability and directs the analysis, development and operation of complete systems.[5] SFIA's software configuration skill at level 4 and above (designing and refactoring complex configurations, choosing methods and tools, leading peer reviews) describes the senior form of the role.[1]

Responsibilities

  • Translate business requirements into designs that use standard features first and extensions only where needed.[10,20]
  • Build and maintain integrations, automations and extensions across platforms.[17]
  • Automate build, test and deployment pipelines for configuration and code.[1,17]
  • Recommend components and monitor operation to detect problems.[5]

Skills and core competencies

  • Solution design within vendor extension limits (clean core levels; governor limits).[9,10]
  • Integration patterns and API design.[22,24]
  • Scripting and programming on at least one platform.
  • SFIA: PORT, SINT, DESN, ARCH.[8]

Tools

  • Microsoft Power Platform, Apache Kafka, IBM WebSphere MQ, Jenkins and enterprise application integration software appear in O*NET's data for this occupation.[5]
  • SAP BTP and ABAP Cloud; Salesforce and ServiceNow development tools.[10,21]

Processes

  • Design review, release management, continuous integration, regression testing at vendor upgrades.[17]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Education: typically a computing degree; O*NET's survey of computer systems engineers/architects shows mixed routes, with 41% of respondents citing a postsecondary certificate, 27% an associate's and 23% a bachelor's degree as required.[5]
  • Developer credentials on the main platform (for example Salesforce Platform Developer, ServiceNow CAD, SAP ABAP Cloud) and an integration credential such as MuleSoft Developer.[29,39–41]

Career moves

  • Up: lead engineer, solution or technical architect, enterprise architect (TOGAF).[42]
  • Across: enterprise integration engineer, platform engineering.

Pay proxy: computer occupations, all other (15-1299), median $116,580, the group O*NET uses for 15-1299.08;[5] software developers (15-1252), median $135,980.[4]

5.3 ERP Consultant

  • Domain ERP: finance, procurement, supply chain, manufacturing, HR
  • Function Analyze, build
  • Specialization A module or end-to-end process (record-to-report, procure-to-pay, order-to-cash) on one vendor's product
  • Level Intermediate to principal (associate consultant at entry)
  • Industry Consultancies and implementation partners; in-house teams in manufacturing, distribution, public sector

The ERP consultant, usually called a functional consultant, bridges business processes and the ERP product. The consultant runs workshops, maps requirements to the standard product, configures it, and supports testing, data migration, training and cut-over. BLS describes management analysts who "gather and organize information about the problems to be solved", "develop solutions or alternative practices" and "recommend new systems, procedures, or organizational changes",[6] and systems analysts who "oversee the installation and configuration of new systems and customize them for the organization".[12] An ERP consultant does both.

Responsibilities

  • Lead fit-to-standard workshops and confirm solution design.[20]
  • Configure the product for the client's organisation structure and processes (for example, company codes, document types and payment runs in SAP finance).[13]
  • Analyse costs and benefits of system options for managers.[12]
  • Write instruction manuals and train end users; confer with managers to ensure changes are working.[6,12]

Skills and core competencies

  • Deep process knowledge of the module (accounting, supply chain, HR).
  • Requirements and business situation analysis; facilitation and stakeholder management.[19,43]
  • Business, communication and analytical skills (BLS's important qualities for systems analysts).[12]
  • SFIA: CNSL, BUSA, REQM, BPRE, PORT.[8]

Tools

  • SAP S/4HANA, Oracle Fusion Applications and E-Business Suite, JD Edwards, Microsoft Dynamics, NetSuite and Infor, all listed by O*NET as ERP software.[15]
  • Implementation tooling such as SAP Cloud ALM and SAP Signavio Process Navigator.[20]

Processes

  • Vendor implementation methodology (SAP Activate phases), fit-gap analysis, data migration, user acceptance testing, cut-over and hypercare.[20]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Education: a bachelor's degree is typical; business, accounting or MIS degrees are common, and BLS notes some employers prefer an MBA with an information systems concentration.[6,12]
  • Vendor module certifications, for example SAP C_TS4FI (financial accounting) or Oracle Fusion Cloud Implementation Professional exams.[13,44]
  • Optional: IIBA CCBA or CBAP; PMP for those moving into delivery leadership.[45,46]

Career moves

  • Up: senior consultant, solution architect, engagement or delivery manager, practice partner.
  • Across: in-house business applications analyst or ERP product owner; independent contracting (14% of management analysts are self-employed).[6]

Pay proxy: management analysts (13-1111), median $101,860; computer systems analysts (15-1211), median $105,850.[6,12]

5.4 ERP Developer

  • Domain ERP extensions, reports, interfaces, conversions and forms
  • Function Build
  • Specialization A vendor language: ABAP (SAP), PL/SQL and Java (Oracle), X++ or Power Platform (Microsoft)
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All ERP users; heavy in manufacturing, distribution, utilities, public sector and consultancies

The ERP developer writes the code that the standard product does not provide: custom reports, interfaces, data conversions, enhancements, forms and workflows. The current direction across vendors is to build these outside or alongside the core. SAP's back-end developer certification targets developers using the ABAP RESTful Application Programming Model (RAP) and SAP's Joule AI assistant, and its topic weights favour data modelling (61% for "Using ABAP and Building Data Models").[41]

Responsibilities

  • Analyse users' needs and design and develop software to meet them; maintain and document it.[4]
  • Build extensions at the upgrade-safe clean core levels where possible, and refactor legacy modifications.[10]
  • Develop interfaces and data conversions for migrations.[17]
  • Test programs and fix faults.[14]

Skills and core competencies

  • The vendor language and data model (for example ABAP Cloud, CDS and RAP on SAP).[41]
  • Object-oriented design (21–30% of the SAP ABAP Cloud exam).[41]
  • SQL and database skills; Oracle PL/SQL appears in O*NET technology data.[3]
  • SFIA: PROG, PORT, TEST, SINT.[8]

Tools

  • ABAP Development Tools and SAP BTP; Oracle PL/SQL and SQL Developer; Microsoft Power Platform.[3,5,10]
  • Version control and transport or deployment tooling.

Processes

  • Technical specification, code review, unit and regression testing, transport through landscapes, upgrade remediation.[1]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Education: bachelor's degree typical for developers.[4]
  • SAP Certified – Back-End Developer – ABAP Cloud (C_ABAPD): three hours, 67% pass mark, English only.[41]
  • Microsoft Intelligent Applications Builder Associate (AB-410) for Dynamics and Power Platform work.[47]

Career moves

  • Up: senior or lead developer, technical architect.
  • Across: integration engineering, functional consulting.

Pay proxy: software developers (15-1252), median $135,980; computer programmers (15-1251), median $100,390, projected to decline 7%.[4,14]

5.5 CRM Developer

  • Domain CRM: sales, service, marketing and customer portals
  • Function Build
  • Specialization Salesforce, Microsoft Dynamics 365 or another CRM platform; user interface and automation
  • Level Intermediate to senior
  • Industry All customer-facing sectors; financial services, retail, telecoms, technology

The CRM developer builds custom logic, user interfaces and integrations on a CRM platform. The title is vendor-neutral; most postings name a platform, and on Salesforce the role becomes the Salesforce Developer (5.8). On Microsoft's platform, the current developer credential validates the ability "to design, build, and implement AI-powered solutions using Microsoft Power Platform", including Dataverse data models, model-driven and canvas apps, Power Pages sites and cloud flows.[47]

Responsibilities

  • Design and develop custom components, automation and integrations to meet users' needs.[4]
  • Integrate agents and AI features into apps and portals.[47,48]
  • Protect customer personal data through design (GDPR Article 25).[33]

Skills and core competencies

  • Platform data modelling and low-code/pro-code development (Power Fx, Dataverse; Apex, Lightning).[39,47]
  • User experience design for sales and service users.
  • SFIA: PROG, PORT, TEST, HCEV.[8]

Tools

  • Salesforce, Microsoft Power Apps, Power Automate and Power Pages, Copilot Studio.[3,47]

Processes

  • Application lifecycle management, environment strategy and solution packaging.[49]

Qualifications and credentials

  • Salesforce Platform Developer; Microsoft AB-410.[39,47]
  • Note: Microsoft's PL-200 Power Platform Functional Consultant exam was retired on 31 August 2026.[49]

Career moves

  • Up: senior developer, CRM technical architect.
  • Across: integration engineering, CRM consulting.

Pay proxy: software developers (15-1252), median $135,980 in May 2025.[4]

5.6 CRM Administrator

  • Domain CRM platforms
  • Function Operate, build (declarative configuration)
  • Specialization Users and security, data quality, reports, low-code automation
  • Level Entry to senior
  • Industry All; often in sales operations or IT in mid-sized firms and non-profits

The CRM administrator configures and maintains the CRM for its business users. The role is one of the most accessible entry points into enterprise applications, because much of the work is declarative configuration rather than code. O*NET lists CRM products ranging from Salesforce to Blackbaud's Raiser's Edge (a non-profit fundraising CRM) among systems analysts' technology skills, which illustrates the breadth of settings.[3]

Responsibilities

  • Manage users, profiles, permissions and sharing.[38]
  • Maintain objects, fields, page layouts and automation; build reports and dashboards.[38]
  • Keep data clean and compliant; respond to data-subject requests where GDPR applies.[33]
  • Train users and support adoption.[12]

Skills and core competencies

  • Declarative configuration and automation.[38]
  • Data management and data quality.[8]
  • Communication and training skills.[12]
  • SFIA: PORT, ASUP, DATM, IAMT.[8]

Tools

  • Salesforce, Microsoft Dynamics 365 and Power Platform, Oracle Eloqua and other CRM products.[3,47]

Processes

  • Release review, sandbox testing, change control, access reviews.[32]

Qualifications and credentials

  • No degree is strictly required for many postings; BLS notes some employers hire business graduates with relevant skills into systems analyst roles.[12]
  • Salesforce Certified Platform Administrator; Microsoft AB-410 on Dynamics.[38,47]

Career moves

  • Up: senior administrator, CRM platform owner or manager.
  • Across: CRM developer, business applications analyst, sales or revenue operations.

The SAP consultant is the most common vendor-specific form of the ERP consultant (5.3), and the SAP ecosystem is large enough to support distinct functional and technical consultant careers. The Salesforce developer builds custom functionality on Salesforce's multitenant platform. 6.2 The functional–technical spectrum

Author's analysis: one ecosystem, two ladders. Every vendor ecosystem in this family has a functional ladder (administrator or analyst, functional consultant, solution architect) and a technical ladder (developer, technical consultant or engineer, technical architect). The vendors' own credential structures reflect the split: Salesforce separates Platform Administrator from Platform Developer, ServiceNow separates CSA from CAD, and SAP separates module consultant certifications from its ABAP Cloud developer certification.[13,37–41] Movement between the ladders is common at junior levels and becomes harder later, while the solution architect role at the top is where the two meet. Low-code tooling and AI agent builders aimed at both administrators and developers are blurring the lower rungs.[21,47,48]

The labour market data support these moves. Systems analysts are projected to grow 8% and management analysts 10%, while programmers are projected to decline 7%.[6,12,14] Median pay rises from $105,850 for systems analysts to $135,980 for software developers and $175,140 for IT managers.[4,7,12] BLS itself describes the upward step: with experience, analysts may become project managers, IT directors or chief technology officers.[12]

Author's analysis: an unusually open entry door. Compared with infrastructure (Part 1.3), this family offers a realistic route in for people from business functions. BLS notes that some firms hire business or liberal arts graduates into systems analyst roles,[12] the administrator credentials have no formal prerequisites and modest suggested experience,[38] and the consultant's most valuable asset (deep knowledge of accounting, HR or supply chain processes) is something accountants, HR professionals and planners already have. The trade-off is vendor lock-in: a career built on one platform rises and falls with that platform's market, which is why architects and managers in this family tend to hold credentials on more than one.

11. Trends shaping the field

  • The SAP 2027 deadline. Mainstream maintenance for SAP Business Suite 7, including ECC, ends at the end of 2027, with optional extended maintenance to the end of 2030.[50] SAP's 2033 transition option is limited to very large customers and is not an extension of those dates.[51] In the author's view, conversion and clean core remediation will remain the largest single source of SAP consulting and development demand through the end of the decade.
  • AI agents inside the platforms. Salesforce made Agentforce generally available in October 2024, letting admins and developers configure agents through Agent Builder.[48] ServiceNow completed its acquisition of Moveworks in December 2025,[55] Workday completed its acquisition of Sana in November 2025 and in October 2026 opened its developer programme with agent-building tools,[53,65] SAP's developer certification now assumes use of its Joule assistant,[41] and Microsoft's new Power Platform credential centres on Copilot and agents.[47] Salesforce's administrator exam now gives 8% of its weight to Agentforce, and Microsoft's AB-410 tests integrating agents into apps.[38,47]
  • Data and integration consolidation. Salesforce completed its acquisition of Informatica in November 2025, aiming to combine Informatica's data integration, governance and master data management with MuleSoft's application integration; Benioff's stated rationale was that "you have to get your data right to get your AI right".[66]
  • Standard first, extend carefully. SAP Activate's fit-to-standard approach and SAP's clean core levels, Salesforce's governor limits and the growth of low-code tools all push work away from deep customisation and towards configuration, released APIs and side-by-side extensions.[9,10,20,21]
  • Developers over programmers. BLS projects software developers to grow 10% while programmers decline 7%, citing AI automation of repetitive programming tasks.[4,14]
  • Credential churn. Microsoft retired PL-200 in August 2026 and introduced AI-focused business credentials such as AB-410,[47,49] and ITIL (Version 5) rolled out during 2026.[35]
  • AI regulation for HR systems, deferred. The EU's Digital Omnibus on AI, adopted in July 2026, moved the application of high-risk obligations for Annex III systems, which include AI used in employment, from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027.[64] Workday, SuccessFactors and other HCM teams therefore have more time, but not less work, to prepare.

12. Limitations

Labour statistics are U.S.-specific and occupation-based, so they approximate rather than measure vendor-named titles. No government code distinguishes a Salesforce administrator from a systems analyst, or an SAP consultant from a management analyst, and the 15-1299 figures used for engineers cover a much broader group.[5] BLS does not report vendor-specific pay, and this article does not use vendor-sponsored salary or ecosystem surveys. Credential details change frequently and some vendor pages (notably ServiceNow's exam blueprints) render only after sign-in, so exam statistics are given only where the issuer's public page states them; all were checked on 3 November 2026. Several sources are paywalled or summarised from publishers' pages, including the BABOK Guide and Davenport's article.
