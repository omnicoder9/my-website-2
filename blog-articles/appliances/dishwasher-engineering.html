How to read a plate

Reading a plate. The dial's four sectors are Sinner's factors: Chemistry, Temperature, Mechanics and Time. A filled sector is the factor the component mainly buys, a hatched one its secondary factor. Dark chips show what the component is paid in; the rounded chips show whether it is found in household machines, commercial machines or both.

01What is paying for clean?

A dishwasher is usually described as a box that sprays hot soapy water. That description is true and useless, because it cannot explain why a household machine now takes three hours to do what a restaurant machine does in ninety seconds, why the household machine is regulated on kilowatt-hours and gallons while the restaurant machine is regulated on degrees Fahrenheit at a manifold, or why a plastic lunch box still comes out wet after sixty years of engineering. This article argues that all three puzzles have one answer.

In 1959 Herbert Sinner, then head chemist of Henkel's detergent department, set out four parameters that every surface-cleaning operation spends: chemistry, mechanical action, temperature and time 1. The idea, usually drawn as a circle cut into four sectors, is that the sectors trade: a process short of one can make up the deficit from the others. The circle is a statement about substitution, and substitution is exactly what engineering decides. The organising question of this article is therefore not how a dishwasher works but which of the four currencies each design is paying in, and who set the exchange rate.

The answer turns out to be sharp. Household machines are regulated, in the United States and the European Union, on the energy and water they use per cycle 2,3. Neither regime limits cycle duration in the way it limits energy, and a household machine can run overnight, so the cheapest currency at home is time. Commercial machines are regulated on the temperature of the sanitising rinse and on hygiene outcomes 4,5, and a restaurant at the dinner rush cannot wait, so the cheapest currencies there are heat, pump flow and chemistry. The same physics, priced differently, produces two different machines. The rest of the article traces that divergence through history, through each subsystem, and through eight small calculations whose code and output are included at the end.

The subsystems are catalogued as 29 numbered plates. Each plate carries the same four fields (what the component does, which Sinner factor it buys, what it is paid in, and when it breaks) and the same small gauge: a four-sector dial with the primary factor filled and any secondary factor hatched, beside chips for the currencies spent and for where the component is found. The plates are collected into a ledger at the close.

02From the copper boiler to the microcomputer

The first American patent for a dishwashing machine is usually credited to Joel Houghton, whose 1850 device was wooden and hand-cranked and did little more than splash water over the dishes 6. The design that established the principle still in use came from Josephine Cochran of Shelbyville, Illinois. Her application was filed on 31 December 1885 and granted as US patent 355,139 on 28 December 1886 7. Its essential move was to hold the dishes still, in wire compartments, and move the water: hot soapy water was driven up onto the racks instead of the dishes being scrubbed 8. Cochran's company soon offered machines in two sizes, the larger said to rinse and dry 240 dishes in about two minutes, and its customers were hotels and restaurants rather than homes 8. Households of the period rarely had enough hot water to supply such a machine 6.

That early split between trade and home never closed. The business passed to Hobart, whose KitchenAid brand introduced a household dishwasher based on Cochran's design in 1949 9,6. In Europe, Miele built what it describes as Europe's first electric dishwasher in 1929: a round metal tub with two baskets, filled by hand, in which an electrically driven propeller threw water over the load 10. It did not sell, because, in the company's own later account, it was expensive and the households that could afford it employed maids 10. Miele's milestone list then reads like a summary of this article's subsystems: an automatic front-loader with rotating spray arms in 1960, a built-in machine whose water softener regenerated itself in 1967, microcomputer control with sensors in 1978, soil and hardness sensing in 2001 11.

Hygiene arrived as evidence before it arrived as regulation. A swab-rinse survey of about 1,000 New York restaurants published in 1947 found that only 17.5 % used a machine for any of their dishwashing, and that 10.5 % of hand-washed glasses and cups met the acceptability limit of 100 colony-forming units against 35.8 % of machine-washed ones 12,8. The machine was better and still not good enough, which is the problem sanitising-rinse rules were written to solve.

For households, the rules that matter came later and concern resources. The US Department of Energy has set dishwasher standards since 1988 13; the EU introduced energy labels in 1997 and ecodesign requirements in 2010 14. Phosphate builders were removed from US household dishwasher detergent in 16 states in July 2010 15 and limited across the EU from 2017 16. Adoption is still far from universal: 74 % of US homes had a dishwasher in 2024, and 14 % of homes had one they did not use 17, while penetration outside Europe, North America and a few other markets remains low 8. Ownership is not use: in 2015 about 16 million of the 80 million US households with a dishwasher did not run it at all 18.

03Anatomy of a household machine

A modern household dishwasher is a sealed stainless tub with two or three racks, a sump at the bottom, and a single recirculating hydraulic circuit. Water enters through a metered inlet valve and, where the supply is hard, through an ion-exchange softener. It collects in the sump, passes through a filter assembly, and is drawn by a circulation pump through a heater to the spray arms, from which it falls back through the load to the sump. A separate drain pump empties the sump between fills. The detergent and rinse-aid dispensers sit in the door. Figure 2 shows the path.

What the diagram hides is the cycle. A household programme is a sequence of fills, each heated or not, recirculated for a time and drained: typically a pre-rinse, a main wash with detergent, one or more interim rinses, and a final rinse with rinse aid, followed by drying. The water bill is the number of fills times the fill volume; the energy bill is dominated by which fills are heated and how far. The machine does not heat a tank and hold it, as a commercial machine does; it heats a few litres at a time and throws them away.

Because the whole load stays in the tub for the whole programme, a household machine can do something a commercial machine cannot: spend the time currency lavishly. Measured in a laboratory against 150 UK consumers washing the same soiled load by hand, an eco programme used a mean 13.2 L and 1.3 kWh against the consumers' 49.2 L and 1.7 kWh, and took 152 minutes against 60 24,8. The machine won on water and energy and lost on elapsed time, but only machine time; the person's time is the loading and unloading. That is the trade the eco programme institutionalises.

04Four currencies and their exchange rates

Lab experiment E1 makes the substitution explicit with a first-order removal model. Soil disappears at a rate that rises with temperature by an Arrhenius factor (apparent activation energy 40 kJ/mol), with mechanical intensity to the power 0.8, and in proportion to a chemistry factor and a soil-condition factor. The model is calibrated so that a residential eco main wash at 45 °C takes 120 minutes to remove 99 % of the soil. The constants are illustrative, chosen to expose the mechanism rather than to predict any product.

Holding everything else equal, raising the wash to 55 °C cuts the time to 75.7 minutes, and an intensive programme at 65 °C with somewhat more flow reaches 39.8 minutes. A commercial door machine's tank at 66 °C, with nothing else changed, would still need 47.0 minutes. Five times the pump flow brings that to 13.0 minutes, three times the chemical strength to 4.3, and fresh soil (ware washed minutes after use, usually scraped or pre-rinsed at a sprayer) to 130 seconds, a speed-up of 55.5 times over the eco wash.

The decomposition in Figure 3 is the first finding. No single currency pays for the commercial speed-up. In logarithmic terms temperature contributes 23.3 %, mechanics 32.1 %, chemistry 27.4 % and soil condition 17.3 %. Buying all of it with temperature would need water at 160 °C. The restaurant machine has no secret; it multiplies four ordinary advantages, each worth a factor of two to four, and it can do so because nothing about its duty cycle prices those advantages the way household regulation does.

Figure 4 draws the same model as iso-clean curves. Moving right along a curve buys time with heat; moving down between curves buys it with flow. The household eco point sits at the top left, where both heat and flow are cheap to give up because the programme can simply run longer.

Where the energy of a household cycle actually goes is the subject of experiment E2, which follows a typical eco-style sequence of four fills totalling 12.4 L (3.28 US gal), with the main wash heated to 50 °C and the final rinse to 65 °C. The modelled cycle uses 0.96 kWh. Heating the water itself accounts for only 33.0 % of it. Heating the dishes accounts for 23.7 % and heating the machine's own tub, racks and plastic parts for 19.9 %; losses and pumps take the rest. Leaving the final rinse unheated would cut the cycle to 0.564 kWh, 41.2 % less, but the heat stored in the load for drying goes with it. That trade, between a cheap wash and a dry load, recurs in every section below.

05The water path

The household water path has six components. Five buy mechanical action or keep it effective; the softener buys chemistry.

A01

Inlet valve and flow meter

Primary factor mechanics.

Does
Opens a solenoid valve and meters each fill, by counting flow-meter pulses or by level, so the sump holds exactly the volume the pump can recirculate.
Buys
Mechanical action indirectly: every litre sprayed was first admitted here.
Paid in
Water. The fill volume multiplied by the number of fills is the machine's whole water bill.
Breaks when
A fill falls below the pump's minimum and the pump draws air: spray pressure collapses mid-wash.
A02

Ion-exchange softener

Primary factor chemistry.

Does
A resin bed swaps calcium and magnesium for sodium before water reaches the tub; brine from the salt reservoir regenerates it every few cycles 11.
Buys
Chemistry: it removes the hardness that would otherwise consume builder and leave scale.
Paid in
Salt, and the water used to flush brine. Lab E8 puts salt near 10 g per cycle at 20 °dH.
Breaks when
The salt runs out or the hardness setting is wrong: films and scale return within a few cycles.
A03

Sump and circulation pump

Primary factor mechanics.

Does
Draws wash liquor from the sump and pushes it to the arms many times per minute. Tracer studies show the fastest water, and most kinetic energy, is at the nozzle exit 25.
Buys
Mechanical action: flow and pressure at the jets.
Paid in
Electricity, mostly as pump work, plus the heel of water that must stay in the sump to prime it.
Breaks when
Foam or a starved sump entrains air; the pump cavitates and the jets weaken.
A04

Rotating spray arms

Primary factor mechanics, secondary time.

Does
Reaction jets spin the arm and sweep an annulus of the rack each revolution. Direct impact covers only part of the load; most surfaces are wetted by water falling back through it 26.
Buys
Mechanical action, spread over the rack by rotation, so coverage accumulates with time.
Paid in
Time: a point under the outer nozzles is struck for about 1 % of each revolution (Lab E5).
Breaks when
A tall item blocks the arm or shades a region; corners outside the swept circle rely on run-off.
A05

Filtration

Primary factor mechanics, secondary chemistry.

Does
A coarse screen, fine mesh and microfilter in the sump keep food particles out of the recirculated liquor; commercial machines use scrap screens over each tank.
Buys
Mechanical action stays effective because soil is not re-sprayed; detergent is not spent on it.
Paid in
Capital, and the owner's time: many domestic filters are cleaned by hand, and the EU treats drain filters as parts that end users must be able to buy 22.
Breaks when
A clogged filter starves the pump and redeposits grit on glasses.
A06

Drain pump and backflow protection

Primary factor mechanics.

Does
Empties the sump between fills through a hose run high or through an air gap, so waste water cannot siphon back into the tub or the supply.
Buys
Mechanical removal of dissolved soil; each drain resets the dilution in Lab E4.
Paid in
Water: the heel left behind after draining sets how much each rinse must dilute.
Breaks when
A kinked or low hose lets soiled water return; a failed check valve refills the sump with it.

Why the water floor sits where it does

Soil dissolved in the main wash leaves the machine in two ways: down the drain, and, for the fraction still clinging to the load and sitting in the sump heel after each drain, on into the next fill. Every rinse fill of volume V dilutes that carried-over fraction by the ratio of the retained volume v to V + v. Experiment E4 turns this into a design rule. For a fixed quantity of rinse water, splitting it into more, smaller fills always lowers the carry-over, and two curves in Figure 6 coincide exactly because only the ratio of heel to fill matters.

The limit is the circulation pump. It needs a minimum fill in the sump to stay primed; below it the pump draws air and the jets collapse. With a minimum fill of 3.0 L and a 0.30 L heel, reaching a carry-over of one part in a thousand takes 3 rinse fills plus the main wash, 12.0 L or 3.17 US gal, which lands just under the 3.3 gal limit the US has adopted for 2027 2. Shrinking the pump's minimum fill to 2.0 L cuts the total to 10.0 L, 16.7 % less. Halving the heel at the 3.0 L minimum changes nothing at all, 12.0 L, because the saving is less than one whole fill. The floor on household water use is set by pump and sump geometry and moves in whole fills; it is not set by detergent chemistry.

What a spray arm actually hits

A rotating arm sweeps a circle, and racks are rectangular. For an arm whose tips just reach the edges of a square rack, 21.46 % of the rack area, 1 − π/4, lies in corners the jets never pass over. Experiment E5 places eight nozzles at radii from 3 to 24 cm with a 1.6 cm jet footprint and finds that only 43.5 % of the rack plane is struck directly at all. The geometry also concentrates water near the hub: a point on the innermost ring sits under a jet 8.0 times longer per revolution than a point on the outermost, so equal-flow nozzles would overdose the centre. Equalising areal flux needs nozzle flows proportional to radius, with the outermost nozzle taking about 22 % of the arm's flow.

These numbers agree with direct observation. Positron emission particle tracking of a radioactive tracer inside a household dishwasher found a periodic sequence of pumping, ejection, impact, downfall and recirculation lasting a few seconds; the highest velocities were at ejection, raising pump speed raised ejection velocity but had a negligible effect on the downfall, and densely packed zones and the edges of the sump pool were slow or stagnant 25. The researchers' summary was that jet impact is very localised and covers a small share of the crockery, most wetting comes from water running down through the load, and plates shed it in about a tenth of a second 26,27.

Hard water and the softener

Calcium and magnesium precipitate as scale on heating and compete with the detergent for builder. Household machines in hard-water regions therefore carry a small ion-exchange column regenerated with salt. Experiment E8 sizes one: 0.35 L of resin at an operating capacity of 1.0 eq/L, regenerated with 120 g of salt per litre of resin, softening 12 L per cycle. At 20 °dH each cycle brings 85.6 milliequivalents of hardness, the column lasts 4.09 cycles between regenerations, and salt use is 10.27 g per cycle; unsoftened, the same water could deposit up to 4.28 g of calcium carbonate per cycle. Regeneration uses 2.05 times the stoichiometric amount of sodium, which is why salt consumption, not resin volume, is the running cost. Commercial machines rarely carry an internal softener; the building supply is treated instead.

06Heat, and the dose that sanitises

Heat does two jobs in a dishwasher, and the designs separate them. In the wash it speeds removal; in the final rinse it delivers a thermal dose to whatever microorganisms survive, and stores heat for drying.

B01

Wash heater

Primary factor temperature.

Does
A tubular element in the tub or, now more often, a flow-through heater on the pump outlet raises the main-wash and final-rinse fills to the programme temperature.
Buys
Temperature, which speeds soil removal and, in the final rinse, stores drying heat in the load.
Paid in
Energy. Lab E2 finds only a third of it heats water; the rest heats dishes, machine and losses.
Breaks when
Scale insulates the element; a failed sensor lets the programme run cold without warning.
B02

Booster heater

Primary factor temperature.

Does
Raises building hot water to the sanitising final-rinse temperature, which the US Food Code puts at 180 °F or more at the manifold on most machines 4.
Buys
Temperature for the thermal dose; it contributes little to cleaning.
Paid in
Energy and capital: a separate electrical supply and a heater sized to the rinse flow.
Breaks when
Undersized for the rush: consecutive racks drain it and the rinse falls below the floor.
B03

Tank heater

Primary factor temperature, secondary time.

Does
Keeps a recirculating wash tank at 150 to 165 °F between racks, depending on machine type 4.
Buys
Temperature, held continuously so that every rack sees hot liquor without waiting.
Paid in
Energy, including when nothing is being washed: ENERGY STAR counts tank heat in the idle rate 28.
Breaks when
Low utilisation: at a few racks an hour idle heat dominates the energy per rack (Lab E7).
B04

Heat recovery

Primary factor temperature.

Does
Exhaust or drain heat exchangers preheat incoming water from the machine's own vapour; ENERGY STAR records list the recovery device and the hot-water energy it offsets 29.
Buys
Temperature, bought back from heat that would leave through the hood or drain.
Paid in
Capital, plus cleaning of heat-exchanger surfaces.
Breaks when
Fouled exchangers lose effectiveness quietly; nothing in the wash result shows it.
B05

Sanitising final rinse

Primary factor temperature, secondary time.

Does
A final rinse hot enough and long enough that the ware surface accumulates the NSF dose of 3,600 heat unit equivalents 30,31.
Buys
Temperature integrated over time: a thermal dose, not a cleaning action.
Paid in
Energy and water: every rack gets fresh water heated to the rinse temperature.
Breaks when
The surface, not the water, fails to reach temperature; Lab E3 shows the post-rinse seconds carry most of the dose.

Two ways to count a dose

US commercial practice counts the dose in heat unit equivalents. NSF's 1996 annex, reproduced by a Canadian health authority, assigns one HUE to one second of plate surface at 143 °F and 346.8 HUE per second at 165 °F, caps the rate there, and deems a surface sanitised when the running total reaches 3,600 30. The residential certification standard NSF/ANSI 184 uses the same heat-unit table 31, and a residential machine, rinsing cooler than a commercial one, compensates with a longer final rinse 32. Hospital washer-disinfectors count differently: ISO 15883 defines A0 as the equivalent time in seconds at 80 °C with an assumed z-value of 10 K 33,34. A patent on multi-tank dishwasher hygiene notes that A0 is the analogue of the NSF heat units built on a different test organism, and that the method had not been used to assess commercial dishwashers in Europe 35.

Experiment E3 recovers the NSF table's slope from its own endpoints: z = 8.660 °F, or 4.81 K. That is about twice as steep as the A0 convention, so the two systems disagree about how much a few extra degrees are worth. At a constant surface of 155 °F the NSF dose takes 2.47 minutes; at 160 °F, 0.65 minutes. Because of the cap, no surface can reach 3,600 HUE in less than 3,600/346.8 = 10.4 seconds, whatever the water temperature. The same surfaces would need 77.4 minutes at 160 °F to reach A0 600, the ISO level for items that are not heat-resistant-pathogen risks 34. Panel A of Figure 8 plots both.

What the manifold thermometer cannot see

The US Food Code regulates what can be measured easily: the fresh sanitising rinse must enter the manifold at no more than 194 °F and at no less than 165 °F on a stationary-rack single-temperature machine or 180 °F on all others 4. The dose, however, accrues on the ware surface, which is why inspectors are told to check that utensil surfaces reach 160 °F with irreversible registering indicators 36 and why guidance notes that sanitisation depends on heat accumulated through wash, power rinse and final rinse together 32.

Experiment E3 models a plate surface that follows the spray water with a 10 s time constant and cools in air with a 240 s one, both illustrative. A door-machine profile of 50 s wash at 155 °F, 10 s rinse at 182 °F and 30 s in the closed machine accumulates 13201 HUE and passes. Of that dose, 2.8 % comes from the wash, 18.1 % from the rinse itself, and 78.8 % from the half-minute after the rinse stops, while the plate is still above 165 °F. A 170 °F rinse also passes in this model (5706 HUE), and the minimum 10 s rinse temperature is 166.7 °F.

That apparent margin is an artefact of the slow-cooling assumption, and the sensitivity table below is the finding. If the load cools quickly, with a 20 s air time constant, and the door opens 10 s after the rinse, the minimum rinse temperature rises to 182.0 °F, close to the Food Code's 180 °F. With the slow-cooling load it is 169.7 °F. With the door opened the instant the rinse stops, no rinse up to the 194 °F ceiling reaches the dose in ten seconds. The number a regulator can read, manifold temperature, controls a dose that is largely earned after the rinse, in seconds governed by the ware's heat capacity and the operator's habits.

Household machines face the same arithmetic with more time. A residential sanitise option with a 10-minute final rinse at 155 °F accumulates 14,509 HUE and a 69 °C rinse 19,909, both far past the threshold; an eco programme's 50 °C rinse earns 5. Eco programmes are not designed to sanitise, and nothing in the energy rules asks them to. Epidemiology offers no clean verdict on whether that matters: a Swedish questionnaire study associated hand dishwashing with fewer childhood allergies, but its design could not establish cause 37,8. What is established is that dishwashers select their own microbial residents. Door seals in 62 % of 189 domestic machines sampled across 101 towns and communities carried fungi, and more than half of those held the black yeasts Exophiala dermatitidis and E. phaeomuriformis, which tolerated heat, salt, detergent and both acid and alkaline water 38.

07Chemistry

Chemistry is the factor household regulation pushed hardest, twice: once to remove phosphates, once to make low-temperature eco programmes work.

C01

Detergent dispenser and dosing

Primary factor chemistry.

Does
Releases a tablet or powder into the main wash at a set step; commercial machines meter liquid detergent into the wash tank with a dosing pump 39.
Buys
Chemistry at the moment it is needed rather than in the pre-rinse.
Paid in
Chemicals, bought per cycle or per litre of tank make-up.
Breaks when
The door compartment opens into a blocked spray path and the tablet never dissolves fully.
C02

Enzymes

Primary factor chemistry, secondary time.

Does
Proteases and amylases hydrolyse egg, meat and starch films at moderate temperature; modern formulations specify amylases active at low temperature 40.
Buys
Chemistry that substitutes for heat, provided it is given time.
Paid in
Chemicals and time: enzyme-led eco programmes are long because the enzymes work slowly at 45 to 50 °C.
Breaks when
Too hot or too harsh: complexing agents can strip the calcium that stabilises proteases 41.
C03

Builders and complexing agents

Primary factor chemistry.

Does
Bind hardness ions and keep soil dispersed. Since phosphate limits, carbonate, MGDA-type complexing agents and crystal-growth inhibitors do the job phosphates did 41,16.
Buys
Chemistry: protects the surfactant and bleach from hardness and prevents redeposition.
Paid in
Chemicals, in grams per dose that the EU caps for phosphorus at 0.3 g 16.
Breaks when
Hard water beyond the builder's capacity: filming and spotting, noticed after the 2010 US switch 15.
C04

Rinse aid

Primary factor chemistry.

Does
A low-foaming surfactant dosed into the final rinse so water sheets off rather than beading; in conveyor machines it is mixed into the fresh final-rinse water 42.
Buys
Chemistry that thins the film left to evaporate, which is what makes drying possible (Lab E6).
Paid in
Chemicals, a few millilitres per cycle.
Breaks when
Overdosing leaves a film; underdosing leaves spots and wet plastics.
C05

Chemical sanitiser

Primary factor chemistry.

Does
Low-temperature commercial machines sanitise with a chlorine solution instead of hot water; the wash must still reach 120 °F 4 and chlorine is typically held at 50 to 100 ppm 36.
Buys
Chemistry in place of the thermal dose.
Paid in
Chemicals, and drying time: ware leaves at a low temperature and does not flash-dry.
Breaks when
Concentration drifts when the container runs empty; only a test strip will show it.

Phosphate builders once made up as much as 8.7 % of US household dishwasher detergent; from July 2010 sixteen states limited phosphorus to 0.5 %, commercial products were exempt, and consumers noticed films on glassware 15. The EU set a limit of 0.3 g of phosphorus in the standard dose of consumer automatic dishwasher detergent from 1 January 2017 16. The replacement chemistry visible in recent patents combines sodium percarbonate bleach with a TAED activator or manganese catalyst, an organic complexing agent such as MGDA at 10 to 50 % of the formula, sodium carbonate, a few percent of non-ionic surfactant, and crystal-growth inhibitors against scale 41.

Enzymes are the chemistry that substitutes most directly for heat. Proteases attack egg and meat residues and amylases attack starch; formulations now specify amylases selected for activity at 25 °C, and high amylase levels are claimed to prevent gritty residues 40. The trade-off runs inside the tablet: the complexing agents that replace phosphate can extract the structural calcium that stabilises proteases 41. In Sinner's terms an enzyme-led eco programme spends chemistry and time to save temperature; it is slow because enzymes are slow at 45 °C.

Commercial machines split the chemistry differently. Detergent is metered into a wash tank that is reused rack after rack, and rinse aid is mixed into fresh final-rinse water 39,42. Where the machine sanitises chemically, the wash must still reach 120 °F 4, chlorine is typically held at 50 to 100 ppm, and verification is by test kit 36.

08Drying: the bill for a cool wash

Drying is where the time currency is spent last and where regulation bites visibly. The EU ecodesign rules set a minimum drying performance index for the eco programme alongside the cleaning index 22, and the regulation's own review clause names drying performance as a topic 3.

E01

Residual heat and condensation

Primary factor temperature, secondary time.

Does
A hot final rinse leaves heat in the load; vapour condenses on the cooler stainless tub walls and drains away.
Buys
Temperature stored in the load, spent slowly.
Paid in
Energy in the final rinse and minutes of drying time.
Breaks when
Thin plastics hold too little heat to evaporate their own film (Lab E6).
E02

Zeolite sorption drying

Primary factor temperature.

Does
A zeolite bed adsorbs moisture from the tub air and releases heat; the next wash's heating drives the water back out. BSH reports up to 0.2 kWh saved per cycle 19.
Buys
Temperature, generated in the drying phase from heat banked during the wash.
Paid in
Capital: a mineral container, a fan and ducting.
Breaks when
Plastics remain the hard case even for active drying systems 43.
E03

Automatic door opening

Primary factor time.

Does
Near the end of the programme the door is pushed ajar so saturated air escapes and room air replaces it.
Buys
Time: drying continues after the programme ends, with no extra heater.
Paid in
Time and some vapour released into the kitchen.
Breaks when
Cabinet fronts under the gap see repeated steam; surrounds need to tolerate it.
E04

Heat pump

Primary factor temperature.

Does
A small refrigerant circuit heats the wash water; in V-ZUG's design the eco programme heats with the heat pump alone 20,44.
Buys
Temperature, at a coefficient of performance above one.
Paid in
Capital and time: heat arrives slowly, and the EU requires the refrigerant to be named on the casing 22.
Breaks when
Claimed savings vary with the reference: about 50 % in one award citation, 30 % on a later product page.
E05

Blowers and drying zones

Primary factor temperature.

Does
Conveyor machines end in a drying zone that blows heated air across the ware before it reaches the unload table; waste air is extracted 45.
Buys
Temperature, applied as forced convection.
Paid in
Energy, plus exhaust handling.
Breaks when
Wet racks at the unload end slow the whole line.

Experiment E6 asks whether a hot item can evaporate its own water. Cooling 30 K from a 65 °C final rinse, a 5 mm porcelain plate releases 306 kJ per square metre. Evaporating a 50 µm film from both faces needs 238 kJ per square metre, so the plate can dry itself with 1.29 times the heat it needs. A 3 mm glass manages only 0.79, and a 1.5 mm polypropylene box 0.33. Rinse aid thins the film; at an illustrative 20 µm the ratios rise to 3.21 for porcelain and 1.99 for glass, but the polypropylene box still stops at 0.82. The box could only dry itself with a film of 16.4 µm per face or less, or with a final rinse of 71.5 °C. Raising the whole final rinse of experiment E2 by 6.5 K to dry one plastic item would cost 0.063 kWh, 6.6 % of the cycle.

This explains the drying technologies that followed the move to cooler programmes. None of them adds heat to the rinse. Condensation drying uses the stainless tub as the cold surface. Zeolite sorption drying, developed by BSH's Dillingen plant with the Bavarian Center for Applied Energy Research in 2008, adsorbs vapour and releases heat, then gives the water back into the next wash's heating; BSH reports up to 0.2 kWh or 20 % saved per cycle 19. Automatic door opening simply lets the drying continue after the programme ends. Heat pumps move the heating itself to a coefficient of performance above one; V-ZUG's heat-pump dishwasher was described in a 2016 design award as the world's first and as saving roughly half the energy 20, while the company's later product page claims 30 % 44. BSH's own presentation to ENERGY STAR still listed drying plastics as an unsolved pain point 43.

Adaptation depends on use, and use is where the laboratory numbers erode. Video and diary observation in 200 households in four countries found full capacity used in only about a third of cycles, an average selected programme temperature of 59 °C, and in Italy nearly half of all items pre-treated before loading against about a tenth in Germany 47. Pre-rinsing by hand before loading was estimated in a US extension study at about 100 L, against about 4 L for the machine's own pre-rinse 48. A survey of more than 5,000 European households found about 15 % of items still washed by hand where a machine was available 49, and six-country modelling put the saving of moving from manual to machine washing at 50 to 60 % for energy, carbon and cost 50. The EU now requires manufacturers to tell users that pre-rinsing is not recommended and that loading to capacity saves energy and water 22.

Automatic programmes that read soil are a partial answer. A consumer-relevant test procedure for automatic programmes found that they respond to load and soil, but that their energy and water use can differ considerably from the eco programme on which labels are based 46. Globally, the largest water saving available is behavioural, in how dishes are washed by hand 51,52.

10Commercial machines: buying time back

Commercial warewashers are a family of four architectures that trade capital for throughput. Undercounter machines are small stationary-rack cabinets. Door or hood machines take a rack in, close, wash for about a minute and rinse. Rack conveyors pull racks through a sequence of zones. Flight-type machines carry ware standing in pegs on a continuous belt. US shipments in 2017 were 75,725 units, of which 67 % were ENERGY STAR certified 53.

Throughput arithmetic is written into the NSF listings: racks per hour for a stationary-rack machine is 3,600 times racks per cycle divided by cycle time in seconds including an assumed load time, 5 s for a straight-through door machine, 7 s for a corner machine and 30 s for a front-opening one; for a conveyor it is belt speed times 60 divided by rack length, and for a flight machine dishes per hour follow from belt speed, belt width and peg pitch 5. A three-tank conveyor rated at 330 racks of 20 by 20 inches an hour is also rated at 8,250 dishes an hour and 0.72 gal of final rinse per rack 54.

Water in a conveyor flows backwards. Fresh final-rinse water is sprayed on the ware, collected in the post-wash tank, pumped over the ware again, and overflows tank to tank against the direction of travel, with a bypass directly to the pre-wash tank; the drain takes water that has been used several times 42,45. That cascade is the commercial answer to the dilution problem of experiment E4: instead of many small fills in one chamber, one stream reused in series.

Energy per rack is the subject of experiment E7, using ENERGY STAR's idle-rate and water-per-rack limits for high-temperature machines 28 and illustrative wash energy and cycle times. At full rate a door machine uses 0.488 kWh per rack, of which 47.4 % is spent in the building's water heater before the water reaches the machine, 17.8 % in the booster and 34.8 % in the machine's own wash. At ten racks an hour it spends 8.5 % of its energy idling, and a conveyor 22.6 %. Per rack, the undercounter machine never loses to the door machine within its own capacity; the choice between them is set by peak demand. A conveyor with automatic pump stop beats the door machine above 6.9 racks an hour. If its pumps run whenever it is switched on, the break-even rises to 21.2 racks an hour, and at ten racks an hour it uses 0.647 kWh per rack against 0.504 with auto-stop, 28 % more. ENERGY STAR records note whether a machine has an energy-saver mode for exactly this reason 29.

12The rules, and where they are moving

US federal standards for residential dishwashers date from 1988 and compliance is tested under appendix C1 to subpart B of 10 CFR part 430 13. A 2012 direct final rule set the limits that took effect in May 2013: 307 kWh a year and 5.0 gal per cycle for standard-size machines 21,55. A 2016 review declined to tighten them 56. In April 2024 the Department adopted, by direct final rule on a joint recommendation of manufacturers and efficiency advocates, limits of 223 kWh a year and 3.3 gal per cycle for standard machines (174 kWh and 3.1 gal for compact ones), with compliance required from 23 April 2027 2,57. The advocates estimated a 15 % energy reduction, 0.31 quadrillion Btu and 240 billion gallons of water saved over 30 years of shipments 57. ENERGY STAR version 7.0 already asks for 240 kWh and 3.2 gal 55.

In May 2025 the Department proposed to rescind the regulatory water limits entirely, arguing that its authority likely does not extend to dishwasher water use, which would return standard machines to the statutory 6.5 gal per cycle 23. As of the Department's product page consulted for this article the rescission was still listed as a proposal 13. Experiment E4 suggests what is at stake: the water floor is a pump-and-sump design constant that moves in whole fills, so a limit either forces a redesign of the sump or it does not bind at all.

The EU took a different route. Since 1 March 2021 Regulation (EU) 2019/2022 has required every household dishwasher to offer a programme named eco, set as default, with an energy efficiency index below 63, tightened to below 56 from March 2024 for machines of ten or more place settings; the eco programme must also clean better than a reference machine and dry adequately 3,22. Programme names such as normal, daily and standard are banned, standby is capped at 0.5 W, and network connections must be off by default. The regulation is also a repairability law: pumps, heaters, heat pumps, circuit boards and sensors must be available to professional repairers for seven years after the last unit is sold, and door seals, drain filters, racks and spray arms to anyone for ten years, delivered within 15 working days 22,14. The labelling companion is Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/2017 58. A review was due by 25 December 2025 3 and a revision is expected under the ecodesign work plan 59.

The two jurisdictions made one choice in common and one differently. Both price energy and water per cycle and leave duration free, which is what makes long eco programmes the rational design. The EU additionally dictates that the frugal programme be the default and that the machine be repairable, treating the appliance's life rather than its cycle as the unit of account.

13The ledger, and the answer

The question was what pays for clean. At home the answer is time. The eco programme, the enzyme tablet, the soil sensor, residual-heat and door-opening drying and the heat pump all spend minutes to save kilowatt-hours or litres, because the rules price kilowatt-hours and litres and leave minutes free. The residual costs of that bargain land where the physics says they must: on plastics that cannot dry themselves, on programmes that no longer sanitise, and on a water floor set by the pump.

In the kitchen of a restaurant the answer is everything except time. The commercial machine is not a cleverer device; lab E1 shows it multiplies four ordinary factors of two to four, and lab E7 shows its energy is governed less by washing than by idling and by the building's water heater. Its hygiene rule counts a dose that is mostly earned in seconds nobody measures, which is why the rinse temperature is set high enough to cover a fast-cooling load.

Cochran's patent held the dishes still and moved the water. Everything since has been a negotiation over how fast, how hot, how chemically and for how long that water moves, and the negotiating position of each design is written in what its owner is charged for.

      f"footprint width {w} cm")

# E6  Drying: can an item's stored heat evaporate its own water film?
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
def e6():
    hdr("E6", "Drying -- stored sensible heat vs latent heat of the film")
    dT = 65.0 - 35.0   # K, final-rinse temperature down to unload temperature
    items = [  # name, thickness m, density, specific heat   ILLUSTRATIVE
        ("porcelain plate", 0.005, 2400.0, 850.0),
        ("soda-lime glass", 0.003, 2500.0, 840.0),
        ("stainless cutlery", 0.002, 7900.0, 500.0),
        ("polypropylene box", 0.0015, 905.0, 1920.0),
        ("silicone lid", 0.002, 1150.0, 1460.0),
    ]
    films = [("no rinse aid", 50e-6), ("with rinse aid", 20e-6)]
    p(f"sensible heat released cooling {dT:.0f} K vs latent heat to evaporate a film "
      f"on both faces (L = {L_EVAP/1e6:.2f} MJ/kg)")
    p("")
    p(f"{'item':20s} {'stored kJ/m2':>13s} " +
      " ".join(f"{'need '+f[0]:>22s} {'ratio':>6s}" for f in films))
    rows = []
    for name, d, rho, c in items:
        stored = rho * c * d * dT
        entry = {"name": name, "stored": stored / 1000.0}
        s = f"{name:20s} {stored/1000:13.1f} "
        for fn, hf in films:
            need = 2.0 * hf * RHO_W * L_EVAP
            ratio = stored / need
            entry[fn] = {"need": need / 1000.0, "ratio": ratio}
            s += f"{need/1000:22.1f} {ratio:6.2f}"
        rows.append(entry)
        p(s)
    # the film thickness at which each item just dries itself
    p("")
    p("film thickness per face that each item can just evaporate by itself:")
    crit = {}
    for name, d, rho, c in items:
        stored = rho * c * d * dT
        hcrit = stored / (2.0 * RHO_W * L_EVAP)
        crit[name] = hcrit * 1e6
        p(f"  {name:20s} {hcrit*1e6:6.1f} um")
    # final-rinse temperature a polypropylene item would need with rinse aid
    name, d, rho, c = items[3]
    need = 2.0 * 20e-6 * RHO_W * L_EVAP
    dT_need = need / (rho * c * d)
    p("")
    p(f"polypropylene with rinse aid needs a {dT_need:.1f} K drop -> final rinse "
      f"{35.0+dT_need:.1f} C instead of 65 C")
    # cost of raising the whole final rinse (water + E2 thermal masses) by that much
    extra_K = 35.0 + dT_need - 65.0
    heat_cap = 3.2 * CP_W + (13.0 * 840 + 1.6 * 500 + 6.0 * 500 + 4.0 * 1700)
    extra = heat_cap * extra_K
    p(f"raising the E2 final rinse (3.2 L + load + machine, {heat_cap/1000:.1f} kJ/K) by "
      f"{extra_K:.1f} K costs {extra/KWH:.3f} kWh per cycle "
      f"({extra/KWH/OUT['E2']['total_kWh']*100:.1f} % of the E2 cycle) to dry one plastic box")
    OUT["E6"] = {"dT": dT, "rows": rows, "crit_um": crit,
                 "pp_dT_need": dT_need, "pp_rinse_C": 35.0 + dT_need,
                 "extra_K": extra_K, "extra_kWh": extra / KWH,
                 "extra_share": extra / KWH / OUT['E2']['total_kWh']}

# E7  Commercial machines: energy per rack against throughput

def e7():
    hdr("E7", "Commercial machines -- energy per rack vs racks actually washed")
    GAL = 3.785411784
    # machine archetypes at ENERGY STAR limits (high temp) with generic cycle
    # data; wash energy per rack and cycle times ILLUSTRATIVE
    machines = [
        # name, idle kW, gal/rack, cycle s (incl. load), rated RPH, wash kWh/rack
        ("undercounter", 0.30, 0.86, 150.0, None, 0.12, 0.0),
        ("door type", 0.55, 0.89, 65.0, None, 0.17, 0.0),
        ("single-tank conveyor", 1.20, 0.70, None, 200.0, 0.14, 0.0),
        ("conveyor, pumps left running", 1.20, 0.70, None, 200.0, 0.14, 1.5),
    ]
    booster_dT = f2c(180) - f2c(140)     # K, building 140 F -> 180 F
    building_dT = f2c(140) - f2c(55)     # K, mains 55 F -> 140 F
    p(f"final-rinse water: booster lift {booster_dT:.1f} K, building water heater lift "
      f"{building_dT:.1f} K (water heater at 80 % efficiency)")
    res = {}
    table = []
    for name, idle, gpr, tc, rph, wash, run_kw in machines:
        rph_max = rph if rph else 3600.0 / tc
        busy_per_rack = 3600.0 / rph_max
        L = gpr * GAL
        e_boost = L * CP_W * booster_dT / KWH
        e_bldg = L * CP_W * building_dT / KWH / 0.80
        e_active = wash + e_boost + e_bldg
        curve = []
        for n in [2, 3, 5, 7, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 80, 100, 130, 160, 200]:
            if n > rph_max + 1e-9:
                continue
            busy_h = n * busy_per_rack / 3600.0
            e_idle = (idle + run_kw) * max(0.0, 1.0 - busy_h)
            per = (e_idle + n * e_active) / n
            curve.append([n, per, e_idle / (e_idle + n * e_active)])
        res[name] = {"idle": idle, "run_kw": run_kw, "gpr": gpr, "rph_max": rph_max,
                     "e_active": e_active, "e_boost": e_boost, "e_bldg": e_bldg,
                     "wash": wash, "curve": curve}
        p(f"{name:22s} RPH max {rph_max:6.1f}; active kWh/rack {e_active:.3f} "
          f"(wash {wash:.2f}, booster {e_boost:.3f}, building {e_bldg:.3f})")
    p("")
    p(f"{'racks/h':>8s} " + " ".join(f"{n[:22]:>22s}" for n in res))
    ns = sorted({pt[0] for m in res.values() for pt in m["curve"]})
    for n in ns:
        cells = []
        for m in res.values():
            v = [pt for pt in m["curve"] if pt[0] == n]
            cells.append(f"{v[0][1]:9.3f} kWh ({v[0][2]*100:4.1f}% idle)" if v else " " * 22)
        p(f"{n:8d} " + " ".join(f"{c:>22s}" for c in cells))
    # crossovers
    def per(name, n):
        m = res[name]
        busy_h = n / m["rph_max"]
        e_idle = (m["idle"] + m["run_kw"]) * max(0.0, 1.0 - busy_h)
        return (e_idle + n * m["e_active"]) / n
    p("")
    for a, b in (("undercounter", "door type"), ("door type", "single-tank conveyor"),
                 ("door type", "conveyor, pumps left running")):
        lo, hi = 1.0, min(res[a]["rph_max"], res[b]["rph_max"])
        if (per(a, lo) - per(b, lo)) * (per(a, hi) - per(b, hi)) < 0:
            for _ in range(80):
                mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
                if (per(a, lo) - per(b, lo)) * (per(a, mid) - per(b, mid)) <= 0:
                    hi = mid
                else:
                    lo = mid
            p(f"per-rack crossover {a} vs {b}: {hi:.1f} racks/h")
            res[f"x_{a}_{b}"] = hi
        else:
            p(f"no per-rack crossover between {a} and {b} inside the common range "
              f"({per(a,lo):.3f} vs {per(b,lo):.3f} at 1/h; {per(a,hi):.3f} vs {per(b,hi):.3f} at {hi:.0f}/h)")
            res[f"x_{a}_{b}"] = None
    d10 = [pt for pt in res["door type"]["curve"] if pt[0] == 10][0]
    c10 = [pt for pt in res["single-tank conveyor"]["curve"] if pt[0] == 10][0]
    p(f"at 10 racks/h the door machine spends {d10[2]*100:.1f} % of its energy idling; "
      f"the conveyor {c10[2]*100:.1f} %")
    rph_nsf = 3600 * 1 / 60.0
    p(f"NSF listing arithmetic: door machine, 1 rack, 55 s cycle + 5 s load -> "
      f"{rph_nsf:.0f} racks/h")
    r10 = [pt for pt in res["conveyor, pumps left running"]["curve"] if pt[0] == 10][0]
    p(f"at 10 racks/h a conveyor whose pumps run whenever it is on uses "
      f"{r10[1]:.3f} kWh/rack ({r10[2]*100:.1f} % idle) vs {c10[1]:.3f} with auto-stop "
      f"({(r10[1]/c10[1]-1)*100:.0f} % more)")
    full = res["door type"]["curve"][-1]
    m = res["door type"]
    p(f"door type at full rate: building water heater {m['e_bldg']/m['e_active']*100:.1f} % of "
      f"active energy, booster {m['e_boost']/m['e_active']*100:.1f} %, machine wash "
      f"{m['wash']/m['e_active']*100:.1f} %")
    OUT["E7"] = {"machines": res, "door10_idle": d10[2], "conv10_idle": c10[2],
                 "run10": r10, "conv10": c10,
                 "booster_dT": booster_dT, "building_dT": building_dT}

def e8():

    rows = []
    for dH in (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30):
        meq = water_L * meq_per_dH * dH
        cyc = resin_L * cap_eq_L * 1000.0 / meq
        salt = resin_L * salt_g_L / cyc
