1The question

A refrigerator is two machines sharing a cabinet. One is a box that leaks: heat seeps through its walls, slips round its door seal, pours in whenever the door opens, arrives inside every warm bag of shopping and is released by its own fans and heaters. The other is a heat pump that picks that heat up at a temperature below the food and puts it down at a temperature above the room. Almost every design decision in the field, from the thickness of the foam to the choice of refrigerant to whether a supermarket case has doors, is a decision about one of those two machines.

This article is organised around one question: where does the heat get in, and how many kelvins do you pay to throw it back out? The first half of the question sorts refrigerators by their dominant heat path. The second half prices every path, because the heat pump's efficiency falls as the gap between the cold side and the hot side widens, and each kelvin of that gap costs a few percent of the electricity bill.

The answer to the first half turns out to separate the classes of machine more cleanly than any list of features. A household fridge-freezer is an insulation problem: in the lab model used throughout this article, walls and gasket carry 90.6% of its heat load. An open supermarket display case is an aerodynamics problem: 81.5% of its load arrives through the air curtain that replaces its door. A walk-in cold room is a door problem, and a blast chiller is a product problem, with 87.8% of its load arriving in the food itself. The answer to the second half is the same for all of them: at the temperatures refrigerators work at, a kelvin of lift is worth roughly 1.6 to 2.6% of the compressor's energy, and much of the engineering is a hunt for kelvins.

Six heat paths recur throughout. Five are ways heat enters: walls (W), gasket and edges (G), openings (O), product (P) and internal gains (I). The sixth, rejection (R), is the machine that pays to remove it. Twenty-five plates describe the design mechanisms that act on those paths, each with the same fields (what it changes, what it costs, when it fails) and a small cabinet diagram showing which path it touches and which lever it pulls. They are collected in a ledger at the end.

The numbers come from a nine-experiment laboratory written in the Python standard library and run at build time; its full output is reproduced in the last section. The model's constants are illustrative engineering values inside published ranges, not measurements of any product, and where a published measurement exists the article sets the two side by side.

2A short history of making cold

Most of the history of cold before the twentieth century was not refrigeration at all but the harvesting and storage of natural ice. Nineteenth-century America built a large trade on cutting lake and river ice in winter and shipping it in insulated holds, a business that shaped the domestic icebox, the brewery and the meat-packing industry long before any machine could compete with it 1.

The science began in 1748, when William Cullen at Glasgow showed that ether evaporating under reduced pressure could freeze water; his account was published in 1756 2. Jacob Perkins patented a closed vapour-compression machine in Britain in 1834, with the four elements every refrigerator still has: a compressor, a condenser, an expansion step and an evaporator 3. John Gorrie, a Florida physician trying to cool fever wards, received a US patent for an air-cycle ice machine in 1851 4. Through the second half of the century Ferdinand Carré's ammonia absorption machines, Carl von Linde's ammonia compression plants and many others made mechanical cold practical at industrial scale, mostly for breweries, cold stores and ships 5.

Carnot had meanwhile supplied the rule that still prices the whole field. In 1824 he showed that the work a heat engine can extract depends only on the temperatures between which it runs 6; run backwards, the same argument sets the minimum work needed to lift heat across a temperature gap. That minimum is the subject of the next section.

Domestic machines arrived in the 1910s and 1920s, and their first problem was the refrigerant. Early household units used refrigerants such as sulfur dioxide, methyl chloride or ammonia, all toxic 7. General Electric's 1927 Monitor Top, designed under chief engineer Christian Steenstrup, sealed the whole mechanism inside a steel shell on top of an all-steel cabinet; it sold for about $525 at launch and fell to around $200 within a few years, and it set the pattern for the appliance 8,9. Absorption refrigerators with no moving parts came from the Swedish students Baltzar von Platen and Carl Munters and were sold by Electrolux 7; Albert Einstein and Leó Szilárd patented a related design in 1930 10.

The refrigerant problem appeared to be solved in 1930, when Thomas Midgley and Albert Henne, working for General Motors, then the owner of Frigidaire, reported fluorinated organic compounds that were non-toxic, non-flammable and stable 11. Chlorofluorocarbons such as R-12 became the default for sixty years. Their stability was the flaw: in 1974 Molina and Rowland showed that chlorofluorocarbons survive to the stratosphere and release chlorine that destroys ozone 12, and in 1985 the British Antarctic Survey reported the ozone hole 13. The Montreal Protocol of 1987 phased out CFCs and later the transitional HCFCs, and its 2016 Kigali Amendment extended control to the hydrofluorocarbons that replaced them, because many HFCs are powerful greenhouse gases 14. Velders and colleagues estimated that the Protocol's climate benefit by 2010 was several times the emission reduction targeted in the first commitment period of the Kyoto Protocol, because the chemicals it removed were themselves strong warming agents 15.

Frozen food changed what freezers were for. Quick-freezing and a frozen-food supply chain grew through the 1930s to 1950s and made the home freezer compartment something more than an ice-cube tray; the long history of the cold chain is as much about commerce and taste as engineering 16,1.

The last half-century has been about efficiency. Mandatory federal efficiency standards have applied in the United States since 1990 17, and new refrigerators now use about a quarter of the energy of 1972 models while offering almost 30% more storage 18. The fifth federal standard, adopted in 2024, applies from 2029 or 2030 depending on product class 19,20. Worldwide the International Institute of Refrigeration counts roughly five billion refrigeration, air-conditioning and heat-pump systems, about two billion of them domestic refrigerators and freezers, and estimates that refrigeration in all its forms uses about a fifth of the world's electricity 21.

3The price of a kelvin

Almost every refrigerator in use runs the vapour-compression cycle Perkins patented. A refrigerant boils in the evaporator at low pressure, absorbing heat from the cabinet air. A compressor raises the vapour's pressure and therefore its condensing temperature. In the condenser the hot vapour gives its heat to the room and turns back to liquid. An expansion device, a capillary tube in a household unit or a valve in commercial equipment, drops the pressure again and the cycle repeats 22,23.

The figure of merit is the coefficient of performance, COP: heat removed from the cabinet per unit of work put in. Carnot's argument sets its ceiling. For heat taken in at an absolute evaporating temperature Te and rejected at a condensing temperature Tc, the best any machine can do is Te / (Tc − Te) 6. Real machines reach a fraction of that. The lab uses a single second-law efficiency of 0.50 applied to saturated evaporating and condensing temperatures, which folds compressor losses, throttling and superheat into one number; it is a model assumption, not a measured value.

The denominator is the lift, and it is where the money goes. The evaporator must be colder than the compartment for heat to flow into it, and the condenser hotter than the room for heat to flow out, so the lift is always the compartment-to-room difference plus two approach temperatures. A household freezer holding −18 °C in a 25 °C kitchen, with the evaporator at −26 °C and the condenser at 37 °C, works across a lift of 63 K and reaches a model COP of 1.96. A supermarket low-temperature rack across a 72 K lift reaches 1.67, and needs 1.65 times the energy per unit of heat of its medium-temperature sibling.

The derivative is the useful number. In the model, warming a freezer evaporator by one kelvin raises COP by about 1.99%, and cooling the condenser by one kelvin raises it by about 1.59%. At fresh-food duty, where the absolute temperatures are higher and the lift smaller, a kelvin at the evaporator is worth 2.6%. These percentages are why engineers fight over approach temperatures of a few kelvins: a larger evaporator, a cleaner condenser, a fan that runs a little faster or a compressor that runs a little slower each buy a kelvin or two, and each kelvin is a couple of percent.

Three plates describe the parts of the circuit that set those approach temperatures and the flow between them.

4The box

The cabinet is where the heat enters, and in a closed household unit it enters mainly by conduction. The lab builds a reference bottom-freezer fridge-freezer: 0.60 m wide, 0.66 m deep and 1.85 m tall, with a 280 L fresh-food compartment at 4 °C over a 140 L freezer at −18 °C, in a 25 °C kitchen at 55% relative humidity. Walls are rigid polyurethane foam with a conductivity of 0.020 W/(m·K), 45 mm thick round the fresh-food compartment and 65 mm round the freezer, with a 12% allowance for corners and edges. The door gasket is treated as a linear thermal bridge. The fresh-food door opens 30 times a day and the freezer 8, each opening exchanging a fixed fraction of the compartment's air; groceries, the evaporator fan and defrost heat make up the rest. Moist-air properties follow the ASHRAE formulation with Alduchov and Eskridge's saturation pressures 26,27.

The result is a load of 84.6 W, and it is dominated by the envelope. Walls carry 78.5%, the gasket and flanges 12%, product 3.6%, internal gains 3.5% and door openings only 2.2%. Each fresh-food opening costs 4.72 kJ and brings in 0.82 g of water; each freezer opening costs 2.82 kJ. Openings matter far more for frost than for energy, which is the subject of the next section.

That breakdown explains the direction of four decades of household design. If walls dominate, the levers are conductivity and thickness, and the constraint is the market's demand for internal volume in a fixed kitchen niche. Adding 20 mm of foam to every wall saves 19.4% in the model but gives up about 87 L of storage. Replacing 60% of the wall area with 12 mm vacuum insulation panels inside the same wall thickness saves 12.6% and gives up nothing, which is why vacuum panels appear in premium and built-in appliances despite their cost 29,30.

Ambient temperature matters twice. The International Electrotechnical Commission's test method runs appliances at 16 °C and at 32 °C and combines the results 28. Between those two points the model's heat load rises by a factor of 1.79, but its electricity use rises by 2.13, from 264 to 561 kWh a year, because the hotter room also raises the condensing temperature and so the lift. Every heat path is priced twice in this way: once in watts, then again in kelvins.

5Frost

Every opening, every leaky gasket and every unwrapped lettuce puts water vapour into the cabinet, and the coldest surface inside is the evaporator. Water leaves the air there as frost. Frost insulates the coil and, on a finned coil with a fan, chokes the airflow, so the evaporator must run colder to move the same heat. That costs lift, which costs a couple of percent per kelvin.

Early refrigerators simply let frost build on the freezer plate until the owner defrosted it by hand. No-frost designs hide a finned evaporator behind a panel, blow cabinet air across it and melt the frost off with an electric heater at intervals, draining the water to a tray over the warm compressor where it evaporates 25. The price is the heater's energy, the heat it leaves behind in the cabinet, and a drier cabinet atmosphere.

The interval is an optimisation. Defrosting often wastes heater energy; defrosting rarely lets frost cost efficiency for longer. If each defrost costs a fixed energy E and the excess power from frost grows roughly in proportion to the frost present, then frost accumulating at a steady rate makes the penalty grow linearly between defrosts with slope a, and the mean penalty over an interval τ is E/τ + aτ/2. It is smallest at τ* = √(2E/a). With a defrost cost of 72.6 Wh (the heater plus the refrigeration needed to remove the heat it leaves behind) and the reference cabinet's frost rate of 2.65 g per hour, the model's optimum interval is 52.3 hours. A simple 12-hour timer carries a mean penalty of 6.37 W against 2.78 W at the optimum.

Real moisture loads are not steady, so the lab simulates a year of door openings with a fixed random seed: weekdays average 30 openings, weekends 55, and a few days a year bring a crowd. A defrost triggered on a running estimate of accumulated frost uses 184 defrost cycles, against 730 for a 12-hour timer, and its annual penalty is 25.8 kWh against 56.4 kWh. The difference, 30.6 kWh a year, is 7.3% of the appliance's total consumption, obtained with a few lines of control logic and no new hardware.

6Running the machine

The compressor in a household refrigerator is a small hermetic unit: a reciprocating or rotary pump and its motor welded into a steel can, cooled by the returning suction gas, with no shaft seal to leak. The sealed design traces directly to the Monitor Top 8. Commercial plug-in cabinets use the same kind of machine, larger; supermarket racks use several semi-hermetic or scroll compressors in parallel so capacity can be staged 23.

For most of the appliance's history the compressor ran at one speed and was switched on and off by a thermostat. A single-speed compressor must be sized for the worst case, which means a hot kitchen and a warm load to pull down, so for most of its life it is oversized. On/off control then has two costs. While running, the compressor delivers far more heat than the load needs, so the evaporator runs colder and the condenser hotter than they would at a matched capacity. And each start costs a transient: refrigerant that migrated during the off-cycle must be pumped back to where it belongs before useful cooling resumes, a loss that detailed transient models of household refrigerators capture explicitly 32.

A variable-speed drive attacks both. The lab models a freezer compartment with an evaporator conductance of 11 W/K and a condenser conductance of 9 W/K, a compressor capacity of 150 W at full speed, a minimum speed of 30%, a start-up loss per cycle, and a mild efficiency penalty at low speed for the inverter and the compressor's own fixed losses. At a load of 30% of full capacity the on/off machine evaporates at −31.6 °C while it runs, and the variable-speed machine at −22.1 °C all the time. Across the loads a household freezer sees, the saving is 8.1 to 34.1%, largest just above the minimum speed and smallest near full load, where the two machines converge.

The shape of that curve, not its height, is the robust result. Below the minimum speed the variable-speed machine must cycle too, and its advantage shrinks; near full load there is no surplus capacity to trade for temperature. Variable-speed compressors earn their cost in the long middle, which is where a household appliance spends most of its life.

7Household architectures

A household refrigerator is a mass-produced object sold on price, volume and features, run by someone who never thinks about it, for a decade or more, in a room it does not control. Those conditions explain its architecture: a hermetic compressor, a capillary tube, a foam-filled steel cabinet assembled in one piece, and a charge of refrigerant sealed in at the factory and never opened again.

The configurations the market recognises are mostly arrangements of compartments: refrigerator-only, top-freezer, bottom-freezer, side-by-side and multi-door "French door" units, plus chest and upright freezers. Behind those shapes the thermodynamic choice is how many evaporators to use. The cheapest arrangement cools both compartments from one coil held cold enough for the freezer, with a damper sending a share of the air to the fresh-food side. That lifts the fresh-food heat from freezer evaporating temperature. A second evaporator lets fresh-food heat be taken up at about −8 °C instead of −26 °C; in the reference cabinet that cuts annual consumption from 417 to 359 kWh, a saving of 14%, and it keeps the fresh-food compartment more humid because its air no longer passes over a freezing coil 33.

Chest freezers deserve a note because they show the openings path in its purest form. Cold air is denser than room air, so an upright freezer's contents pour out of the bottom of an open door, while a chest freezer's lid opens onto a pool of cold air that mostly stays put. The Gosney and Olama analysis used later for walk-in doorways is the physics of that difference 34.

Absorption and thermoelectric machines occupy the niches where a compressor is unwelcome. Both pay heavily in efficiency, and both are therefore confined to small cabinets or special duties.

Tests, labels and standards

What a household appliance is tuned to do is decided largely by how it is tested. The international method, IEC 62552, measures energy at two ambient temperatures, 16 °C and 32 °C, with compartments held at reference temperatures of 4 °C for fresh food and −18 °C for frozen food, and it defines climate classes (SN, N, ST and T) that state the range of room temperatures an appliance is designed to work in, up to 43 °C for tropical class T 28. The US method in 10 CFR Part 430 tests at 90 °F (32.2 °C) with standardised compartment temperatures 36. The European Union sets minimum requirements through ecodesign regulation and grades products on a rescaled A to G label 37,38.

Neither test opens the door. That is a deliberate simplification, and the lab's result that openings are a small share of household load suggests it is not a serious one for energy. It is more serious for food, and for the way frost and humidity behave in use; reviews of how refrigerators are actually used in homes report compartment temperatures that are often warmer than recommended and wide variation between households 31.

In the United States the fifth round of federal standards, adopted by direct final rule in January 2024 on the basis of a joint agreement between manufacturers and efficiency advocates, takes effect on 31 January 2029 or 31 January 2030 depending on product class 19,20. The rule's technology options are the ones this article has priced: better compressors including variable-speed designs, better fans and heat exchangers, and vacuum insulation panels 18.

8Commercial machines

Commercial refrigeration covers a wider range of machines than the household market, and its heat budget looks different because its purpose is different. A household refrigerator stores food out of sight. A supermarket case must display it, a restaurant reach-in must give a cook instant access dozens of times an hour, a walk-in must admit trolleys, and a blast chiller must cool cooked food fast enough to be legal. Commercial equipment also runs harder: food retail is among the most electricity-intensive commercial building types, and refrigeration is typically its largest single use 39.

The open display case

An open multideck case has no door. A sheet of cold air is blown down across the open front from a grille at the top and collected at the bottom; that curtain is the door. It is a leaky one. A turbulent jet entrains air from both sides as it falls, and some of the warm, moist store air it picks up ends in the return grille and must be cooled and dried by the coil. Measured load breakdowns put this infiltration at roughly 70 to 80% of the total load of a medium-temperature open vertical case, with radiation from the store's warm surfaces, lights, fans and conduction making up the rest 40. Low-front cases, with more open area to seal, are worse; one tracer-gas study found infiltrated air carrying at least 85% of the load 41.

The lab models a 2.44 m case with a 1.6 m opening in a store at 23.9 °C and 55% relative humidity, the condition used in controlled tests of these cases 42. The curtain leaves the grille at 0.90 m/s through a 90 mm slot at −1 °C, and a fraction ξ of the curtain's mass flow is taken to be store air that the coil must cool. The fraction depends on jet velocity, turbulence at the discharge and the case geometry, and has been measured by tracer-gas methods 43,44; the model takes 0.20 as its central value and sweeps the range. At ξ = 0.20 the case carries 2,621 W, 1.07 kW per metre of case, and infiltration is 81.5% of it. Across ξ = 0.10 to 0.40 the share runs from 68.8 to 89.8%; the published 73% sits between ξ = 0.10 and 0.15 in this model.

Curtain design is a balance between momentum and buoyancy. Too slow and the cold, dense curtain is pushed outward by the pressure difference across the opening and breaks; too fast and it entrains more air. Hayes and Stoecker expressed the balance as a dimensionless deflection modulus in 1969 45, and later work has refined the effects of jet velocity, turbulence at the discharge, and the case's geometry 43,44. Stocking practice undoes much of the design: products that protrude into the curtain or block the return grille wreck it.

Covers and doors attack the path directly. Night blinds pulled down for eight closed hours cut the model case's daily load by 19.6%. Glass doors do more. In the controlled test cited above, retrofitting doors to an open five-deck dairy case reduced its cooling load by 68% and its compressor power demand by 87% 42. The lab's door model, with 60 openings an hour through the trading day and heated frames, predicts 77.3%; under an extreme assumption of 240 openings an hour each exchanging twice the air, the saving falls to 27.2%. When Evans and co-workers tested retail display and commercial catering cabinets under the same conditions, energy use and temperature performance varied widely between designs 46. European ecodesign regulation now sets minimum efficiency for refrigerated display cabinets, beverage coolers and similar equipment 47, tested under ISO 23953 climate classes 48.

Reach-ins and walk-ins

A glass-door reach-in or bottle cooler is a household cabinet opened far more often, usually with worse insulation in the glass. The lab asks how many openings it takes before the door carries as much heat as the envelope. For the household fresh-food compartment the answer is about 661 a day, far beyond normal use. For a commercial reach-in in a 27 °C kitchen it is about 506: at 50 openings a day the door is 9% of the combined envelope-and-door load, at 200 it is 28%, and at 600 it is 54%. A busy reach-in is therefore a mixed machine, with walls and door both mattering.

A walk-in cold room is a door with a room behind it. When the door opens, the cold dense air inside flows out along the bottom of the doorway and warm air flows in along the top. Gosney and Olama analysed this buoyancy-driven exchange in 1975 34, and their result, confirmed by later ASHRAE research, is the doorway infiltration method in the ASHRAE Handbook 23. The lab applies it to a 3.0 m by 2.4 m cooler at 2 °C behind a 0.9 m by 2.0 m door in a 27 °C kitchen. With the door fully open the exchange carries 28.4 kW, more than thirty times the room's entire average load. Averaged over sixty 15-second openings and ten minutes of deliveries a day, the doorway is 50.3% of the load. A strip curtain with an assumed effectiveness of 0.80 cuts total load by 40.3%; one extra ten-minute propped-open door a day adds 20.1%. For a −20 °C walk-in freezer the fully open exchange is 70 kW, 2.46 times the cooler's, because both the density difference that drives the flow and the enthalpy difference it carries are larger, and every kilogram of moisture it brings becomes ice on the coil.

Blast chillers and freezers

Cooked food has to be cooled through the temperatures at which bacteria grow fastest. The US Food Code requires cooked food to fall from 57 °C (135 °F) to 21 °C (70 °F) within two hours, and to 5 °C (41 °F) within six hours in total 49. A blast chiller exists to meet that rule. Its load is almost all product: 87.8% in the lab's 40 kg, 90-minute cycle. It meets the rule by driving air at high velocity over the pans at a temperature below freezing, which raises the surface heat-transfer coefficient several-fold.

The lab solves transient conduction through a pan of food cooled from above and below. A 50 mm deep pan in the still air of a 3 °C reach-in takes 5.51 hours to pass 21 °C at its warmest point and 16.47 hours to reach 5 °C: it fails the rule. The same pan in a blast chiller at −2 °C passes in 1.53 and 3.42 hours. A 100 mm pan fails even in the blast chiller, taking 4.12 hours to reach 21 °C, because conduction time grows with the square of depth. The practical rule that follows, shallow pans, is not a preference but a consequence of the heat equation.

Supermarket systems

A supermarket does not usually put a compressor in every case. The traditional arrangement is a remote rack: a set of parallel compressors in a machine room serving dozens of cases and cold rooms through long pipe runs, with an air-cooled condenser on the roof. It lets the store remove compressor heat and noise from the sales floor and lets condensing pressure float down in cool weather, recovering lift. Its costs are a very large refrigerant charge and leaks from thousands of joints 39,23.

Two responses to the refrigerant problem are reshaping those systems. One is carbon dioxide. Lorentzen argued for its revival as a refrigerant in the early 1990s 51; its critical point of about 31 °C means that in warm weather the high side operates above the critical point, rejecting heat in a gas cooler rather than a condenser, at pressures far above those of fluorinated refrigerants, which changes component design and control throughout 52. The other is to decentralise: self-contained propane cabinets on the sales floor, often rejecting heat into a shared water loop, each holding a charge small enough to satisfy the flammability standards discussed in the next section 53.

9Refrigerants

The choice of refrigerant is where thermodynamics, safety, chemistry and law meet. A refrigerant must boil and condense at useful pressures across the temperatures of its duty, carry a lot of heat per unit of volume pumped, stay chemically stable in a sealed system for decades, be compatible with oil and materials, and be safe in a kitchen or a shop. The chlorofluorocarbons met all of those requirements and failed on the ozone layer 12,56. The hydrofluorocarbons that replaced them met all of them and failed on climate: R-134a has a 100-year global warming potential of 1,430, and the common commercial blend R-404A, computed from its components' published values, 3,922 57.

The difficulty is that the molecules that meet every other requirement with a low GWP are few. A systematic screen of chemical databases by McLinden and colleagues concluded that low-GWP options for most applications are limited and that most of them are at least mildly flammable 59. The remaining choices are the old natural refrigerants (hydrocarbons, carbon dioxide and ammonia) and the new hydrofluoroolefins and their blends, each with a disqualifying property somewhere: flammability, pressure or toxicity. ASHRAE Standard 34 records that trade in its two-part safety class, a letter for toxicity and a number for flammability 58.

Household refrigerators resolved the trade first. Isobutane (R-600a) has a low GWP, runs at low pressures and needs a charge of well under 150 g in a household circuit. By the time the United States permitted a meaningful charge, more than two billion hydrocarbon refrigerators were estimated to be in use elsewhere 60. The US had capped hydrocarbon charge at 57 g; UL 60335-2-24, revised in 2017, raised it to 150 g, and EPA adopted that limit in 2018 61,62. Commercial self-contained equipment followed: IEC 60335-2-89:2019 allowed flammable charges up to 13 times the lower flammability limit, about 500 g of propane, subject to minimum room sizes and construction requirements 53,63, and the 2021 edition of UL 60335-2-89 adopted comparable limits for North America, with a lower cap of eight times the limit (about 300 g) for closed cabinets 64.

Regulation is now closing off the fluorinated options. Under the American Innovation and Manufacturing Act, EPA's 2023 Technology Transitions rule set a GWP limit of 150 for refrigerants in new household refrigerators and freezers, and in self-contained commercial units, from 1 January 2025 65,66. A reconsideration signed on 21 May 2026 left those limits alone but eased the schedule for larger systems: remote condensing units and supermarket systems get an interim limit of 1,400 (from July 2026 and January 2027 respectively) before the original 150 or 300 limits return on 1 January 2032, and a supermarket may now expand cooling capacity by up to 15% over its life in remodels without being treated as a new system 67,68. In the European Union, Regulation 2024/573 prohibits placing on the market household refrigerators and freezers containing any fluorinated greenhouse gas from 1 January 2026, except where safety requirements demand one, and bars self-contained commercial equipment using fluorinated gases with a GWP of 150 or more from 2025 69, with a temporary derogation until 30 June 2026 for certain equipment including blast cabinets 50.

The practical consequence runs through the plates. Household machines have converged on hydrocarbons under charge caps. Commercial plug-in cabinets are converging on propane. Large supermarket systems are divided between carbon dioxide, lower-GWP fluorinated blends and distributed propane, and the interim US limits give the fluorinated route more time.

10The product

The last heat path is the one the whole machine exists for. Food safety guidance in the United States asks for a refrigerator at or below 4 °C (40 °F) and a freezer at or below −18 °C (0 °F) 70, and the Food Code requires cold holding at or below 5 °C (41 °F) in food businesses 49. Those temperatures are not arbitrary: cold slows microbial growth, and the freezer's job is to lock water away as ice where microbes cannot use it. The cold chain as a whole, from farm to home, is only as good as its weakest link, and household refrigerators are often the weakest 71,31.

Freezing time sets the difference between a household freezer and a commercial blast freezer. Plank's 1913 equation estimates the time to freeze a slab from its thickness, the air temperature and the surface heat-transfer coefficient 72. For a 50 mm slab of a typical food, the model gives 14.7 hours in still air at −18 °C and 2.3 hours in moving air at −35 °C, a factor of 6.5. Halving the thickness to 25 mm brings the household time to 7.1 hours. Freezing quickly produces smaller ice crystals and better texture 23, which is why commercial freezing is fast and why a household freezer is a store, not a freezer in the industrial sense.

The same latent heat that makes freezing slow makes thawing slow, and that gives a freezer its resilience. When the power fails, federal guidance says a full freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours and a half-full one for about 24, provided the door stays shut 73. The lab reproduces both the numbers and the reason. It treats the freezer contents as food that is 74% water with a freezing point of −1.7 °C, whose unfrozen water fraction rises as it warms, inside the reference cabinet's envelope (conductance 0.887 W/K) in a 25 °C room. A full freezer of 63 kg of food takes 50 hours to reach −5 °C; a half-full one takes 26 hours, a ratio of 1.96. Between −18 °C and 0 °C, 87% of the heat the food absorbs goes into melting ice rather than raising its temperature, so the freezer's reserve is its contents, and an empty freezer has almost none.

What the lab can and cannot say

The lab is a set of lumped models with illustrative constants. It is useful for ratios, shares, shapes and orders of magnitude, and it is used that way throughout. It is not a product model. Four limits deserve stating. The cycle model applies one second-law efficiency to all machines, so it cannot rank compressor technologies against each other. The household cabinet uses one-dimensional conduction with an edge multiplier and a single gasket conductance; real cabinets have three-dimensional thermal bridges, and the gasket share is sensitive to a parameter that is hard to measure, which is why the gasket result is reported as a range. The open-case model reduces a turbulent jet to one entrainment fraction; the share of load it predicts depends on that fraction, and the published measurement sits at the low end of the plausible range. The glass-door model predicts a larger saving than the one measured in the cited retrofit, and the measured figure should be preferred.

def p_ws(t):
    """Saturation vapour pressure, Pa. Alduchov & Eskridge (1996) Magnus forms;
    over water for t >= 0 C, over ice below."""
    if t >= 0:
        return 610.94 * math.exp(17.625 * t / (t + 243.04))
    return 611.21 * math.exp(22.587 * t / (t + 273.86))

def hum_ratio(t, rh):
    pw = rh * p_ws(t)
    return 0.621945 * pw / (P_ATM - pw)

def enthalpy(t, w):
    """Moist-air enthalpy, kJ per kg dry air (ASHRAE Fundamentals form)."""
    return 1.006 * t + w * (2501.0 + 1.86 * t)

def rho_moist(t, w):
    """Moist-air density, kg/m3 (mass of mixture per volume)."""
    return P_ATM * (1 + w) / (287.042 * (t + T0) * (1 + 1.607858 * w))

def areas():
    a_ff = W * D + 2 * D * H_FF + W * H_FF + W * H_FF
    a_fz = W * D + 2 * D * H_FZ + W * H_FZ + W * H_FZ
    return a_ff, a_fz

def opening_load(t_in, rh_in, vol, frac, t_amb=AMB, rh_amb=AMB_RH, frost=False):
    """Energy (kJ) and water (g) per door opening: exchanged air cooled to the
    compartment state; water removed as liquid (fresh food) or frost (freezer)."""
    w_in = hum_ratio(t_in, rh_in); w_a = hum_ratio(t_amb, rh_amb)
    m_da = rho_moist(t_in, w_in) * vol * frac / (1 + w_in)
    dw = max(w_a - w_in, 0.0) * m_da
    q = m_da * (enthalpy(t_amb, w_a) - enthalpy(t_in, w_in))
    if frost:
        q += dw * (333.4 + 2.1 * (0 - t_in))   # water leaves as ice at t_in
    return q, dw * 1000.0

def res_budget(t_amb=AMB, t_ff=T_FF, t_fz=T_FZ, th_ff=0.045, th_fz=0.065,
               lam_ff=LAM_PU, lam_fz=LAM_PU, psi=PSI_G, n_ff=N_FF, n_fz=N_FZ, vip_frac=0.0):
    a_ff, a_fz = areas()
    def u(th, lam):
        u_foam = lam / th * EDGE
        if vip_frac > 0:   # VIP 12 mm (lambda_eff 0.007 incl. edge effect) + foam remainder
            u_vip = 1.0 / (0.012 / 0.007 + (th - 0.012) / lam) * EDGE
            return (1 - vip_frac) * u_foam + vip_frac * u_vip
        return u_foam
    q = {}
    q["W_ff"] = u(th_ff, lam_ff) * a_ff * (t_amb - t_ff)
    q["W_fz"] = u(th_fz, lam_fz) * a_fz * (t_amb - t_fz)
    mull = 0.54 * 0.60 * (LAM_PU / 0.060) * (t_ff - t_fz)
    q["W_ff"] -= mull; q["W_fz"] += mull            # internal transfer, conserves total
    per_ff = 2 * (W + H_FF); per_fz = 2 * (W + H_FZ)
    q["G_ff"] = psi * per_ff * (t_amb - t_ff)
    q["G_fz"] = psi * per_fz * (t_amb - t_fz)
    e_ff, w_ff = opening_load(t_ff, 0.85, V_FF, X_FF, t_amb)
    e_fz, w_fz = opening_load(t_fz, 1.0, V_FZ, X_FZ, t_amb, frost=True)
    q["O_ff"] = e_ff * n_ff * 1000 / 86400
    q["O_fz"] = e_fz * n_fz * 1000 / 86400
    q["P_ff"] = 1.5 * 3.7 * (t_amb - t_ff) * 1000 / 86400          # 1.5 kg/day groceries
    q["P_fz"] = 0.5 * (3.7 * 5 + 0.74 * 334 + 2.0 * (-1 - t_fz)) * 1000 / 86400   # 0.5 kg/day frozen
    q["I_fan"] = 2.0          # evaporator fan, average W delivered into cabinet
    q["I_defrost"] = 0.40 * 180 * (20 / 60) / 24   # 40% of heater energy stays as load, 1 defrost/day
    frost_g_day = w_ff * n_ff + w_fz * n_fz
    return q, frost_g_day, (e_ff, w_ff, e_fz, w_fz)

def group(q):
    g = {"W": 0, "G": 0, "O": 0, "P": 0, "I": 0}

    return g

def res_energy(q, t_amb=AMB, single=True, cond_dt=12.0, eta=ETA_II):
    tc = t_amb + cond_dt
    tot = sum(q.values())
    heater_elec = 180 * (20 / 60) / 24     # defrost heater electricity, W average
    if single:
        p = tot / cop(-26.0, tc, eta)
    else:
        ff = sum(v for k, v in q.items() if k.endswith("_ff"))
        fz = tot - ff
        p = ff / cop(-8.0, tc, eta) + fz / cop(-26.0, tc, eta)
    return p + heater_elec + 2.0, tot     # + fan electricity

say("E5  WALK-IN DOORWAYS (Gosney-Olama, as used in the ASHRAE Handbook)")
def gosney_olama(A, H, t_i, rh_i, t_r, rh_r):
    w_i = hum_ratio(t_i, rh_i); w_r = hum_ratio(t_r, rh_r)
    rho_i = rho_moist(t_i, w_i); rho_r = rho_moist(t_r, w_r)
    fm = (2.0 / (1.0 + (rho_r / rho_i) ** (1.0 / 3.0))) ** 1.5
    dh = enthalpy(t_i, w_i) - enthalpy(t_r, w_r)
    q = 0.221 * A * dh * rho_r * math.sqrt(1 - rho_i / rho_r) * math.sqrt(9.81 * H) * fm
    return q, fm, rho_i, rho_r, dh
def walkin(t_r, rh_r, u_wall, n_open, sec_open, long_open, E=0.0, prod_w=62.0, fans=80.0, defrost=0.0):

    q_open, fm, ri, rr, dh = gosney_olama(A, H, 27.0, 0.50, t_r, rh_r)
    dt = (n_open * sec_open + long_open) / 86400.0
    q = {}
    q["O"] = q_open * 1000 * dt * 0.8 * (1 - E)
    q["W"] = u_wall * 40.3 * (27.0 - t_r)
    q["P"] = prod_w; q["I_fans"] = fans; q["I_other"] = 16.0 + defrost
    return q, q_open, dt, fm

def cool_slab(depth, h_top, h_bot, t_air, t0=60.0, k=0.50, rho=1050.0, cp=3700.0, n=40):
    dx = depth / n; alpha = k / (rho * cp)
    dt = 0.2 * dx * dx / alpha
    T = [t0] * (n + 1); t = 0.0; t21 = t5 = t57 = None; hist = []
    while t < 48 * 3600:
        Tn = T[:]
        for i in range(1, n):
            Tn[i] = T[i] + alpha * dt / dx ** 2 * (T[i + 1] - 2 * T[i] + T[i - 1])
        Tn[0] = T[0] + 2 * alpha * dt / dx ** 2 * (T[1] - T[0] + dx * h_bot / k * (t_air - T[0]))
        Tn[n] = T[n] + 2 * alpha * dt / dx ** 2 * (T[n - 1] - T[n] + dx * h_top / k * (t_air - T[n]))
        T = Tn; t += dt
        tmax = max(T)
        if len(hist) == 0 or t - hist[-1][0] >= 600:
            hist.append((t, tmax))
        if t57 is None and tmax <= 57.2:
            t57 = t / 3600.0
        if t21 is None and tmax <= 21.0:
            t21 = t / 3600.0
        if t5 is None and tmax <= 5.0:
            t5 = t / 3600.0; break
    # the Food Code clock starts when the hottest point passes 57.2 C (135 F)
    return t21 - t57, t5 - t57, hist
