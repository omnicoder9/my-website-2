1What MHD is, and where it came from

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) is the study of the motion of electrically conducting fluids in the presence of magnetic fields. The essential physics is a two-way coupling [1]:

  1. A conducting fluid moving through a magnetic field induces an electric current (Faraday's law of induction acting on a moving conductor).
  2. That current produces its own magnetic field, which modifies the original field.
  3. The current also interacts with the total field to produce a body force on the fluid, the Lorentz force, which modifies the flow.

The fluid therefore carries the magnetic field and the field pushes back on the fluid. MHD combines the Navier–Stokes equations of fluid mechanics with a reduced (low-frequency) form of Maxwell's equations [1][2].

The idea is older than its name. In January 1832 Michael Faraday tried to measure the voltage induced by the River Thames flowing through the Earth's magnetic field, using electrodes lowered from Waterloo Bridge; he did not detect a convincing signal [3]. A century later, Julius Hartmann in Copenhagen made systematic theoretical and experimental studies of mercury flow in a transverse magnetic field, which he called "Hg-dynamics" [4][5]. In 1942 Hannes Alfvén predicted the existence of "electromagnetic-hydrodynamic waves" in a conducting fluid [6]. Alfvén received the 1970 Nobel Prize in Physics "for fundamental work and discoveries in magnetohydro-dynamics with fruitful applications in different parts of plasma physics" [7].

2Electrically conducting fluids

MHD applies to any fluid whose electrical conductivity σ is large enough for induced currents to matter. Three broad classes are important [1][8]:

  • Liquid metals such as mercury, sodium, gallium alloys, lithium–lead and molten iron. They are used in metallurgy (electromagnetic stirring, braking and levitation), in sodium-cooled fission reactors, in proposed fusion-blanket coolants, and they make up the Earth's liquid outer core [1][9].
  • Plasmas: ionised gases in stars, the solar wind, planetary magnetospheres, accretion discs and fusion devices. Most of the visible matter in the universe is in the plasma state [8].
  • Electrolytes such as seawater. Their conductivity is about a million times lower than that of liquid metals, so magnetic effects are weak unless the fields are very strong [1].

Magnetic pressure and magnetic tension

Using Ampère's law, , and a vector identity, the Lorentz force splits into two physically distinct parts [1][2][13]:

  • Magnetic pressure acts like an isotropic pressure added to the fluid pressure. At B = 1 T it is about 4 × 10⁵ Pa, roughly four atmospheres.
  • Magnetic tension acts along curved field lines, like the tension in a stretched elastic string, and tries to straighten them. Field lines behave like elastic bands that resist bending and repel one another sideways [1].

Part of the tension term cancels the component of the pressure gradient along B, so the net force is always perpendicular to B: a Lorentz force can never accelerate fluid along a field line.

Worked example 3.1 · Electromagnetic pump

A DC conduction pump drives liquid sodium through a duct of width w = 0.05 m (in the direction of the current) and height h = 0.02 m. A transverse field B = 0.5 T is applied and a current I = 2000 A crosses the duct. What pressure rise does the Lorentz force produce, ignoring losses?

Solution. The current density is j = I/(hL), where L is the active length along the duct. The force per unit volume is jB, acting over length L, so the pressure rise is Δp = jBL = IB/h = (2000 × 0.5)/0.02 = 5 × 10⁴ Pa (about 0.5 bar), independent of L. The same principle underlies electromagnetic pumps for liquid-metal coolants [1][14].

Limit 1: pure diffusion (u = 0)

For a copper sphere of 1 m radius this is about a second; for the Earth's core (L ~ 10⁶ m, η ~ 1 m² s⁻¹) it is of order 10⁴–10⁵ years, much shorter than the age of the geomagnetic field (more than three billion years). The Earth's field must therefore be continually regenerated by fluid motion, a dynamo [15][1].

Limit 2: ideal fluid (η → 0) and Alfvén's frozen-flux theorem

. This is the same equation that governs vorticity in an inviscid fluid, and it implies that the magnetic flux through any material surface moving with the fluid is constant. Field lines are "frozen into" the fluid and move with it, as Alfvén first pointed out [6][1][2]. Fluid can slide freely along field lines, but motion across them drags the lines along. Stretching a flux tube concentrates the field (by flux conservation, B·A = constant, and by mass conservation, A·ℓ is constant in an incompressible fluid, so B ∝ ℓ). Repeated stretching, twisting and folding of field lines is the basic mechanism of dynamo action [15].

Not every flow is a dynamo

Cowling's anti-dynamo theorem (1933) shows that an axisymmetric magnetic field cannot be maintained by dynamo action [16]. Self-sustaining dynamos therefore require genuinely three-dimensional fields and flows. The first fully self-consistent 3-D computer simulation of a geodynamo that spontaneously reversed its dipole was published by Glatzmaier and Roberts in 1995 [17], and laboratory liquid-sodium dynamos were demonstrated in Riga [18] and Karlsruhe [19] around 1999–2000.

5The magnetic Reynolds number (and its relatives)

Scaling the induction equation with a velocity U and length L, the ratio of the advection term to the diffusion term is the magnetic Reynolds number [1][15]:

  • Rm ≪ 1 (most laboratory and industrial liquid-metal flows): the induced field is a small perturbation, of order Rm times the applied field. The field diffuses through the fluid almost instantly, so the applied field can be treated as fixed; this is the quasi-static or low-Rm approximation [1][9]. The Lorentz force mostly acts as a brake that damps motion across field lines.
  • Rm ≫ 1 (astrophysics, geophysics, hot plasmas): field lines are nearly frozen in, the field is strongly distorted by the flow and dynamo action becomes possible [15][13].

Worked example 5.1 · Laboratory versus planetary Rm

(a) Mercury (σ ≈ 1.0 × 10⁶ S m⁻¹) flows at U = 0.1 m s⁻¹ in a pipe with L = 0.1 m. (b) Earth's outer core: U ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹ (inferred from secular variation of the geomagnetic field), L ≈ 2 × 10⁶ m, η ≈ 1 m² s⁻¹ [1].

Solution. (a) Rm = μ₀σUL = 1.257 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.04 × 10⁶ × 0.1 × 0.1 ≈ 0.013. The induced field is about 1 % of the applied field, so the low-Rm approximation is excellent. (b) Rm = UL/η ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁴ × 2 × 10⁶ / 1 ≈ 10³, comfortably above the threshold (typically Rm of order 10–100) needed for dynamo action [1][15]. The difference is almost entirely due to the length scale: to reach Rm ~ 50 in a laboratory, the Riga and Karlsruhe experiments used metre-scale volumes of liquid sodium pumped at several m s⁻¹ [18][19].

6Worked example: Hartmann flow

Hartmann flow is the MHD analogue of plane Poiseuille flow and the classic exactly solvable problem of the subject [4][1][9]. A conducting fluid flows in the x-direction between two infinite, electrically insulating plates at y = ±a, driven by a uniform pressure gradient G = −dp/dx. A uniform magnetic field B₀ is applied in the y-direction, perpendicular to the plates.

Worked example 6.1 · Numbers for mercury

Mercury (σ = 1.04 × 10⁶ S m⁻¹, ρ = 13 534 kg m⁻³, ν = 1.15 × 10⁻⁷ m² s⁻¹) flows between plates 2 cm apart (a = 0.01 m) in B₀ = 1 T. Find Ha and the Hartmann-layer thickness.

Solution. Ha = 1 × 0.01 × √(1.04 × 10⁶ / (13 534 × 1.15 × 10⁻⁷)) = 0.01 × √(6.68 × 10⁸) ≈ 258. The layer thickness is δ ≈ a/Ha ≈ 0.01/258 ≈ 39 µm. Almost the whole channel moves as a plug, and all the velocity shear lives in films thinner than a human hair.

7MHD waves

Because magnetic field lines carry tension and pressure, and the fluid carries inertia, a conducting fluid supports waves that have no counterpart in ordinary hydrodynamics. Linearising the ideal MHD equations about a uniform state (density ρ₀, pressure p₀, field B₀) and seeking plane-wave solutions ∝ exp[i(k·r − ωt)] gives three distinct wave families [2][11][8].

The Alfvén wave

The simplest is a transverse, incompressible oscillation of the field lines, analogous to a wave on a plucked string where magnetic tension provides the restoring force and the fluid density provides the inertia [6][1]. Its dispersion relation is

The string analogy is direct: a string under tension T with mass per length m has wave speed √(T/m); here the "tension" per unit area is B₀²/μ₀ and the "mass" per unit volume is ρ₀. Alfvén waves were first observed in the laboratory in liquid mercury by Lundquist in 1949 [20].

Fast and slow magnetosonic waves

In a compressible fluid with sound speed cs = √(γp₀/ρ₀), the compressive perturbations couple gas pressure and magnetic pressure. The phase speeds vph = ω/k satisfy [2][11][8]:

  • The fast magnetosonic wave (+ sign): gas and magnetic pressure perturbations act in phase. Perpendicular to B₀ it travels at √(cs² + vA²).
  • The slow magnetosonic wave (− sign): gas and magnetic pressure act in antiphase. It cannot propagate perpendicular to B₀.
  • Along B₀ (θ = 0) the waves decouple into an ordinary sound wave (speed cs) and the Alfvén wave (speed vA).

The ordering vslow ≤ vAlfvén ≤ vfast always holds. A polar plot of phase speed against propagation angle is called a Friedrichs diagram (Figure 5).

Worked example 7.1 · Alfvén speed in the solar wind

Near Earth (1 AU), the solar wind typically has B ≈ 5 nT and proton number density n ≈ 5 cm⁻³ [21][8]. Estimate vA.

Solution. ρ = n mp = 5 × 10⁶ × 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ ≈ 8.4 × 10⁻²¹ kg m⁻³. Then vA = 5 × 10⁻⁹ / √(1.257 × 10⁻⁶ × 8.4 × 10⁻²¹) ≈ 49 km s⁻¹. The solar wind flows at roughly 300–800 km s⁻¹, so near Earth it is strongly super-Alfvénic; that is why a bow shock stands in front of the Earth's magnetosphere [21][13].

8Plasma flow

Treating a plasma as a single conducting fluid is a large simplification: a plasma is made of electrons and ions that can move differently. MHD is valid when the length scales of interest are much larger than the ion gyroradius and ion inertial length, and the time scales are much longer than the ion gyroperiod [8][2][21]. Remarkably, ideal MHD describes large-scale behaviour of many plasmas well, including tokamak equilibria and instabilities, the solar corona and the solar wind [11][13].

Equilibrium and confinement

A static plasma equilibrium satisfies ∇p = j×B. The plasma beta β = 2μ₀p/B² measures how much plasma pressure a field can hold. Fusion devices such as tokamaks confine plasma with β of a few percent, using strong toroidal fields and a plasma current that generates a poloidal field; their stability against kink, sausage and ballooning modes is the province of ideal MHD stability theory [11][22].

The solar wind

In 1958 Eugene Parker showed that a hot solar corona cannot be in hydrostatic equilibrium and must expand into a supersonic outflow, the solar wind. Because the coronal field is frozen into this outflow while the Sun rotates, the interplanetary field is wound into an Archimedean spiral, now called the Parker spiral [23].

Close to the Sun, the magnetic field dominates and the wind is slower than the Alfvén speed. The surface where the outflow speed equals vA is the Alfvén critical surface, often taken as the outer boundary of the corona. On 28 April 2021, NASA's Parker Solar Probe became the first spacecraft to pass below it, at about 18.8 solar radii [24].

Magnetic reconnection

Ideal MHD forbids field lines from breaking, but in thin current sheets the diffusion term in the induction equation becomes important locally, and oppositely directed field lines can reconnect, releasing magnetic energy as heat and fast flows. Reconnection powers solar flares, coronal mass ejections and magnetospheric substorms, and limits confinement in fusion devices [13][25]. For a coronal loop with L ≈ 10⁷ m, B ≈ 20 G (2 × 10⁻³ T), n ≈ 10¹⁵ m⁻³ and η ≈ 1 m² s⁻¹, vA ≈ 1400 km s⁻¹ and S ≈ 10¹³, giving vin/vA ~ 3 × 10⁻⁷. That is far too slow to explain flares that release energy in minutes, a mismatch that motivated the Petschek model, Hall/collisionless reconnection and plasmoid-instability theories [13][25].

Engineering plasma and liquid-metal flows

  • MHD generators extract electrical power directly from a hot, seeded combustion plasma flowing through a magnetic field, via the motional EMF u×B [14][1].
  • Electromagnetic flowmeters measure the voltage induced across a pipe carrying a conducting liquid, the same principle Faraday tried on the Thames [14].
  • MHD propulsion: the experimental ship Yamato-1, sea-trialled in Japan in 1992, was propelled by Lorentz forces on seawater in thrusters fitted with superconducting magnets [27]. The low conductivity of seawater made the efficiency low.
  • Metallurgy: magnetic stirring, braking of continuous-casting flows and electromagnetic levitation melting all rely on low-Rm Lorentz forces [1].

9Python toolkit

The module below (NumPy only) implements every formula used in this article. It was run to generate the numbers in the worked examples and the curves in Figures 4 and 5. Constants are CODATA 2018 values [12].

"""mhd_tools.py - small, dependency-light MHD calculator (NumPy only)."""
import numpy as np

MU0 = 1.25663706212e-6      # vacuum permeability, N A^-2 (CODATA 2018)
M_P = 1.67262192369e-27     # proton mass, kg

def magnetic_diffusivity(sigma):
    """eta = 1/(mu0*sigma)  [m^2/s]"""
    return 1.0 / (MU0 * sigma)

def magnetic_reynolds(U, L, sigma):
    """Rm = U L / eta = mu0 sigma U L"""
    return MU0 * sigma * U * L

def alfven_speed(B, rho):
    """v_A = B / sqrt(mu0 rho)  [m/s]"""
    return B / np.sqrt(MU0 * rho)

def hartmann_number(B, a, sigma, rho, nu):
    """Ha = B a sqrt(sigma/(rho nu)), a = channel half-width"""
    return B * a * np.sqrt(sigma / (rho * nu))

def interaction_parameter(B, L, U, sigma, rho):
    """N = sigma B^2 L / (rho U)  (Stuart number)"""
    return sigma * B**2 * L / (rho * U)

def lundquist_number(B, L, rho, sigma):
    """S = v_A L / eta"""
    return alfven_speed(B, rho) * L / magnetic_diffusivity(sigma)

def plasma_beta(p, B):
    """beta = 2 mu0 p / B^2"""
    return 2 * MU0 * p / B**2

def hartmann_profile(y, Ha):
    """u/u_centre for Hartmann flow, y in [-1, 1] (scaled by a)."""
    y = np.asarray(y, float)
    if Ha < 1e-8:
        return 1.0 - y**2               # Poiseuille limit
    return (np.cosh(Ha) - np.cosh(Ha * y)) / (np.cosh(Ha) - 1.0)

def magnetosonic_speeds(cs, vA, theta):
    """Phase speeds (slow, Alfven, fast) for angle theta between k and B0."""
    c2 = np.cos(theta)**2
    s = cs**2 + vA**2
    disc = np.sqrt(s**2 - 4 * cs**2 * vA**2 * c2)
    fast = np.sqrt(0.5 * (s + disc))
    slow = np.sqrt(np.maximum(0.5 * (s - disc), 0))
    alf = vA * np.sqrt(c2)
    return slow, alf, fast

10Exercises with solutions

Exercise 1 (conducting fluids). Compute the magnetic diffusivity of liquid sodium (σ = 1.0 × 10⁷ S m⁻¹) and of seawater (σ = 5 S m⁻¹). How long does a field of scale 1 m take to diffuse out of each?

Solution

η = 1/(μ₀σ). Sodium: η = 1/(1.257 × 10⁻⁶ × 10⁷) ≈ 0.080 m² s⁻¹, so τ ≈ L²/η ≈ 12.6 s. Seawater: η ≈ 1/(1.257 × 10⁻⁶ × 5) ≈ 1.6 × 10⁵ m² s⁻¹, so τ ≈ 6 µs. In seawater the field is essentially unaffected by the flow.

Exercise 2 (Lorentz force). Show that the Lorentz force has no component along B, and explain why this means MHD effects cannot slow a flow that is everywhere parallel to a uniform field.

Solution

B·(j×B) = 0 identically, because a vector triple product with two equal vectors vanishes. Also, for u ∥ B uniform, u×B = 0, so the motional EMF vanishes, no current is induced, and there is no Lorentz force at all. The flow behaves as if no field were present.

Exercise 3 (magnetic pressure). A tokamak has a toroidal field B = 5 T. What is the magnetic pressure? If β = 3 %, what plasma pressure is confined?

Solution

pB = B²/(2μ₀) = 25/(2 × 1.257 × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 9.9 × 10⁶ Pa (about 100 atmospheres). Plasma pressure p = β pB ≈ 0.03 × 9.9 × 10⁶ ≈ 3.0 × 10⁵ Pa, about 3 atmospheres.

Exercise 4 (induction equation). Starting from the induction equation with η = 0 and an incompressible flow, show that , and interpret this.

Solution

Expand ∇×(u×B) = u(∇·B) − B(∇·u) + (B·∇)u − (u·∇)B. With ∇·B = 0 and ∇·u = 0, this is (B·∇)u − (u·∇)B. Moving the advective term to the left gives ∂B/∂t + (u·∇)B = (B·∇)u. The right side represents stretching of field lines by velocity gradients along B, exactly as vortex stretching amplifies vorticity in Euler flow. It is the mechanism by which a flow amplifies field.

Exercise 5 (magnetic Reynolds number). The Sun's convection zone has U ~ 100 m s⁻¹ on granulation scales L ~ 10⁶ m, with η ~ 10² m² s⁻¹ (an order-of-magnitude estimate). Estimate Rm and say which regime applies.

Solution

Rm = UL/η ~ 100 × 10⁶ / 10² = 10⁶. Field lines are very nearly frozen in; the flow sweeps field into intergranular lanes where it concentrates, consistent with the observed small-scale flux concentrations in the photosphere.

Exercise 7 (MHD waves). In a tokamak plasma with B = 5 T and deuterium density n = 10²⁰ m⁻³, calculate the Alfvén speed. Compare with the speed of light.

Solution

ρ = n × 2mp ≈ 10²⁰ × 3.34 × 10⁻²⁷ = 3.34 × 10⁻⁷ kg m⁻³. vA = 5/√(1.257 × 10⁻⁶ × 3.34 × 10⁻⁷) ≈ 5/6.48 × 10⁻⁷ ≈ 7.7 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹, about 2.6 % of c. (If vA approaches c, relativistic and displacement-current corrections are required: vA → vA/√(1 + vA²/c²) [8].)

Exercise 8 (MHD waves). Prove that the fast speed perpendicular to B₀ equals √(cs² + vA²) and that the slow speed is zero there.

Solution

At θ = 90°, cos θ = 0, so the square root becomes (cs² + vA²). Then vf² = ½[2(cs² + vA²)] = cs² + vA², and vs² = 0. Physically, a perpendicular compression is resisted by the sum of gas and magnetic pressure (magnetic "sound speed").

Exercise 9 (plasma flow). For the Sweet–Parker example in §8, estimate the reconnection time τ = L/vin and compare with a flare time scale of ~10³ s.

Solution

vin ≈ vAS−1/2 ≈ 1.38 × 10⁶ × (1.4 × 10¹³)−1/2 ≈ 0.37 m s⁻¹. τ = L/vin ≈ 10⁷/0.37 ≈ 2.7 × 10⁷ s, close to a year. This exceeds the observed flare time by more than four orders of magnitude, which is the "fast reconnection problem" [13][25].

Exercise 10 (programming). Extend mhd_tools.py with a function that returns the angle ψ of the Parker spiral field with the radial direction, tan ψ = Ωr/Vsw, and evaluate it at 1 AU for Vsw = 400 km s⁻¹.

Solution 
def parker_angle_deg(r, V_sw, P_rot_days=25.38):
    Omega = 2*np.pi / (P_rot_days*86400)
    return np.degrees(np.arctan(Omega*r / V_sw))

print(parker_angle_deg(1.496e11, 4e5))   # ≈ 45 degrees

Ω ≈ 2.87 × 10⁻⁶ s⁻¹, Ωr ≈ 4.3 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹, so tan ψ ≈ 1.07 and ψ ≈ 47°, consistent with the roughly 45° spiral angle observed at Earth [23][13].

11Trivia questions with answers

  1. Which scientist tried to detect the voltage induced by the River Thames, and from which bridge?
    Answer

    Michael Faraday, in 1832, from Waterloo Bridge [3].

  2. Who won the 1970 Nobel Prize in Physics for work in magnetohydrodynamics?
    Answer

    Hannes Alfvén (shared that year with Louis Néel, who was honoured for work on magnetism) [7].

  3. What liquid did Julius Hartmann use in his 1930s experiments, giving them the name "Hg-dynamics"?
    Answer

    Mercury (chemical symbol Hg) [4][5].

  4. In which journal and year did Alfvén announce MHD waves, in a paper less than two pages long?
    Answer

    Nature, 1942 [6].

  5. Which theorem says that an axisymmetric magnetic field cannot be sustained by dynamo action?
    Answer

    Cowling's anti-dynamo theorem (1933) [16].

  6. Which two European cities hosted the first successful liquid-sodium laboratory dynamos?
    Answer

    Riga, Latvia, and Karlsruhe, Germany [18][19].

  7. What was the name of the Japanese ship propelled by superconducting MHD thrusters in 1992?
    Answer

    Yamato-1 [27].

  8. What is the name of the spiral shape traced by the interplanetary magnetic field?
    Answer

    The Parker spiral, after Eugene Parker's 1958 solar-wind theory [23].

  9. Which spacecraft was the first to "touch the Sun" by crossing the Alfvén critical surface, and in what year?
    Answer

    NASA's Parker Solar Probe, in April 2021 [24].

  10. If B is doubled in a given fluid, by what factor does the Alfvén speed change? And the magnetic pressure?
    Answer

    vA ∝ B, so it doubles; magnetic pressure ∝ B², so it quadruples [1].

  11. What dimensionless number compares advection of magnetic field to its diffusion?
    Answer

    The magnetic Reynolds number, Rm = UL/η [1].

  12. Who first observed Alfvén waves in a laboratory, and in what fluid?
    Answer

    Stig Lundquist, in 1949, in liquid mercury [20].
