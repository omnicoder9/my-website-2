1. What is fluid–structure interaction?

Fluid–structure interaction (FSI) is the mutual, two-way interaction between a deformable or movable structure and a surrounding or internal fluid flow. The flow loads the structure; the structure moves or deforms; the motion changes the flow boundary and hence the loads1,2. When the coupling is weak (for example, wind load on a stiff building) a one-way analysis — compute pressures, then apply them to the structure — is adequate. When the structural motion substantially alters the flow, as in flutter, lock-in or a flexible heart valve, the problem must be solved as a coupled system3,4.

FSI phenomena are conventionally classified by the dominant mechanism of energy transfer2,5:

  • Forced (extraneously induced) vibration – turbulence buffeting or wave loads that exist independently of the motion.
  • Instability-induced vibration – a flow instability, such as periodic vortex shedding, that locks on to structural motion (vortex-induced vibration).
  • Movement-induced (self-excited) vibration – fluid forces that are generated by the motion itself and can feed energy into it, giving flutter, galloping and divergence.

2. Coupled fluid–solid motion

Governing equations

The fluid is usually described by the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations written on a moving domain Ωf(t). In the arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE) form the mesh moves with velocity w, neither fixed in space nor attached to particles10,11:

ρf [ ∂u/∂t|χ + ((u − w)·∇)u ] = ∇·σf,   ∇·u = 0,   σf = −pI + μ(∇u + ∇uT)(1)

The structure obeys elastodynamics in the Lagrangian (reference) configuration, with displacement d, first Piola–Kirchhoff stress P and body force b:

ρs ∂²d/∂t² = ∇0·P + ρsb(2)

The two fields are tied together on the wetted interface Γ by two conditions3,4:

Kinematic (no-slip): u = ∂d/∂t on Γ;    Dynamic (traction balance): σf·n = σs·n on Γ(3)

Added mass

Any body accelerating in a fluid must also accelerate some of the surrounding fluid, so it behaves as if it were heavier. For a circular cylinder of diameter D in an ideal fluid the added mass per unit length is the mass of displaced fluid, ma = CAρπD²/4 with CA = 15,12. Added mass lowers natural frequencies in water, and — as discussed below — it is the main reason simple partitioned FSI solvers become unstable when the fluid is dense relative to the structure13.

Worked example 2.1 — natural frequency of a cylinder in water

A pipe has structural mass ms = 20 kg m⁻¹ and outer diameter D = 0.10 m. Its natural frequency in air is fair. What is it when submerged in water (ρ = 1000 kg m⁻³)?

ma = 1 × 1000 × π × 0.10²/4 = 7.85 kg m⁻¹. Stiffness is unchanged, so f ∝ 1/√m:

fwater/fair = √[20/(20 + 7.85)] = 0.847, a 15 % drop in natural frequency.

Numerical strategies

Monolithic schemes assemble fluid, structure and mesh equations into one system and solve them simultaneously; they are robust but need a dedicated solver. Partitioned schemes couple separate, often existing, fluid and structure codes by exchanging interface data (Fig. 1)3,4. Explicit ("loosely coupled") partitioned schemes exchange data once per time step and can be unconditionally unstable when the fluid density is comparable to the structural density — a result proved for a model problem by Causin, Gerbeau and Nobile13. Implicit ("strongly coupled") schemes iterate each step to convergence, often accelerated by Aitken dynamic relaxation14. Interface tracking is handled either with body-fitted ALE meshes11 or with non-conforming methods such as the immersed boundary method, invented by Peskin to model blood flow in the heart15. The Turek–Hron benchmark (an elastic flag behind a cylinder) is a standard test for verifying FSI codes16.

3. Aeroelasticity

Aeroelasticity studies the interaction of aerodynamic, elastic and inertial forces. Collar organised the field as a triangle (Fig. 2): each edge pairs two kinds of force and the centre needs all three17,18.

Static aeroelasticity: divergence and control reversal

Consider the "typical section" (Fig. 3): a rigid airfoil of chord c = 2b on a torsional spring kθ at the elastic axis (EA), with the aerodynamic centre (AC) a distance e·b ahead of the EA. A twist θ produces lift L = qcCLαθ, whose moment about the EA, L·e·b, twists the section further. Equilibrium requires18,19

kθθ = q c CLα (e b)(α0 + θ)  ⇒  θ = q c e b CLα α0 / (kθ − q c e b CLα)(4)

The twist grows without bound when the dynamic pressure reaches the divergence dynamic pressure

qD = kθ / (c · e b · CLα)(5)

Divergence is avoided by putting the elastic axis close to, or ahead of, the aerodynamic centre. A related static effect, control reversal, occurs when deflecting an aileron twists the wing enough to cancel the intended rolling moment18,19. Wing twist-induced divergence has been suggested as a factor in the 1903 failures of Langley's Aerodrome and was a real problem for the Fokker D-VIII monoplane in 191820.

4. Flutter

Flutter is a dynamic, self-excited aeroelastic instability: above a critical airspeed the aerodynamic forces created by the motion do net positive work on the structure each cycle, and oscillations grow until something limits or breaks them18,21. The classic aircraft form is bending–torsion (binary) flutter, in which a plunge-dominated mode and a pitch-dominated mode coalesce in frequency and their phasing lets the airflow pump energy into the structure19. Flutter killed aircraft from the First World War onward; Garrick and Reed record the tail flutter of a Handley Page O/400 bomber in 1916 as one of the first documented cases20.

Equations of the typical section

With mass m, static unbalance S = m xθb, pitch inertia I = m r²b² and quasi-steady lift L = ρU²bCLαθ, the plunge–pitch equations are19

m ḧ + S θ̈ + kh h + ρU²b CLα θ = 0
S ḧ + I θ̈ + (kθ − ρU²b²e CLα) θ = 0(6)

Assuming solutions ∝ eiωt turns (6) into the generalised eigenproblem det(K(U) − ω²M) = 0, a quadratic in ω². Because the aerodynamic stiffness matrix is non-symmetric, the two real roots can merge and become a complex-conjugate pair; the root with negative imaginary part grows as e|Im ω|t. The speed at which this first happens is the flutter speed UF.

The quasi-steady model above captures coalescence but neglects the lag of the wake. Theodorsen's 1935 unsteady theory supplies the exact incompressible lift and moment on a harmonically oscillating thin airfoil through the complex function C(k) of the reduced frequency k = ωb/U6; industrial practice uses the p–k method and doublet-lattice or CFD aerodynamics19,21.

The closer the uncoupled bending and torsion frequencies, the less aerodynamic coupling is needed to merge them, so the flutter speed falls. Designers therefore keep torsional frequencies well above bending frequencies and move the centre of gravity forward (mass balancing), reducing xθ18,19.

Bridge flutter and the Tacoma Narrows Bridge

Long-span bridge decks are bluff sections, and their aeroelastic loads are characterised experimentally by the flutter derivatives introduced by Scanlan and Tomko22,23. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed on 7 November 1940 in a wind of about 19 m s⁻¹ (42 mph) after it switched to a large torsional oscillation. Billah and Scanlan showed that the textbook explanation — simple resonance between vortex shedding and the deck — is wrong: the vortex-shedding frequency at that wind speed was far from the torsional frequency of about 0.2 Hz (roughly 12 cycles per minute), and the failure was a self-excited, single-degree-of-freedom torsional flutter driven by negative aerodynamic damping24.

5. Vortex-induced vibration

Vortex shedding and the Strouhal number

Above Re ≈ 47 the wake of a circular cylinder becomes unsteady and sheds alternating vortices, the von Kármán street9. The shedding frequency fs scales with U/D through the Strouhal number, named after Čeněk (Vincenz) Strouhal, who studied the "aeolian tones" of wires moving through air in 18788:

St = fs D / U ≈ 0.2   (roughly 300 < Re < 2×10⁵ for a circular cylinder)(7)

Each shed vortex produces an oscillating lift force at fs and an oscillating drag at 2fs5,9.

Lock-in (synchronisation)

If the cylinder can move, the shedding frequency stops following the Strouhal line when it approaches the structural natural frequency fn: the wake locks in to the body motion, and large-amplitude, nearly sinusoidal oscillations occur over a band of reduced velocity centred near Ur ≈ 1/St ≈ 525–27. VIV is self-limiting — amplitudes saturate at about one diameter — because the wake decorrelates at large motion26. Key experimental findings:

  • At high mass–damping (typical in air) the response has an initial and a lower branch; at low m*ζ (typical in water) a high-amplitude upper branch appears with peak amplitudes near A*/D ≈ 17,26.
  • For low mass ratios the oscillation frequency in lock-in exceeds fn, and below a critical mass ratio m*crit ≈ 0.54 the lock-in band extends to infinite reduced velocity28.
  • The wake modes are labelled by vortices shed per cycle, e.g. 2S (two single vortices) and 2P (two pairs)26.

Wake-oscillator model

A popular reduced-order model treats the wake as a van der Pol oscillator q (proportional to the fluctuating lift coefficient) coupled to the structure through an acceleration term. In dimensionless time t = ΩfT, Ωf = 2πSt U/D, Facchinetti, de Langre and Biolley proposed29

ÿ + (2ζδ + γ/μ) ẏ + δ²y = M q,      q̈ + ε(q² − 1) q̇ + q = A ÿ(8)

with δ = 1/(St Ur), γ = CD/(4πSt), M = CL0/(16π²St²μ), and empirical constants ε = 0.3, A = 12 calibrated to experiments29. Figure 6 shows the result from viv_lockin.py for a light, lightly damped cylinder (m* = 2.4, ζ = 0.0045, close to the conditions of Khalak & Williamson7).

Worked example 5.1 — will a riser lock in?

A marine riser span of D = 0.5 m has a first natural frequency in water of fn = 0.40 Hz. A 1.0 m s⁻¹ current flows past it.

Shedding frequency for a fixed cylinder: fs = 0.2 × 1.0/0.5 = 0.40 Hz. Reduced velocity: Ur = 1.0/(0.40 × 0.5) = 5.0. This sits in the middle of the lock-in range, so large VIV (order one diameter) is to be expected unless m*ζ is high. Common remedies are helical strakes or fairings that disrupt spanwise correlation of shedding, extra damping, or a stiffness change that moves Ur out of the band5,26.

Galloping is not VIV

Non-circular sections (iced cables, square towers) can gallop: a quasi-steady instability in which transverse motion changes the apparent angle of attack, and lift acts in the direction of motion. Den Hartog's criterion for instability is dCL/dα + CD < 05,30. Unlike VIV it is not self-limiting at one diameter and its amplitude keeps growing with wind speed.

6. Hydroelasticity

Hydroelasticity is the water counterpart of aeroelasticity, but with two big differences: water is about 800 times denser than air, so added mass and fluid damping are large, and a free surface supports waves31,32.

Added mass of submerged plates

In 1920 Lamb analysed a clamped circular plate in contact with water on one side and showed that the fluid lowers its fundamental frequency through an added virtual mass incremental factor β33:

fwater / fvacuum = 1/√(1 + β),   β = Γ ρf a / (ρs t)(9)

where a is the plate radius, t its thickness and Γ a non-dimensional coefficient that depends on the mode and boundary conditions (Lamb found Γ ≈ 0.67 for the fundamental mode of a clamped plate)33.

Ships and offshore structures

  • Springing: steady resonant two-node vertical bending of a ship hull excited by waves whose encounter frequency (or its harmonics) matches the hull's natural frequency31,32.
  • Whipping: a transient hull-girder vibration triggered by bow slamming impacts31,34.
  • Hydroelastic slamming: for impacts on flexible plating the local structural deformation interacts with the impact pressure, and peak pressures alone can be misleading34.
  • Very large floating structures (VLFS): pontoon-type floating airports or platforms are so long and thin relative to wavelength that their elastic deflection dominates over rigid-body motion35.

Worked example 6.1 — hydroelastic frequency drop

A steel panel (ρs = 7850 kg m⁻³, t = 10 mm, radius a = 0.5 m) is wetted on one side by seawater (ρf = 1025 kg m⁻³). With Γ ≈ 0.67:

β = 0.67 × 1025 × 0.5 / (7850 × 0.010) = 4.37, so fwater/fvacuum = 1/√5.37 = 0.43. The wetted panel vibrates at less than half its dry frequency — a typical size of effect in ship structures.

7. Sloshing

Sloshing is the motion of a liquid free surface inside a partially filled container. It matters for road and rail tankers, LNG carriers, rocket propellant tanks, seismic loads on storage tanks and for tuned liquid dampers that deliberately use sloshing to damp buildings36–38. The space programme drove much early research; NASA SP-106 (1966) remains a classic compendium38.

Linear natural frequencies

For small amplitudes and potential flow, the natural frequencies of a rectangular tank of length L (in the excitation direction) filled to depth h are36,37

ωn² = g kn tanh(knh),   kn = nπ/L,  n = 1, 2, 3, …(10)

For an upright circular cylinder of radius R, the antisymmetric (m = 1) modes that respond to lateral excitation are

ω1n² = (g ξ1n/R) tanh(ξ1nh/R),   J1′(ξ1n) = 0 ⇒ ξ11 = 1.8412, ξ12 = 5.3314, …(11)

The tanh factor gives the shallow-water limit ω ≈ k√(gh) and the deep-water limit ω ≈ √(gk) as h grows.

Worked example 7.1 — road-tanker compartment

A compartment 2.0 m long is filled to h = 0.6 m. k1 = π/2.0 = 1.571 m⁻¹, tanh(1.571 × 0.6) = tanh(0.942) = 0.737.

ω1 = √(9.81 × 1.571 × 0.737) = 3.37 rad s⁻¹ ⇒ f1 = 0.54 Hz (period 1.9 s), which agrees with sloshing.py. Braking and lane-change manoeuvres at this frequency can amplify the slosh loads; transverse baffles are used to split the compartment and raise the frequency36.

Beyond linear theory, sloshing exhibits nonlinear phenomena — amplitude-dependent frequency, swirling in circular tanks, hydraulic jumps and violent impacts on tank roofs — reviewed by Ibrahim, Pilipchuk and Ikeda39 and Faltinsen and Timokha37. When the liquid mass is a substantial fraction of the system mass (spacecraft, tankers, liquid dampers), slosh and container motion must be solved as a coupled FSI problem.

8. Structural deformation and reconfiguration

When Ca is large, structures undergo large deflections that change their shape — and therefore the flow. This "geometric" nonlinearity is central to flexible wings, sails, parachutes, membranes, vegetation and biological tissues, and it is what makes FSI computations hard: large mesh motion in ALE methods, or the need for immersed or fictitious-domain methods3,15,40.

Drag reduction by bending

A rigid bluff body has drag F ∝ U². Flexible bodies bend and streamline as the speed rises, so their drag grows more slowly. Vogel measured leaves rolling into cones in high winds with sharply reduced drag41. Alben, Shelley and Zhang showed with a flexible fibre in a flowing soap film, together with theory, that at large speed the drag scales as U4/3 instead of U²42. A common way to quantify this is the Vogel exponent 𝒱 in F ∝ U2+𝒱; the fibre result gives 𝒱 = −2/3.

Biological and biomedical FSI

Heart valves, arteries and vocal folds have densities close to that of blood or air-loaded tissue, which makes them strongly coupled. Peskin's immersed boundary method was developed precisely for heart-valve flow, representing the elastic fibres as forces spread onto a fixed Cartesian fluid grid15. Isogeometric and ALE space–time methods are now used for patient-specific cardiovascular FSI40.

9.1 Strouhal shedding and VIV lock-in (wake oscillator)

"""
Vortex-induced vibration (VIV) of an elastically mounted rigid cylinder:
Strouhal shedding + coupled structure/wake-oscillator model of
Facchinetti, de Langre & Biolley (J Fluids Struct 2004;19:123-40).

Dimensionless time t = Omega_f * T, with Omega_f = 2*pi*St*U/D (shedding
frequency of a fixed cylinder). y = Y/D is the cross-flow displacement and
q is the wake variable (q = 2*C_L/C_L0).

  structure:  y'' + (2*xi*delta + gamma/mu) y' + delta^2 y = M q
  wake:       q'' + eps (q^2 - 1) q' + q          = A y''

  delta = omega_s / Omega_f = 1 / (St * Ur),  Ur = U / (f_n D)
  gamma = C_D / (4 pi St),   M = C_L0 / (16 pi^2 St^2 mu)
  mu    = (m_s + m_f) / (rho D^2),  m_f = C_A * rho * pi D^2 / 4
"""
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

# --- fluid / wake constants (values used by Facchinetti et al. 2004) ---
St, CL0, eps, A, gamma = 0.2, 0.3, 0.3, 12.0, 0.8

# --- structure: low mass-damping case similar to Khalak & Williamson (1999)
m_star, CA, xi = 2.4, 1.0, 0.0045           # mass ratio, added-mass coeff., damping ratio
mu = (m_star + CA) * np.pi / 4.0            # (m_s + m_f)/(rho D^2)
M = CL0 / (16.0 * np.pi**2 * St**2 * mu)


def strouhal_frequency(U, D, St=0.2):
    """Vortex-shedding frequency f_s = St*U/D of a fixed cylinder [Hz]."""
    return St * U / D


def rhs(t, s, delta):
    y, yd, q, qd = s
    ydd = M * q - (2 * xi * delta + gamma / mu) * yd - delta**2 * y
    qdd = A * ydd - eps * (q**2 - 1.0) * qd - q
    return [yd, ydd, qd, qdd]


def response(Ur, t_end=1200.0):
    """Return steady amplitude y0 = Y0/D and frequency ratio f/f_n."""
    delta = 1.0 / (St * Ur)
    sol = solve_ivp(rhs, (0, t_end), [0.0, 0.0, 2.0, 0.0], args=(delta,),
                    max_step=0.05, rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-10)
    t, y = sol.t, sol.y[0]
    keep = t > 0.6 * t_end                         # discard transient
    y0 = 0.5 * (y[keep].max() - y[keep].min())
    # oscillation frequency from zero up-crossings
    yk, tk = y[keep] - y[keep].mean(), t[keep]
    up = np.where((yk[:-1] < 0) & (yk[1:] >= 0))[0]
    omega = 2 * np.pi / np.mean(np.diff(tk[up]))   # in units of Omega_f
    f_over_fn = omega / delta
    return y0, f_over_fn


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"Example: U = 1.5 m/s, D = 0.05 m -> f_s = {strouhal_frequency(1.5, 0.05):.1f} Hz")
    print(f"mass ratio m* = {m_star}, mu = {mu:.3f}, M = {M:.4f}")
    print(" Ur    Y0/D    f/f_n   St*Ur (unlocked shedding)")
    for Ur in np.arange(2.0, 12.1, 1.0):
        y0, fr = response(Ur)
        print(f"{Ur:4.1f}  {y0:6.3f}  {fr:6.3f}   {St*Ur:5.2f}")

9.2 Two-degree-of-freedom flutter eigenvalue model

"""
Binary (pitch-plunge) flutter of a typical section with steady aerodynamics,
after Hodges & Pierce, Introduction to Structural Dynamics and Aeroelasticity,
2nd ed., Ch. 5.

Per unit span, h = plunge (positive down), theta = pitch (positive nose up):
    m h''     + S theta'' + k_h h          = -L
    S h''     + I theta'' + k_theta theta  =  L * e * b
with steady lift L = rho U^2 b C_La theta (lift-curve slope C_La = 2*pi),
S = m x_theta b,  I = m r^2 b^2,  k_h = m w_h^2,  k_theta = I w_theta^2.
Assume x = x0 exp(i w t)  ->  [K(U) - w^2 M] x0 = 0.
Flutter: two real frequencies coalesce and w^2 becomes complex.
Divergence: effective pitch stiffness k_theta - rho U^2 b^2 e C_La -> 0.
"""
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import eigvals

# Non-dimensional parameters (Hodges & Pierce example section)
mu, r2, sigma, x_th, e = 20.0, 6/25, 2/5, 1/10, 1/10   # sigma = w_h / w_theta
CLa = 2 * np.pi
# Scale: b = 1, w_theta = 1, rho chosen so that m = mu*rho*pi*b^2 = 1
b, w_th, m = 1.0, 1.0, 1.0
rho = m / (mu * np.pi * b**2)
S, I = m * x_th * b, m * r2 * b**2
k_h, k_th = m * (sigma * w_th)**2, I * w_th**2
Mmat = np.array([[m, S], [S, I]])


def frequencies(V):
    """Return the two complex w/w_theta at reduced speed V = U/(b w_theta)."""
    U = V * b * w_th
    q_term = rho * U**2 * b * CLa
    K = np.array([[k_h, q_term], [0.0, k_th - q_term * e * b]])
    lam = eigvals(K, Mmat)                    # lam = w^2 (may be complex)
    w = np.sqrt(lam.astype(complex))
    return np.sort_complex(w)


def is_unstable(V, tol=1e-9):
    return np.max(np.abs(frequencies(V).imag)) > tol


def flutter_speed(V_lo=0.0, V_hi=10.0, n=2000):
    Vs = np.linspace(V_lo, V_hi, n)
    for v0, v1 in zip(Vs[:-1], Vs[1:]):
        if not is_unstable(v0) and is_unstable(v1):
            for _ in range(60):               # bisection
                vm = 0.5 * (v0 + v1)
                v0, v1 = (vm, v1) if not is_unstable(vm) else (v0, vm)
            return 0.5 * (v0 + v1)
    return None


if __name__ == "__main__":
    VF = flutter_speed()
    VD = np.sqrt(mu * r2 / (2 * e))           # closed-form divergence speed
    print(f"Flutter speed     U_F/(b w_theta) = {VF:.4f}")
    print(f"Divergence speed  U_D/(b w_theta) = {VD:.4f}")
    print(f"Coalescence frequency w/w_theta   = {frequencies(VF)[0].real:.4f}")
    print("\n  V     w1/w_th          w2/w_th")
    for V in np.arange(0.0, 3.01, 0.25):
        w1, w2 = frequencies(V)
        print(f"{V:4.2f}  {w1.real:.4f}{w1.imag:+.4f}i  {w2.real:.4f}{w2.imag:+.4f}i")

9.3 Sloshing natural frequencies

"""
Linear natural sloshing frequencies (potential flow, small amplitude),
see Ibrahim (2005) and Faltinsen & Timokha (2009).

Rectangular tank, length L along excitation, fill depth h:
    w_n^2 = g * k_n * tanh(k_n h),   k_n = n*pi/L,   n = 1, 2, ...
Upright circular cylinder, radius R, fill depth h (antisymmetric modes, m = 1):
    w_1n^2 = g * (xi_1n / R) * tanh(xi_1n h / R),  xi_1n = zeros of J1'(x)
"""
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import jnp_zeros

g = 9.81


def rect_tank(L, h, n_modes=3):
    n = np.arange(1, n_modes + 1)
    k = n * np.pi / L
    return np.sqrt(g * k * np.tanh(k * h)) / (2 * np.pi)        # Hz


def cyl_tank(R, h, n_modes=3):
    xi = jnp_zeros(1, n_modes)                                  # 1.8412, 5.3314, 8.5363

10. Exercises with solutions

  1. Aeolian vibration. A 25 mm diameter overhead conductor is exposed to a steady 8 m s⁻¹ crosswind. Estimate the vortex-shedding frequency.
    SolutionRe = 8 × 0.025 / 1.5×10⁻⁵ ≈ 1.3×10⁴, within the range where St ≈ 0.29. fs = 0.2 × 8 / 0.025 = 64 Hz. This is the high-frequency, low-amplitude "aeolian vibration" that fatigues conductors at clamps; Stockbridge dampers are used to absorb it5.
  2. Added mass. A cylinder with ms = 12 kg m⁻¹ and D = 0.20 m is moved from air into fresh water. By what factor does its natural frequency change? Take CA = 1.
    Solutionma = 1000 π (0.2)²/4 = 31.4 kg m⁻¹. fw/fa = √[12/(12 + 31.4)] = 0.53. Because this cylinder is light relative to the water it displaces (m* = 0.38), the added mass more than triples its effective mass. Note that m* is below the critical value of about 0.54 found by Govardhan and Williamson, so if it were free to vibrate it would show lock-in that persists to very high reduced velocity28.
  3. Divergence speed. A wing section has kθ = 5.0×10⁴ N m rad⁻¹ per metre span, chord c = 1.5 m, CLα = 2π, and its aerodynamic centre lies 0.15 m ahead of the elastic axis. Find the divergence dynamic pressure and speed at sea level (ρ = 1.225 kg m⁻³).
    SolutionFrom Eq. (5) with eb = 0.15 m: qD = 5.0×10⁴/(1.5 × 0.15 × 2π) = 35.4 kPa. UD = √(2qD/ρ) = √(2 × 35 368 / 1.225) = 240 m s⁻¹. Halving the AC–EA offset doubles qD and raises UD by √2.
  4. Ship tank sloshing. A ballast tank is 10 m long and filled to 4 m. Find the first natural sloshing period. Would it be of concern for a ship with a roll period of about 10 s?
    Solutionk1 = π/10 = 0.314 m⁻¹; tanh(0.314 × 4) = tanh(1.257) = 0.850. ω1 = √(9.81 × 0.314 × 0.850) = 1.62 rad s⁻¹, so T1 = 3.9 s (f1 = 0.26 Hz). This is well away from a 10 s roll period, so first-mode resonance is unlikely in roll, but shorter-period pitch or heave motions could still excite it. (Passive anti-roll tanks are deliberately tuned to the roll period36.)
  5. Limits of the dispersion relation. Show that Eq. (10) reduces to the shallow-water result ω = k√(gh) when kh ≪ 1 and to the deep-water result ω = √(gk) when kh ≫ 1. For L = 2 m, how deep must the fill be for the first mode frequency to be within 1 % of the deep-water value?
    SolutionFor small x, tanh x ≈ x, so ω² ≈ gk·kh = gk²h ⇒ ω = k√(gh). For large x, tanh x → 1 ⇒ ω² = gk. Within 1 % in frequency means √tanh(kh) ≥ 0.99, i.e. tanh(kh) ≥ 0.9801, giving kh ≥ 2.30. With k = π/2 = 1.571 m⁻¹, h ≥ 1.47 m (h/L ≈ 0.73). The table printed by sloshing.py shows f1 barely changes beyond h/L ≈ 0.5.
  6. Flutter parameter study. Using flutter_2dof.py, (a) set xθ = 0 (CG on the elastic axis, so S = 0) and look for the flutter speed; (b) explain the result.
    Solution(a) With xθ = 0 the mass matrix is diagonal. The stiffness matrix is upper-triangular, so the eigenvalues are simply ωh² and (kθ − qceb CLα)/I: both stay real, so there is no flutter. The script's first instability is now at U/(bωθ) = 4.90, which is the divergence speed: there the pitch root ω² passes through zero and becomes negative (a static, non-oscillatory instability), not a frequency coalescence. (b) In this steady-aerodynamics model, binary flutter needs both aerodynamic coupling (pitch causes lift, which drives plunge) and inertial coupling (plunge acceleration drives pitch through S = m xθb). Removing the inertial coupling breaks the loop. This is the theoretical basis of mass balancing control surfaces and wings18,19. With unsteady (Theodorsen) aerodynamics, flutter can still occur at xθ = 0 for some parameter sets, so mass balance is a strong aid, not a guarantee6,21.
  7. Mass–damping comparison. Riser A has m* = 3.0, ζ = 0.003; cylinder B in a wind tunnel has m* = 250, ζ = 0.002. Which is likely to show the upper branch of VIV response?
    Solutionm*ζ(A) = 0.009; m*ζ(B) = 0.5. Riser A has a mass–damping two orders of magnitude lower and will show the high-amplitude upper branch (A*/D near 1) with a wide lock-in range. Cylinder B, typical of experiments in air, will show only the initial and lower branches with much smaller amplitude7,26.
  8. Galloping check. Wind-tunnel tests on an iced conductor give CD = 1.2 and dCL/dα = −2.5 rad⁻¹ at the operating angle. Is it susceptible to galloping?
    SolutionDen Hartog's criterion: dCL/dα + CD = −2.5 + 1.2 = −1.3 < 0, so the aerodynamic damping is negative and galloping is possible once it exceeds the structural damping, i.e. above an onset wind speed proportional to the mass–damping5,30.
  9. Partitioned-solver stability. A student couples an existing CFD code to an FE code with a loosely coupled explicit scheme. It works for an aluminium panel in air but blows up for a thin rubber membrane in water, even with smaller time steps. Why, and what should they do?
    SolutionThe instability is the added-mass effect. With an explicit partitioned scheme the fluid force on the structure depends on the previous-iteration acceleration through the added mass; when the ratio of added mass to structural mass exceeds a threshold the error is amplified at each exchange. Causin et al. showed that for a model problem this instability does not go away as the time step is reduced — it gets worse13. Rubber in water (ρs/ρf ≈ 1) has a large added-mass ratio; aluminium in air (≈ 2200) does not. Remedies: strongly coupled sub-iterations with Aitken relaxation14, Robin-type interface conditions, or a monolithic solver3.
  10. Hydroelastic panel. Repeat Worked example 6.1 for an aluminium panel (ρs = 2700 kg m⁻³) of the same size. How does the frequency ratio change?
    Solutionβ = 0.67 × 1025 × 0.5/(2700 × 0.010) = 12.7, so fwater/fvacuum = 1/√13.7 = 0.27. The lighter panel is affected far more, because what matters is the ratio of the fluid mass "seen" by the plate to its own mass33.

11. Trivia questions with answers

  1. Which bridge's 1940 collapse is the most famous FSI failure, and was it caused by vortex-shedding resonance?
    AnswerThe Tacoma Narrows Bridge (7 November 1940). No — Billah and Scanlan showed it failed by self-excited torsional flutter, not by resonance with vortex shedding24.
  2. Who is the Strouhal number named after, and what was he studying?
    AnswerČeněk (Vincenz) Strouhal, a Czech physicist, who in 1878 studied the tones produced by wires whirled through air — the "aeolian tones"8.
  3. What are the three vertices of Collar's aeroelastic triangle?
    AnswerAerodynamic, elastic and inertial forces17.
  4. Which 1935 NACA report gave the classic unsteady aerodynamic theory of flutter?
    AnswerNACA Report 496, "General theory of aerodynamic instability and the mechanism of flutter", by Theodore Theodorsen6.
  5. What is the added mass of a circular cylinder accelerating in an ideal fluid?
    AnswerExactly the mass of the fluid it displaces: ρπD²/4 per unit length (CA = 1)12.
  6. Approximately what is the Strouhal number of a circular cylinder over a wide range of Reynolds numbers?
    AnswerAbout 0.2 (roughly 300 < Re < 2×10⁵)5,9.
  7. What numerical method for FSI was invented to simulate blood flow in the heart?
    AnswerCharles Peskin's immersed boundary method15.
  8. Why does coffee spill when you walk, according to a 2012 Physical Review E paper?
    AnswerMayer and Krechetnikov showed that the natural sloshing frequency of a typical mug is close to the frequencies in the cup's motion during walking (step frequency and its harmonics, plus random back-and-forth motion), so walking excites the first slosh mode; holding the mug more loosely, walking slowly and watching the cup all help43. The work received an Ig Nobel Prize in 2012.
  9. Name one of the earliest documented aircraft flutter accidents.
    AnswerTail flutter of the Handley Page O/400 twin-engine bomber in 191620.
  10. How does a flexible fibre's drag scale with speed at high flow speeds?
    AnswerAs U4/3 rather than U², because it bends into a more streamlined shape42.
  11. What is the zero ξ11 = 1.8412 used for?
    AnswerIt is the first zero of J1′, giving the fundamental antisymmetric sloshing frequency of an upright cylindrical tank, Eq. (11)36.
  12. What is the "2P" mode in VIV?
    AnswerA wake pattern in which two pairs of counter-rotating vortices are shed per oscillation cycle, associated with the upper and lower response branches26.
