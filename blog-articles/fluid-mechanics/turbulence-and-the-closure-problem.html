The equation we cannot close

Turbulence is the last unsolved problem of classical physics that a working engineer has to solve before lunch. This is how the profession learned to do it — what averaging buys, what it destroys, and why every constant in a turbulence model is a negotiated position rather than a measurement.

The Navier–Stokes equations are exact. So why does averaging them produce a system that no amount of mathematics can close?

The short answer is that the equations are nonlinear and averaging is linear, and the two do not commute. The long answer is the whole of turbulence modelling — and it turns out to be a story about what engineers are willing to pay, in generality, for a number they can put in a report.

Section I

One number, and a filament of dye

Manchester, 1883. An experiment that settled a question by changing exactly one thing.

Osborne Reynolds built an apparatus of almost insulting simplicity: a glass tube drawing water from a large tank, a valve at the downstream end, and a fine needle injecting a filament of coloured dye on the tube's axis.1 At low flow rates the dye drew a single unbroken thread down the whole length of the tube — you could see the streamline. Open the valve further and the thread began to waver, then to break into discrete eddies at a definite station. Open it further still and the dye was gone, dispersed into the water within a few diameters of the needle.

What made this an experiment rather than a demonstration was that Reynolds varied the flow rate, the tube diameter and the water temperature independently, and found that the transition always happened at the same value of a single dimensionless combination — velocity times diameter divided by kinematic viscosity. The group had appeared in the equations before,2 but Reynolds gave it a physical criterion and a measured value, and that is why it carries his name.

Two features of the result deserve more attention than they usually get. The first is that the transition is a change of regime, not of degree. Nothing about the dye filament is halfway between straight and dispersed; the flow is in one state or the other, and the state changes abruptly. The second is that Reynolds could not pin the transition to a fixed number. With a sufficiently undisturbed inlet the laminar state persisted far past the nominal critical value, and modern experiments in carefully conditioned pipes have sustained laminar flow beyond a Reynolds number of 105.3 Laminar pipe flow is, in the linear sense, stable at every Reynolds number; what happens at 2 300 is that finite-amplitude disturbances stop decaying.4

That subtlety matters for what follows, because it establishes the shape of the whole subject. Turbulence is not a fluid property. It is a property of a flow, and specifically of a flow in which the nonlinear term of the equations of motion has become large compared with the viscous term. Tennekes and Lumley open their textbook by refusing to define turbulence at all, offering instead a list of characteristics.5 That is not evasiveness. It is an accurate report of the state of the field, and it is where we should start too.

Section II

The four characteristics

Chaotic, diffusive, dissipative, three-dimensional — and each one has a number attached to it.

Chaotic, but not random

A turbulent velocity signal looks like noise. It is not noise: it is the output of a deterministic partial differential equation with no stochastic term anywhere in it. What makes it behave like noise is sensitive dependence on initial conditions, and that property is quantifiable.

The canonical demonstration is not turbulence itself but Lorenz's three-mode truncation of Rayleigh–Bénard convection, which he obtained from a fluid problem and found to be aperiodic.6 Take one trajectory, let it settle onto the attractor, clone it, and displace the clone by 10−12 in a single coordinate. The lab below integrates both with identical code and identical arithmetic; the separation grows exponentially at a rate of 0.9050 per time unit, against a published largest Lyapunov exponent of 0.9056.

The accounting is brutal and it is the reason turbulence is treated statistically. Twelve orders of magnitude of initial precision buy 30.5 time units of forecast. Each further decimal digit of measurement accuracy buys 2.54 more. To double the horizon you must square the precision. Determinism and predictability are simply different properties, and no amount of computing power converts one into the other.

So the individual realisation is not worth computing, even in principle. What is worth computing is the statistics — and every practical turbulence method in existence is a way of computing statistics without first computing the realisation they are statistics of.

Diffusive

Turbulence mixes. It mixes momentum, heat and species far faster than molecular processes do, and this is the single property that makes it worth modelling rather than avoiding. The enhancement is not marginal. In the wall layer computation in Section VIII, the ratio of turbulent to molecular momentum diffusivity reaches 1.39 at y+ = 12, 39.6 at y+ = 100, and 410 at y+ = 1 000. Two to three orders of magnitude, achieved by the flow rearranging itself at no cost to the person operating the pipe.

This is also why a laminar solution is not a conservative estimate of anything. A laminar prediction of heat transfer in a turbulent pipe is not slightly wrong; it is wrong by the same two or three orders of magnitude.

Dissipative

Turbulence converts kinetic energy into internal energy through viscous work at the smallest scales, and it does so continuously. Left alone, it decays — the lab integrates exactly that decay in Section IX. Sustained turbulence therefore requires a continuous supply of energy from the mean flow, extracted by the same Reynolds stresses we are about to spend the rest of the article trying to model.

The striking part is the rate. The dissipation rate becomes independent of viscosity in the limit of large Reynolds number: halve the viscosity and the flow reorganises to dissipate just as much, by pushing the cascade further down in scale.7 Viscosity determines where the energy is dissipated, not how much. That is a genuinely strange result — dissipation is a viscous process whose magnitude does not depend on viscosity — and it is the empirical fact that underwrites Kolmogorov's whole framework.

Three-dimensional and rotational

Turbulence is inescapably three-dimensional, and the reason is a single term. The vorticity equation for an incompressible flow contains a vortex-stretching term, ωj ∂ui/∂xj, which vanishes identically in two dimensions because vorticity is then perpendicular to every velocity gradient. Vortex stretching is what intensifies vorticity and transfers energy to smaller scales. Remove it and the cascade reverses: two-dimensional turbulence transfers energy to larger scales, which is why it is a different subject with different phenomenology, not a simplified version of the same one.8

Practically, this means no turbulent flow is ever genuinely two-dimensional, however two-dimensional the geometry and the mean flow are. A model that computes only mean quantities in a plane still has to account for fluctuations in all three directions, and the failure to do so is a real source of error in swirling and rotating flows.

A fifth characteristic, usually left implicit

Turbulence is a continuum phenomenon. The smallest dynamically active eddy is enormous compared with the molecular mean free path — in the car computed later, 26.6 µm against roughly 68 nm, a ratio of about 400. The Navier–Stokes equations therefore remain valid all the way down, and turbulence is genuinely a solution of them rather than a place where they break down. Everything difficult about the subject happens inside a theory we believe is correct.

Section III

The cascade and its price

Richardson's picture, Kolmogorov's arithmetic, and the exponent that forced an entire discipline into existence.

Lewis Fry Richardson, in a book mostly about weather forecasting, described turbulence as a hierarchy in which large eddies pass their energy to smaller eddies, which pass it on again, until the scales are small enough for viscosity to consume it.9 He put the picture into a four-line verse that is quoted in nearly every turbulence text, and the verse has done more work than most equations.

Kolmogorov made the picture quantitative in 1941 with two similarity hypotheses.10 First, at sufficiently high Reynolds number, the small-scale motions are statistically isotropic and independent of the large-scale geometry, and depend only on the viscosity ν and the energy flux ε passing through them. Dimensional analysis then fixes the smallest scale uniquely: η = (ν3/ε)1/4. Second, over a range of scales much smaller than the energy-containing scales but much larger than η, the motion does not depend on viscosity either, and depends on ε alone. That gives the energy spectrum in the inertial subrange, E(κ) ∝ ε2/3κ−5/3 — the most-tested prediction in the subject, and one that Onsager reached independently.11

Now combine the two ends of the cascade. Writing ReL for the Reynolds number of the energy-containing motion, Kolmogorov's scaling gives

Eq. 1
η / L = ReL−3/4 ,    uη / u′ = ReL−1/4 ,    τη / T = ReL−1/2
η, uη, τη Kolmogorov length, velocity and time scales  ·  L, u′, T integral scale, r.m.s. velocity, large-eddy turnover time

Resolving every scale therefore needs Re3/4 grid points per direction, or Re9/4 in three dimensions. A time step limited by the Courant condition on the smallest cell needs Re3/4 steps per large-eddy turnover. The total space–time work goes as Re3. Doubling the Reynolds number multiplies the cost by eight; adding a decade multiplies it by a thousand.

The concrete case in the lab is a four-metre car at 30 m/s in air: Reynolds number 8.0 × 106, smallest active eddy 26.6 µm, and 3.4 × 1015 grid points for a fully resolved box. One scalar field, stored once, is 27 petabytes. The velocity field has three components, and you need them at every step.

This exponent was recognised early — Corrsin was already computing the required machine in 1961 and finding the answer absurd12 — and it has not softened. The largest well-resolved channel simulations remain in the low thousands of friction Reynolds numbers: the Lee and Moser channel reached Reτ = 5 186 using up to 786 000 cores.1314 That is a spectacular achievement and it is roughly three orders of magnitude in Reynolds number short of a commercial aircraft wing.

Direct simulation is not slow. It is asymptotically unaffordable, and the exponent belongs to the physics rather than to the computer.

Everything that follows is a response to that arithmetic. If you cannot compute the flow, compute its statistics — and the first person to write down what that would mean was, again, Reynolds.

Section IV

Reynolds decomposition

Split every quantity into a mean and a fluctuation. Two rules follow, and the second one is the whole problem.

Twelve years after the dye experiment, Reynolds returned to the subject with a theoretical paper that did something more consequential than the experiment.15 He proposed treating the instantaneous velocity as the sum of a mean and a deviation from it:

Eq. 2
ui(x, t) = Ui(x) + u′i(x, t) ,     p = P + p′
steel mean quantities  ·  carmine fluctuating quantities. This colour convention holds for every equation and every plate in the article.

The decomposition is a definition, so it costs nothing and asserts nothing. Everything depends on what the overbar means. Three operators are used in practice, and they are not interchangeable:

The ensemble average is an average over many independent realisations of the same experiment. It is the only one for which the required algebra is exactly true, and it is the one the theory is written in. The time average replaces the ensemble with a long record at one point; it agrees with the ensemble average only if the flow is statistically stationary and ergodic. The spatial average does the same over a homogeneous direction. Practitioners use whichever is available and rely on ergodicity to connect them, which is almost always sound and occasionally is not — in a flow with slow large-scale unsteadiness, a time average silently blends genuine mean flow with organised motion that a model was never meant to represent. That is the ambiguity URANS and hybrid methods live inside.

What the operator must satisfy

Whatever averaging you choose, the derivation requires it to be linear, to commute with differentiation in space and time, and to be idempotent — averaging an already-averaged quantity changes nothing. From those three properties everything else follows, including the two rules that matter:

Eq. 3
⟨u′i⟩ = 0      but      ⟨u′i u′j⟩ ≠ 0
Rule 1 is an algebraic identity of the operator — exactly zero, not approximately.  ·  Rule 2 is the entire closure problem in one line.

Rule 1 is why the linear terms of the equations of motion survive averaging unchanged. Rule 2 is why the nonlinear term does not. In the lab, a million-sample synthetic record built from a shear-layer eddy mechanism gives ⟨u′⟩ = 3.7 × 10−16 and ⟨v′⟩ = −5.5 × 10−18 — machine epsilon, as an identity should be. The same record gives ⟨u′v′⟩ = −0.3529 m²/s², a correlation coefficient of −0.387, and the reconstruction identity ⟨uv⟩ = UV + ⟨u′v′⟩ closing to 5.6 × 10−17.

The physical mechanism behind that non-zero average is worth stating plainly, because it is the reason the term is not a mathematical artefact. In a shear layer with dU/dy > 0, a fluid parcel swept upward carries a positive v′ and arrives from a region of lower streamwise momentum, so it brings a negative u′. A parcel swept downward does the reverse. Both events contribute a negative product. The average of u′v′ is therefore systematically negative even though the average of each factor is exactly zero — and in the lab record, 94.3 per cent of the total cross-stream momentum flux is carried by that correlation rather than by the mean motion.

Two signals can each average to nothing and still have a product that averages to almost everything.
Section V

The RANS equations

Average the equations of motion term by term. Exactly one term misbehaves.

Start from incompressible continuity and momentum, which we take as exact:

Eq. 4
∂ui/∂xi = 0 ,     ∂ui/∂t + uj ∂ui/∂xj = −(1/ρ) ∂p/∂xi + ν ∂2ui/∂xj∂xj
The instantaneous Navier–Stokes equations. No approximation has been made and none is about to be.

Substitute the decomposition and average. Continuity is linear, so it splits cleanly into two statements: the mean field is divergence-free and so is the fluctuating field. Every term in the momentum equation is linear in the velocity except one, and every linear term averages to the same term written in mean quantities. The unsteady term, the pressure gradient and the viscous term all pass through untouched.

The convective term does not. Writing it in conservative form using continuity, uj∂ui/∂xj = ∂(uiuj)/∂xj, the average of the product is not the product of the averages. Rule 2 leaves a remainder, and after rearranging it onto the right-hand side we get the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes equations:

Eq. 5 — RANS
ρ Uj ∂Ui/∂xj = −∂P/∂xi + ∂/∂xj [ μ ∂Ui/∂xj − ρ ⟨u′iu′j⟩ ]
brass marks a term that is exact but has no equation of its own. From here to the end of the article, brass means: true, and unavailable. The whole of turbulence modelling is a proposal for what to write in its place.

The equations are identical to the laminar ones apart from that single group. It sits inside the divergence alongside the viscous stress, it has the dimensions of a stress, and it appears in the momentum balance exactly where a stress would. Reynolds accordingly treated it as an apparent stress exerted by the turbulence on the mean flow, and the name has stuck.

The name is a little dangerous, and it is worth saying why. A viscous stress is a constitutive object: it is determined pointwise by the local state of the fluid through a relation that follows from the molecular physics. The Reynolds stress is a momentum flux: an average of a product of velocities, determined by the entire history and geometry of the turbulence that produced it. It behaves like a stress in the momentum equation and it does not behave like one anywhere else. Nearly every failure discussed in Section X is a case of that difference asserting itself.

Section VI

The closure problem

Six new unknowns, no new equations, and a hierarchy that never shuts.

Count what we now have. In three dimensions the averaged system offers four equations — three components of mean momentum plus mean continuity — for four mean unknowns: U, V, W and P. That would be a determinate system. But the Reynolds stress tensor has arrived carrying six more unknowns, and the averaging supplied no equations for them. Four equations, ten unknowns.

The obvious move is to go and get equations for them. You can: take the momentum equation for the fluctuating field, multiply by another fluctuating velocity, symmetrise, and average. The result is an exact transport equation for ⟨u′iu′j⟩ with a recognisable structure — production by mean shear, redistribution by pressure, transport, and dissipation:

Eq. 6 — Reynolds-stress transport
Uk ∂⟨u′iu′j⟩/∂xk = Pij + Πij − εij + ∂/∂xk[ ν ∂⟨u′iu′j⟩/∂xk − ⟨u′iu′ju′k⟩ − …]
Pij production — exact, and computable from quantities already being solved for  ·  Πij pressure–strain, εij dissipation tensor, ⟨u′iu′ju′k⟩ triple correlations — all exact, all unknown

Notice what happened. The production term is exact and free, which is genuinely valuable and is the reason Reynolds-stress models capture things eddy-viscosity models cannot. But the new equation has generated triple correlations, a pressure–strain correlation and a dissipation tensor, none of which have equations either. Write equations for the triple correlations and you generate quadruple ones. Keller and Friedmann set out this chain in 1924,16 and it does not terminate.

The counting is exact and unforgiving. A fully symmetric velocity-moment tensor of order n in three dimensions has (n+1)(n+2)/2 independent components, and the transport equation for the moment of order n always generates the moment of order n+1. Truncating at level 1 leaves 6 unknowns; at level 2, 10; at level 3, 15; and the deficit grows as (n+2)(n+3)/2 forever. Level 2 in fact carries 21 unclosed quantities once the pressure–strain and dissipation tensors are counted alongside the ten triple correlations.

This is not a defect of the equations

The same structure appears in kinetic theory, where the BBGKY hierarchy relates the one-particle distribution to the two-particle distribution, and that to the three-particle distribution, without end. Boltzmann's equation is obtained by truncating it with an assumption — molecular chaos — that is not derivable from the mechanics and is, strictly speaking, false.

Turbulence closure has exactly this character. The hierarchy is open because averaging discards information, and the discarded information has to be re-supplied from outside. Where a model puts that information is the model's real content; the equations it prints are largely bookkeeping around it.

So closure is not a theorem waiting to be proved. It is a decision, and the honest way to read any turbulence model is as an answer to a question about what its author was willing to assume. The rest of this article works through the three answers that have mattered most.

Section VII

Boussinesq's analogy

1877: suppose turbulence transports momentum the way molecules do. It does not, and it took a century to establish exactly how it does not.

Six years before Reynolds' dye experiment, Joseph Boussinesq proposed the idea that still carries almost all industrial computational fluid dynamics.17 If the Reynolds stress appears in the momentum equation where a viscous stress would, perhaps it can be modelled the way a viscous stress is modelled — as proportional to the mean rate of strain, with a coefficient that is a property of the turbulence rather than of the fluid:

Eq. 7 — the Boussinesq hypothesis
−ρ⟨u′iu′j⟩ = μt (∂Ui/∂xj + ∂Uj/∂xi) − (2/3) ρ k δij
verdigris marks a modelled quantity — something invented to stand in for a brass term.  ·  μt eddy viscosity  ·  k = ½⟨u′iu′i⟩, turbulent kinetic energy. The trace term is a later correction: without it the trace of the modelled tensor would be zero, and the trace is 2k.

What this buys is enormous and should not be sneered at. Six unknown functions of position collapse into one scalar field. The averaged equations become formally identical to laminar equations with an effective viscosity μ + μt, which means an existing laminar solver becomes a turbulent solver with a change to one coefficient. Essentially every commercial CFD code in the world is built on that fact.

What it assumes

The hypothesis makes three separate assertions, and it is worth separating them because they fail differently.

One. That the Reynolds stress responds to the local, instantaneous mean strain rate. Turbulence has memory: the stress at a point reflects strain the fluid experienced upstream, over roughly one eddy turnover time. A local algebraic relation cannot represent that lag, which is why eddy-viscosity models mispredict flows with rapidly changing strain — through a sudden contraction, past a step, around a corner.

Two. That the deviatoric Reynolds stress is aligned with the mean strain-rate tensor. Two symmetric tensors having the same principal axes is a strong statement, and measurements in all but the simplest shear flows say they do not.18

Three. That a diffusivity is the right kind of object at all — and this is the one that is quantitatively checkable.

A diffusion coefficient is legitimate when the agent doing the transporting travels a distance far shorter than the distance over which the transported quantity's gradient changes. Molecules satisfy that condition spectacularly: in a 50 mm pipe of air the mean free path is 1.36 × 10−6 of the pipe diameter, so a molecule samples a gradient that is exactly linear over its excursion. Eddies do not. A mixing length at y = 0.2δ is 8.2 × 10−2 of the boundary-layer thickness — a factor of 6 × 104 worse. The eddy transports momentum across a distance comparable to the one over which the mean gradient changes.

Boussinesq's hypothesis is not a weak approximation. It is an approximation whose premise is false — which is why its failures are structural rather than quantitative, and cannot be tuned away.

None of which stopped it working. It works because in a thin shear layer near a wall, which is the geometry the entire calibration set was drawn from, the mean strain has essentially one component, the turbulence is close to local equilibrium, and the alignment assumption is nearly satisfied by symmetry alone. The hypothesis is an excellent interpolation device inside that regime, and its reputation was made there. What remained was to say what μt actually is — because Boussinesq did not, and a coefficient with no prescription is not yet a model.

Section VIII

Prandtl's mixing length

A length scale guessed from the only thing a wall provides, and the thirty years of engineering it bought.

In 1925 Ludwig Prandtl supplied the missing prescription by borrowing the rest of the molecular analogy.19 Kinetic theory gives the molecular viscosity as roughly the product of a characteristic speed and a mean free path. So define an eddy analogue — a Mischungsweg, a mixing length ℓm, over which a lump of fluid retains its momentum before merging with its surroundings — and take the characteristic velocity to be the velocity difference across that length, ℓm|dU/dy|. Then:

Eq. 8 — mixing length
νt = ℓm2 |dU/dy| ,     ℓm = κ y   near a wall
The second statement is the real content. In a wall layer with no imposed length scale, the only length available at distance y from the wall is y itself. κ is a dimensionless constant of proportionality.

The second equation is the piece of physical reasoning worth admiring. It is not fitted; it is a dimensional argument about what lengths exist. Far from the wall relative to the viscous scale, and close to it relative to the boundary-layer thickness, the wall distance is the only length in the problem, so the mixing length must be proportional to it. Von Kármán reached the same conclusion by a similarity argument from the mean velocity profile itself, which is why κ is named after him rather than Prandtl.20

Combine that with a constant total shear stress and you get the logarithmic law of the wall, which Nikuradse's pipe measurements had already established empirically.21 Prandtl's model does not merely fit the log law — it predicts its functional form from an argument about available length scales, and that is a categorically better thing for a model to do.

Van Driest's damping, and a genuine prediction

The mixing-length model as stated fails in the viscous sublayer, where ℓm = κy over-predicts turbulent transport that viscosity has in fact suppressed. Van Driest fixed it with an exponential damping factor borrowed from the Stokes oscillating-plate solution, introducing exactly one new constant A+ = 26:22

Eq. 9 — van Driest damping
ℓm+ = κ y+ [ 1 − exp(−y+/A+) ] ,     A+ = 26
y+ = y uτ/ν, the wall distance in viscous units. One empirical constant.

The lab integrates the resulting constant-stress balance from the wall outward, supplying only κ = 0.41 and A+ = 26. Fitting the computed profile over 50 ≤ y+ ≤ 500 recovers κ = 0.4094, which proves only that the integration works — κ was put in. The interesting output is the additive constant: the fit gives B = 5.2654, and B was never supplied. It falls straight inside the measured band of 5.0 to 5.5.

That is a real prediction, and it is a fair statement of what a good turbulence model can be expected to do: one empirical constant buys the viscous sublayer, the buffer layer, the blend between them and the intercept of the log law. For thirty years this was enough to design aircraft with.

Where it stops

The model has one defect so clean that it defines the next generation of models. Because νt is proportional to |dU/dy|, the eddy viscosity is exactly zero wherever the mean shear vanishes. At a channel centreline, at a pipe axis, at the centreline of a jet, at any symmetry plane, the model reports no turbulent transport at all — and the measured turbulent kinetic energy at those locations is emphatically not zero.

The reason is that an algebraic model has no memory. It computes the turbulence at a point from the mean flow at that point, so it cannot represent turbulence that was produced somewhere else and convected or diffused to where it now is. Everything that comes afterwards — one-equation models, two-equation models, Reynolds-stress models — is a response to that single sentence.

κ is not a constant either

The von Kármán constant has been quoted as 0.41 for decades, but the value is flow-dependent and the modern measurements are lower. The Lee and Moser channel simulation at Reτ = 5 186 gives κ = 0.384 ± 0.004,13 and systematic comparisons across canonical flows find it varying by several per cent between pipes, channels and boundary layers.2324 Whether a true logarithmic region even exists below Reτ ≈ 3 × 103 is still argued over.

Keep that in view for Section IX, where the standard two-equation constants turn out to contain their own value of κ — and it is not 0.384.

Section IX

Two equations

k–ε: where the five constants came from, what they are worth, and why only four of them are free.

If the problem with an algebraic length scale is that it has no memory, the remedy is to solve transport equations for the turbulence quantities themselves. Kolmogorov proposed the first two-equation scheme in 1942,25 Prandtl independently proposed an equation for the turbulent kinetic energy, and Rotta developed the framework that made Reynolds-stress modelling possible.26 The version that took over industry came from Imperial College: Jones and Launder's formulation,27 presented in the form the world adopted by Launder and Spalding two years later.28

The scheme needs two quantities, because an eddy viscosity needs a velocity scale and a length scale. Take k for the velocity scale — √k has the dimensions of velocity and k has an exact transport equation. For the second, take ε, the dissipation rate. Then dimensional analysis fixes the eddy viscosity up to a constant:

Eq. 10
νt = Cμ k2 / ε ,     Cμ = 0.09
velocity scale √k, length scale k3/2/ε, time scale k/ε. The product of the first two, times a constant.

The k equation is derived by contracting the exact Reynolds-stress transport equation, so its production term is exact. Its turbulent transport term is modelled by — inevitably — a gradient-diffusion assumption. The ε equation is the weak point and deserves an honest description: it is not the exact transport equation for dissipation. The exact equation exists, it is derivable, and its terms are dominated by processes at the smallest scales that the model does not represent. The modelled ε equation is instead written by analogy with the k equation, with production and destruction terms whose form is postulated and whose coefficients are fitted:

Eq. 11 — the standard model
Uj∂k/∂xj = P − ε + ∂/∂xj[(ν + νt/σk)∂k/∂xj]
Uj∂ε/∂xj = Cε1 (ε/k)P − Cε2 ε2/k + ∂/∂xj[(ν + νt/σε)∂ε/∂xj]
P = −⟨u′iu′j⟩ ∂Ui/∂xj, production — exact in the k equation  ·  standard constants: Cμ = 0.09, Cε1 = 1.44, Cε2 = 1.92, σk = 1.0, σε = 1.3

Where the constants came from

This is the part usually skipped, and it is the part that tells you what the model is.

Cμ = 0.09 is the square of a measurement. In a shear layer in local equilibrium, where production balances dissipation, the model forces two exact consequences: the non-dimensional shear rate Sk/ε must equal 1/√Cμ = 3.3333, and the stress ratio |⟨u′v′⟩|/k must equal √Cμ = 0.3000. That second number is Bradshaw's structure parameter, measured at a1 = |⟨u′v′⟩|/2k ≈ 0.15 in equilibrium boundary layers.29 The model reproduces it exactly, and it does so because Cμ was set to 0.3² for that purpose. The agreement is real and it is circular.

Cε2 = 1.92 comes from decaying grid turbulence — and does not fit it. Strip the model of production and transport and it reduces to a pair of ordinary differential equations with an exact power-law solution, k ∝ t−n with n = 1/(Cε2 − 1). This is the one flow where the model can be checked in closed form.

The standard constant gives n = 1.0870. Measured decay exponents behind grids lie between roughly 1.15 and 1.45,3031 so the model undershoots even the bottom of the band by 5.5 per cent. Reproducing n = 1.25 would need Cε2 = 1.80, and n = 1.45 would need 1.6897. The value in use is 1.92 because that is what keeps the shear-flow predictions acceptable. Every constant in the model is a negotiated position across a calibration set, and no single flow in the set is reproduced exactly.

And the fifth constant is not free at all. Requiring the model to reproduce a logarithmic layer imposes an algebraic constraint among the others, κ² = σε(Cε2 − Cε1)√Cμ. Substituting the standard set gives a von Kármán constant of 0.4327 — implied, not chosen. Set against the best direct measurement of 0.384 ± 0.004,13 that is a discrepancy of 12.7 per cent, buried inside a model that is used to certify heat exchangers.

Only four of the five constants are free. The fifth is whatever the other four leave over, and what they leave over disagrees with the best measurement of κ by more than a tenth.

The descendants

The standard model's worst behaviour is at walls, where the high-Reynolds-number form is invalid and the ε equation is badly behaved in the viscous sublayer. Two families of repair followed: wall functions, which bridge the near-wall region with an assumed log-law profile and impose a hard constraint on where the first grid point may sit; and low-Reynolds-number variants, which add damping functions and integrate to the wall at the cost of much finer meshes and more constants.

Wilcox's k–ω model replaced ε with a specific dissipation rate ω = ε/k, which behaves far better at the wall but is notoriously sensitive to the free-stream value of ω.32 Menter's shear-stress transport model resolved that by blending: k–ω near the wall, k–ε away from it, with a limiter on the eddy viscosity in adverse pressure gradients.33 SST is the closest thing the field has to a default, and its construction is instructive — it is not a better theory of turbulence, it is a well-engineered switch between two calibrations, each used where it was calibrated.

Section X

Where the models break

Not randomly, and not by a little. The failures are predictable from the assumptions, which is the one consolation on offer.

The anisotropy that cannot be seen

In simple shear the mean strain-rate tensor has S11 = S22 = S33 = 0. A linear eddy-viscosity model therefore predicts that the three normal stresses are exactly equal, each 2k/3, in every shear flow that has ever existed. Real shear flows are strongly anisotropic: streamwise fluctuations are typically twice the wall-normal ones.

The lab quantifies it using the anisotropy tensor bij = ⟨u′iu′j⟩/2k − δij/3, plotted on the Lumley invariant map inside which every physically possible turbulence must lie.34 For log-layer ratios of σu : σv : σw = 1 : 0.5 : 0.7, the measured anisotropy is b11 = +0.2414, b22 = −0.1897, b33 = −0.0517, with second invariant 0.0969. Near the wall it is worse: b11 = +0.3684 and an invariant of 0.2116. The model's prediction in every case is the origin.

The consequence is not a percentage error. Any phenomenon driven by normal-stress differences is structurally invisible. Secondary flow of the second kind in a straight square duct — a genuine, measurable, cross-sectional circulation driven entirely by the imbalance between the two normal stresses in the cross-plane — is predicted by a linear eddy-viscosity model to be exactly zero. So is much of the response to streamline curvature and system rotation. Nonlinear and explicit algebraic stress models exist precisely to restore these terms,35 and full Reynolds-stress closures solve for the anisotropy rather than assuming it away.

Negative variances

A more embarrassing failure follows directly from the linear relation. Along the most extensional principal direction of the strain-rate tensor, with principal strain λ, the model predicts ⟨u′²⟩ = k[2/3 − 2Cμ(k/ε)λ]. A variance cannot be negative, so the model is physically admissible only while (k/ε)λ ≤ 1/(3Cμ) = 3.7037.

Equilibrium shear sits at 1.667, comfortably inside. Stagnation regions, impingement points and strongly accelerated flow routinely go past 3.70, and the model then reports a negative variance alongside a production-to-dissipation ratio that grows without bound — 36 at λk/ε = 10. In practice this manifests as the notorious stagnation-point anomaly: standard k–ε computes an enormous spurious peak of turbulent kinetic energy at the leading edge of a bluff body, and that error convects downstream through the entire solution.36

Realizability constraints — that normal stresses be non-negative and that the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality hold between components — were set out by Schumann and by Lumley,3738 and the practical remedies followed: Kato and Launder's production modification,39 Shih and colleagues' realizable variant with a variable Cμ,40 and Durbin's time-scale bound, T = min(k/ε, α/(√3 CμS)), which enforces exactly the inequality above without touching the calibration.36 Note what that fix implies. The constraint has to be imported from outside the model, because the model has no way of knowing that a variance is a variance.

The rest of the standard list

The round-jet/plane-jet anomaly: the same constants that give the correct spreading rate for a plane jet over-predict the round jet's by about 15 per cent, and no adjustment fixes both.41 Adverse pressure gradients and separation: eddy-viscosity models systematically delay separation and under-predict the recovery, which is why external aerodynamics is the hardest RANS problem in industry. Laminar–turbulent transition: RANS models assume the flow is turbulent, so transition is bolted on through separate correlation-based models. Buoyancy, compressibility, combustion: each requires its own extension with its own constants.

How to read this list

Not as an indictment. A standard k–ε or k–ω SST computation of an attached, thin, wall-bounded shear flow is accurate to a few per cent in quantities engineers care about, runs in minutes, and has been validated against decades of experiment. That is a genuine technical achievement.

The point is that the accuracy is local to the calibration set, and the calibration set is knowable. Every failure above is predictable from the assumptions listed in Section VII. Somebody who knows which assumption is violated by the flow in front of them can predict which way the answer will be wrong, and by roughly how much. That is the real professional skill, and no model replaces it.

Section XI

The ladder of closures

Fifty years of modelling settled one thing conclusively: what generality costs.

The diagonal on that map is the whole subject. Algebraic models are nearly free and work in one geometry. One- and two-equation models add memory and cover a wider range. Reynolds-stress models solve seven transport equations and get anisotropy, curvature and rotation right, at the cost of stiffness, expense and a pressure–strain model that carries its own contested constants.42 Large-eddy simulation resolves the energy-containing motions and models only the small scales, which are closer to universal and therefore easier to model.4344 Direct simulation models nothing and costs Re3.

Nobody has found a rung that is cheap, general and faithful, and the moment-hierarchy count in Plate 06 explains why. Information destroyed by averaging must be re-supplied from outside, and every source of it costs either computation or generality. That is not a temporary state of the art; it is a structural feature of the problem.

What is actually moving

Three things are worth watching.

Hybrid methods. Detached-eddy simulation and its successors run RANS in attached boundary layers, where RANS is good and LES would be ruinous, and switch to LES in separated regions, where the reverse holds.45 They are now standard for bluff-body and separated flows, and their difficulties — grey-area transitions, grid-induced separation — are exactly the difficulties of any switch between two calibrations.

Data-driven closure. Machine learning has been applied to the closure problem for a decade. Ling and colleagues' tensor-basis neural network is the landmark, because it embeds Galilean and rotational invariance in the architecture rather than hoping the network learns it, and predicts the anisotropy tensor from invariants of the strain and rotation rates.46 The field has been surveyed thoroughly.4748 The honest status as of the middle of this decade is that progress has been slower than initially projected: models trained to high accuracy on held-out anisotropy fields frequently fail when coupled back into a RANS solver, because small errors in the anisotropy tensor are amplified by the mean-momentum equation. The constraint is not regression accuracy; it is the conditioning of the coupled problem.

Requirements, not methods. The NASA CFD Vision 2030 study named the prediction of turbulent flows with significant separation as the single greatest impediment to computational aerodynamics, and it named RANS turbulence modelling as the pacing item rather than hardware.49 More than a decade on, that assessment has not needed revising.

A note on the mathematics

The existence and smoothness of solutions to the three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations remains one of the Clay Millennium Prize problems, unresolved: no proof of global regularity, no accepted finite-time blowup.50 The nearest recent landmark is a proof of singularity formation for the three-dimensional Euler equations with smooth initial data and a boundary, by Chen and Hou, using computer-assisted analysis.51

It is worth being clear that this is not what stops engineers. Even a complete regularity theorem would leave the closure problem exactly where it is, because closure is not about whether solutions exist — it is about what happens to information when you average.

The thesis, answered

Averaging is a linear operator; the equations of motion are nonlinear; the two do not commute, and the remainder is the Reynolds stress. That remainder is exact and has no equation of its own, and the hierarchy you would build to get one is open at every level. Closure is therefore not a mathematical difficulty inside the theory. It is the point at which information deliberately discarded has to be put back by hand, and every turbulence model is a proposal about where to get it.

What half a century established is that the proposals are rankable, that the ranking is a trade of computation against generality, and that no proposal is free. The plates below are ordered by exactly that criterion.

EXP 2  THE MOMENT HIERARCHY: COUNTING THE CLOSURE DEFICIT
==============================================================================
  A fully symmetric velocity-moment tensor of order n in three
  dimensions has C(n+2, 2) = (n+1)(n+2)/2 independent components.

  The transport equation for the moment of order n is exact, and it
  always generates the moment of order n+1 through the convective
  nonlinearity. Truncating the chain at level n therefore leaves the
  order-(n+1) moment with no equation of its own.

  level n     equations    unknowns   deficit  the object with no equation
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  1                   4          10         6  Reynolds stresses <u'i u'j>
  2                  10          20        10  triple correlations <u'i u'j u'k>
  3                  20          35        15  quadruple correlations
  4                  35          56        21  5th-order moments
  5                  56          84        28  6th-order moments
  6                  84         120        36  7th-order moments
  7                 120         165        45  8th-order moments
  8                 165         220        55  9th-order moments

  The deficit is never zero and grows as (n+2)(n+3)/2. Every equation
  you add brings more unknowns than constraints. The hierarchy is open
  at every level, so closure is not a hard problem to be solved inside
  it -- it is a decision to step outside it.

  Level 2 (Reynolds-stress transport) carries further unclosed objects
  beyond the 10 triple correlations:
    pressure-strain correlation Pi_ij (symmetric, traceless) = 5
    dissipation-rate tensor     eps_ij (symmetric)           = 6
    total unclosed quantities at level 2                     = 21

  Historical note: this is the Keller-Friedmann chain of 1924, and it is
  the reason turbulence modelling is an engineering discipline rather
  than a branch of applied mathematics.
