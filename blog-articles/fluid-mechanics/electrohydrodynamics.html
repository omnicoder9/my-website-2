1. What electrohydrodynamics is

Electrohydrodynamics (EHD) is the study of fluid motion driven or modified by electric fields. It sits beside magnetohydrodynamics, but the coupling is different: in EHD the magnetic field is negligible and the fluid responds to electric stresses that act on free charge and on gradients of permittivity [1,2]. The subject spans liquids that are near-perfect insulators (transformer oils, hydrocarbons), poorly conducting “leaky dielectrics” (castor and silicone oils), and aqueous electrolytes in which charge lives in thin electrical double layers next to walls [3,4].

Three ingredients recur throughout the field:

  • Electric-field-driven flow: free charge in a field feels a Coulomb force that it hands to the fluid by collisions; polarization gradients add further body and interfacial forces [1].
  • Charge transport: charge is carried by conduction (ion migration), by diffusion and by convection with the fluid, so the electric problem and the flow problem are two-way coupled [3,5].
  • Interfaces and instabilities: because stresses concentrate at interfaces where permittivity and conductivity jump, electric fields can deform drops, sharpen menisci into cones, and destabilize flat surfaces and bulk liquid layers [6,7].

2. Governing equations and electric stresses

2.1 The electroquasistatic approximation

In EHD the time scales of interest (milliseconds to seconds, or nanoseconds for charge relaxation in electrolytes) are far longer than the time for light to cross the system, and currents are small, so magnetic induction can be neglected. The electric field is then irrotational, E = −∇φ, and obeys Gauss’s law [1,8]:

∇·(εE) = ρf,    ∇×E = 0 (1)

with ε the permittivity and ρf the free charge density. Free charge is conserved:

∂ρf/∂t + ∇·J = 0 (2)

2.2 Force density and Maxwell stress

For a linear, isotropic fluid whose permittivity depends on mass density ρ and temperature, the Korteweg–Helmholtz force density is [1,8]

fe = ρfE − ½ E² ∇ε + ∇[ ½ ρ E² (∂ε/∂ρ)T ] (3)

Equivalently, the force can be written as the divergence of the Maxwell stress tensor,

Tij = ε ( EiEj − ½ δij E² ),    fe,i = ∂Tij/∂xj (4)

(the electrostriction term being lumped into a modified pressure). The stress form is the practical one at interfaces: the electric traction on a surface with unit normal n is the jump ⟦T·n⟧ across it. For an interface carrying surface charge qs = ⟦εEn⟧, the tangential electric traction is qsEt, and it is this tangential stress, which only viscous stress can balance, that drives interfacial flows in leaky dielectrics [3,7].

2.3 Coupling to the flow

The fluid obeys the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations with the electric force added,

ρ (∂u/∂t + u·∇u) = −∇p + μ∇²u + fe,   ∇·u = 0 (5)

The flow feeds back on the electrical problem because the current includes a convective part, ρfu. Whether that feedback matters is decided by the dimensionless groups in Table 1.

3. Charge transport and the charge relaxation time

3.1 The Nernst–Planck description

In a liquid, current is carried by ions. Each species i, with number density ni, valence zi, diffusivity Di and mobility Ki, moves by diffusion, migration and convection [5,10]:

ji = −Di∇ni ± Ki ni E + niu,    J = Σ zi e ji (6)

Mobility and diffusivity are linked by the Einstein relation Ki = |zi|eDi/(kBT). When the ion densities are nearly uniform, diffusion is negligible and the current reduces to Ohm’s law plus convection, J = σE + ρfu [3].

3.2 Charge relaxation

Combining Ohm’s law with Gauss’s law and charge conservation in a uniform fluid gives ∂ρf/∂t = −(σ/ε)ρf: any free charge in the bulk decays exponentially with the charge relaxation time [1,8]

τe = ε / σ (7)

Bulk charge therefore survives only near interfaces, where ε and σ jump, or where charge is continuously injected. Comparing τe with the time scale of the flow or of an applied AC field tells you which picture applies: a liquid behaves as a conductor when τe is short and as an insulator when it is long [1,11].

Worked example 1: relaxation times of common liquids

Using τe = εrε0/σ with ε0 = 8.854 × 10−12 F/m and order-of-magnitude property values (conductivities of oils vary by decades with purity, so treat these as illustrative):

3.3 Where the charge comes from in insulating liquids

In a liquid with very low conductivity, charge carriers have three main sources [2,9]:

  • Dissociation–recombination of trace ionizable impurities. A strong field enhances the dissociation rate, which is the Onsager (second Wien) effect [12], so conductivity grows with field.
  • Injection of ions at an electrode by electrochemical reactions, which creates a unipolar space charge that is not neutralized in the bulk. This is the main driver of electroconvection in insulating liquids.
  • Induction at interfaces between liquids with different ε/σ ratios, the mechanism behind the leaky dielectric model.

3.4 The electrical double layer

In electrolytes, a charged wall attracts a cloud of counter-ions. Its thickness is the Debye length [10,13]

λD = [ ε kBT / (2 n∞ z² e²) ]1/2 (8)

which is about 9.6 nm for a 1 mM monovalent salt in water at 25 °C and 0.96 nm for 0.1 M (see the code output). Outside this thin layer the liquid is electroneutral, so the electric force acts only inside it. That is the origin of electrokinetic slip [4,14].

4. Electric-field-driven flow

4.1 Electro-osmosis

Electro-osmosis was first reported by Reuss in 1809, who saw water move through wet clay toward the cathode when a voltage was applied [15]. Helmholtz and later Smoluchowski explained it: the tangential field pulls on the counter-ion charge in the double layer, which drags the liquid with it [16,17]. For λD much smaller than the channel, the result is an effective slip velocity at the wall, the Helmholtz–Smoluchowski velocity [5,14]:

ueo = − ε ζ Et / μ (9)

where ζ is the zeta potential, the potential drop across the diffuse layer. Because the slip is applied at every wall and the bulk feels no force, the velocity profile is flat (plug-like) except within a few Debye lengths of the wall (Figure 2). This low-dispersion profile is why electro-osmosis is valuable in capillary electrophoresis and microfluidics [4].

Worked example 2: electro-osmotic speed in a glass microchannel

Problem. A glass channel filled with 1 mM KCl (εr = 78.5, μ = 1.0 mPa·s) has ζ = −50 mV. A field of 100 V/cm (104 V/m) is applied. Find λD and ueo.

Solution. From Eq. (8), λD ≈ 9.6 nm, far thinner than any practical channel, so the thin-layer limit holds. From Eq. (9):

ueo = (78.5 × 8.854×10−12)(0.050)(104) / 10−3 ≈ 3.5 × 10−4 m/s = 0.35 mm/s

The flow is toward the cathode, because the counter-ions are cations. A 100 µm × 20 µm channel then carries 0.35 mm/s × 2 × 10−9 m² ≈ 0.7 nL/s, independent of channel length at fixed field. Note that ueo does not depend on channel size, which is why electro-osmosis wins over pressure driving as channels shrink [4].

4.2 Ion-drag (injection) pumping

In insulating liquids, an electrode that injects ions creates a unipolar space charge that drifts to the counter-electrode and drags fluid with it. The pressure such an ion-drag pump can develop follows from integrating the Coulomb force across the gap [1,18]. Since ρf = ε dE/dx in one dimension,

Δp = ∫ ρf E dx = ½ ε ( Ed² − E0² ) (10)

For space-charge-limited injection the field vanishes at the injector (E0 = 0) and the classical solution E(x) = (3V/2d)(x/d)1/2 gives Ed = 3V/2d, hence the upper bound

Δpmax = (9/8) ε V² / d² (11)

Worked example 3: pressure limit of an ion-drag pump

For a hydrocarbon (εr = 2.2) with 20 kV across a 5 mm gap, Eq. (11) gives Δpmax = 9 × 2.2 × 8.854×10−12 × (2×104)² / (8 × (5×10−3)²) ≈ 350 Pa. That is a few centimetres of liquid head per stage, which is why practical EHD pumps stack many electrode pairs. Real pumps reach only part of this bound because injection is weaker than space-charge-limited and back-flow occurs [2].

4.3 Interfacial flows in leaky dielectrics: Taylor’s drop

Before 1966 it was puzzling that drops in a steady field sometimes flattened into oblate shapes, which no purely electrostatic theory allowed. G. I. Taylor resolved this with the leaky dielectric model: in a poorly conducting liquid, conduction delivers charge to the interface but bulk charge has relaxed away, so all the free charge sits on the surface. The tangential field pulls on that charge and drives a circulation inside and outside the drop [7,19]. Saville’s review established the model’s formal basis as the limit of a thin diffuse charge layer and weak convection [3].

The interfacial conditions are continuity of normal current (steady state), Gauss’s law for the surface charge, and a tangential stress balance:

⟦σEn⟧ = 0,   qs = ⟦εEn⟧,   ⟦τnt⟧ + qsEt = 0 (12)

Here ⟦·⟧ denotes the jump of a quantity across the interface (outside minus inside), and Et is the tangential field, which is continuous.

For a drop of radius a in a uniform field E, write R = σin/σout, Q = εin/εout, λ = μin/μout and CaE = εoutE²a/γ. The small-deformation result is [19–21]

D = (L−B)/(L+B) = 9 CaE Φ / [16 (2+R)²],   Φ = 1 + R² − 2Q + 3(R−Q)(2+3λ)/[5(1+λ)] (13)

where L and B are the drop’s lengths parallel and perpendicular to the field. Φ is Taylor’s discriminating function: Φ > 0 gives a prolate drop and Φ < 0 an oblate one. The last term is the contribution of the EHD flow. When R < Q (charge relaxes more slowly inside than outside) the surface flow runs from the poles toward the equator; when R > Q it runs from the equator toward the poles [19,21]. Two limits check the formula: a perfectly conducting drop (R → ∞) gives D = 9CaE/16 [6], and a perfect dielectric (R = Q) gives D = 9CaE(Q−1)²/[16(2+Q)²] ≥ 0, so a purely dielectric drop can never be oblate.

Worked example 4: will the drop flatten?

Problem. A drop with R = 0.1, Q = 1.37 and λ = 1 sits in an ambient oil with εr,out = 2.8 and interfacial tension γ = 4.5 mN/m. The drop radius is 1 mm and the field is 100 kV/m. Find D.

Solution. Φ = 1 + 0.01 − 2.74 + 3(0.1 − 1.37)(5)/(10) = −1.73 − 1.905 = −3.635. Then D/CaE = 9(−3.635)/(16 × 2.1²) = −0.464. CaE = (2.8 × 8.854×10−12)(105)²(10−3)/(4.5×10−3) = 0.055, so D ≈ −0.026. The drop is slightly oblate, and the circulation term (−1.905) supplies more than half of the effect. Small-deformation theory is reliable here because |D| ≪ 1; for larger CaE, numerical solutions such as Feng and Scott’s are needed [20].

4.4 Other field-driven flows

  • Electrothermal flow. Joule heating creates gradients of σ and ε, and an AC field acting on those gradients produces a body force. This often dominates in high-conductivity buffers in microdevices [11,22].
  • AC electro-osmosis and induced-charge electro-osmosis. A field induces a double layer on a polarizable surface and then drives it, giving slip that scales with E² and works with AC fields [23].
  • Dielectrophoresis (DEP). A polarizable particle in a non-uniform field feels a net force, attractive or repulsive depending on frequency (see Section 5.3) [24,25].
  • Corona (ionic) wind. In gases, ions from a corona discharge drag neutral air, producing velocities of metres per second [26,27].

5. Dielectric fluids

5.1 Perfect dielectrics, leaky dielectrics and conductors

The useful classification compares τe with the process time tp (inverse frequency or flow time) [1,3]:

  • Perfect dielectric (τe ≫ tp): no free charge reaches interfaces. Only polarization forces act, and those forces are normal to a clean interface, so they deform it but drive no steady circulation.
  • Leaky dielectric (τe comparable to tp on at least one side): conduction brings charge to interfaces, and tangential stresses drive flow (Section 4.3).
  • Conductor/electrolyte (τe ≪ tp, thin double layers): the field is excluded from the liquid bulk and forces act in double layers (electrokinetics) or on the surface as a pure normal pressure ½εE².

5.2 Polarization force on a dielectric liquid

The term −½E²∇ε in Eq. (3) pulls the higher-permittivity liquid into the region of stronger field. A classic demonstration is a dielectric liquid rising between the vertical plates of a capacitor. A force balance on the meniscus gives the rise height h = (ε − ε0)E²/(2ρg) [1,8]. For a liquid with εr = 2.2 at E = 5 MV/m and ρ = 880 kg/m³, h ≈ 1.2 × 8.854×10−12 × 2.5×1013/(2 × 880 × 9.81) ≈ 15 mm.

5.3 Dielectrophoresis of suspended particles

A sphere of radius a in a medium, both with complex permittivities ε* = ε − iσ/ω, feels the time-averaged DEP force [22,24,25]

⟨FDEP⟩ = 2π εm a³ Re[K(ω)] ∇|Erms|²,   K = (εp* − εm*)/(εp* + 2εm*) (14)

K is the Clausius–Mossotti factor. At low frequency it is set by conductivities, at high frequency by permittivities. Where Re[K] changes sign, at the crossover frequency, particles switch from being pulled toward field maxima (positive DEP) to being pushed away (negative DEP). Figure 4 shows this for a latex bead whose effective conductivity comes from its surface conduction.

5.4 Electrorotation and Quincke rotation

A dielectric sphere in a weakly conducting liquid in a steady field can begin to spin spontaneously when the field exceeds a threshold. Quincke reported this in 1896 [28]. The mechanism is again charge relaxation: if the particle’s τe is longer than the liquid’s, its induced dipole points against the field, and that orientation is unstable to rotation [25]. Drops show an analogous electrorotation instability [21].

6. Electrohydrodynamic instabilities

Electric stresses usually act to amplify perturbations: a bump on a charged surface concentrates the field and is pulled further outward. Instability occurs when this destabilizing stress overcomes the restoring effects of gravity, surface tension, viscosity and charge diffusion [1,7].

6.1 Rayleigh’s limit for a charged drop

Rayleigh showed in 1882 that an isolated conducting drop of radius a and surface tension γ becomes unstable when its charge exceeds [29]

QR = 8π ( ε0 γ a³ )1/2 (15)

For a 1 mm water drop this is about 6.4 × 10−10 C. Evaporating electrospray droplets reach this limit repeatedly and shed fine charged progeny, which is part of how electrospray ionization produces gas-phase ions [30,31].

6.2 The Tonks–Frenkel instability of a flat surface

Consider a deep, perfectly conducting liquid (density ρ, surface tension γ) under a vacuum or gas, with a normal field E above it. Perturbing the surface as exp(ikx − iωt) gives the dispersion relation [1,32]

ω² = g k + (γ/ρ) k³ − (ε0E²/ρ) k² (16)

Gravity and capillarity stabilize, and the electric pressure term destabilizes. The surface first becomes unstable (ω² < 0) at kc = (ρg/γ)1/2, the inverse capillary length, when

ε0 Ec² = 2 ( ρ g γ )1/2 (17)

Taylor and McEwan confirmed the corresponding criterion for an interface between two liquids experimentally [33]. Above the threshold, the surface erupts into an array of cones whose spacing is set by the fastest-growing wavelength (Figure 5).

Worked example 5: can you lift a water surface into cones?

From Eq. (17), Ec = [2(1000 × 9.81 × 0.072)1/2/8.854×10−12]1/2 ≈ 2.45 × 106 V/m. Dry air at atmospheric pressure breaks down at roughly 3 MV/m in uniform fields, so the margin is thin. In practice the instability is usually studied with a dense dielectric gas or a liquid–liquid interface, where a second liquid replaces the air [1,33].

6.3 Charge relaxation at interfaces

Between two leaky liquids, a perpendicular field produces interfacial charge whose dynamics depend on both relaxation times. Melcher and Smith showed that the critical field and the growth rate move smoothly between the perfect-conductor and perfect-dielectric limits as the ratio of τe to the hydrodynamic time changes, and that tangential stresses from the relaxing charge can stabilize or destabilize the interface [34].

6.4 Taylor cones, cone-jets and electrospray

When a conducting liquid fed through a capillary is raised to a high voltage, its meniscus sharpens into a cone. Zeleny photographed such menisci in 1917 [35], and Taylor showed in 1964 that a conical surface can be in equilibrium only if the electric pressure ½ε0E² balances the capillary pressure γ/r at every radius. This requires a potential that varies as r1/2P1/2(cos θ), whose zero sets the cone angle [6]:

P1/2(cos θ0) = 0  ⟹  liquid half-angle αT = 180° − θ0 ≈ 49.3° (18)

The taylor_cone_half_angle function in Section 8 finds the root numerically and returns 49.29°. Real cones emit a thin jet from the tip that breaks into charged droplets (the cone-jet mode). The jet diameter and emitted current follow scaling laws in the flow rate and conductivity, for example current ∝ (γσQ/ε)1/2 for highly conducting liquids [31,36]. Similar tip streaming occurs from free drops in strong fields [37].

Why exactly 49.3°?

For the electric pressure to cancel γ/r on a cone, E must scale as r−1/2, so the potential must scale as r1/2. Laplace’s equation in spherical coordinates then makes the angular part a Legendre function of degree ½. The cone surface must be an equipotential, so it lies at a zero of P1/2(cos θ), and there is only one such zero, at θ0 ≈ 130.7°. The angle is universal and independent of the liquid [6,31].

6.5 Electroconvection in insulating liquids under unipolar injection

A layer of insulating liquid between plane electrodes, with ions injected at one of them, is the electrical analogue of Rayleigh–Bénard convection. The injected space charge drifts across at speed KE, and any fluid motion that carries charge sideways creates a net Coulomb torque. Linear stability theory for strong injection (C → ∞) gives a critical value Tc ≈ 161 for the onset of hexagonal or roll cells [38,39]. Experiments show that convection, once present, persists down to a lower value Tf ≈ 100 (a subcritical, hysteretic bifurcation), and that the measured onset is often well below the linear value [9,40].

Worked example 6: onset voltage for electroconvection

Take a hydrocarbon with εr = 2.2, μ = 1 mPa·s and ion mobility K = 10−8 m²/(V·s). Then T/V = ε/(Kμ) = 2.2 × 8.854×10−12/(10−8 × 10−3) ≈ 1.95 V−1. For strong injection, Tc ≈ 161 gives Vc ≈ 83 V. Remarkably, the onset depends on voltage, not on gap width, because both the driving charge and the drift velocity scale with V/d [9]. Weaker injection (finite C) raises the threshold.

6.6 Instabilities in electrolytes

Electrolytes have their own EHD instabilities. In microchannels with conductivity gradients, a field creates free charge where σ varies, and above a critical field the interface between the two streams breaks into waves and rapid mixing (electrokinetic instability) [41]. Near ion-selective membranes, concentration polarization at high current produces vortices (electroconvection) that carry “overlimiting” current [42].

7. Applications

  • Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry, which made it possible to weigh large biomolecules intact. John Fenn shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it [30,43].
  • Electrospinning of polymer nanofibres from an electrified jet that whips and stretches [44].
  • Microfluidic pumping and separation by electro-osmosis, DEP and AC electrokinetics [4,22].
  • EHD pumps and heat-transfer enhancement in dielectric coolants, with no moving parts [2,18].
  • Plasma actuators for aerodynamic flow control and ionic-wind propulsion. In 2018 an MIT team flew a 5 m-span aeroplane powered only by ionic wind [27,45].
  • Emulsion breaking and drop coalescence in crude oil processing, which relies on field-induced drop deformation and attraction [21].

8. Source code

The module below collects every formula used in the worked examples and figures. It needs only NumPy. Run python ehd.py to reproduce the numbers quoted in this article.

"""ehd.py - small calculators for the electrohydrodynamics article.
Requires: numpy only."""
import numpy as np

EPS0 = 8.8541878128e-12   # F/m, vacuum permittivity
KB, E_CH, NA = 1.380649e-23, 1.602176634e-19, 6.02214076e23

# 1. Charge relaxation time  tau_e = eps/sigma
def relaxation_time(eps_r, sigma):
    return eps_r * EPS0 / sigma

# 2. Debye length of a symmetric z:z electrolyte, concentration c in mol/L
def debye_length(c_molar, eps_r=78.5, T=298.15, z=1):
    n = c_molar * 1e3 * NA                       # ions per m^3 (each species)
    return np.sqrt(eps_r * EPS0 * KB * T / (2 * n * (z * E_CH) ** 2))

# 3. Helmholtz-Smoluchowski electro-osmotic slip velocity
def u_eo(zeta, E, eps_r=78.5, mu=1.0e-3):
    return -eps_r * EPS0 * zeta * E / mu

# 4. Taylor-Melcher small-deformation of a leaky-dielectric drop
#    R = sigma_in/sigma_out, Q = eps_in/eps_out, lam = mu_in/mu_out,
#    Ca = eps_out*E^2*a/gamma.  D>0 prolate, D<0 oblate.
def taylor_deformation(R, Q, lam, Ca):
    phi = 1 + R**2 - 2*Q + 3*(R - Q)*(2 + 3*lam)/(5*(1 + lam))
    return 9*Ca*phi/(16*(2 + R)**2)

# 5. Taylor cone: root of the Legendre function P_{1/2}(cos theta) = 0
def legendre_P(nu, x, nterms=4000):
    """P_nu(x) = 2F1(-nu, nu+1; 1; (1-x)/2), valid for -1 < x <= 1."""
    z, term, total = (1 - x)/2, 1.0, 1.0
    for n in range(nterms):
        term *= (-nu + n)*(nu + 1 + n)/((n + 1)**2)*z
        total += term
    return total

def taylor_cone_half_angle():
    f = lambda th: legendre_P(0.5, np.cos(th))
    lo, hi = np.radians(100), np.radians(170)   # bracket in the gas region
    for _ in range(60):                         # plain bisection
        mid = 0.5*(lo + hi)
        if f(lo)*f(mid) <= 0: hi = mid
        else: lo = mid
    return 180 - np.degrees(0.5*(lo + hi))      # liquid half-angle

# 6. Rayleigh charge limit of a drop
def rayleigh_limit(a, gamma):
    return 8*np.pi*np.sqrt(EPS0*gamma*a**3)

# 7. Tonks-Frenkel: conducting liquid below vacuum, deep layer
def tonks_frenkel_growth(k, E, rho, gamma, g=9.81):
    w2 = g*k + gamma*k**3/rho - EPS0*E**2*k**2/rho   # omega^2
    return np.sqrt(np.clip(-w2, 0, None))             # growth rate s^-1

def tonks_frenkel_Ec(rho, gamma, g=9.81):
    return np.sqrt(2*np.sqrt(rho*g*gamma)/EPS0)

# 8. Ion-drag pump: space-charge-limited planar injection
def scl_pressure(eps_r, V, d):
    return 9*eps_r*EPS0*V**2/(8*d**2)

# 9. Unipolar-injection stability number T = eps*V/(K*mu)
def stability_T(eps_r, V, K, mu):
    return eps_r*EPS0*V/(K*mu)

# 10. Clausius-Mossotti factor for DEP
def cm_factor(f, eps_p, sig_p, eps_m, sig_m):
    w = 2*np.pi*f
    ep = eps_p*EPS0 - 1j*sig_p/w
    em = eps_m*EPS0 - 1j*sig_m/w
    return ((ep - em)/(ep + 2*em)).real

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("--- charge relaxation times ---")
    for name, er, s in [("sea water", 80, 5.0), ("tap water", 80, 5e-2),
                        ("deionised water", 80, 5.5e-6),
                        ("castor oil", 4.7, 1e-10),
                        ("transformer oil", 2.2, 1e-12)]:
        print(f"{name:16s} tau = {relaxation_time(er, s):.3g} s")
    print("--- electro-osmosis ---")
    for c in (1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2, 1e-1):
        print(f"c = {c:g} M  lambda_D = {debye_length(c)*1e9:.3g} nm")
    print(f"u_eo(zeta=-50 mV, E=10 kV/m) = {u_eo(-0.05, 1e4)*1e3:.3f} mm/s")
    print("--- Taylor drop ---")
    print("perfect conductor R->inf:", taylor_deformation(1e9, 1, 1, 1)/(9/16))
    for R, Q, lam in [(10, 1, 1), (0.1, 1.37, 1), (2, 2, 1), (0.03, 1.37, 1.0)]:
        print(f"R={R}, Q={Q}, lam={lam}: D/Ca = {taylor_deformation(R,Q,lam,1):+.4f}")
    print(f"Taylor cone half-angle = {taylor_cone_half_angle():.2f} deg")
    print(f"Rayleigh limit water a=1mm: {rayleigh_limit(1e-3, 0.072):.3g} C")
    print(f"Tonks-Frenkel Ec water: {tonks_frenkel_Ec(1000, 0.072)/1e6:.3f} MV/m")
    kc = np.sqrt(1000*9.81/0.072)

9. Exercises with solutions

  1. Conductor or insulator? A field oscillating at 1 kHz is applied to deionised water (τe ≈ 0.13 ms) and to castor oil (τe ≈ 0.4 s). Which liquid behaves as a conductor?
    Solution

    The period is 1 ms. Water: ωτe = 2π × 10³ × 1.3×10−4 ≈ 0.8, so it is in the transition regime, partly conductive. Castor oil: ωτe ≈ 2.5 × 10³ ≫ 1, so it behaves as a perfect dielectric at 1 kHz. Going to 10 Hz makes the water clearly conductive (ωτe ≈ 0.008) while the oil is still dielectric (ωτe ≈ 26) [1].

  2. Debye length scaling. By what factor does λD change when the salt concentration is raised from 1 mM to 100 mM?
    Solution

    λD ∝ n−1/2 (Eq. 8), so a 100-fold increase shrinks λD by a factor of 10, from about 9.6 nm to 0.96 nm [10].

  3. Electro-osmotic flow rate. A cylindrical capillary of radius 25 µm and length 5 cm carries an aqueous buffer with ζ = −40 mV (εr = 78.5, μ = 0.89 mPa·s). Find the volumetric flow rate for 1 kV applied across it.
    Solution

    E = 1000/0.05 = 2×104 V/m. ueo = 6.95×10−10 × 0.040 × 2×104/8.9×10−4 ≈ 6.2×10−4 m/s. Plug flow: Q = πr²u = π(25×10−6)² × 6.2×10−4 ≈ 1.2×10−12 m³/s = 1.2 nL/s [5].

  4. Perfect dielectric drop. Using Eq. (13), show that a drop that is a perfect dielectric in a perfect dielectric medium can never become oblate.
    Solution

    With no free charge, the field depends only on the permittivity ratio, which is the case R = Q. The circulation term (proportional to R − Q) vanishes and Φ = 1 + Q² − 2Q = (Q − 1)² ≥ 0. So D ≥ 0: the drop is prolate (or spherical if Q = 1) [19,21].

  5. Rayleigh limit. An electrospray droplet of radius 5 µm (methanol, γ = 0.022 N/m) carries 2.0 × 10−14 C. Is it below the Rayleigh limit?
    Solution

    QR = 8π(8.854×10−12 × 0.022 × (5×10−6)³)1/2 = 8π(2.43×10−29)1/2 ≈ 8π × 4.93×10−15 ≈ 1.24×10−13 C. The droplet carries about 16% of the limit, so it is stable. It will reach the limit after its radius shrinks by evaporation by a factor of about (0.16)2/3 ≈ 0.30, since QR ∝ a3/2 [29].

  6. Tonks–Frenkel for mercury. Estimate Ec for mercury (ρ = 13 530 kg/m³, γ = 0.485 N/m) under vacuum.
    Solution

    (ρgγ)1/2 = (13 530 × 9.81 × 0.485)1/2 ≈ 254 Pa. Ec = (2 × 254/8.854×10−12)1/2 ≈ 7.6 MV/m, about three times the water value, because both density and surface tension are much larger [32].

  7. Ion-drag pump design. How many stages of the pump in Worked example 3 (350 Pa each, ideal) are needed to lift a dielectric oil of density 880 kg/m³ by 0.5 m?
    Solution

    Required Δp = ρgh = 880 × 9.81 × 0.5 ≈ 4.3 kPa. 4300/350 ≈ 12.3, so at least 13 ideal stages. Because real injection falls short of the space-charge limit, a practical design would need several times more [2,18].

  8. Cone angle check. Using the series Pν(x) = 2F1(−ν, ν+1; 1; (1−x)/2), verify numerically that P1/2(cos 130.71°) ≈ 0.
    Solution

    Call legendre_P(0.5, np.cos(np.radians(130.71))) from Section 8. It returns a value of order 10−4, and bisection converges to θ0 = 130.71°, giving α = 49.29° [6].

  9. Electroconvection scaling. If the liquid in Worked example 6 is replaced by one ten times more viscous with the same ε and with Kμ unchanged (Walden’s rule), how does Vc change?
    Solution

    T = εV/(Kμ). If Kμ is constant, T per volt is unchanged and Vc stays about 83 V. Raising viscosity alone, at fixed K, would raise Vc tenfold to about 830 V [9].

10. Trivia questions with answers

  1. Who first recorded that a water drop is pulled into a cone by a nearby charged object, and in what book?
    Answer

    William Gilbert, in De Magnete (1600), with a rubbed piece of amber held near a water drop [31,46].

  2. Electro-osmosis was discovered in which year, and in which city?
    Answer

    1809, in Moscow, by Ferdinand Friedrich Reuss, who published in the memoirs of the Moscow society of naturalists [15].

  3. What is the half-angle of a Taylor cone?
    Answer

    About 49.3°, set by the zero of the Legendre function P1/2 [6].

  4. Which Nobel Prize rewarded an application of electrohydrodynamic spraying?
    Answer

    The 2002 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, half of which was shared by John B. Fenn and Koichi Tanaka for soft ionization methods. Fenn’s method was electrospray ionization [30,43].

  5. Who first calculated the maximum charge a liquid drop can hold, and when?
    Answer

    Lord Rayleigh, in 1882 [29].

  6. What puzzle did G. I. Taylor’s 1966 leaky dielectric paper solve?
    Answer

    Why some drops become oblate in a steady field. Purely electrostatic theory predicted prolate shapes only; Taylor showed that interfacial charge and the circulation it drives explain oblate drops [3,19].

  7. What did a 2018 MIT experiment fly using EHD?
    Answer

    A fixed-wing aeroplane with a 5 m wingspan propelled only by ionic wind, with no moving parts in its propulsion system [45].

  8. Who reported in 1896 that insulating spheres in a weakly conducting liquid spin spontaneously in a steady field?
    Answer

    Georg Quincke [28].

  9. Who coined the term “dielectrophoresis”?
    Answer

    Herbert A. Pohl, who wrote the first monograph on the subject [24].

  10. In unipolar-injection electroconvection, why does the critical voltage not depend on gap width?
    Answer

    Because the stability parameter T = εV/(Kμ) contains the voltage but no length. The Coulomb drive and the ion drift velocity both scale with V/d, so the gap cancels [9].
