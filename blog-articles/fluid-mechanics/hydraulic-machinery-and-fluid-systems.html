Hydraulic Machinery and Fluid Systems

Pumps, turbines, compressors and fans share one physical idea: a rotating or reciprocating element exchanges energy with a flowing fluid. This article develops that idea from the Euler turbomachinery equation, applies it to each machine family, and then turns to the transient behaviour of the pipe systems in which the machines sit. Every section has worked examples; exercises and trivia (with solutions) close the article, and short Python programs reproduce the numbers.

Units are SI throughout; g = 9.81 m/s², ρwater = 1000 kg/m³ unless stated. Numbered citations follow the Vancouver system; the reference list is at the end. All worked-example numbers were produced by the Python code shown (standard library only, Python ≥ 3.8).

1. Core relations and notation

A hydraulic machine changes the total energy per unit weight of the liquid passing through it, called the head. Between inlet section 1 and outlet section 2 the head rise of a pump (or head drop of a turbine) is [1,2]

H = ( p/ρg + V²/2g + z )2 − ( p/ρg + V²/2g + z )1(1.1)

The power delivered to the fluid (pump) or extracted from it (turbine) is the hydraulic power Ph = ρgQH. The shaft power of a pump is P = ρgQH/η, and that of a turbine is P = ηρgQH, where η is the overall efficiency [2,3]. For compressible flow, head is replaced by the specific stagnation-enthalpy change Δh0 (J/kg).

2. Pumps

Pumps fall into two families. Rotodynamic (kinetic) pumps add angular momentum to the liquid in a rotating impeller and convert part of it to pressure in a casing; their flow rate depends strongly on the system resistance. Positive-displacement pumps trap a fixed volume and push it to the outlet; their flow rate is nearly independent of discharge pressure [3,4]. Rotodynamic pumps are subdivided by the direction of flow leaving the impeller: radial (centrifugal), mixed (diagonal) and axial, a progression that tracks increasing specific speed (Section 5.3) [4,5].

2.1 Centrifugal pumps

Liquid enters the impeller eye axially, turns into the radial direction between the blades and leaves at the impeller periphery with high velocity. A spiral volute, or a ring of diffuser vanes, collects the flow and decelerates it, recovering kinetic energy as pressure [3,4]. Most impellers use backward-curved blades, with outlet angle β2 commonly between about 15° and 40° from the tangent, because they give a stable, steadily falling head–flow curve [4,5].

The ideal head follows from the Euler equation (Section 5.2). With no inlet swirl, Hth = u2cu2/g. Because an impeller has a finite number of blades, the flow leaves with less whirl than the blade angle implies; this slip is represented by a slip factor σ, of which Wiesner's correlation is the most widely used [4,6]:

σ = 1 − √(sin β2) / Z0.7,    cu2 = σu2 − cm2/tan β2(2.1)

where Z is the blade count and β2 is measured from the tangent. Hydraulic losses (friction, separation, mixing, shock at off-design incidence) reduce the actual head further; leakage across the wear rings, disc friction on the shrouds and mechanical losses reduce the overall efficiency [4].

Worked example 2.1: centrifugal impeller head

An impeller has D2 = 0.30 m, outlet width b2 = 20 mm, Z = 7 blades, β2 = 25°, and runs at 1450 rpm delivering 0.050 m³/s with no inlet swirl. Take the hydraulic efficiency as 0.82 and ignore blade blockage.

u2 = πD2N/60 = 22.78 m/s; cm2 = Q/(πD2b2) = 2.65 m/s.
Without slip: cu2 = 22.78 − 2.65/tan 25° = 17.09 m/s, so H∞ = 22.78 × 17.09/9.81 = 39.7 m.
Wiesner: σ = 1 − √(sin 25°)/70.7 = 0.834, cu2 = 13.30 m/s, Hth = 30.9 m.
Actual head ≈ 0.82 × 30.9 = 25.3 m. Slip alone removes about 22% of the infinite-blade head, which is why it cannot be neglected in design [4,6]. (Code: triangles.py.)

Cavitation and NPSH

If the static pressure near the impeller eye falls to the vapour pressure, vapour cavities form and collapse downstream, causing head loss, noise and erosion [4,7]. The suction margin is expressed as net positive suction head. The value available from the installation is

NPSHA = (ps − pv)/ρg + zs − hL,s(2.2)

with ps the pressure on the suction-tank surface, zs the height of that surface above the pump datum (negative for a suction lift) and hL,s the suction-line loss. The pump's requirement is normally quoted as NPSH3, the NPSH at which the total head has dropped by 3% at constant flow, as defined in the ISO 9906 acceptance-test standard [8]. Because cavitation begins well before a 3% head drop, a margin of NPSHA over NPSH3 is required [4,7].

Worked example 2.2: suction lift

Water at 20 °C (pv = 2.34 kPa) is lifted from an open sump 3.0 m below the pump centreline at sea level (101.3 kPa), with 1.2 m of suction-line loss. NPSHA = (101 325 − 2339)/(1000 × 9.81) − 3.0 − 1.2 = 5.9 m. A pump with NPSH3 = 5 m at this duty would have an uncomfortably small margin.

2.2 Axial-flow pumps

In an axial-flow (propeller) pump the liquid enters and leaves parallel to the shaft; the rotor adds whirl and downstream guide vanes remove it, converting the kinetic energy to pressure. Because the radius does not change through the rotor, there is no centrifugal contribution to the head, so axial pumps deliver large flows at low heads: high specific speed, with nq roughly above 160 in the European units of Section 5.3 [4,5]. Two features of their characteristics matter in operation. The head–flow curve is steep and usually shows an unstable dip at part load, caused by rotating stall in the blade rows. The power demand is highest at shut-off, so axial pumps are started against an open discharge valve, the opposite of centrifugal practice [3,5]. Large axial pumps often have adjustable-pitch blades for flow control. Typical uses are flood control, irrigation, cooling-water circulation and drainage.

2.3 Mixed-flow pumps

In mixed-flow (diagonal) pumps the flow leaves the impeller at an angle between radial and axial. The head comes partly from the radius increase and partly from blade lift, so specific speeds lie between the radial and axial ranges (very roughly nq ≈ 35–160) [4]. Their power curve is comparatively flat across the flow range, and they are common as multistage vertical "turbine" pumps in wells and as large circulating pumps [3].

2.4 Positive-displacement pumps

Positive-displacement (PD) pumps move liquid by changing the volume of sealed chambers. Rotary types include gear (external and internal), lobe, vane, screw and progressing-cavity pumps; reciprocating types include piston, plunger and diaphragm pumps [3]. The delivered flow is

Q = ηv Vd n(2.3)

where Vd is the displacement per revolution, n the speed (rev/s) and ηv the volumetric efficiency, which accounts for internal leakage (slip) back to suction. Slip grows with pressure difference and falls with viscosity, so PD pumps handle viscous liquids such as oils, polymers and food products well, where rotodynamic efficiency collapses [3]. Because a PD pump will keep generating pressure against a closed outlet until something fails, the system must include a pressure-relief valve [3,9].

2.5 Reciprocating pumps

A piston or plunger of area A and stroke s driven by a crank at n strokes per second displaces Qth = Asn per cylinder (single-acting), or nearly twice that when both faces of a piston do work (double-acting). Suction and discharge check valves rectify the flow [3]. With a crank drive, the piston velocity is close to sinusoidal, so the instantaneous flow pulsates. Adding cylinders phased around the crankshaft smooths the flow, which is why triplex (three-cylinder) plunger pumps dominate high-pressure service [3].

The pulsating flow also forces the liquid column in the suction pipe to accelerate every stroke. The resulting acceleration head must be subtracted from NPSHA, and pulsation dampeners (gas-charged accumulators, Section 6.6) are fitted close to the pump on both suction and discharge to limit it [3].

Worked example 2.3: plunger pump

A single-acting simplex pump has a 100 mm bore and a 150 mm stroke, and runs at 120 strokes/min. Qth = (π/4)(0.10)² × 0.15 × 120/60 = 2.36 × 10⁻³ m³/s = 2.36 L/s. The peak instantaneous flow is π × 2.36 = 7.4 L/s; a triplex pump of the same total capacity would peak at only 2.47 L/s.

2.6 Pump similarity (affinity) laws

Dimensional analysis shows that, for geometrically similar pumps operating at the same flow coefficient and with Reynolds-number effects neglected, the groups Q/(ND³), gH/(N²D²) and P/(ρN³D⁵) are equal [1,2]. For one pump at two speeds (D fixed) these give the affinity laws:

Q2/Q1 = N2/N1,   H2/H1 = (N2/N1)²,   P2/P1 = (N2/N1)³(2.4)

Each point of the curve moves along a parabola H ∝ Q² through the origin, so efficiency is (nearly) preserved along it. Two cautions apply. First, these "similar points" lie on a parabola through the origin; a system curve with a static lift is not such a parabola, so slowing a pump that works against a high static head moves it towards shut-off and away from best efficiency. Second, trimming an impeller is not a geometrically similar change, and the simple rules Q ∝ D, H ∝ D² are only approximate [3,4].

Worked example 2.4: speed change

At 1450 rpm a pump delivers 0.050 m³/s at 30 m and absorbs 18 kW. At 1750 rpm the similar point is Q = 0.0603 m³/s, H = 43.7 m and P = 31.6 kW. A 21% speed increase raises power by 76%. (Code: similarity.py.)

2.7 Pump performance curves

A rotodynamic pump is described by its head–flow (H–Q), power–flow, efficiency–flow and NPSH3–flow curves at a stated speed, measured according to standards such as ISO 9906 [8]. The best efficiency point (BEP) is the design duty. Running far from it raises hydraulic losses, radial thrust, vibration and recirculation at part load, and raises NPSH3 at high flow [4]. The operating point is where the pump curve meets the system curve, the head the piping needs to pass a flow Q:

Hsys = Hst + KQ²(2.5)

where Hst is the static lift plus any pressure difference between the reservoirs, and KQ² is the total friction and fitting loss in turbulent flow [1,3]. Identical pumps in parallel add flows at equal head; pumps in series add heads at equal flow. Because the system curve rises, two pumps in parallel never deliver twice the flow of one.

3. Turbines

3.1 Impulse and reaction turbines

In an impulse turbine the whole available head is converted to jet velocity in stationary nozzles; the runner works at atmospheric pressure and only changes the direction of the jet. In a reaction turbine part of the pressure drop occurs in the runner itself, which runs full of water inside a closed casing, so the relative velocity increases through the blade passages [1,2]. The degree of reaction (Section 5.4) is zero for an ideal impulse turbine. The Pelton wheel is the classical impulse machine; Francis and Kaplan turbines are reaction machines. The choice among them follows from the site head and flow, or equivalently from the specific speed (Figure 6).

3.2 Pelton turbines

One or more nozzles, each with a needle (spear) valve for flow control, direct free jets at double-cupped buckets with a central splitter. Lester Pelton's 1880 patent introduced the split bucket that turns the jet sideways and back [10,11]. A deflector can swing into the jet to cut the power quickly without closing the needle quickly, which protects the long penstock from water hammer (Section 6.1) [2].

Applying the Euler equation to the bucket with jet velocity c1 = Cv√(2gH), bucket speed u = φ√(2gH), deflection θ and relative-velocity ratio k = W2/W1 gives the hydraulic efficiency [1,2]:

ηh = P/(ρgQH) = 2φ(Cv − φ)(1 − k cos θ)(3.1)

which peaks at φ = Cv/2, that is, when the bucket moves at half the jet speed. Real wheels run slightly below this ratio (about 0.46) because of windage and bucket losses [2].

Worked example 3.1: Pelton wheel

H = 500 m, Q = 2.0 m³/s, Cv = 0.98, φ = 0.46, θ = 165°, k = 0.90.
c1 = 0.98√(2 × 9.81 × 500) = 97.1 m/s; u = 45.6 m/s; Δcu = (97.1 − 45.6)(1 − 0.9 cos 165°) = 96.3 m/s.
P = 1000 × 2.0 × 45.6 × 96.3 = 8.77 MW, ηh = 0.894. At 500 rpm the pitch-circle diameter is 60u/(πN) = 1.74 m; a single jet would be 0.162 m in diameter, a jet ratio D/d ≈ 10.7. (Code: pelton.py; the optimum φ it finds is 0.490 = Cv/2.)

3.3 Francis turbines

The Francis turbine is a mixed-flow reaction machine. Water passes from a spiral casing through stay vanes and adjustable guide vanes (wicket gates), which set the inlet swirl and flow rate, then flows radially inward and turns axial through the runner before leaving through a draft tube [2]. The machine is named after James B. Francis, whose systematic tests of inward-flow turbines at Lowell, Massachusetts, were published in 1855 [12]. Francis turbines cover the widest range of heads and are the most common large hydroelectric units; reversible Francis pump-turbines are the standard machine for pumped storage [2,13].

The draft tube is a diverging conduit. It recovers much of the runner exit kinetic energy, and it allows the runner to be set above tailwater while still using the full head, because the pressure at the runner exit is below atmospheric. That sub-atmospheric pressure is also where cavitation risk is highest. Cavitation is characterised by the Thoma number σ = NPSE/(gH), and the plant σ must exceed the turbine's critical σ [2,7].

Worked example 3.2: choosing the turbine type

A site offers H = 120 m and Q = 40 m³/s; assume η = 0.93. P = 0.93 × 1000 × 9.81 × 40 × 120 = 43.8 MW. At a synchronous speed of 300 rpm the dimensional specific speed Ns = N√P(kW)/H5/4 = 158, and the dimensionless power specific speed Ωsp = Ω√(P/ρ)/(gH)5/4 = 0.95 rad. Both lie squarely in the Francis range (Ns roughly 50–400) [1,2].

3.4 Kaplan turbines

The Kaplan turbine is an axial-flow propeller turbine whose runner blades, as well as its guide vanes, are adjustable while running. Coordinating blade pitch with gate opening (double regulation) keeps the flow at near-optimal incidence over a wide load range, so the efficiency curve is much flatter than that of a fixed-blade propeller turbine [2]. Kaplan and bulb turbines (a horizontal variant with the generator inside a submerged bulb) serve low-head, high-flow sites such as river barrages [2].

3.5 Wind turbines

A wind turbine extracts kinetic energy from an unconfined stream, so there is no "head" in the hydraulic sense. Actuator-disc momentum theory treats the rotor as a permeable disc that reduces the wind speed from V far upstream to V(1 − a) at the disc and V(1 − 2a) far downstream, where a is the axial induction factor [14,15]. The power and thrust are

P = ½ρAV³ Cp,   Cp = 4a(1 − a)²,   CT = 4a(1 − a)(3.2)

Setting dCp/da = 0 gives a = 1/3 and Cp,max = 16/27 ≈ 0.593, the Betz limit [14,16]. The same result was obtained independently by Lanchester and by Joukowsky, and it is sometimes called the Lanchester–Betz–Joukowsky limit [17]. Wake rotation, finite blade number, tip losses and profile drag bring the best practical rotor Cp to around 0.45–0.50 [14]. The tip-speed ratio λ = ΩR/V plays the role of a flow coefficient. Design load cases and wind classes for horizontal-axis machines are set by IEC 61400-1 [18].

Worked example 3.3: rotor power

A 120 m rotor (A = 11 310 m²) in a 10 m/s wind with ρ = 1.225 kg/m³ sees a kinetic-energy flux ½ρAV³ = 6.93 MW. The Betz limit allows 4.11 MW; with a realistic Cp = 0.45 the rotor delivers 3.1 MW. Because power scales with V³, the same rotor at 8 m/s yields only 51% of this.

3.6 Hydraulic efficiency

The overall efficiency of a hydraulic machine is the product of three parts: η = ηh ηv ηm (hydraulic, volumetric and mechanical) [2,4]. Hydraulic efficiency relates the head to the Euler work:

pump: ηh = gH / (u2cu2 − u1cu1)     turbine: ηh = (u1cu1 − u2cu2) / gH(3.3)

Volumetric efficiency accounts for leakage that bypasses the runner (wear-ring and labyrinth leakage); mechanical efficiency accounts for bearing, seal and disc-friction losses. Large machines are more efficient than their small models, because relative roughness and Reynolds-number effects shrink. Moody's step-up formula is a classical estimate [2]:

(1 − ηp) / (1 − ηm) = (Dm/Dp)1/5(3.4)

Contractual model-to-prototype transposition of turbine and pump-turbine efficiency follows the more detailed procedures of IEC 60193 [13].

Worked example 3.4: scale-up

A model runner with Dm = 0.35 m reaches ηm = 0.90. For a prototype with Dp = 3.5 m: 1 − ηp = 0.10 × (0.1)0.2 = 0.063, so ηp ≈ 0.937.

4. Compressors and fans

Gas machines are classified by pressure rise. Fans raise pressure so little that the gas can be treated as incompressible; blowers sit in between; compressors produce significant density change and must be analysed with stagnation enthalpy and temperature [2,19]. Exact boundaries vary between standards and textbooks. For a compressor stage with isentropic efficiency ηc and a perfect gas,

p02/p01 = [1 + ηc ΔT0/T01]γ/(γ−1),    ΔT0 = Δh0/cp(4.1)

[2,20].

4.1 Centrifugal compressors

A centrifugal compressor has an inducer that turns the axial inflow onto the blades, an impeller, a vaneless or vaned diffuser and a volute or return channel. The large radius ratio gives a high work input per stage. Pressure ratios of about 4:1 in a single stage are routine, and higher ratios are achieved in modern designs with backswept blades [2,20]. With no inlet swirl the Euler work is Δh0 = U2cθ2, and for radial blades cθ2 = σU2. Disc friction and windage add a few percent of work input; Saravanamuttoo et al. represent this with a power input factor [20]. Centrifugal machines are robust, compact and tolerant of fouling. They are used in turbochargers, small gas turbines, refrigeration and process gas service [2,21].

Worked example 4.1: single-stage pressure ratio

A radial-bladed impeller with tip speed 450 m/s, slip factor 0.90 and no prewhirl draws air at T01 = 288.15 K. Δh0 = 0.9 × 450² = 182.3 kJ/kg, ΔT0 = 181.3 K. With ηc = 0.80 and γ = 1.4, Eq. (4.1) gives p02/p01 = (1 + 0.8 × 181.3/288.15)3.5 = 4.17. Including a power input factor of 1.04 raises ΔT0 to 188.6 K and the ideal ratio to 4.37, but the extra work is dissipated, so designers apply the factor to work and temperature rise, not to the pressure ratio [20].

4.2 Axial compressors

An axial compressor alternates rotor and stator blade rows. Each row is a diffusing cascade. Boundary layers on the blade suction surfaces tolerate only a limited deceleration before they separate, so each stage gives a modest pressure ratio (typically around 1.1–1.4) and many stages are needed. In exchange, the axial machine handles very large mass flow per unit frontal area at high efficiency, which is why it dominates large gas turbines and jet engines [2,20,22]. Inlet guide vanes and variable stators adjust the incidence at part speed, and bleed valves and multiple spools help to avoid stall at low speed [20]. Stage design in terms of φ, ψ and degree of reaction is treated in Section 5, with a numerical stage in Worked example 5.2.

4.3 Positive-displacement compressors

Reciprocating compressors are used for high pressure ratios at modest flow. Rotary screw compressors (twin-screw, oil-flooded or dry) dominate industrial compressed air. Scroll compressors are used in air conditioning, sliding-vane compressors in small duties, and Roots (lobe) blowers for low-pressure, high-flow service [21]. A Roots blower has no internal compression: the trapped gas is compressed by back-flow when the pocket opens to the discharge, so it is inefficient at higher pressure ratios. A screw compressor has a built-in volume ratio and is most efficient when the system pressure ratio matches it [21]. In a reciprocating compressor, clearance gas re-expands before fresh gas can enter, which limits the volumetric efficiency [21]:

ηv = 1 + C − C (p2/p1)1/n(4.2)

where C is the clearance volume as a fraction of swept volume and n the polytropic re-expansion exponent. For C = 0.05, p2/p1 = 6 and n = 1.3, ηv = 0.85; this is why high overall ratios are split into stages with intercooling.

4.4 Fans

Centrifugal fans are built with forward-curved, radial or backward-curved (including aerofoil) blades. Forward-curved blades give a compact fan with a high flow coefficient, but the power rises steeply with flow, so the motor can overload. Backward-curved and aerofoil blades give the highest efficiency and a non-overloading power curve. Radial blades tolerate dust and erosion [19]. Axial fans range from propeller fans with no casing to tube-axial and vane-axial fans with guide vanes; cross-flow (tangential) fans make long, narrow air curtains [19]. Fan pressure is quoted as total pressure rise or as static pressure rise, so the convention must be checked when comparing catalogues. Laboratory performance testing follows standards such as ISO 5801 [23]. Because density is almost constant, fan laws are the affinity laws with an extra diameter scaling:

Q ∝ ND³,   Δp ∝ ρN²D²,   P ∝ ρN³D⁵(4.3)

Worked example 4.2: fan laws

A 0.8 m fan at 1200 rpm moves 5 m³/s at 800 Pa and absorbs 5 kW. A geometrically similar 1.0 m fan at 1000 rpm, with the same air density, gives Q = 5 × (1000/1200)(1.0/0.8)³ = 8.14 m³/s, Δp = 868 Pa and P = 8.83 kW.

4.5 Blowers

"Blower" covers machines with pressure rises between those of fans and compressors. Examples are multistage centrifugal blowers for wastewater aeration and Roots or screw blowers for pneumatic conveying [19,21]. Centrifugal blowers behave like low-ratio compressors: they have a surge limit (Section 4.7) and their discharge pressure varies strongly with inlet temperature and density. Rotary lobe blowers behave like PD machines, delivering nearly constant volume flow regardless of back-pressure, so they need relief protection [21].

4.6 Performance maps

Compressor characteristics are plotted as a map of total pressure ratio against corrected (non-dimensional) mass flow ṁ√T01/p01, with lines of constant corrected speed N/√T01 and contours ("islands") of constant efficiency. Corrected variables collapse data taken at different inlet conditions onto one chart, by the similarity arguments of Section 5.7 [2,20]. Each speed line is bounded on the left by the surge line and on the right by choke, where some blade row reaches sonic conditions and the flow can no longer increase. The engine or process fixes an operating line across the speed lines. The distance from it to the surge line is the surge margin, commonly defined as SM = (PRsurge − PRop)/PRop at constant corrected flow, although several definitions are in use [20,22].

4.7 Compressor surge

Surge is a system instability. When a compressor operates where its characteristic slope d(PR)/dṁ is positive (near the peak of a speed line), a small drop in flow lowers the delivery pressure below the plenum pressure. Flow then falls further, can reverse, the plenum empties, and the cycle repeats. Mild surge is a limit cycle about the characteristic peak; deep surge involves reversed flow, large forces on the blades and high temperatures [22,24]. Greitzer's lumped-parameter model treats the compressor duct as a fluid inertia and the downstream plenum as a gas spring, a Helmholtz resonator, and shows that the behaviour depends on

B = U / (2ωHLc),    ωH = a0√(Ac/(VpLc))(4.4)

with a0 the speed of sound, Ac and Lc the duct area and effective length, and Vp the plenum volume. Large B (large plenum, high speed) leads to surge, and small B leads to the compressor settling into rotating stall. In Greitzer's experiments the transition occurred near B ≈ 0.8 [24,25]. Industrial practice prevents surge with anti-surge control: a recycle or blow-off valve keeps the flow to the right of a control line set at a margin from the measured surge line [21].

Worked example 4.3: Greitzer parameter

U = 300 m/s, a0 = 340 m/s, Ac = 0.05 m², Lc = 1.5 m, Vp = 2.0 m³. ωH = 340√(0.05/(2 × 1.5)) = 43.9 rad/s (7.0 Hz); B = 300/(2 × 43.9 × 1.5) = 2.28. This is well above 0.8, so instability would be expected to develop as surge rather than as rotating stall.

4.8 Rotating stall

Rotating stall is a local instability. One or more cells of stalled, low-flow fluid form in a blade row and travel around the annulus in the direction of rotor rotation, but more slowly than the rotor. The time-mean annulus flow stays steady, but the blades see a strong periodic load that can excite vibration [26,27]. Emmons, Pearson and Grant explained the propagation as follows. A stalled passage blocks flow and diverts it towards its neighbours. On one side this increases incidence and stalls the next blade; on the other side it reduces incidence and the blade unstalls. The cell therefore moves from blade to blade [26]. Stall can begin as long-wavelength "modal" disturbances or as short-wavelength "spikes" at the rotor tip, and once established it often shows hysteresis: the throttle must be opened beyond the stall point to recover [22,27]. Cell speeds are commonly a fraction of rotor speed, roughly 20–70%; part-span cells generally travel faster than full-span cells [22,27].

5. Turbomachinery theory

5.1 Velocity triangles

At any station the absolute velocity c is the vector sum of the relative velocity w seen by the blade and the blade speed U: c = w + U. In a well-designed blade row the relative flow approximately follows the blade camber line, so blade angles fix w, and the triangle then fixes c and its whirl component cθ, which carries the energy transfer [2].

5.2 Euler turbomachinery equation

Applying the angular-momentum theorem to a steady flow through a rotor gives the torque, and multiplying by Ω gives the specific work [2]:

T = ṁ(r2cθ2 − r1cθ1),    ΔW = Δh0 = U2cθ2 − U1cθ1(5.1)

Positive ΔW is work done on the fluid (pumps, compressors, fans); for a turbine the sign reverses. The result needs no assumption about losses inside the rotor; losses only make the actual head or pressure rise smaller than the work implies. Using the triangle identities, the work can be split into three terms [2]:

ΔW = ½[(c2² − c1²) + (U2² − U1²) − (w2² − w1²)](5.2)

These are the kinetic-energy rise, the centrifugal ("free") static-pressure rise and the static-pressure rise from relative diffusion. The second term is absent in axial machines, which is why their stage pressure rise is limited. An equivalent statement is that rothalpy I = h + w²/2 − U²/2 is conserved along a streamline in the rotating frame (adiabatic, steady flow) [2].

5.3 Specific speed

Eliminating D between the flow and head coefficients gives a number that depends only on the duty and the speed, and that classifies the best-suited machine geometry [2,4]:

Ωs = Ω√Q / (gH)3/4   (pumps),    Ωsp = Ω√(P/ρ) / (gH)5/4   (turbines)(5.3)

Both are dimensionless when Ω is in rad/s. In practice dimensional forms are common: the European nq = N√Q/H3/4 (rpm, m³/s, m) = 52.9 Ωs, and the US Ns (rpm, US gpm, ft) = 2733 Ωs [4,7]. Low specific speed means a high head for the flow, which calls for narrow radial impellers or Pelton wheels; high specific speed calls for axial machines. Cordier found that well-performing machines cluster along a single curve linking specific speed to a specific diameter Δs = D(gH)1/4/√Q. This Cordier diagram is widely used for preliminary sizing [2,28].

Worked example 5.1

5.4 Degree of reaction

The degree of reaction R is the fraction of the stage static-enthalpy change that occurs in the rotor [2]:

R = (h2 − h1) / (h3 − h1)   ⇒   R = 1 − (cθ1 + cθ2)/(2U)    (axial compressor, constant cx)(5.4)

where 1→2 is the rotor and 2→3 the stator of a repeating stage. With R = 0.5 the rotor and stator velocity triangles are mirror images (α1 = β2, α2 = β1), and the diffusion is shared equally between the two rows, a popular choice for compressors. R = 0 is an impulse stage (the Pelton wheel and impulse steam turbine stages). In axial turbines, higher reaction gives accelerating, lower-loss rotor passages [2,20].

5.5 Blade loading

Blade loading measures how hard a blade row is asked to work. For a whole stage it is expressed by the stage loading coefficient ψ = Δh0/U² and flow coefficient φ = cx/U. For compressor cascades, whose blade surfaces diffuse the flow, two classical limits apply [2,22]:

  • de Haller criterion: w2/w1 ≥ 0.72 (for a rotor; c3/c2 for a stator). Greater deceleration risks separation [2].
  • Lieblein diffusion factor: DF = 1 − w2/w1 + |Δwθ|/(2σw1), with σ = c/s the solidity. Loss rises sharply above roughly DF ≈ 0.6, and lower limits (about 0.4) are used at rotor tips [2,29].

For turbine cascades, where the flow accelerates, loading is limited instead by suction-surface diffusion near the trailing edge. The Zweifel tangential loading coefficient (optimum near 0.8) is the classical measure for choosing blade pitch [2].

Worked example 5.2: repeating axial compressor stage

U = 300 m/s, φ = 0.5, ψ = 0.4, R = 0.5, rotor solidity 1.5, inlet 288.15 K, η = 0.90.
From Eq. (5.4) and ψ = Δcθ/U: cθ1 = 90 m/s, cθ2 = 210 m/s; cx = 150 m/s; β1 = 54.5°, β2 = 31.0°.
w2/w1 = 174.9/258.1 = 0.678, which fails the de Haller limit of 0.72, while DF = 0.48 is acceptable. Δh0 = 36.0 kJ/kg, ΔT0 = 35.8 K and the stage pressure ratio is 1.45. The two criteria disagree here: higher solidity lowers DF but cannot fix de Haller, which depends only on the triangles. The cure is to reduce ψ or raise φ. (Code: axial_stage.py.)

5.6 Loss mechanisms

Denton argued that entropy creation is the only rigorous measure of loss in turbomachines, because it is frame-independent and additive. He defined loss coefficients as TΔs divided by a reference kinetic energy, and traced entropy to three processes: viscous friction in boundary layers and free shear layers, heat transfer across temperature differences, and non-equilibrium processes such as shock waves [30]. In design practice losses are grouped by location [2,30]:

  • Profile loss: blade-surface boundary layers and trailing-edge wake mixing.
  • Endwall (secondary) loss: hub and casing boundary layers, plus the secondary vortices formed when the endwall flow is turned.
  • Tip and seal leakage loss: flow through the clearance, which then mixes with the main stream.
  • Shock loss in transonic blade rows, and incidence loss at off-design inflow angles.

Denton suggested that in many machines profile, endwall and leakage losses are of similar magnitude, each roughly a third of the total [30]. Pumps add losses usually treated separately: disc friction on impeller shrouds, leakage through wear rings, part-load recirculation and volute or diffuser losses [4].

5.7 Similarity laws

For an incompressible machine, Buckingham's Π theorem reduces the variables (gH, P, η, Q, N, D, ρ, μ, roughness) to [1,2]:

gH/(N²D²),   P/(ρN³D⁵),   η = f[ Q/(ND³), ρND²/μ, ε/D ](5.5)

Since machine Reynolds numbers are very high, the Reynolds and roughness effects are weak and are handled by scale-up corrections (Eq. 3.4, IEC 60193) [2,13]. For compressible flow, Mach-number groups enter, and the map is plotted in the corrected quantities ṁ√(RT01)/(D²p01) and ND/√(γRT01); for a given machine and gas these reduce to ṁ√T01/p01 and N/√T01 [2,20]. Cavitation adds a further group, the cavitation number (Thoma σ), that model and prototype must share [7].

6. Hydraulic system dynamics

6.1 Water hammer

If the flow in a pipe is stopped suddenly, the kinetic energy of the liquid column is converted into elastic strain of the liquid and the pipe wall. A pressure wave travels from the disturbance at the acoustic speed a. Joukowsky's analysis of his experiments on Moscow's water mains gave the pressure change for an instantaneous velocity change [31,32]:

Δp = −ρaΔV   or   ΔH = −(a/g)ΔV(6.1)

The wave speed depends on the liquid bulk modulus K and on the pipe's elasticity. For a thin-walled pipe of diameter D, wall thickness e and Young's modulus E, the Korteweg result, extended with a restraint factor c1, is [33,34]:

a = √( (K/ρ) / [1 + c1 KD/(Ee)] )(6.2)

with c1 = 1 for a pipe with expansion joints and slightly smaller values (1 − ν/2, 1 − ν²) for other axial restraints. Water in a rigid pipe gives about 1480 m/s; in steel about 1000–1300 m/s; in plastic pipes a few hundred m/s [34,35]. A wave takes 2L/a to travel to the reservoir and back. A valve closure faster than this produces the full Joukowsky rise; slower closures give smaller peaks, which Michaud's classical formula ΔH ≈ 2LV0/(gtc) estimates [32,34].

Worked example 6.1: steel penstock

Water (K = 2.2 GPa) flows at 2.0 m/s in a 1200 m steel pipe, D = 0.5 m, e = 10 mm, E = 200 GPa, c1 = 1.
a = √(2.2×10⁹/1000 / (1 + 2.2×10⁹ × 0.5/(200×10⁹ × 0.010))) = 1191 m/s.
ΔH = 1191 × 2.0/9.81 = 243 m (Δp = 2.38 MPa); 2L/a = 2.01 s. Closing in 1 s still produces essentially the full rise; Figure 12 shows the simulated head at the valve for an instantaneous closure and a 6 s linear closure.

6.2 Pressure surges

Valve closure is only one cause of surges. Others are pump trip (power failure), pump start-up, check-valve slam, rapid turbine load changes, filling of empty lines and the collapse of vapour or air pockets [34,36]. A pump trip is often the design case for pumping mains. The pump decelerates and a down-surge travels along the line. Where the pressure reaches vapour pressure, typically at high points, the liquid column separates. When the columns rejoin, the pressure spike can exceed the Joukowsky value for the original velocity [32,34]. Protection measures include surge vessels (air chambers), surge tanks, flywheels to prolong pump run-down, air-admission and air-release valves, pressure-relief and surge-anticipating valves, and slow-closing or two-stage-closing valves [35,36].

6.3 Transient pipe flow

One-dimensional elastic water-hammer theory uses the continuity and momentum equations for a slightly compressible liquid in an elastic pipe [34,35]:

∂H/∂t + (a²/g) ∂V/∂x = 0,    ∂V/∂t + g ∂H/∂x + fV|V|/(2D) = 0(6.3)

(The convective terms are dropped because V ≪ a.) This hyperbolic pair has characteristic directions dx/dt = ±a, along which it reduces to ordinary differential equations, the compatibility relations. The method of characteristics (MOC) integrates them on a grid with Δx = aΔt [34,35]:

C⁺: HP = HA − B(QP − QA) − RQA|QA|,    C⁻: HP = HB + B(QP − QB) + RQB|QB|(6.4)

with B = a/(gA) and R = fΔx/(2gDA²). Boundary conditions (reservoirs, valves, pumps, surge tanks) supply the missing equation at the pipe ends [34]. Chaudhry characterises a pipeline by the Allievi parameter ρA = aV0/(2gH0) [35]. When transients are slow compared with 2L/a, a rigid-column model (liquid incompressible, pipe rigid) is adequate; this is the model used for surge tanks below. Steady-state friction under-predicts the damping of real transients, and unsteady-friction models (for example those of Zielke and Brunone) correct for it [32].

6.4 Fluid–structure interaction

Classical water-hammer theory treats the pipe only as a compliance that slows the wave. In reality pipes move. Axial wall stress waves travel faster than pressure waves, and pipe motion at bends and supports feeds back into the liquid. Tijsseling's review identifies three coupling mechanisms [37]:

  • Poisson coupling: pressure changes the hoop strain, and through Poisson's ratio the axial strain. This produces "precursor" waves in the liquid that run ahead of the main pressure front at the speed of axial stress waves in the pipe wall.
  • Friction coupling: shear stress between the liquid and the wall, usually weak.
  • Junction coupling: unbalanced pressure forces at bends, tees, closed ends and valves set the pipe moving. It is usually the strongest mechanism in unrestrained systems.

Models range from the four-equation axial model (pressure, fluid velocity, axial stress, axial wall velocity) to fourteen-equation models that include bending and torsion in three dimensions. Fluid–structure interaction matters for loosely supported piping, such as power-plant, aerospace and pipe-rack systems, where it can raise or lower peak pressures and shift frequencies compared with classical theory [34,37].

6.5 Surge tanks

A surge tank is an open standpipe or chamber connected to a pipeline, usually where a long low-pressure tunnel meets a steep penstock. It reflects water-hammer waves, so the high-frequency transients are confined to the short penstock, and it supplies or stores water while the slow tunnel flow adjusts after a load change [35,38]. Types include simple, restricted-orifice, differential (Johnson) and closed air-cushion tanks. With a rigid-column tunnel of length L, area At and loss hf = cV|V|, and tank area As, the mass oscillation obeys [35,38]:

(L/g) dV/dt = −z − cV|V|,    As dz/dt = AtV − Qturbine(6.5)

where z is the tank level above the reservoir. Without friction, a full load rejection gives an amplitude Zmax = V0√(LAt/(gAs)) and a period T = 2π√(LAs/(gAt)). A tank feeding a governed turbine can oscillate unstably, because the governor holds power constant and so draws more flow as head falls. Thoma's criterion gives the minimum stable area [35,38]:

ATh = LAtV0² / [2g hf0(H0 − hf0)](6.6)

Designers usually add a safety factor of 1.5 or more [35].

Worked example 6.2: load rejection

Tunnel L = 2000 m, diameter 4 m (At = 12.57 m²), V0 = 2.5 m/s; tank diameter 12 m (As = 113.1 m²). Frictionless: Zmax = 2.5√(2000 × 12.57/(9.81 × 113.1)) = 11.9 m, T = 269 s. Integrating Eq. (6.5) with hf0 = 3 m (surge_tank.py) gives 10.0 m. With H0 = 150 m, Eq. (6.6) gives ATh = 18.2 m², so the 113 m² tank is comfortably stable.

6.6 Accumulators

A hydraulic accumulator stores liquid under pressure against a compressed gas (bladder, piston or diaphragm type) or, less often, a spring or weight. It supplements pump flow during peak demand, provides emergency power, absorbs shocks and thermal expansion, and damps pump pulsations [9,39]. The gas is pre-charged to p0, slightly below the minimum working pressure p1, so the separating element never bottoms out. If the gas follows pVn = const, the volume delivered between p2 and p1 fixes the required gas volume:

V0 = ΔV / [ (p0/p1)1/n − (p0/p2)1/n ](6.7)

with n = 1 for slow (isothermal) cycles and n ≈ 1.4 for fast (adiabatic) discharge of nitrogen [9]. Real-gas effects at high pressure make these estimates optimistic.

Worked example 6.3

ΔV = 2.0 L between 200 and 100 bar, with a pre-charge of 90 bar: V0 = 4.44 L (isothermal) or 5.52 L (adiabatic). A fast emergency stroke therefore needs a 25% larger accumulator (accumulator.py).

6.7 Hydraulic control systems

Electrohydraulic servo and proportional valves meter flow to actuators through variable orifices. For a spool opening xv with area gradient w the orifice equation is Q = Cdwxv√(2Δp/ρ). Linearising about an operating point gives the load flow [9,39]

QL = Kqxv − KcpL(6.8)

with flow gain Kq and flow-pressure coefficient Kc. Fluid compressibility in the actuator chambers acts as a spring. For a symmetric cylinder of area Ap with total trapped volume Vt, driving mass m, Merritt's hydraulic natural frequency is [39]

ωh = √( 4βeAp² / (Vtm) )(6.9)

where βe is the effective bulk modulus. Entrained air and flexible hoses can lower βe far below the value for pure oil (about 1.4–1.7 GPa) [9,39]. The damping of this mode is usually light, and ωh bounds the achievable closed-loop bandwidth, so position loops are commonly designed with crossover well below ωh [39]. Mobile machinery increasingly uses load-sensing and electronically controlled pumps, which supply only the pressure and flow that the loads need [9].

Worked example 6.4

An 80 mm cylinder (Ap = 5.03 × 10⁻³ m²), Vt = 2.0 L, βe = 1.4 GPa and m = 500 kg: ωh = √(4 × 1.4×10⁹ × (5.03×10⁻³)²/(2.0×10⁻³ × 500)) = 376 rad/s (59.9 Hz). If air entrainment halves βe, ωh falls by a factor √2, to 42 Hz.

  1. A run-of-river site has H = 15 m and Q = 200 m³/s. With η = 0.92 and N = 100 rpm, find the power and dimensional specific speed, and choose a turbine type.
    Solution

    P = 0.92 × 1000 × 9.81 × 200 × 15 = 27.1 MW; Ns = 100√27 076/151.25 = 557, so a Kaplan or bulb turbine.

  2. A Pelton nozzle with Cv = 0.97 works under 800 m. Find the jet velocity and the ideal bucket speed.
    Solution

    c1 = 0.97√(2 × 9.81 × 800) = 121.5 m/s; ideal u = c1/2 = 60.8 m/s (Eq. 3.1).

  3. Show that a Pelton wheel with θ = 180° and k = 1 can, in principle, reach ηh = Cv².
    Solution

    With cos θ = −1 and k = 1, Eq. (3.1) gives ηh = 4φ(Cv − φ). Its maximum at φ = Cv/2 is 4(Cv/2)(Cv/2) = Cv². All the jet kinetic energy is then extracted, and only the nozzle loss remains.

  4. An 80 m diameter wind turbine operates at 12 m/s with rotor Cp = 0.42 and drive-train/generator efficiency 0.94 (ρ = 1.225 kg/m³). Find the electrical output and compare it with the Betz limit.
    Solution

  5. A 0.40 m model turbine reaches η = 0.88. Use Moody's formula to estimate the efficiency of a 4.8 m prototype.

Compressors and fans

  1. A reciprocating compressor has 5% clearance and compresses air from 1 to 6 bar; re-expansion follows n = 1.3. Find the volumetric efficiency. What happens at a pressure ratio of 50?
    Solution

    ηv = 1.05 − 0.05 × 61/1.3 = 0.85. At 50: 61/1.3 becomes 501/1.3 = 20.3, so ηv = 1.05 − 1.02 = 0.03. Almost no gas is delivered, which is why high ratios need staging.

  2. A fan delivers 3 m³/s at 500 Pa and absorbs 2.2 kW at 1450 rpm. The speed is reduced to 1160 rpm. Find the new duty.
    Solution

    Ratio 0.80: Q = 2.4 m³/s, Δp = 320 Pa, P = 2.2 × 0.512 = 1.13 kW (Eq. 4.3).

  3. A test compressor has U = 150 m/s, duct Ac = 0.02 m², Lc = 1.0 m, and a plenum of 0.5 m³ (a0 = 340 m/s). Compute Greitzer's B. How large must the plenum be to make B = 2?
    Solution

    ωH = 340√(0.02/0.5) = 68.0 rad/s; B = 150/(2 × 68 × 1.0) = 1.10. Since B ∝ √Vp, B = 2 needs Vp = 0.5 × (2/1.10)² = 1.65 m³.

  4. Explain why a forward-curved centrifugal fan can overload its motor when a duct is disconnected, while a backward-curved fan does not.
    Solution

    With forward-curved blades, cθ2 grows as flow increases, so the Euler work per kilogram rises with flow and the power rises steeply. When system resistance falls, as with an open duct, the flow and power run away. With backward-curved blades, cθ2 falls with flow, so the power curve peaks near the BEP and then falls, a "non-overloading" characteristic [2,19].

Turbomachinery theory

  1. Convert nq = 40 to Ωs and to US Ns.
    Solution

    Ωs = 40/52.9 = 0.757; Ns ≈ 2733 × 0.757 = 2070.

  2. An axial turbine stage has U = 250 m/s, inlet whirl cθ1 = 400 m/s and exit whirl cθ2 = −50 m/s (opposite to U). Find the specific work.
    Solution

    For a turbine, ΔW = U(cθ1 − cθ2) = 250 × (400 + 50) = 112.5 kJ/kg. Swirl against the rotation at exit adds to the work.

  3. Prove that R = 0.5 makes the rotor and stator velocity triangles of a repeating axial stage mirror images.
    Solution

    With constant cx, R = (wθ1 + wθ2)/(2U), because h2 − h1 = (w1² − w2²)/2 in the rotor. Setting R = ½ gives wθ1 + wθ2 = U. Since cθ = U − wθ, it follows that cθ1 = wθ2 and cθ2 = wθ1, so α1 = β2 and α2 = β1 [2].

  4. For the stage of Worked example 5.2, what stage loading ψ just satisfies de Haller (w2/w1 = 0.72), keeping φ = 0.5 and R = 0.5?

8. Trivia questions with answers

  1. Who patented the split-bucket impulse water wheel, and in what year?
    Answer

    Lester A. Pelton, US Patent 233,692, granted in 1880 [10]. ASME designates a collection of early Pelton wheels as a historic mechanical engineering landmark [11].

  2. The Francis turbine is named after an engineer who worked in which American mill town?
    Answer

    Lowell, Massachusetts, where James B. Francis carried out the experiments published as Lowell Hydraulic Experiments (1855) [12].

  3. What is the maximum fraction of the wind's kinetic-energy flux that an ideal open rotor can extract?
    Answer

    16/27 ≈ 59.3%, published by Betz in 1920 [16]. Lanchester and Joukowsky obtained it independently [17].

  4. Which hydroelectric station has the highest installed capacity in the world?
    Answer

    The Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze River in China, about 22.5 GW [40].

  5. Whose name is on the equation Δp = ρaΔV, and where were his experiments carried out?
    Answer

    Nikolai Joukowsky (Zhukovsky), from tests on the Moscow water-supply system at the end of the 1890s, published in German in 1900 [31,32]. He is the same Joukowsky as in the Lanchester–Betz–Joukowsky limit [17].

  6. Korteweg's 1878 wave-speed formula for elastic tubes is also used in which medical field?
    Answer

    Cardiovascular physiology: the Moens–Korteweg equation gives the arterial pulse-wave velocity [33].

  7. Why is an axial-flow pump started with its discharge valve open, unlike a centrifugal pump?
    Answer

    Its power demand is highest at zero flow, so starting against a closed valve would overload the motor [5].

  8. Why do Pelton buckets not turn the jet through the full 180° that maximises the theoretical efficiency?
    Answer

    Water leaving at 180° would strike the back of the following bucket. About 165° is used instead [2].

  9. Do rotating-stall cells move with or against the rotor, as seen from the casing?
    Answer

    With the rotor, but more slowly. Relative to the blades they therefore move backwards [26,27].

  10. In the three-term form of Euler's equation, which term is missing in a purely axial machine?
    Answer

    The centrifugal term (U2² − U1²)/2, because U1 = U2 [2].

  11. What does the quantity 2L/a represent in a pipeline?
    Answer

    The pipe period: the round-trip time of a pressure wave between the disturbance and the reflecting reservoir. A closure faster than this produces the full Joukowsky rise [34].

  12. According to Denton, what single thermodynamic quantity is the proper measure of loss in a turbomachine?
    Answer

    Entropy generation [30].

  13. What is a "power input factor"?
    Answer

    A multiplier (typically about 1.04) on centrifugal-compressor Euler work that accounts for disc friction and windage. That work heats the gas without raising its pressure [20].

  14. Which non-dimensional parameter decides whether a compression system surges or settles into rotating stall?
    Answer

    Greitzer's B parameter, Eq. (4.4) [24,25].

similarity.py: affinity laws and specific speed

"""Pump/fan similarity (affinity) laws and specific speed."""
import math
g = 9.81

def affinity(Q1, H1, P1, N1, N2, D1=1.0, D2=1.0):
    """Scale a duty point to a new speed N2 and/or diameter D2
    (geometrically similar machine, same flow coefficient)."""
    n, d = N2/N1, D2/D1
    return Q1*n*d**3, H1*n**2*d**2, P1*n**3*d**5

def specific_speed(N_rpm, Q, H):
    """Dimensionless (Omega_s, radians) and European n_q (rpm, m3/s, m)."""
    omega = N_rpm*2*math.pi/60
    Omega_s = omega*math.sqrt(Q)/(g*H)**0.75
    n_q = N_rpm*math.sqrt(Q)/H**0.75
    return Omega_s, n_q

if __name__ == "__main__":
    Q, H, P = affinity(0.050, 30.0, 18.0, 1450, 1750)
    print(f"At 1750 rpm: Q = {Q:.4f} m3/s, H = {H:.1f} m, P = {P:.1f} kW")
    Om, nq = specific_speed(1450, 0.050, 30.0)
    print(f"Omega_s = {Om:.3f}, n_q = {nq:.1f}  -> radial-flow impeller")
Output

triangles.py: centrifugal impeller with slip

"""Outlet velocity triangle of a centrifugal impeller with Wiesner slip."""
import math
g = 9.81

def impeller(D2, b2, N_rpm, beta2_deg, Q, Z, eta_h=1.0):
    u2 = math.pi*D2*N_rpm/60                 # blade speed
    cm2 = Q/(math.pi*D2*b2)                  # meridional (radial) velocity
    beta2 = math.radians(beta2_deg)          # blade angle from tangent
    cu2_ideal = u2 - cm2/math.tan(beta2)     # infinite blades
    sigma = 1 - math.sqrt(math.sin(beta2))/Z**0.7   # Wiesner (1967)
    cu2 = sigma*u2 - cm2/math.tan(beta2)     # with slip
    H_inf = u2*cu2_ideal/g                   # Euler head, no prerotation
    H_slip = u2*cu2/g

"""Operating point of a pump on a system curve (bisection, no libraries)."""
def pump(Q):   return 60.0 - 8000.0*Q**2      # head, m
def system(Q): return 20.0 + 6000.0*Q**2      # static lift + friction, m

def operating_point(f=lambda q: pump(q) - system(q), lo=0.0, hi=0.2, tol=1e-9):
    while hi - lo > tol:
        mid = 0.5*(lo + hi)
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if f(lo)*f(mid) > 0 else (lo, mid)
    return 0.5*(lo + hi)

pelton.py: Pelton wheel efficiency

"""Pelton wheel: power and hydraulic efficiency from the bucket velocity triangles."""
import math
g, rho = 9.81, 1000.0

def pelton(H, Q, Cv=0.98, phi=0.46, theta_deg=165.0, k=0.90):
    """phi = u / sqrt(2gH); k = relative-velocity ratio W2/W1 (bucket friction)."""
    c1 = Cv*math.sqrt(2*g*H)                 # jet velocity
    u = phi*math.sqrt(2*g*H)                 # bucket speed
    W1 = c1 - u
    dcu = W1*(1 - k*math.cos(math.radians(theta_deg)))
    P = rho*Q*u*dcu                          # Euler: P = m_dot * u * delta(c_u)
    return c1, u, P, P/(rho*g*Q*H)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    c1, u, P, eta = pelton(H=500, Q=2.0)
    print(f"c1 = {c1:.1f} m/s, u = {u:.1f} m/s, P = {P/1e6:.2f} MW, eta_h = {eta:.3f}")
    best = max((pelton(500, 2.0, phi=p/1000)[3], p/1000) for p in range(300, 600))
    print(f"best eta_h = {best[0]:.3f} at u/sqrt(2gH) = {best[1]:.3f}")

axial_stage.py: repeating axial compressor stage

"""Repeating axial compressor stage from (phi, psi, R)."""
import math

def stage(U, phi, psi, R, solidity):
    cx = phi*U
    # R = 1 - (cu1 + cu2)/(2U) and psi = (cu2 - cu1)/U
    cu1 = U*(1 - R - psi/2); cu2 = cu1 + psi*U
    wu1, wu2 = U - cu1, U - cu2
    W1, W2 = math.hypot(cx, wu1), math.hypot(cx, wu2)
    DF = 1 - W2/W1 + abs(wu1 - wu2)/(2*solidity*W1)   # Lieblein rotor diffusion factor
    beta = [math.degrees(math.atan(w/cx)) for w in (wu1, wu2)]
    return dict(cx=cx, cu1=cu1, cu2=cu2, beta1=beta[0], beta2=beta[1],
                deHaller=W2/W1, DF=DF, dh0=psi*U**2)

if __name__ == "__main__":

    PR = (1 + eta*s["dh0"]/(cp*T01))**3.5
    print(f"stage pressure ratio = {PR:.3f}")

moc.py: water hammer by the method of characteristics

"""Water hammer by the Method of Characteristics (Wylie & Streeter form).
Reservoir (x=0) -> single pipe -> valve (x=L), valve closes linearly in tc seconds."""
import math

def water_hammer(L=1200.0, D=0.5, a=1191.0, f=0.018, H0=300.0, V0=2.0,
                 tc=0.0, t_end=12.0, N=20, g=9.81):
    A = math.pi*D**2/4
    Q0 = V0*A
    dx = L/N; dt = dx/a                    # Courant number = 1
    B = a/(g*A)                            # characteristic impedance
    R = f*dx/(2*g*D*A**2)                  # friction coefficient
    # steady state: head drops linearly with friction
    H = [H0 - R*Q0*abs(Q0)*i for i in range(N+1)]
    Q = [Q0]*(N+1)
    Hv0 = H[-1]                            # steady head at the valve
    Cv = Q0**2/(2*Hv0)                     # valve: Q = tau*sqrt(2*Cv*H)
    t, out = 0.0, [(0.0, H[-1])]
    while t < t_end:
        t += dt
        Hn, Qn = H[:], Q[:]
        for i in range(1, N):              # interior nodes
            Cp = H[i-1] + B*Q[i-1] - R*Q[i-1]*abs(Q[i-1])
            Cm = H[i+1] - B*Q[i+1] + R*Q[i+1]*abs(Q[i+1])
            Hn[i] = 0.5*(Cp + Cm); Qn[i] = (Cp - Hn[i])/B
        # upstream reservoir: H fixed
        Cm = H[1] - B*Q[1] + R*Q[1]*abs(Q[1])
        Hn[0] = H0; Qn[0] = (H0 - Cm)/B
        # downstream valve
        tau = max(0.0, 1.0 - t/tc) if tc > 0 else 0.0
        Cp = H[N-1] + B*Q[N-1] - R*Q[N-1]*abs(Q[N-1])
        Cvt = (tau*Q0)**2/(2*Hv0)
        if Cvt == 0.0:
            Qn[N] = 0.0
        else:                              # solve Q^2 + 2*Cvt*B*Q - 2*Cvt*Cp = 0
            Qn[N] = -B*Cvt + math.sqrt((B*Cvt)**2 + 2*Cvt*Cp)
        Hn[N] = Cp - B*Qn[N]
        H, Q = Hn, Qn
        out.append((t, H[N]))
    return out

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for tc in (0.0, 1.0, 6.0):
        res = water_hammer(tc=tc)
        hmax = max(h for _, h in res)
        print(f"closure {tc:4.1f} s : max valve head = {hmax:6.1f} m")

surge_tank.py: surge-tank mass oscillation

"""Mass oscillation in a simple surge tank after full load rejection.
State: tunnel velocity V and tank level z (positive up from reservoir level)."""
import math
g = 9.81

def surge(L=2000.0, At=math.pi*4**2/4, As=math.pi*12**2/4, V0=2.5,
          hf0=3.0, t_end=600.0, dt=0.05):
    c = hf0/V0**2                         # tunnel loss coefficient, hf = c V|V|
    V, z = V0, -hf0                       # steady state before rejection
    def rhs(V, z):                        # turbine flow = 0 after t = 0
        return (-g*z/L - g*c*V*abs(V)/L,  # momentum in tunnel
                V*At/As)                  # continuity in tank
    t, zmax = 0.0, z
    while t < t_end:                      # classical RK4
        k1 = rhs(V, z)
        k2 = rhs(V + dt/2*k1[0], z + dt/2*k1[1])
        k3 = rhs(V + dt/2*k2[0], z + dt/2*k2[1])
        k4 = rhs(V + dt*k3[0], z + dt*k3[1])
        V += dt/6*(k1[0] + 2*k2[0] + 2*k3[0] + k4[0])
        z += dt/6*(k1[1] + 2*k2[1] + 2*k3[1] + k4[1])
        t += dt; zmax = max(zmax, z)
    return zmax

if __name__ == "__main__":
    L, At, As, V0 = 2000.0, math.pi*4**2/4, math.pi*12**2/4, 2.5
    print(f"frictionless estimate  Zmax = {V0*math.sqrt(L*At/(g*As)):.2f} m")
    print(f"period T = {2*math.pi*math.sqrt(L*As/(g*At)):.0f} s")
    print(f"with tunnel friction   Zmax = {surge():.2f} m")
