1. What is a porous medium?

A porous medium is a solid matrix (grains, fibres or a fractured rock frame) permeated by an interconnected network of voids through which a fluid can flow. Examples include aquifer sands and gravels, sandstone and carbonate reservoirs, soils, bone, paper, catalyst beds and filter cakes. The pore geometry is far too complex to resolve in most engineering problems, so porous-media mechanics replaces the true pore-scale velocity field with averaged quantities defined over a representative elementary volume (REV): a volume large compared with individual pores but small compared with the scale over which averaged properties change [1]. Averaging the Stokes equations over such a volume is one rigorous route to Darcy's law [2].

2. Porosity

Porosity is the fraction of the bulk volume occupied by voids:

\[ \phi = \frac{V_v}{V_T}, \qquad 0 \le \phi < 1. \]

Only interconnected pores conduct flow, so hydrogeologists distinguish the effective porosity \(n_e\) (connected pores that transmit fluid) from total porosity [3,4]. In an unconfined aquifer the volume of water that drains by gravity per unit area per unit decline in the water table is the specific yield \(S_y\); the water held back against gravity by surface tension is the specific retention \(S_r\), and \(\phi = S_y + S_r\) [5].

Second, porosity of an ideal packing of equal spheres does not depend on the sphere size: it is about 0.476 for simple cubic packing and 0.260 for the densest (rhombohedral) packing [1]. Poorly sorted sediments have lower porosity because small grains fill the gaps between large ones [4].

Worked example 1 · Porosity from a core

A dry core of bulk volume 100 cm³ has a mass of 212 g. The grain (mineral) density is 2.65 g/cm³ (quartz). Find φ.

Solution. Solid volume \(V_s = 212/2.65 = 80.0\) cm³, so \(V_v = 20.0\) cm³ and \(\phi = 0.20\). Equivalently \(\phi = 1-\rho_b/\rho_s = 1 - 2.12/2.65 = 0.20\).

3. Darcy's law

3.1 The experiment

In 1856 the French engineer Henry Darcy published the results of experiments, carried out in Dijon with Charles Ritter, on water flowing downward through vertical columns of sand. They appeared in an appendix (Note D) of his report on the design of the city's public fountains and water supply [6,7]. He found that the discharge \(Q\) was proportional to the cross-sectional area \(A\) and to the difference in manometer heads \(\Delta h\), and inversely proportional to the column length \(L\):

\[ Q = K A \frac{h_1 - h_2}{L}. \]

3.2 Modern forms

The hydraulic head \(h = z + p/(\rho g)\) is the mechanical energy per unit weight of water (velocity head is negligible in porous media). Hubbert showed that the correct driving potential is the fluid potential \(\Phi = gz + p/\rho\), of which head is the per-unit-weight version, and that Darcy's \(K\) combines a property of the medium with properties of the fluid [8]. In three dimensions, for a homogeneous fluid,

\[ \mathbf{q} = -\mathbf{K}\,\nabla h \qquad\text{or}\qquad \mathbf{q} = -\frac{\mathbf{k}}{\mu}\left(\nabla p - \rho\,\mathbf{g}\right), \]

where \(\mathbf{q}\) is the specific discharge or Darcy flux (volume per unit total area per unit time, m/s), \(\mathbf{K}\) the hydraulic-conductivity tensor, \(\mathbf{k}\) the intrinsic-permeability tensor, \(\mu\) the dynamic viscosity and \(\mathbf{g}\) the gravity vector [1].

Key distinction · flux vs. velocity

\(q\) is a flux, not the speed of water particles. Because water only moves through the connected pores, the average linear (seepage) velocity is

\[ v = \frac{q}{n_e}, \]

which is always larger than \(q\). Use \(v\) for travel times of water and dissolved contaminants [3,4].

3.3 Range of validity

Darcy's law is a linear, creeping-flow (Stokes) law. It holds when viscous forces dominate inertial forces in the pores. With the Reynolds number defined as \(Re = \rho q d/\mu\), where \(d\) is a representative grain diameter, experiments show Darcy's law is valid up to \(Re\) somewhere between about 1 and 10 [1]. Nearly all natural groundwater flow satisfies this, though flow close to pumping wells, in coarse gravels, in fractures and in karst can exceed it [3]. Darcy's law can also fail at the other extreme: in very tight clays, a threshold gradient may be needed before flow begins, and in gas flow through tight rocks slip at pore walls (the Klinkenberg effect) raises the apparent permeability [1].

Whitaker derived Darcy's law by volume-averaging the Stokes equations, showing that \(\mathbf{k}\) emerges as a property of pore geometry alone [2].

Worked example 2 · A Darcy column

A sand column with \(A = 0.01\) m² and \(L = 0.5\) m has \(K = 1\times10^{-4}\) m/s. The head drops by 0.20 m across it, and \(n_e = 0.35\). Find \(q\), \(Q\) and \(v\).

Solution. Gradient \(i = 0.20/0.5 = 0.4\). \(q = Ki = 4.0\times10^{-5}\) m/s. \(Q = qA = 4.0\times10^{-7}\) m³/s (0.4 mL/s, about 35 L/day). \(v = q/n_e = 1.14\times10^{-4}\) m/s, so water crosses the column in \(L/v \approx 73\) min.

Is Darcy valid? With \(d = 0.5\) mm, \(Re = 998\times4\times10^{-5}\times5\times10^{-4}/1.0\times10^{-3} \approx 0.02 \ll 1\). Yes.

4. Permeability

Intrinsic permeability \(k\) (m²) measures the ability of the medium alone to transmit fluid, independently of the fluid. It scales with the square of a characteristic pore size [1]. The petroleum industry uses the darcy (D): a medium has a permeability of 1 D if a fluid of viscosity 1 cP flows at 1 cm³/s through 1 cm² under a gradient of 1 atm/cm. In SI, \(1\ \text{D} = 9.869233\times10^{-13}\ \text{m}^2 \approx 1\ \mu\text{m}^2\) [9]. Reservoir rocks commonly lie between about 1 mD and a few darcy [9].

4.1 Anisotropy

In general \(\mathbf{k}\) is a symmetric second-order tensor. In layered sediments the principal directions usually align with bedding, and the horizontal permeability often exceeds the vertical permeability by a factor of 2 to 10 or more in clean sediments, and by much more where clay layers are present [3]. Section 5.2 shows how layering alone produces this.

4.2 Kozeny–Carman equation

Kozeny modelled the pore space as a bundle of tortuous capillaries [10]; Carman calibrated the model for packed beds [11]. For a bed of spheres of diameter \(d\),

\[ k = \frac{d^2\,\phi^3}{180\,(1-\phi)^2}. \]

The constant 180 comes from Carman's fit; Ergun's viscous term (Section 6) is the same expression with 150 instead [12]. The strong \(\phi^3/(1-\phi)^2\) dependence explains why small changes in porosity, for example from compaction or cementation, change permeability a lot.

4.3 Empirical grain-size formulas

For clean, uniform sands, Hazen proposed \(K\,[\text{cm/s}] \approx C\,d_{10}^2\), with \(d_{10}\) (cm) the grain size than which 10 % of the sample by mass is finer and \(C\) roughly 100 (range about 40–150) [4,13]. Such formulas are screening tools; they do not replace permeameter or field tests.

Worked example 3 · Kozeny–Carman for a medium sand

Estimate \(k\) and \(K\) (water, 20 °C) for spheres with \(d = 0.5\) mm and \(\phi = 0.35\).

Solution. \(k = (5\times10^{-4})^2 (0.35)^3 / [180(0.65)^2] = 1.41\times10^{-10}\) m² \(\approx 143\) D. With \(\rho = 998.2\) kg/m³ and \(\mu = 1.002\times10^{-3}\) Pa·s [14], \(K = k\rho g/\mu = 1.38\times10^{-3}\) m/s. If compaction lowered φ to 0.30, \(k\) would fall by a factor of 1.84.

5. Hydraulic conductivity

Hydraulic conductivity \(K\) (m/s) is the coefficient in Darcy's original law. It depends on both the medium and the fluid [3,8]:

\[ K = \frac{k\,\rho\, g}{\mu}. \]

For water at 20 °C, \(\rho g/\mu \approx 9.77\times10^{6}\ \text{m}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}\), so a permeability of 1 darcy corresponds to \(K \approx 9.6\times10^{-6}\) m/s, or roughly \(10^{-5}\) m/s. Because water's viscosity falls as temperature rises (1.306 mPa·s at 10 °C, 1.002 mPa·s at 20 °C) [14], the same aquifer conducts about 30 % more water at 20 °C than at 10 °C.

5.1 Transmissivity and storage

For an aquifer of saturated thickness \(b\), the transmissivity is \(T = Kb\) (m²/s): the rate at which water flows through a strip of aquifer one metre wide under unit gradient [5]. The specific storage \(S_s = \rho g(\alpha + n\beta)\) (m⁻¹) is the volume released per unit volume of aquifer per unit head decline, through compression of the matrix (compressibility α) and expansion of water (compressibility β) [3]. The storativity of a confined aquifer is \(S = S_s b\), typically \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-3}\), while the specific yield of unconfined aquifers is typically 0.01 to 0.30 [3,5]. That contrast of two to four orders of magnitude is why pumping effects spread much faster and farther in confined aquifers.

5.2 Layered media: effective conductivity

6. The Darcy–Forchheimer equation

As velocity increases, inertial losses from repeated acceleration and deceleration of fluid through pore throats add to viscous drag. Forchheimer proposed adding a term quadratic in velocity [15]:

\[ -\nabla p = \frac{\mu}{k}\,\mathbf{q} + \beta\,\rho\,|\mathbf{q}|\,\mathbf{q}, \]

where \(\beta\) (m⁻¹) is the non-Darcy or Forchheimer coefficient. The departure from Darcy's law is not turbulence: it begins at Reynolds numbers well below those at which turbulence appears in pores, and is due to inertial effects in laminar flow [1,16]. Ergun's correlation for packed beds of spheres gives both coefficients from grain size and porosity [12]:

\[ -\frac{\Delta p}{L} = \underbrace{150\,\frac{\mu(1-\phi)^2}{\phi^3 d^2}}_{a}\,q \;+\; \underbrace{1.75\,\frac{\rho(1-\phi)}{\phi^3 d}}_{b}\,q^2 . \]

The ratio of inertial to viscous resistance is the Forchheimer number \(Fo = k\beta\rho q/\mu = bq/a\). Zeng and Grigg proposed \(Fo = 0.11\) as a practical criterion for the onset of non-Darcy flow, corresponding to a 10 % non-Darcy contribution to the pressure gradient [17]. Non-Darcy effects matter near high-rate gas wells, in packed-bed reactors and filters, in coarse rock fill and gabions, and in karst conduits [16]. A related extension, the Brinkman equation, adds a viscous-diffusion term \(\tilde\mu\nabla^2\mathbf{q}\) so that no-slip walls and free-fluid interfaces can be handled [16,18].

Worked example 5 · When Darcy fails

For the 5 mm gravel above at 20 °C, \(a = 4.21\times10^{4}\) Pa·s/m² and \(b = 3.95\times10^{6}\) Pa·s²/m³. At \(|\nabla p| = 1\) kPa/m, solving \(bq^2 + aq - 1000 = 0\) gives \(q = 1.15\times10^{-2}\) m/s. Darcy's law alone would give \(1000/a = 2.37\times10^{-2}\) m/s, an overestimate by a factor of 2.07. Here \(Fo = bq/a = 1.07\) and \(Re = \rho q d/\mu \approx 57\), well beyond Darcy's range.

7. Groundwater flow

Groundwater makes up about 30 % of Earth's fresh water; most of the rest is locked in ice caps and glaciers, with lakes and rivers holding only a small fraction [19]. Aquifers are geological units that store and transmit useful quantities of water; aquitards transmit water only slowly [5].

7.1 The governing equation

Combining Darcy's law with conservation of mass for slightly compressible water and matrix gives the groundwater-flow equation [1,3]:

\[ \nabla\cdot(\mathbf{K}\,\nabla h) + W = S_s\,\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}, \]

with \(W\) a volumetric source per unit volume (positive for recharge, negative for pumping). For steady flow in a homogeneous, isotropic aquifer without sources it reduces to Laplace's equation \(\nabla^2 h = 0\), the same equation as steady heat conduction and electrostatics. For a confined aquifer integrated over thickness, \(\nabla\cdot(T\nabla h) + R = S\,\partial h/\partial t\). The U.S. Geological Survey's MODFLOW, the most widely used groundwater code, solves a finite-difference form of this equation [20].

7.2 Unconfined flow: the Dupuit–Forchheimer approximation

When the water table slopes gently, flow is nearly horizontal and the head is nearly constant on each vertical line. Dupuit used this to write the discharge per unit width as \(q' = -Kh\,\mathrm{d}h/\mathrm{d}x\) [21]. With uniform recharge \(W\) (m/s) between two water bodies with heads \(h_0\) at \(x=0\) and \(h_L\) at \(x=L\),

\[ h^2(x) = h_0^2 - \frac{h_0^2 - h_L^2}{L}\,x + \frac{W}{K}\,x(L-x). \]

7.3 Transient radial flow to a well: the Theis solution

For a well pumping at constant rate \(Q\) from an infinite, homogeneous, confined aquifer, Theis obtained, by analogy with heat conduction [22]:

\[ s(r,t) = \frac{Q}{4\pi T}\,W(u), \qquad u = \frac{r^2 S}{4Tt}, \qquad W(u) = \int_u^\infty \frac{e^{-y}}{y}\,dy, \]

where \(s\) is drawdown and \(W(u)\) the well function (the exponential integral \(E_1\)). For small \(u\) (below about 0.01), \(W(u) \approx -0.5772 - \ln u\); this is the Cooper–Jacob straight-line method, which lets \(T\) and \(S\) be read from a semi-log plot of drawdown against time [23].

Worked example 6 · Drawdown after one day

\(Q = 0.01\) m³/s, \(T = 10^{-3}\) m²/s, \(S = 10^{-4}\), \(r = 50\) m, \(t = 1\) day. Then \(u = (50^2\times10^{-4})/(4\times10^{-3}\times86\,400) = 7.23\times10^{-4}\), \(W(u) = 6.655\), so \(s = 0.01/(4\pi\times10^{-3})\times6.655 = 5.30\) m. Cooper–Jacob gives 5.30 m as well, since \(u \ll 0.01\).

7.4 Numerical solution in 2-D

Real aquifers have irregular boundaries and heterogeneity, so the flow equation is solved numerically. The five-point finite-difference stencil for steady confined flow on a square grid of spacing \(\Delta x\) is

\[ h_{i,j} = \tfrac14\left(h_{i+1,j} + h_{i-1,j} + h_{i,j+1} + h_{i,j-1} - \frac{Q_{i,j}}{T}\right), \]

with \(Q_{i,j}\) the pumping rate assigned to the cell. MODFLOW uses the same block-centred idea in three dimensions [20]. The code in Section 9 solves it with successive over-relaxation.

Why it matters

Pumping that exceeds recharge causes long-term depletion. The USGS estimated that U.S. groundwater storage declined by about 1,000 km³ between 1900 and 2008 [24]. Groundwater pumping also captures water that would otherwise discharge to streams and wetlands, which is why sustainable yield cannot be judged from recharge alone [25].

8. Multiphase porous-media flow

When two or more immiscible fluids share the pore space (water and air in soils; oil, water and gas in reservoirs; CO₂ and brine in carbon storage; NAPL contaminants in aquifers), each fluid occupies a fraction of the pores and interfaces between them carry capillary pressure [1,9].

8.1 Saturation, wettability and capillary pressure

The saturation \(S_\alpha\) is the fraction of pore volume filled by phase α, and \(\sum_\alpha S_\alpha = 1\). The fluid that preferentially spreads on the solid is the wetting phase. Across a curved interface in a pore of radius \(r\), the Young–Laplace equation gives the capillary pressure [1,9]:

\[ p_c = p_{nw} - p_w = \frac{2\sigma\cos\theta}{r}, \]

with σ the interfacial tension and θ the contact angle measured through the wetting phase. Small pores hold the wetting phase most strongly, so \(p_c\) rises as wetting saturation falls. Displacing a wetting phase with a non-wetting one is drainage; the reverse is imbibition. The two processes follow different \(p_c(S_w)\) curves (hysteresis), and some non-wetting fluid is left trapped as disconnected ganglia after imbibition, giving a residual saturation [9].

Leverett showed that capillary-pressure curves for rocks of different permeability collapse approximately onto a single dimensionless curve, the J-function \(J(S_w) = \dfrac{p_c}{\sigma\cos\theta}\sqrt{k/\phi}\), because \(\sqrt{k/\phi}\) is a characteristic pore radius [26].

8.2 Extended Darcy's law and relative permeability

Each phase is assumed to obey its own Darcy law, with its flow area reduced by the presence of the other phases [9,27]:

\[ \mathbf{q}_\alpha = -\frac{k\,k_{r\alpha}(S_\alpha)}{\mu_\alpha}\left(\nabla p_\alpha - \rho_\alpha\mathbf{g}\right), \qquad 0\le k_{r\alpha}\le 1. \]

The relative permeabilities \(k_{r\alpha}\) are strongly non-linear: the sum \(k_{rw}+k_{ro}\) is typically well below 1 at intermediate saturation because each phase blocks the other's paths. Common parameterizations include Brooks–Corey [28], with \(p_c = p_d S_e^{-1/\lambda}\), and van Genuchten [29], with \(S_e = [1+(\alpha h_c)^n]^{-m}\), which combined with Mualem's pore-size model [30] gives a closed-form unsaturated conductivity widely used in soil physics.

8.3 Unsaturated flow: the Richards equation

In the vadose zone the air phase is usually taken to be at atmospheric pressure and continuous, so only the water equation is solved. Richards combined Darcy's law (with \(K\) depending on water content or pressure head ψ) and mass conservation [31]:

\[ \frac{\partial\theta_w}{\partial t} = \nabla\cdot\left[K(\psi)\,\nabla(\psi + z)\right]. \]

The strong non-linearity of \(K(\psi)\), which can fall by several orders of magnitude as soil dries [29], makes infiltration fronts sharp and the equation notoriously hard to solve numerically.

8.4 Immiscible displacement: Buckley–Leverett theory

For 1-D horizontal displacement of oil by water, with incompressible fluids and capillary pressure neglected, the water fractional flow is

\[ f_w(S_w) = \frac{q_w}{q_w+q_o} = \frac{k_{rw}/\mu_w}{k_{rw}/\mu_w + k_{ro}/\mu_o}, \]

and mass conservation gives the hyperbolic equation [32]

\[ \phi\,\frac{\partial S_w}{\partial t} + q\,\frac{\partial f_w}{\partial x} = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \left.\frac{dx}{dt}\right|_{S_w} = \frac{q}{\phi}\,\frac{df_w}{dS_w}. \]

Because \(f_w\) is S-shaped, this construction gives a multivalued profile; physically, a sharp shock front forms. Welge showed that the front saturation \(S_{wf}\) is located where a line from \((S_{wc}, 0)\) is tangent to \(f_w\), and that the tangent's intersection with \(f_w=1\) gives the average saturation behind the front at breakthrough [33].

Worked example 7 · Waterflood breakthrough

For the parameters in Figure 11 the tangent point is \(S_{wf} = 0.554\), \(f_w(S_{wf}) = 0.795\), with slope \(df_w/dS_w = 2.246\). 8.5 Instabilities and flow regimes

When a less viscous fluid displaces a more viscous one, the interface is unstable and viscous fingers form; Saffman and Taylor analysed this instability in a Hele-Shaw cell, the classic analogue for porous media [34]. At the pore scale, Lenormand and co-workers mapped drainage into three regimes, viscous fingering, capillary fingering and stable displacement, controlled by the capillary number and the viscosity ratio [35]. These regimes govern sweep efficiency in oil recovery, CO₂ plume shape in geological storage and the spread of contaminant solvents in aquifers [9].

9. Source code

The module below implements everything used in this article with NumPy only. It was run to produce every number in the worked examples and every plot in Figures 4, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11 and 12. Save it as porous_media.py.

"""porous_media.py - small, dependency-light (NumPy only) toolkit for porous-media flow.
Units: SI throughout (m, s, kg, Pa)."""
import numpy as np

G = 9.80665          # m/s^2
DARCY = 9.869233e-13 # m^2 per darcy

# ---------- 1. Darcy's law ----------
def darcy_flux(K, dh, L):
    """Specific discharge q = -K dh/dL (m/s). dh = h_out - h_in."""
    return -K * dh / L

def seepage_velocity(q, n_e):
    """Average linear (pore) velocity v = q / n_e."""
    return q / n_e

def K_from_k(k, rho=998.2, mu=1.002e-3, g=G):
    """Hydraulic conductivity from intrinsic permeability: K = k rho g / mu."""
    return k * rho * g / mu

def k_from_K(K, rho=998.2, mu=1.002e-3, g=G):
    return K * mu / (rho * g)

def reynolds_pore(q, d, rho=998.2, mu=1.002e-3):
    """Grain-diameter Reynolds number Re = rho q d / mu (Bear's definition)."""
    return rho * q * d / mu

# ---------- 2. Kozeny-Carman ----------
def kozeny_carman(d, phi, C=180.0):
    """k = d^2 phi^3 / (C (1-phi)^2)  (spheres of diameter d; C=180 Carman form)."""
    return d**2 * phi**3 / (C * (1 - phi)**2)

# ---------- 3. Forchheimer / Ergun ----------
def ergun_coeffs(d, phi, rho=998.2, mu=1.002e-3):
    """Return (a, b) in -dp/dx = a q + b q^2 from the Ergun correlation."""
    a = 150 * mu * (1 - phi)**2 / (phi**3 * d**2)
    b = 1.75 * rho * (1 - phi) / (phi**3 * d)
    return a, b

def forchheimer_q(grad_p, a, b):
    """Solve b q^2 + a q - |grad p| = 0 for q >= 0."""
    G_ = abs(grad_p)
    return (-a + np.sqrt(a*a + 4*b*G_)) / (2*b)

# ---------- 4. Groundwater ----------
def dupuit_unconfined(x, L, h0, hL, K, W=0.0):
    """Steady 1-D unconfined flow with uniform recharge W (Dupuit-Forchheimer):
    h^2 = h0^2 - (h0^2-hL^2) x/L + (W/K) x (L-x)."""
    return np.sqrt(h0**2 - (h0**2 - hL**2) * x / L + (W / K) * x * (L - x))

def solve_head_1d(K, dx, h_left, h_right, W=None, b=1.0):
    """Finite-difference steady confined 1-D flow d/dx(T dh/dx) + W = 0 with
    heterogeneous K (array of cell values, cell-centred, Dirichlet ends)."""
    K = np.asarray(K, float); n = K.size
    T = K * b
    Tf = 2 * T[:-1] * T[1:] / (T[:-1] + T[1:])     # harmonic mean at faces
    A = np.zeros((n, n)); r = np.zeros(n) if W is None else -np.asarray(W, float) * dx**2
    for i in range(n):
        if i == 0 or i == n - 1:
            A[i, i] = 1.0; r[i] = h_left if i == 0 else h_right; continue
        A[i, i-1] = Tf[i-1]; A[i, i+1] = Tf[i]; A[i, i] = -(Tf[i-1] + Tf[i])
    return np.linalg.solve(A, r)

def solve_head_2d(nx, ny, dx, T, h_bc, wells=(), tol=1e-8, maxit=20000, omega=1.8):
    """Steady 2-D confined flow, homogeneous T (m^2/s), SOR iteration.
    h_bc: dict with 'left','right' fixed heads; top/bottom no-flow.
    wells: iterable of (i, j, Q) with Q>0 = pumping (m^3/s)."""
    h = np.full((ny, nx), 0.5 * (h_bc['left'] + h_bc['right']))
    h[:, 0] = h_bc['left']; h[:, -1] = h_bc['right']
    src = np.zeros((ny, nx))
    for (i, j, Q) in wells:
        src[j, i] -= Q / (dx * dx)            # sink per unit area
    for it in range(maxit):
        err = 0.0
        for j in range(ny):
            jn = j - 1 if j > 0 else 1        # mirror -> no-flow
            js = j + 1 if j < ny - 1 else ny - 2
            for i in range(1, nx - 1):
                new = 0.25 * (h[jn, i] + h[js, i] + h[j, i-1] + h[j, i+1] + src[j, i] * dx * dx / T)
                d = omega * (new - h[j, i]); h[j, i] += d; err = max(err, abs(d))
        if err < tol:
            break
    return h, it

def theis_W(u, terms=60):
    """Well function W(u) = E1(u) via series (u<~5) or continued fraction."""
    u = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(u, float)); out = np.empty_like(u)
    for k, x in enumerate(u):
        if x < 1.0:
            s, term = 0.0, 1.0
            for n in range(1, terms):
                term *= -x / n
                s -= term / n
            out[k] = -0.5772156649 - np.log(x) + s
        else:                                   # Lentz continued fraction
            b = x + 1.0; c = 1e300; d = 1.0 / b; hcf = d
            for i in range(1, terms):
                an = -i * i; b += 2.0
                d = 1.0 / (an * d + b); c = b + an / c; de = c * d; hcf *= de
                if abs(de - 1) < 1e-15: break
            out[k] = hcf * np.exp(-x)
    return out

def theis_drawdown(Q, T, S, r, t):
    u = r**2 * S / (4 * T * t)
    return Q / (4 * np.pi * T) * theis_W(u)

# ---------- 5. Buckley-Leverett ----------
def corey_relperm(Sw, Swc=0.2, Sor=0.2, krw0=0.3, kro0=0.8, nw=2.0, no=2.0):
    Se = np.clip((Sw - Swc) / (1 - Swc - Sor), 0, 1)
    return krw0 * Se**nw, kro0 * (1 - Se)**no

def frac_flow(Sw, mu_w=1e-3, mu_o=5e-3, **kw):
    krw, kro = corey_relperm(Sw, **kw)
    lw, lo = krw / mu_w, kro / mu_o
    return lw / (lw + lo + 1e-300)

def welge_front(Swc=0.2, Sor=0.2, n=20001, **kw):
    """Shock saturation from Welge tangent: f(Sf)/(Sf-Swc) = f'(Sf)."""
    S = np.linspace(Swc + 1e-6, 1 - Sor, n)
    f = frac_flow(S, Swc=Swc, Sor=Sor, **kw)
    slope = f / (S - Swc)
    i = np.argmax(slope)
    return S[i], f[i], slope[i]

def bl_profile(xD_t, Swc=0.2, Sor=0.2, **kw):
    """Saturation vs xD at dimensionless time tD (pore volumes injected)."""
    Sf, ff, sf = welge_front(Swc, Sor, **kw)
    S = np.linspace(1 - Sor, Sf, 400)
    dS = 1e-6
    dfdS = (frac_flow(S + dS, Swc=Swc, Sor=Sor, **kw) - frac_flow(S - dS, Swc=Swc, Sor=Sor, **kw)) / (2 * dS)
    return S, dfdS * xD_t, Sf, sf

10. Exercises with solutions

Use water at 20 °C (\(\rho = 998.2\) kg/m³, \(\mu = 1.002\times10^{-3}\) Pa·s, σ = 0.0728 N/m) unless stated [14,36].

  1. Constant-head permeameter. A sample of length 0.30 m and area 0.002 m² passes 250 mL in 120 s under a constant head difference of 0.15 m. Find \(K\) and \(k\).
    Solution

    \(Q = 2.083\times10^{-6}\) m³/s. \(K = QL/(A\Delta h) = 2.083\times10^{-6}\times0.30/(0.002\times0.15) = 2.08\times10^{-3}\) m/s (coarse sand). \(k = K\mu/(\rho g) = 2.13\times10^{-10}\) m² ≈ 216 D.

  2. Falling-head permeameter. Standpipe area \(a = 1\times10^{-4}\) m², sample area \(A = 2\times10^{-3}\) m², length 0.20 m. The head falls from 1.0 m to 0.5 m in 600 s. Find \(K\).
    Solution

    \(K = \dfrac{aL}{At}\ln\dfrac{h_1}{h_2} = \dfrac{10^{-4}\times0.2}{2\times10^{-3}\times600}\ln 2 = 1.16\times10^{-5}\) m/s (fine or silty sand).

  3. Layered aquifer. Three horizontal layers: 2 m with \(K = 10^{-4}\) m/s, 1 m with \(K = 10^{-7}\) m/s, 5 m with \(K = 10^{-5}\) m/s. Find \(K_h\), \(K_v\) and the anisotropy ratio.
    Solution

    \(K_h = (2\times10^{-4} + 10^{-7} + 5\times10^{-5})/8 = 3.13\times10^{-5}\) m/s. \(K_v = 8/(2\times10^{4} + 10^{7} + 5\times10^{5}) = 7.60\times10^{-7}\) m/s. \(K_h/K_v \approx 41\). The thin clayey layer contributes almost nothing to \(K_h\) but controls \(K_v\).

  4. Kozeny–Carman sensitivity. By what factor does \(k\) change if (a) grain size halves; (b) porosity falls from 0.40 to 0.30?
    Solution

    (a) \(k \propto d^2\): a factor of 1/4. (b) \(\dfrac{0.30^3/0.70^2}{0.40^3/0.60^2} = \dfrac{0.0551}{0.1778} = 0.31\): permeability drops to about a third.

  5. Contaminant travel time. A spill lies 500 m upgradient of a supply well. \(K = 5\times10^{-4}\) m/s, hydraulic gradient 0.002, \(n_e = 0.25\). Ignoring dispersion and sorption, how long until it arrives?
    Solution

    \(q = Ki = 1.0\times10^{-6}\) m/s; \(v = q/n_e = 4.0\times10^{-6}\) m/s; \(t = 500/v = 1.25\times10^{8}\) s ≈ 4.0 years. Using \(q\) instead of \(v\) would wrongly give 16 years.

  6. Is the flow Darcian? Water flows through 5 mm gravel (φ = 0.38) under a pressure gradient of 1 kPa/m. Using Ergun coefficients, find \(q\), \(Fo\) and the Darcy-only error.
    Solution

    See Worked example 5: \(q = 0.0115\) m/s, \(Fo = 1.07\) (well above Zeng and Grigg's 0.11 threshold [17]); Darcy alone overpredicts \(q\) by a factor of 2.07.

  7. Theis and Cooper–Jacob. A well pumps 10 L/s from a confined aquifer with \(T = 10^{-3}\) m²/s and \(S = 10^{-4}\). Estimate drawdown 50 m away after 1 day, and check whether Cooper–Jacob is acceptable.
    Solution

    \(u = 7.23\times10^{-4} < 0.01\), so Cooper–Jacob applies. \(s = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi T}(-0.5772 - \ln u) = 0.796\times6.655 = 5.30\) m, matching the full Theis value.

  8. Flow between two rivers. An unconfined aquifer with \(K = 10^{-4}\) m/s separates rivers 1,000 m apart with water levels 20 m and 15 m above the impermeable base. No recharge. Find the discharge per metre of river bank.
    Solution

    Dupuit: \(q' = K(h_0^2 - h_L^2)/(2L) = 10^{-4}\times(400-225)/2000 = 8.75\times10^{-6}\) m²/s ≈ 0.76 m³/day per metre [21].

  9. Capillary rise. Treat a fine sand's pores as tubes of radius 0.05 mm with a contact angle of zero. How high does water rise above the water table?
    Solution

    \(h_c = 2\sigma\cos\theta/(\rho g r) = 2\times0.0728/(998.2\times9.807\times5\times10^{-5}) = 0.30\) m.

  10. Waterflood. For the Buckley–Leverett example (Figure 11), how many pore volumes of water have been injected at breakthrough, and how much oil has been produced by then?
    Solution

    0.445 PV. Before breakthrough only oil is produced, at the injection rate, so oil produced equals 0.445 PV, which is 0.445/0.8 = 55.6 % of the initial oil in place.

  11. Temperature. A field test at 10 °C gave \(K = 2\times10^{-4}\) m/s. Estimate \(K\) at 20 °C.
    Solution

    \(K \propto \rho/\mu\). Density changes by under 0.2 %, so \(K_{20} \approx K_{10}\,\mu_{10}/\mu_{20} = 2\times10^{-4}\times1.306/1.002 = 2.6\times10^{-4}\) m/s [14]. The intrinsic permeability is unchanged.

  12. Code exercise. Modify the call to solve_head_2d to add a second well, then find the pumping rate at which the head at the first well drops below 94 m.
    Hint

    Wrap the solver in a function of \(Q\) and bisect on \(Q\). Because the equation is linear, drawdowns from two wells add (superposition), so you can also compute the unit response of each well once and scale.

11. Trivia questions with answers

  1. In what document did Darcy's law first appear?
    Answer

    In an appendix (Note D) to Darcy's 1856 book on the public fountains of Dijon, a municipal water-supply report [6,7].

  2. Darcy's name is attached to another famous fluid-mechanics equation. Which one?
    Answer

    The Darcy–Weisbach equation for head loss in pipes; Darcy also carried out careful pipe-flow experiments and improved the Pitot tube [7].

  3. Which unit equals about one square micrometre?
    Answer

    The darcy: 1 D = 9.869233 × 10⁻¹³ m² [9].

  4. Which has the higher porosity, clay or gravel? Which conducts water better?
    Answer

    Clay typically has higher porosity (0.40–0.70 versus 0.25–0.40), but gravel conducts water many orders of magnitude better (Table 2) [3].

  5. Theis's 1935 well solution came from an analogy with which branch of physics?
    Answer

    Heat conduction: Theis noted that radial groundwater flow to a well is mathematically the same as radial heat flow, and borrowed the solution [22].

  6. What fraction of Earth's fresh water is groundwater?
    Answer

    About 30 % [19].

  7. Is the departure from Darcy's law at moderate Reynolds number caused by turbulence?
    Answer

    No. It starts in laminar flow and is caused by inertial (form-drag) effects in the tortuous pores; turbulence appears only at much higher Reynolds numbers [1,16].

  8. What year was the Buckley–Leverett paper published, and in which journal?
    Answer

    1942, in the Transactions of the AIME [32].

  9. Name the USGS groundwater code first released in the 1980s that is now the de facto standard.
    Answer

    MODFLOW [20].

  10. Which dimensionless function lets capillary-pressure curves from rocks of different permeability be compared?
    Answer

    The Leverett J-function, \(J = p_c\sqrt{k/\phi}/(\sigma\cos\theta)\) [26].

  11. How much did U.S. groundwater storage decline between 1900 and 2008, according to the USGS?
    Answer

    About 1,000 km³ [24].

  12. What is the porosity of the densest possible packing of equal spheres?
    Answer

    About 0.26 (rhombohedral packing), independent of sphere size [1].
