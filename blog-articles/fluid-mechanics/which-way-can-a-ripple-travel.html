Every question in open-channel hydraulics reduces to one question, and it is not a question about water. It is a question about information: given a disturbance at one point in a channel, which parts of the channel can find out about it?

The argument of this article. The Froude number is not one topic among several. It is the answer to the signalling question, and the classification of flow, the two invariants, uniform and gradually varied flow, the direction in which a profile must be computed, and the existence of the hydraulic jump are all consequences of it. Read that way, the syllabus stops being a list.

What follows works the consequence out in six parts, with twenty computed figures, twenty-two numbered control plates collected into a closing ledger, and a runnable laboratory whose output supplies every number quoted in the text.

PART IThe signalling question

01The ripple that cannot travel upstream

Drop a pebble into a slow canal and a ring spreads in every direction, including back toward you. Drop the same pebble into the chute below a spillway and the ring does not spread at all; it is folded into a narrow wedge and carried away downstream before it has finished forming. Nothing about the water has changed. Only its depth and its speed have, and with them the answer to a single question: can a disturbance travel upstream?

That question is the organising fact of open-channel hydraulics, and this article is an argument that every topic in the standard syllabus is an answer to it. The classification into subcritical, critical and supercritical flow is the question asked directly. Specific energy and specific force are the two bookkeeping systems that survive in the two cases. Uniform flow is the state a channel reaches when nothing is signalling anything. Gradually varied flow profiles are the shape of a signal decaying with distance, and the rule about which end of a reach you compute from is nothing but the rule about which way signals go. The hydraulic jump is what happens when two regions of channel cannot exchange information continuously and have to do it in a single violent step instead.

A small disturbance on shallow water travels relative to the water at the celerity 1

c = √(g y)

and the water itself travels at the mean velocity V. Signals therefore move over the ground at V + c downstream and V − c upstream. The second of those changes sign when V = c. The ratio that decides it is the Froude number,

Fr = V / √(g y)

and the whole of what follows is downstream of that sign change.

The lab that accompanies this article makes the point numerically before making it verbally. In a rectangular channel carrying 3.00 m²/s per metre of width, a disturbance covers a kilometre upstream in about 1,793 s at a depth of 1.10 m (Fr = 0.830). Reduce the depth by 7.3 per cent, to 1.02 m (Fr = 0.930), and the same kilometre takes 4,503 s. At 0.9724 m it takes 292,696 s — more than three days — and a hair below that, the answer stops being a number. It is not that the signal becomes slow. It is that one of the two signal paths is destroyed.

Everything downstream of that fact is engineering.

02What an open channel is, and the unknown it adds

A pipe running full and a canal running open are governed by the same conservation laws, and the difference between the two problems is entirely a matter of what is given. In the pipe, the cross-sectional area is a property of the pipe. In the channel, the cross-sectional area is a function of the depth, and the depth is part of what you are trying to find 2. The boundary of the flow domain is itself unknown, which is why the free surface is called free.

Closing the system requires a second condition, and gravity supplies it: at the free surface the pressure is atmospheric. That is the whole of the difference, and it is responsible for essentially every phenomenon in this article. Because the surface can move, the flow can store energy as depth as well as speed; because it can move, there are two ways to carry a given discharge past a given section; and because it can move, small disturbances propagate as gravity waves whose speed sets the Froude number.

Most of what follows depends on a further, harsher simplification: that the flow can be described by one space coordinate along the channel, with a single depth and a single velocity at each station. This is the one-dimensional or gradually varied model, and it is the model behind the standard textbooks 3, 4, 5 and behind nearly every hydraulic computation performed in practice. It rests on three assumptions, all of which are false and all of which are usually harmless:

  • Hydrostatic pressure. Streamlines are assumed straight enough in the vertical that pressure at a point is ρg times the depth below the surface. Where they curve sharply — over a weir crest, at a brink, inside an undular jump — this fails, and the Boussinesq corrections that were discarded in 1877 come back 6, 7.
  • Uniform velocity over the section. The real velocity varies from zero at the boundary to a maximum near the surface, so the true kinetic energy flux exceeds V²/2g and the true momentum flux exceeds ρQV. Correction coefficients α and β absorb the difference; in a well-behaved prismatic channel α is about 1.03 to 1.10 and β about 1.01 to 1.04, and on a compound section with flood berms α can exceed 2 2, 5.
  • Small slope. Depth measured normal to the bed is treated as depth measured vertically, an error of cos θ that stays below one per cent for slopes flatter than about 1 in 7.

Throughout this article α and β are taken as one, as they are in the lab. That is not a claim that they are one. It is a decision to keep the argument about signalling visible, and a debt that section 26 pays back.

03Celerity, the Froude number, and the two regimes

Write the shallow-water equations for a small disturbance about a uniform state and they separate into two families of characteristics travelling at V + c and V − c 1, 8. Each family carries information. The number of boundary conditions a reach requires, and the end at which they must be applied, is simply the number of characteristics entering the reach at that end.

When Fr < 1 — subcritical, or in the older British usage tranquil flow — one characteristic runs each way. The reach needs one condition at its downstream end and one at its upstream end, and a change made downstream is felt upstream. When Fr > 1 — supercritical, or shooting flow — both characteristics run downstream, both conditions must be applied at the upstream end, and nothing downstream can be felt at all. When Fr = 1, one characteristic stands still relative to the ground.

Several equivalent readings of the same number are worth holding at once. It is a ratio of speeds, V to the wave celerity. It is the square root of a ratio of inertial to gravitational forces. It is the square root of twice the ratio of velocity head to depth. And, most usefully for what follows, it is the derivative test that decides the sign of every water-surface profile in the language of section 16.

For a non-rectangular section the correct generalisation uses the hydraulic depth D = A/T, where T is the top width:

Fr² = Q² T / (g A³)

This form, not V/√(gy), is what any implementation should use; the two agree only for a rectangle. For compound sections with floodplains the definition itself becomes ambiguous, because there is no single celerity when the main channel and the berms are moving at very different speeds, and several competing definitions are in use 9, 5.

One consequence deserves stating early, because it governs everything in part IV. A control — a section where depth is fixed by something other than the flow upstream and downstream of it — can only influence the reach on the side its signals can reach. A downstream weir controls a subcritical reach. An upstream gate controls a supercritical one. A critical section controls both directions at once, which is exactly why it is so useful for measurement.

04Where the number came from

The formula usually attributed to William Froude was written by Ferdinand Reech in 1852, twenty years before Froude's towing-tank work made the similarity principle famous 10, 11, 12, 13, 14. Froude himself never used the dimensionless group that bears his name; the naming was settled later and stuck, and in continental European literature the parameter was long called the Reech–Froude number. The history of open-channel hydraulics is full of such displacements, and it is worth walking through them because they explain several of the field's odder conventions.

Antoine Chézy derived the resistance formula that carries his name in 1775, in a memorandum on the water supply of Paris; it was not published, remained essentially unknown for a century, and was recovered by Clemens Herschel and later documented by Mouret 15, 16, 17. Philippe Gauckler proposed the two-thirds power law in 1867 18, Robert Manning argued for a very similar form in 1889 and published it in 1891 while explicitly disliking it on dimensional grounds 19, and Albert Strickler independently arrived at the same relation in 1923 with an estimate of the coefficient from grain size 20. Manning's own preferred formula was different from the one now universally called Manning's, a point made forcefully by Williams and reconstructed in detail by Dooge 21, 22.

The theory of varied flow is French. Jean-Baptiste Bélanger wrote the backwater equation and the momentum treatment of the hydraulic jump in 1828, in an essay whose numerical method is recognisably the direct-step calculation still taught today 23, 24, 25. Giorgio Bidone had demonstrated the jump experimentally in Turin a few years earlier 26. Jacques Bresse produced the first systematic integration of the backwater equation in 1860 27, and Joseph Boussinesq supplied both the momentum correction coefficient and the non-hydrostatic corrections in 1877 6. Barré de Saint-Venant had written the unsteady equations in 1871 28.

The organising concepts of the modern treatment are twentieth-century and largely Russian and American. Boris Bakhmeteff introduced specific energy in 1912 and gave it its modern form in his 1932 book 29, 30. Ven Te Chow's 1959 monograph fixed the classification of water-surface profiles into the M, S, C, H and A families and the zone numbering that the whole world now uses 2, and Francis Henderson's 1966 text made the control-based way of thinking about them standard 3. Hunter Rouse and Simon Ince's history remains the best single account of how the field assembled itself 13, 31.

05The one-dimensional idealisation and what it costs

Before using the machinery it is worth being explicit about what the one-dimensional steady model claims, because most of the field disputes of the last forty years are disputes about its limits rather than about its content.

The model asserts that at each station along a channel there is a single depth y, a single discharge Q, and enough regularity in the velocity distribution that two scalar corrections suffice. From those, and a resistance law, it derives everything else: the energy grade line, the momentum flux, the profile shape, the position of controls. It is a remarkably productive claim. It is also the reason that the two most consequential numbers in any hydraulic calculation — Manning's n and the discharge — are both quantities the model cannot produce for itself.

Where it holds well, it holds very well. Analytic benchmark solutions constructed by MacDonald and colleagues, and the extended SWASHES collection, show that careful one-dimensional schemes reproduce steady gradually varied profiles to well within the accuracy of any field survey 32, 33. Where it fails, it fails in identifiable places, and section 26 catalogues them. In between lies the honest working region, and the discipline of using the model well is largely the discipline of knowing which region you are in.

The worked scheme used throughout this article is a small diversion — a reservoir, an ogee crest, a 200 m chute at a slope of 0.080, a lined rectangular channel 10 m wide at a slope of 0.0008, and a check weir downstream — carrying a design discharge of 30 m³/s. Downstream of the works a trapezoidal earth canal, 6 m wide at the invert with 2:1 side slopes and n = 0.030, carries the same flow onward. That scheme contains, in about a kilometre, a subcritical pool, a critical section, a supercritical chute, a hydraulic jump and a subcritical backwater: every regime in the syllabus, and three separate controls. Section 20 assembles it.

PART IIThe two invariants

06Specific energy

Specific energy is the energy per unit weight of water measured from the channel bed rather than from a fixed datum 30, 2:

E = y + α V² / 2g = y + α Q² / (2 g A²)

It is not a conserved quantity in any general sense. It is a bookkeeping device that isolates the part of the total head that depends on depth, so that the bed elevation can be dealt with separately. For a rectangular section carrying a unit discharge q, and with α set to one, it reduces to E = y + q²/(2gy²), which is the curve everyone draws.

The shape of that curve carries the argument. As y grows, the velocity head vanishes and E approaches y: the curve is asymptotic to the 45° line. As y shrinks, the velocity head grows without bound and E approaches infinity: the curve is asymptotic to the horizontal axis. Between the two it must turn, and the turning point is

dE/dy = 1 − Fr² = 0

which occurs exactly at Fr = 1. For the article's chute, q = 3.00 m²/s gives yc = (q²/g)1/3  = 0.9717 m and Emin = 1.4575 m, which is exactly 1.5 yc — a relation the lab verifies to ten decimal places, and one that holds only for a rectangular section.

Two consequences follow immediately. First, for any specific energy above the minimum there are two depths that carry the same discharge: the alternate depths, one on each limb. At E = 2.10 m the pair is 1.983 m at Fr = 0.343 and 0.543 m at Fr = 2.396. Energy alone cannot tell you which one you have; only a boundary condition can. Second, for any specific energy below the minimum, no depth carries that discharge at all. The flow must either reduce its discharge, raise its energy, or — if it can do neither — back up until it can, which is what a choked bridge opening does.

The critical depth itself deserves a moment. It depends only on discharge and geometry. It does not depend on slope, on roughness, on what lies upstream, or on what lies downstream. That independence is precisely what makes a critical section a control, and what makes a broad-crested weir or a Parshall flume a measuring device 34, 35, 36, 37.

07Alternate depths and the conditioning of the answer

Turn the specific-energy curve on its side and it says something less comfortable. If depth is a function of specific energy, then the sensitivity of that function is

dy/dE = 1 / (1 − Fr²)

which is unbounded as Fr approaches one from either side. The lab tabulates the consequence: a 10 mm uncertainty in specific energy becomes a 10.4 mm uncertainty in depth at Fr = 0.2, a 53 mm uncertainty at Fr = 0.9, a 103 mm uncertainty at Fr = 0.95, and a full metre at Fr = 0.995.

This is not numerical weakness and it is not a defect of any particular solver. It is the geometry of a function near its own minimum, and it means that a one-dimensional model asked to report depth in near-critical flow is being asked for a quantity that its own governing equation declines to determine sharply. In practice this shows up as models that will not converge in transitional reaches, as rating curves that scatter badly near the point of transition, and as the long-standing rule of thumb in flood-mapping practice that results in the range 0.9 < Fr < 1.1 should be treated with suspicion 38, 39.

The condition itself also deserves more scrutiny than the textbook derivation gives it. Minimum specific energy, Fr = 1 in the section-averaged sense, and the critical depth of the standard formula are three statements that coincide exactly only under the assumptions of section 2. Liggett showed that the averaging implicit in α shifts the depth at which the true energy is minimised, and Chanson has argued that for non-rectangular and curvilinear flows the three criteria genuinely separate 40, 41. For most engineering this is a refinement; for a critical-depth flume being certified to a fraction of a per cent, it is not.

The dual reading of the same family of curves is the basis of every critical-flow measuring structure. Fix the specific energy instead of the discharge and ask which discharge is largest: the answer is the one whose curve is tangent to the cut, which is to say the critical one, at qmax = √(g(2E/3)³). A device that forces the flow through critical therefore converts a single upstream head measurement into a discharge, with no need to know anything about downstream conditions — and, more to the point, with no way for downstream conditions to interfere.

08Specific force

The second invariant comes from momentum rather than energy. Apply the momentum equation to a short control volume between two sections, neglect boundary shear and the weight component, and what is conserved is

M = A ŷ + β Q² / (g A)

where ŷ is the depth of the centroid of the section below the surface. The first term is the hydrostatic pressure force on the section, divided by ρg; the second is the momentum flux through it, divided by the same. The sum has units of volume, and is variously called the specific force, the momentum function, or simply M 2, 3. For a rectangle per unit width it is y²/2 + q²/(gy).

The curve looks superficially like the specific-energy curve and behaves very differently. A horizontal cut across it gives a pair of depths with the same specific force: the sequent depths, sometimes called conjugate depths. These are the depths that a hydraulic jump can join, because a jump exchanges no net force with its surroundings even though it destroys energy freely.

The distinction between sequent and alternate depths is the most consequential piece of bookkeeping in the subject, and the lab quantifies it. At the article's gate opening, y1 = 0.350 m and Fr1 = 4.63, momentum gives a sequent depth of 2.121 m while energy gives an alternate depth of 4.067 m. The difference is 1.946 m, which is 91.7 per cent of the sequent depth. Choosing the wrong invariant does not produce a small error. It produces a tailwater requirement almost twice too large and a stilling basin designed for a jump that will never form in it.

The reason momentum survives where energy does not is worth stating plainly. Over the few metres a jump occupies, the boundary shear force is small compared with the pressure forces at the two end sections, so the momentum balance closes without needing a loss term. Energy has no such luck: the roller converts a large fraction of the incoming kinetic energy into turbulence, air entrainment, noise and heat, and there is no way to predict that loss from first principles. The loss is computed after the sequent depth is known, not before.

09One depth, two minima

Differentiate the specific force with respect to depth and, for a channel of any shape,

dM/dy = A (1 − Fr²)

which vanishes at exactly the same depth as dE/dy = 1 − Fr². Two different conservation principles, applied to two different quantities, are stationary at one shared depth. The lab confirms it numerically: for q = 3.00 m²/s both derivatives vanish at y = 0.971683 m, with Emin = 1.457524 m and Mmin = 1.416251 m².

This is not a coincidence, and the reason is instructive. Both stationarity conditions are statements that the flow can no longer adjust its depth to accommodate a change — that the two mechanisms by which a channel stores the same discharge, as depth and as velocity, have reached the point where trading one for the other buys nothing. Fenton makes the case that this equivalence, rather than either principle taken alone, is what makes critical flow the natural organising point of the subject 42, 43, 44.

The practical consequence is that critical depth can be located by whichever argument is convenient. At a broad-crested weir, where nothing is being destroyed, the energy argument is convenient and gives the discharge from the upstream head. At the toe of a jump, where a great deal is being destroyed, the momentum argument is the only one available and gives the sequent depth. The two never disagree about where critical depth is. They disagree only about what is conserved on the way there, which is a different and much more consequential question.

10Which invariant survives

The single most common error in open-channel work is applying the wrong conservation statement across a transition, and the rule for choosing is simple enough to state in a sentence: use energy where you can account for the losses, and momentum where you cannot.

Energy is available across a short, smooth, accelerating transition — a weir crest, a flume throat, a gate opening, a gradual contraction — because in those places the loss is small and can be absorbed into a coefficient calibrated by experiment. Momentum is available across a short, violent transition — a hydraulic jump, a sudden expansion, a bridge pier, a baffle block — because in those places the external force is small or calculable even though the loss is neither.

Three practical implications follow.

  • Accelerating flows use energy; decelerating flows use momentum. Acceleration is efficient and its losses are second order. Deceleration in an open channel almost always separates, and a separated flow loses energy in ways no simple analysis captures.
  • A structure that exerts a force must appear in the momentum equation. Bridge piers, baffle blocks, end sills and gate reactions all contribute a term to M, and every one of them reduces the sequent depth that a given approach flow requires. This is precisely the mechanism by which a stilling basin is made shorter and shallower than a plain apron 45, 46.
  • An unexplained energy loss is a signal that you are using the wrong equation. If a computation requires a large empirical loss coefficient to close, the transition it describes is probably governed by momentum and the coefficient is hiding a force.

There is one more distinction that costs practitioners real money. The specific-energy curve tells you what depths are possible. It says nothing about which one occurs. That is decided by the boundary conditions and by the signalling rule of section 3, and the rest of this article is the working out of that decision.

PART IIIUniform flow

11Uniform flow as a force balance

Uniform flow is the state in which depth, velocity and area are constant along the reach. It is not a common condition in nature and it is not, strictly, ever attained; it is the asymptote toward which every gradually varied profile tends. Its importance is that it fixes the reference depth against which all other profiles are classified.

Take a control volume of length L in a prismatic channel at slope S0. Because the depth does not change, the hydrostatic pressure forces on the two end sections are equal and opposite; because the velocity does not change, there is no net momentum flux. What remains is the downslope component of the weight against the boundary shear, and the balance is

τ0 P L = ρ g A L S0 → τ0 = ρ g R S0

where R = A/P is the hydraulic radius. If the shear stress is taken to vary as ρV², which is the standard turbulent-flow assumption, then

V = C √(R S0)

which is Chézy's formula 15, 16, 13. It is a statement about equilibrium, and it says nothing whatever about how C should depend on the channel.

That last omission is the whole of the subsequent literature. Chézy's C is not a constant of the channel: it is a function of relative roughness and, weakly, of Reynolds number, in exactly the way that the Darcy–Weisbach friction factor is — and indeed C = √(8g/f) converts one to the other exactly. Keulegan derived the logarithmic form for open channels in 1938, Colebrook produced the transition function that same year, and Rouse's 1965 review argued that the field ought to have adopted the Darcy–Weisbach framework outright 47, 48, 49, 50.

12Manning, and the dimensional embarrassment

The formula that practice actually uses replaces Chézy's undetermined C with a power of the hydraulic radius:

V = (1/n) R2/3 S1/2   (SI)

This is Manning's equation, though as section 4 noted it was Gauckler's first, Strickler's independently, and not the form Manning preferred 18, 19, 20, 21, 22. It is entirely equivalent to Chézy with C = R1/6/n, an identity the lab verifies to machine precision on the article's canal: C = 35.645 m½/s, and both formulas return V = 1.232893 m/s.

Since the two are the same formula, the question is not which is right but which coefficient is more nearly constant. Holding n fixed is equivalent to asserting that C rises as R1/6 and that the Darcy–Weisbach f falls as R−1/3. Over the operating range of the article's canal, from 0.4 m to 3.6 m depth, that assumption moves C by 35.4 per cent. Whether that is a better bet than holding C constant is an empirical question, and for fully rough turbulent flow in natural channels the answer has generally been yes — which is why Manning won.

The awkwardness is that n is dimensional. Its units are s m−1/3, which is why the US customary form carries the factor 1.486 = (3.28084)1/3 and why n tabulated in one system transfers unchanged to the other only by accident of that convention. Yen proposed a dimensionally homogeneous rewriting, V = (√g/ng)R2/3S1/2 with ng = n√g, which for the canal's n = 0.030 gives a dimensionless 0.0940 51, 52. The proposal is correct and has been ignored, and the reason it has been ignored is that seventy years of tabulated n values exist and nothing else does.

Strickler's estimate, n = d501/6/21.1 in SI, gives a useful order of magnitude for gravel beds: 0.017 for 2 mm sand, 0.022 for 10 mm gravel, 0.029 for 50 mm cobbles and 0.036 for 200 mm boulders. For steep mountain streams it substantially underestimates resistance, and Jarrett's and Bathurst's relations, and more recently Ferguson's variable-power form, do better 53, 54, 55.

13Conveyance, normal depth, and the best section

Manning's equation is normally written to give discharge from depth. Every engineering question asks the reverse. Grouping the geometry into a single quantity, the conveyance

K = (1/n) A R2/3, so that Q = K √S0

makes the inversion explicit: find the depth whose conveyance equals Q/√S0. That depth is the normal depth, yn.

The inversion is well behaved for any open section whose top width does not decrease with depth, because K is then monotone and the root is unique; bisection cannot fail and Newton's method converges quickly. For a closed conduit running nearly full it is not monotone, which is why a circular sewer carries its greatest discharge at about 0.94 of full depth and why the same discharge there admits two normal depths — a genuine source of instability in sewer models 56, 57.

For the article's earth canal — 6 m invert, 2:1 side slopes, n = 0.030, S0 = 0.0008 — carrying 30 m³/s, the required conveyance is 1,060.7 m³/s and the normal depth is 2.2969 m, giving A = 24.333 m², R = 1.4954 m, V = 1.2329 m/s and Fr = 0.311.

A related classical result is the best hydraulic section: for a given area, the section with the least wetted perimeter has the greatest conveyance. For a trapezoid this occurs when R = y/2, and the lab confirms it — at the canal's area of 24.333 m² the perimeter is minimised at y = 3.137 m with an invert width of 1.481 m, a perimeter of 15.512 m against the design section's 16.272 m. The design section is therefore 4.9 per cent worse than optimal, and is chosen anyway. Bank stability, maintenance access, the cost of deep excavation and the requirement that a man be able to walk the invert are all constraints that the perimeter functional does not know about. The best hydraulic section is a useful sanity check and almost never a design.

14What n contains, and how badly it is known

Manning's n is not a property of the bed material. It is a residual: it absorbs everything the one-dimensional model has left out. Cowan's decomposition, still the basis of the standard field guides, adds contributions for surface material, degree of irregularity, variation in cross-section, obstructions, vegetation, and a multiplier for meandering 58, 59. Barnes' photographic atlas of gauged reaches remains the most-used calibration aid in the English-speaking world, and its existence is itself a statement about the nature of the coefficient: n is chosen by matching a photograph 60, 61.

What follows from that is a band of uncertainty that dominates most hydraulic computations. The lab imposes a ±20 per cent band on n — narrower than the honest range for a vegetated natural channel — and finds that the canal's normal depth moves from 2.049 m to 2.518 m, a band of 0.469 m or 20.4 per cent of the design depth. At a fixed depth the same band moves the discharge from 25.0 to 37.5 m³/s.

This deserves saying without softening. Freeboard on a canal is not principally a margin against floods; it is a margin against n. A hydraulic model calibrated by adjusting n until the computed water surface matches three surveyed high-water marks has not validated its physics; it has fitted one parameter to three points, and the recognition of that fact is what motivated the uncertainty-estimation literature in flood modelling 62, 63, 64.

Several further points are worth carrying:

  • n is not constant with stage. It usually falls as depth rises in a main channel and rises sharply once flow spills onto a vegetated berm.
  • Composite sections require a composite n, and at least six weighting schemes are in circulation, differing by more than 20 per cent on the same section 50, 65.
  • In sand-bed rivers the bed itself changes form — ripples to dunes to plane bed to antidunes — and n can halve within a single flood 66, 67.
  • The hydraulic radius, on which the whole formula rests, is itself a crude device for handling non-circular sections; Einstein's 1934 partitioning of the perimeter is the standard refinement 68, 69, and the measured boundary shear distribution around a section is markedly non-uniform in any case 70.

15Mild or steep: a comparison, not a slope

Compare the normal depth with the critical depth and the channel classifies itself. If yn > yc the slope is mild; if yn < yc it is steep; if they coincide it is critical. Two more cases have no normal depth at all: a horizontal bed (S0 = 0) and an adverse one (S0 < 0). Chow's letters — M, S, C, H and A — are exactly this classification 2.

The slope at which a channel is critical is not a universal number. It is

Sc = g n² / yc1/3   (wide rectangular section)

and for any other shape it is the slope at which the two depths coincide, found numerically. For the article's canal at 30 m³/s the lab gives Sc = 0.0096947, or 1 in 103. The design slope of 0.0008 is 8.25 per cent of that, so the canal is comfortably mild.

The point that catches practitioners is that Sc depends on the discharge. For a wide section, critical depth grows as Q2/3 while normal depth grows as Q3/5. The exponents differ by 1/15, and that small difference means critical depth eventually overtakes normal depth: a reach that is mild at low flow becomes steep at high flow. The lab makes it concrete. Put the same prismatic section on a slope of 0.0090 and it is mild below 51.2 m³/s and steep above it. Nothing about the channel changed. Only the discharge did.

The consequence is that a profile label written on a drawing without a discharge beside it is not a statement. When a reach crosses Sc during a flood, every profile letter changes, the controlling section moves from one end of the reach to the other, and the direction in which the computation must be marched reverses. Models that switch between subcritical and supercritical solution modes during an unsteady run are managing exactly this transition, and it is the single most common source of instability in one-dimensional river computation 71, 39.

16The gradually varied flow equation

Take the total head H = z + y + αV²/2g, differentiate along the channel, and identify dH/dx = −Sf as the friction slope and dz/dx = −S0 as the bed slope. What falls out is the equation on which part IV rests 23, 27, 2:

dy/dx = (S0 − Sf) / (1 − Fr²)

The friction slope is evaluated from the uniform-flow formula applied locally, which is the central approximation of gradually varied flow: the resistance at a station is assumed to be whatever it would be if that station were part of a uniform reach at that depth. That assumption is why n calibrated from uniform reaches is used in profiles that are not uniform, and it is one reason calibrated n values differ between steady and unsteady computations.

The structure of the equation is the whole of the classification. Numerator and denominator each change sign once, at y = yn and at y = yc respectively, and the three depth zones those two lines create — above both, between them, below both — are Chow's zones 1, 2 and 3. Five slope classes times three zones gives fifteen combinations, three of which are impossible, leaving the twelve profiles of the next section.

Two special cases carry more meaning than the algebra suggests.

When Sf = S0 the numerator vanishes and dy/dx = 0: the surface is parallel to the bed and the flow is uniform. Because the approach is exponential, a profile gets arbitrarily close to normal depth and never arrives. Any statement that a backwater curve is "3.6 km long" is therefore a statement about a tolerance, not about the water, a point section 17 makes with numbers.

When Fr = 1 the denominator vanishes and dy/dx becomes infinite: the surface is predicted to be vertical. This is not a physical prediction; it is the model reporting that it has left its domain of validity. Real channels answer that report in one of three ways — a hydraulic jump, a free overfall, or exactly the streamline curvature that the hydrostatic assumption forbade. Which of the three occurs is not decided by this equation.

17Reading the twelve profiles

The twelve profiles are not twelve facts to memorise. They are twelve readings of one sign table, and anyone who can reconstruct the table can reconstruct the profiles in about a minute.

The procedure is mechanical. Draw the bed. Draw the normal-depth line and the critical-depth line, whichever is higher on top. Identify which of the three zones the depth is in. Determine the sign of the numerator by asking whether the actual depth is greater or less than normal depth (greater means Sf < S0, so the numerator is positive). Determine the sign of the denominator by asking whether the depth is greater or less than critical. Divide.

Some readings that repay attention:

  • M1 is the backwater curve behind every weir, bridge and dam on a mild river, and is the single most computed profile in civil engineering. It approaches the horizontal far upstream of the structure — asymptotically to normal depth — and approaches the structure's level at its downstream end.
  • M2 falls in the direction of flow while remaining subcritical. It is the case that most reliably defeats intuition, because acceleration is accompanied by a falling surface rather than a rising one.
  • M3 and S3 both rise downstream and both are supercritical. They differ in what they are heading toward: M3 toward critical, and therefore toward a jump; S3 toward normal depth, and therefore toward nothing.
  • C1 and C3 are nearly horizontal, because numerator and denominator vanish together and the limit is finite. Zone 2 does not exist on a critical slope, since there is no gap between the two depth lines.
  • H and A profiles have no normal depth, so zone 1 is empty. Only H2, H3, A2 and A3 exist. These are the profiles of stilling basin floors, culvert barrels laid flat, and short rising aprons.

The lengths are as informative as the shapes. For the article's canal the lab computes an M1 curve above a weir holding 3.500 m: it is within 10 per cent of normal depth 1.97 km upstream, within 1 per cent at 3.65 km, and within 0.1 per cent at 5.19 km. The last nine per cent of the recovery occupies 1.69 km, or 46 per cent of the length. The M2 drawdown to a free overfall in the same channel reaches within 1 per cent of normal depth 1.79 km upstream of the brink.

Contrast the S2 profile on the chute, where the same equation with a negative denominator gives a very different answer: starting from critical depth at the crest, the profile is 99.95 per cent complete 75 m downstream, and the remaining 125 m of the 200 m chute is uniform flow in all but name. Supercritical profiles settle in tens of metres; subcritical profiles settle in kilometres. It is the same equation. Only the sign of the denominator has changed.

18Controls, and which way you march

A control is a section at which depth is determined by something other than the flow in the reach: a weir crest, a gate opening, a critical section, a reservoir level, a channel junction. Every profile computation starts at a control, and the signalling rule of section 3 decides which control it starts from.

Subcritical flow is controlled from downstream, so subcritical profiles are computed upstream. Supercritical flow is controlled from upstream, so supercritical profiles are computed downstream. This is not a numerical convention; it is the physics, and violating it produces answers that diverge.

The lab measures the divergence. Linearising the gradually varied flow equation about normal depth gives

d(δy)/dx = λ δy,   λ = −(dSf/dy) / (1 − Frn²)

For the mild canal, λ = +1.518×10−3 m−1: perturbations grow downstream and decay upstream, with an e-folding length of 659 m. Marching a 1 mm error upstream, it decays to 1/e within 660 m; marching the same error downstream, it reaches 100 mm in 3,115 m. For the steep chute the sign reverses: λ = −3.14×10−2 m−1, an e-folding length of 31.8 m downstream, and a 1 mm error marched upstream becomes 100 mm in 138 m.

The stable direction of integration is the direction from which the control can be heard. That is not a coincidence and it is not a property of the numerical scheme. The same one-way structure that makes a downstream weir invisible to supercritical flow makes the arithmetic diverge if you march against it.

Two families of control deserve a note because they are so common. A sluice gate under free flow has a discharge coefficient that depends on the ratio of upstream depth to gate opening; Henry's classic diagram, Rajaratnam and Subramanya's analysis and Swamee's explicit fits are the standard references, and all three break down once the gate is submerged 72, 73, 74. A culvert may be under inlet control, where the entrance is the critical section and the barrel is irrelevant, or under outlet control, where the barrel and the tailwater govern; the whole of highway culvert design is the business of deciding which, and it is decided by exactly the signalling argument of section 3 75. Stage-discharge relations for natural gauging sections are standardised on the same reasoning 76, 77.

Two further points about controls in practice. First, a section can be a control at one discharge and not at another: a bridge that passes the design flood freely becomes a choked control at the check flood, and the afflux appears abruptly. Second, the reason a critical section makes such a good measuring device is that it controls in both directions at once, which is to say that nothing downstream can change the rating — up to the modular limit, past which submergence destroys the control silently and without any visible change at the structure 34, 35.

19Direct step, standard step, and what a river model is

Two integration schemes account for essentially all hand and machine computation of gradually varied profiles, and the difference between them is which variable is chosen as independent.

The direct step method takes depth as independent. Choose a depth increment, compute the specific energy at each end, and solve for the distance between them:

Δx = ΔE / (S0 − S̄f)

where the mean friction slope is evaluated at the two end depths. There is no iteration. The method is exact in the limit for a prismatic channel and it is what Bélanger was doing in 1828 23, 25. The lab uses it throughout, with depth steps clustered geometrically near the asymptote, because uniform depth steps waste effort in the middle of the profile and starve the tail. Before computers the same integral was evaluated with Bresse's function and with graphical constructions; Escoffier's nomograph and Posey's summary in Rouse's Engineering Hydraulics record the state of that art 78, 79.

The standard step method takes distance as independent. Choose the stations — in practice, the stations where cross-sections have been surveyed — and iterate on depth at each one until the energy equation balances:

z1 + y1 + αV1²/2g = z2 + y2 + αV2²/2g + hf + he

with hf the friction loss and he an expansion or contraction loss. It accepts arbitrary geometry and pays for it with an inner iteration at every station. This is what a one-dimensional river model is, underneath the interface: a standard-step solver marching from a boundary condition, with a cross-section database, an interpolation scheme, and special routines for bridges, culverts and junctions 71, 39.

Two practical matters. First, the choice of how to average the friction slope between stations — arithmetic mean, geometric mean, harmonic mean, or mean conveyance — is not innocuous, and different choices give different profiles on steep or rapidly converging reaches; the mean-conveyance form is the usual default and Prasad's scheme remains a common alternative 80. Second, where the profile passes through critical, the standard-step iteration has no solution and models fall back on a critical-depth assumption at that station, which is the origin of most of the warnings a river model emits.

Modern practice for anything unsteady has moved to finite-volume solutions of the Saint-Venant equations with shock-capturing schemes, which handle the transition between regimes without special-casing it, at the cost of requiring well-balanced treatment of the bed-slope source term 81, 82, 83, 84. The steady backwater computation survives because it is transparent, cheap, and defensible to a regulator — and because, for the question it answers, it is right.

Three rules govern the joining, and they are worth stating explicitly because they are what turns a set of profiles into a design.

  • Start at every control and march away from it in the permitted direction. A reach with controls at both ends generates two profiles that must be reconciled.
  • Where a subcritical profile and a supercritical profile overlap, a jump lies between them, and its position is fixed by the specific-force balance, not by anything in the energy equation.
  • Where a profile would cross critical depth smoothly, it does not. Passing from supercritical to subcritical requires a jump; passing from subcritical to supercritical happens smoothly, but only at a control.

That asymmetry — smooth going down through critical, violent coming back up — is the last and most consequential consequence of the signalling question, and it is a statement about entropy as much as about hydraulics. The reverse transition would require energy to appear from nowhere.

21The hydraulic jump and Bélanger's equation

Bidone demonstrated the jump to the Turin Academy around 1819; Bélanger analysed it correctly with momentum in 1828; Bakhmeteff and Matzke gave it its first systematic modern treatment in 1936, and Rajaratnam reinterpreted it as a wall jet in 1965 26, 23, 25, 85, 86, 87.

Apply momentum to a control volume spanning the jump on a horizontal floor. The boundary shear over the short length involved is negligible beside the pressure forces at the two end sections, so

ρ Q (V1 − V2) = ρ g (A2ŷ2 − A1ŷ1) → M1 = M2

For a rectangular section this rearranges to the relation that carries Bélanger's name:

y2/y1 = ½ ( √(1 + 8 Fr1²) − 1 )

The lab solves the specific-force equality numerically and compares: over approach depths from 0.15 m to 0.90 m the two agree to within 3×10−13 m, which is machine precision. Bélanger's relation is not an approximation; it is the exact solution of the momentum equation for a rectangle with β = 1.

The energy destroyed follows afterward, as a consequence rather than an input:

ΔE = (y2 − y1)³ / (4 y1 y2)

Two limitations of the classical result are worth naming. It assumes a horizontal floor: on a sloping apron the weight component enters the momentum balance and the sequent depth ratio changes, for which Hager gives the standard treatment 88. And it assumes a fully developed approach flow; Leutheusser and Kartha showed experimentally that the inflow boundary-layer condition — whether the approach is a developing jet or a fully developed channel flow — changes the sequent depth and roller length measurably 89, 90.

The depth ratio grows without bound: a stronger jump needs a deeper tailwater, and deep tailwater means excavation, wall height and cost. The relative dissipation flattens above Fr1 ≈ 9: past that point you are buying very little extra energy destruction for a great deal of concrete. The oscillating jump is avoided not because it dissipates poorly but because the jet's oscillation launches a surface wave train that travels kilometres and attacks bank protection far outside the works.

Length is the other design quantity and it is stubbornly empirical. The USBR charts give a roller length of roughly six sequent depths in the steady range; Hager and Bremen's correlation, Lj/y1 = 220 tanh((Fr1−1)/22), is the most widely used closed form and is what the lab reports 92, 88. For the article's chute, with y1 = 0.327 m and Fr1 = 5.12, it gives a jump 13.3 m long.

Nothing else in civil hydraulics converts organised kinetic energy into heat and sound at that density, and the whole discipline of stilling-basin design exists because that conversion has to be made to happen somewhere the engineer has put concrete.

23Where the jump sits

The jump does not sit where the designer draws it. It sits at the first station where the specific force of the approaching supercritical flow can be matched by the specific force the tailwater can supply.

The construction is standard and worth working through. Compute the M3 or S3 profile downstream from the supercritical control. At every station, compute the sequent depth that the local approach depth would require. That gives a sequent-depth curve, which falls as the supercritical profile rises. Separately, compute the subcritical profile upstream from the downstream control. The jump sits where the two curves cross.

For the article's scheme — a 10 m wide concrete apron at n = 0.014 and S0 = 0.0008, taking y1 = 0.3269 m and Fr1 = 5.12 from the chute — the lab finds the toe 101.5 m below the chute foot. There the approach depth has risen to 0.6423 m and Fr1 has fallen to 1.86, so the jump that actually occurs is a weak one destroying 0.121 m of head: 6.9 per cent of the local specific energy, not the 50.1 per cent that a jump at the toe itself would have destroyed. The energy did not disappear. It was spent on friction over 101.5 m of concrete apron that was never intended to take it.

The sensitivity is the finding that matters. A band of ±0.20 m on the tailwater — narrower than the band that a ±20 per cent uncertainty on n already implies — moves the toe from 110.3 m to 86.1 m, a travel of 24.2 m. Asked through the apron's own roughness instead, the same ±20 per cent band moves the toe from 219.8 m to 49.5 m: a travel of 170.3 m, from a coefficient normally chosen by matching a photograph to a table.

This is why a basin sized to the base case alone is a basin that will one day pass a supercritical jet over its downstream lip, and why the sequence of design decisions in the next section exists.

24Stilling basins, and what the force balance omits

The engineering response to section 23 has three parts, and each of them is a way of putting a term into the momentum equation that a plain apron does not have.

Depress the floor. Setting the basin invert below the downstream bed raises the available tailwater depth relative to the jump and moves the balance point upstream, onto the concrete. This is the reason a stilling basin is a hole rather than a slab, and the reason the tailwater rating curve and the sequent-depth curve are plotted on the same sheet in every design manual 45, 93.

Add force. Chute blocks at the entrance, baffle piers in the body and a dentated end sill each exert a drag force on the flow. That force enters the specific-force balance directly, which reduces the sequent depth the flow requires and shortens the basin. Bradley and Peterka's six standardised basins — the familiar USBR Types I to VI — are the codification of how much force each arrangement supplies over which Froude range. The Federal Highway Administration's HEC-14 covers the same ground for the smaller structures typical of highway drainage 91, 45, 46.

Design for the band, not the point. Because the jump position is as sensitive as section 23 shows, basins are conventionally checked at several discharges and with tailwater curves displaced by an explicit margin. A basin that works only at the design discharge is not a design.

Two costs of the force-adding approach deserve stating. The lesson for this article is narrow and worth taking: energy that is not destroyed where the concrete is will be destroyed somewhere else, and the somewhere else is generally the foundation.

25Undular jumps, air, and the edge of the model

Below Fr1 ≈ 1.7 the jump has no roller. The surface rises through a train of stationary undulations, energy loss is very small, and the pressure distribution is emphatically not hydrostatic. This is the undular jump, and it is exactly where the one-dimensional model is weakest — the Boussinesq curvature terms that were dropped in section 2 are precisely what generates the undulations 6, 96, 97, 98, 99, 7.

Undular jumps matter practically because they occur in irrigation canals and urban drainage far more often than strong jumps do, because their surface waves propagate a long way downstream, and because a section-averaged model reports them as a smooth transition when in reality the local surface is oscillating by a substantial fraction of the depth.

At the other end, air entrainment dominates. A strong jump entrains a large air flux across the roller, and the resulting bubbly two-phase flow changes the effective density, the pressure field, and the loading on structures. Chanson's group has produced the most detailed experimental characterisation of the void fraction and turbulence fields, and it is now clear that the classical one-dimensional treatment gives the sequent depth well and the internal flow field not at all 100, 101, 102, 103, 104.

Two further consequences of air are worth recording. Entrained air bulks the flow, so the wall height a basin needs exceeds the sequent depth computed from clear-water theory, sometimes by 20 to 40 per cent. And air in the water is what protects concrete surfaces from cavitation damage in high-velocity chutes, which is why deliberate aerators are installed on tall spillways — a case of a phenomenon that the one-dimensional model cannot represent being the thing the design most depends on 94, 105.

26What the framework cannot do

The steady one-dimensional model earns its place by being cheap, transparent and auditable. It is worth closing by being specific about the four places it stops being true, all of which are visible in Figure 20 and none of which is exotic.

Curvature. Wherever streamlines curve sharply in the vertical — a weir crest, a free overfall, an undular jump, a steep chute with a convex break — pressure is not ρg times the depth below the surface, and the correction is not small. The systematic treatment is non-hydrostatic, and Castro-Orgaz and Hager's monograph is the modern reference 7, 106, 107.

Instability. Above the Vedernikov limit — near Fr ≈ 2 for a wide channel with a Chézy resistance law, and Fr ≈ 1.5 with Manning — uniform flow is linearly unstable and breaks into roll waves: a train of shock-fronted surges marching down a steep chute at constant discharge. Jeffreys identified the mechanism in 1925, Vedernikov gave the criterion, and Brock's laboratory work established the wave properties 108, 109, 110, 111, 112, 113. The steady solution still exists; nothing converges to it, and the peak depth in a roll-wave train can be twice the uniform depth. Chute walls designed to the uniform depth overtop.

Unsteadiness. A flood is not a sequence of steady states. A rising limb stores water in the reach, so discharge at a given stage is higher on the rise than on the fall, and the stage-discharge relation becomes a loop. The Saint-Venant equations handle this, and the kinematic and diffusive approximations to them — Lighthill and Whitham's kinematic wave, and the family of simplifications analysed by Ponce and Simons — describe when the steady picture is recoverable and when it is not 28, 114, 115, 116, 117, 118.

Dimensionality. On a floodplain, at a confluence, around a bend or through a bridge opening, the flow direction is not the channel direction, the section-averaged velocity is a fiction, and α and β are nowhere near one. Two-dimensional shallow-water solvers are now routine for these cases, and the literature on when the added complexity pays is substantial and not unanimous 119, 120, 64.

None of this makes the framework obsolete. A two-dimensional model needs the same roughness coefficient, with the same ±20 per cent band; it needs the same boundary conditions applied at the same ends for the same signalling reasons; and it will produce a hydraulic jump in the same place, for the same specific-force reason, if it is any good. The framework in this article is not a crude approximation to the modern methods. It is the conceptual vocabulary in which the modern methods are checked.

Which returns to the ripple. Every piece of the apparatus — the Froude number, the two invariants, the profile letters, the marching direction, the jump — is an answer to a single question about whether a disturbance can travel upstream. Get that question wrong and no amount of resolution will save the computation. Get it right and a surprising amount can be done with a pocket calculator and a long section.

Further reading

Standard texts

Chow's 1959 monograph 2 is still the reference for the profile classification and for the tables of roughness coefficients; Henderson's 1966 text 3 is the best short account of control-based reasoning; Sturm 5, Chaudhry 121, Jain 122, Akan 57 and Subramanya 123 are the most usable modern teaching texts; Montes 124 and Chanson 125 go deeper on the physics of rapidly varied flow; Castro-Orgaz and Hager 7 are the reference for everything the hydrostatic assumption excludes. Rouse 126 and Liggett 127 remain the best short bridges from general fluid mechanics into this one.

Roughness selection

Barnes' photographic atlas of gauged reaches (USGS WSP 1849) and Arcement and Schneider's guide (WSP 2339) are the standard field aids for natural channels; Cowan's additive decomposition underlies both. For steep gravel and boulder beds, Jarrett, Bathurst and Ferguson give substantially better estimates than Strickler.

Structures and energy dissipation

USBR Engineering Monograph 25 remains the source for stilling basin types and jump classification; FHWA HEC-14 covers the smaller structures; Hager's Energy Dissipators and Hydraulic Jump is the most complete modern synthesis; Bos, and Ackers and colleagues, are the references for weirs and flumes.

Computation

Cunge, Holly and Verwey remains the clearest account of what a river model does and why; Toro and LeVeque cover shock-capturing schemes for the shallow-water equations; MacDonald and colleagues, and the SWASHES collection, provide analytic benchmarks against which any implementation should be checked.

Reproducing this article

The laboratory below is a single stdlib-only Python file. It has no dependencies, takes a few seconds to run, and every number quoted in the prose and drawn in the figures comes from its output. Changing the channel geometry at the top of the file changes the article's worked scheme end to end.
