Counting and Combinatorics

The fundamental counting principle, factorials, permutations, combinations, binomial and multinomial coefficients, inclusion–exclusion, and sampling with and without replacement, with worked examples, Python code and exercises.

Summary. When every outcome of an experiment is equally likely, a probability is a ratio of two counts, so much of elementary probability is really counting. This article builds the standard counting toolkit from a single rule (multiply the number of choices at each stage), derives permutations, combinations and their generalisations from it, shows how to correct for overcounting with inclusion–exclusion, and ends with the four ways of drawing a sample, which lead directly to the binomial, hypergeometric and multinomial distributions. Every number in the text was produced by the Python code shown, and every non-obvious claim carries a numbered reference in Vancouver style.

1. Why counting matters in statistics

If a sample space contains finitely many outcomes and each is equally likely, the probability of an event A is the number of outcomes in A divided by the total number of outcomes.1,2 This is the classical definition of probability, and it turns probability questions into counting questions: how many five-card hands are a full house, how many seatings leave nobody in their own seat, how many samples of four items contain no defective.

Classical probabilityP(A) = |A||S|    when all outcomes in the finite sample space S are equally likely

Counting also underlies the most common discrete distributions. The binomial coefficient appears in the binomial distribution, the multinomial coefficient in the multinomial distribution, and ratios of binomial coefficients in the hypergeometric distribution.2,3 Combinatorics, the branch of mathematics concerned with counting arrangements and selections, supplies the tools.4,5

Two questions decide which tool applies to a problem, and they recur throughout the article:

  • Does order matter? Is AB a different outcome from BA? If yes, we count arrangements (permutations); if no, we count selections (combinations).
  • Is repetition allowed? Can the same object be chosen more than once? Drawing a card and putting it back before the next draw is sampling with replacement; dealing a hand is sampling without replacement.
Notation. Throughout, n is the number of distinct objects available and k the number chosen. We write n! for the factorial, P(n, k) for the number of ordered selections without repetition, and C(n, k) or the column symbol (nk) for the binomial coefficient, read “n choose k”.6,7

2. Fundamental counting principle

Multiplication ruleIf a procedure consists of k stages, and stage i can be done in ni ways regardless of how the earlier stages were done, the whole procedure can be done in n1 × n2 × ⋯ × nk ways.

This is the fundamental counting principle (also called the basic principle of counting, or the product rule).2,5 The key condition is that the number of choices at each stage does not depend on what was chosen earlier, even if the choices themselves do. A tree diagram makes the rule visible: each stage branches every path by the same factor, so the number of leaves is the product of the branching factors.1

Worked examples

  • Menu. 3 starters, 4 mains and 2 desserts give 3 × 4 × 2 = 24 three-course meals.
  • Licence plates. Three letters followed by four digits: 263 × 104 = 175,760,000 plates.
  • Passwords. Eight characters, each one of 62 letters or digits: 628 = 218,340,105,584,896.
  • PIN with no repeated digit. 10 choices for the first digit, 9 for the second, 8, then 7: 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 5,040. The set of digits available at stage 2 depends on stage 1, but the number (9) does not, so the rule still applies.
  • Subsets. A subset of a 10-element set is built by deciding in or out for each element: 210 = 1,024 subsets, including the empty set.5

The addition rule

The companion rule handles alternatives rather than stages: if a choice can be made from one of several disjoint sets, the number of ways is the sum of their sizes.4,5 For example, the number of “words” of one, two or three letters is 26 + 262 + 263 = 18,278. When the sets overlap, simple addition overcounts; Section 8 shows how inclusion–exclusion corrects it.

import itertools
from math import prod

print(prod([3, 4, 2]))                                         # 24
print(len(list(itertools.product(range(3), range(4), range(2)))))  # 24, by enumeration
print(f"{26**3 * 10**4:,}")                                    # 175,760,000
print(26 + 26**2 + 26**3)                                      # 18278

itertools.product generates the Cartesian product of its inputs, which is exactly the set of outcomes counted by the multiplication rule.8 Enumerating small cases like this is the best way to check a counting argument before trusting it on large ones.

3. Factorials

Definitionn! = n × (n − 1) × ⋯ × 2 × 1   for n ≥ 1,    0! = 1,    equivalently n! = n · (n − 1)!

The number of ways to arrange n distinct objects in a row is n!, by the multiplication rule: n choices for the first position, n − 1 for the second, and so on down to 1.2 The convention 0! = 1 is the value of an empty product, and it is the only value that keeps the recursion n! = n(n − 1)! and the formulas below valid at their boundaries (there is exactly one way to arrange nothing).6 The factorial extends to non-integers through the gamma function, with n! = Γ(n + 1).6

Factorials grow faster than exponentials

The factorial outgrows any fixed power and any fixed exponential. 20! = 2,432,902,008,176,640,000 (about 2.4 × 1018); 52!, the number of orderings of a deck of cards, has 68 digits (8.0658 × 1067); and 100! has 158 digits, ending in 24 zeros.

Stirling's approximation

Stirling's formulan! ≈ √(2πn) (n/e)n,   and more precisely   n! = √(2πn) (n/e)n ern   with   112n + 1 < rn < 112n

Stirling's formula says the ratio of the two sides tends to 1 as n grows.1 Robbins gave the sharp two-sided bound on the error term shown above, valid for every n ≥ 1.9 Because er ≈ 1 + r for small r, the relative error of the plain formula is close to 1/(12n), which the table confirms. Computing factorials in practice

Python integers have arbitrary precision, so math.factorial returns exact values of any size.10 For probabilities you usually need ratios of huge factorials, and it is safer to work with logarithms: math.lgamma(n + 1) returns ln(n!) as a float without ever forming n!.10 For example, log10(1000!) = 2567.6046, consistent with 1000! having 2,568 digits.

from math import factorial, lgamma, log, sqrt, pi, e

print(f"{factorial(52):.4e}")             # 8.0658e+67
print(len(str(factorial(100))))           # 158 digits
print(lgamma(1001) / log(10))             # 2567.6046...  = log10(1000!)
stirling = lambda n: sqrt(2*pi*n) * (n/e)**n
print((factorial(10) - stirling(10)) / factorial(10))   # 0.00830
Floating-point overflow. 171! exceeds the largest double-precision float (about 1.8 × 10308), so float(factorial(171)) raises OverflowError. Libraries that compute factorials or binomial coefficients in floating point either return infinity or lose precision long before that. Use exact integers, fractions.Fraction, or logarithms.

4. Permutations

k-permutations of n objectsP(n, k) = n(n − 1)(n − 2)⋯(n − k + 1) = n!(n − k)!,   0 ≤ k ≤ n

A permutation is an ordered arrangement. The number of ways to fill k ordered positions from n distinct objects, using each at most once, follows from the multiplication rule with k stages of n, n − 1, …, n − k + 1 choices.2,5 The product of k descending factors is also called the falling factorial nk.6 When k = n it reduces to n!; when k > n there are no such arrangements and the count is 0.

  • Podium. Gold, silver and bronze among 10 finalists: P(10, 3) = 10 × 9 × 8 = 720.
  • Bookshelf. Eight different books on a shelf: 8! = 40,320.

Circular permutations

If n people sit around a round table and only their relative positions matter, each seating is one of n rotations of the same arrangement, so there are n!/n = (n − 1)! distinct circular arrangements.5 Six people can be seated in 5! = 120 ways. This is the first instance of a pattern used repeatedly below: count a larger set in which every object of interest appears the same number of times, then divide.

The birthday problem

How many people are needed before a shared birthday becomes more likely than not? Assume 365 equally likely birthdays and ignore leap years. The number of ways n people can have all-different birthdays is P(365, n), out of 365n equally likely assignments, so

Birthday problemP(at least one shared birthday) = 1 − P(365, n)365n = 1 − 365 × 364 × ⋯ × (365 − n + 1)365n

The probability first exceeds one half at n = 23 (0.5073), a result that surprises most people because they compare 23 with 365 rather than counting the C(23, 2) = 253 pairs that could match.1,11 With 57 people it reaches 0.99.

from math import perm
from fractions import Fraction

def birthday(n, days=365):
    return 1 - Fraction(perm(days, n), days**n)

print(float(birthday(23)))                                          # 0.5072972...
print(next(n for n in range(1, 366) if birthday(n) >= Fraction(1, 2)))  # 23

math.perm(n, k) returns n!/(n − k)! for k ≤ n and 0 for k > n; called with one argument it returns n!.10 itertools.permutations(iterable, k) generates the arrangements themselves, and its output length is math.perm(n, k).8

5. Combinations

k-combinations of n objectsC(n, k) = P(n, k)k! = n!k! (n − k)!,   0 ≤ k ≤ n

A combination is an unordered selection: a subset of size k. Each k-subset can be arranged in k! orders, so every subset is counted exactly k! times among the P(n, k) ordered selections. Dividing removes the overcount.2,5

Worked examples

  • Committee. Two representatives from five people: C(5, 2) = 10.
  • Poker hands. Five cards from 52: C(52, 5) = 2,598,960 equally likely hands.2
  • Lottery. Choose 6 numbers from 49: C(49, 6) = 13,983,816, so a single ticket wins the jackpot with probability 1 in 13,983,816. Matching exactly 3 of the 6 winning numbers means choosing 3 of the 6 winners and 3 of the 43 losers: C(6, 3) C(43, 3)/C(49, 6) = 246,820/13,983,816 = 0.01765.

Poker probabilities by the multiplication rule

Many card-hand counts are products of combinations, one factor per decision.2 A full house (three of one rank, two of another) is: choose the rank of the triple (13), choose its three suits (C(4, 3) = 4), choose the rank of the pair from the remaining ranks (12), choose its suits (C(4, 2) = 6). That gives 13 × 4 × 12 × 6 = 3,744 hands. The order of the factors reflects real asymmetry: the triple's rank and the pair's rank play different roles, so choosing them is ordered (13 × 12), not C(13, 2).

The most common counting error. Counting ordered choices when the outcome is unordered (or the reverse). A quick test: swap two of the things you chose. If the result is the same outcome, those choices must be made with a combination, or the count must be divided by the number of orders.
from math import comb
print(comb(52, 5), comb(49, 6))                  # 2598960 13983816
full_house = 13 * comb(4, 3) * 12 * comb(4, 2)
print(full_house, full_house / comb(52, 5))      # 3744 0.00144...

6. Binomial coefficients

The numbers C(n, k) are called binomial coefficients because they are the coefficients in the expansion of a power of a binomial:6,7

Binomial theorem(x + y)n = ∑k=0n C(n, k) xk yn−k

The combinatorial proof is short. Expanding (x + y)(x + y)⋯(x + y) means picking x or y from each of the n factors; the term xkyn−k arises once for every choice of which k factors supply the x, which is C(n, k) times.5

Pascal's triangle and Pascal's rule

Pascal's ruleC(n, k) = C(n − 1, k − 1) + C(n − 1, k),   1 ≤ k ≤ n − 1,   with C(n, 0) = C(n, n) = 1

Fix one object. Every k-subset either contains it (choose the other k − 1 from the remaining n − 1) or does not (choose all k from the remaining n − 1). Arranging the coefficients in rows gives Pascal's triangle, in which each entry is the sum of the two above it.5,6

Lattice paths

A path on a grid from (0, 0) to (a, b) using unit steps right (R) or up (U) is a word of a R's and b U's. Choosing which b of the a + b steps are U determines the path, so there are C(a + b, b) paths.7 Pascal's rule reappears: the last step into any point comes either from the left or from below.

From binomial coefficients to the binomial distribution

If each of n independent trials succeeds with probability p, any particular sequence with k successes has probability pk(1 − p)n−k, and there are C(n, k) such sequences, so the number of successes X has the binomial distribution2,3

Binomial probability mass functionP(X = k) = C(n, k) pk (1 − p)n−k,   k = 0, 1, …, n

For n = 10 fair coin tosses every sequence has probability 2−10, so the distribution is row 10 of Pascal's triangle divided by 1,024: the probability of exactly 5 heads is 252/1,024 = 0.2461. The binomial theorem with x = p, y = 1 − p shows that the probabilities sum to (p + 1 − p)n = 1.

Computing. math.comb(n, k) returns the exact integer C(n, k), and 0 when k > n; it raises ValueError for negative arguments.10 It was added in Python 3.8. From Python 3.15 the integer functions also live in a new math.integer module, and math.comb, math.perm and math.factorial remain available as soft-deprecated aliases, so the code in this article runs on both.10

7. Multinomial coefficients

Multinomial coefficientC(n; n1, n2, …, nr) = n!n1! n2! ⋯ nr!,   n1 + n2 + ⋯ + nr = n

The multinomial coefficient counts the ways to divide n distinct objects into r labelled groups of prescribed sizes n1, …, nr. Equivalently, it counts the distinct arrangements of a word with n1 copies of one letter, n2 of another, and so on.2,7 The binomial coefficient is the case r = 2: C(n, k) = C(n; k, n − k).

Two derivations give the same answer. Divide out the overcount: label the repeated letters (S1, S2, …) so all n! arrangements are distinct, then erase the labels; each word was counted n1! n2! ⋯ times. Fill positions stage by stage: choose positions for the first letter, then for the second among those left, and so on:7

Product formC(n; n1, …, nr) = C(n, n1) · C(n − n1, n2) · ⋯ · C(nr, nr)

The product form is also the better way to compute: its intermediate values are no larger than the answer, while n! can be astronomically larger.

Worked examples

  • MISSISSIPPI. 11 letters: M once, I four times, S four times, P twice. 11!/(1! 4! 4! 2!) = 34,650 distinct arrangements.
  • STATISTICS. 10 letters: S 3, T 3, I 2, A 1, C 1. 10!/(3! 3! 2! 1! 1!) = 50,400.
  • Teams. Ten people into a team of 5, a team of 3 and a team of 2 (teams distinguishable by size): 10!/(5! 3! 2!) = 2,520.
  • Bridge deal. 52 cards into four labelled hands of 13: 52!/(13!)4 ≈ 5.3645 × 1028.
Labelled versus unlabelled groups. The multinomial coefficient assumes the groups are labelled. Dividing 6 people into three unlabelled pairs gives 6!/(2! 2! 2!)/3! = 90/6 = 15, because the 3! orderings of identical-sized, interchangeable groups describe the same division.

The multinomial theorem and distribution

Multinomial theorem(x1 + ⋯ + xr)n = ∑ C(n; n1, …, nr) x1n1 ⋯ xrnr, summed over all n1 + ⋯ + nr = n

For example, the coefficient of x2yz in (x + y + z)4 is 4!/(2! 1! 1!) = 12; the expansion has 15 distinct terms, and its coefficients sum to 34 = 81.2 If each of n independent trials falls into category i with probability pi, the counts (X1, …, Xr) follow the multinomial distribution:12

Multinomial probability mass functionP(X1 = n1, …, Xr = nr) = C(n; n1, …, nr) p1n1 ⋯ prnr

Roll 12 fair dice. The probability that every face appears exactly twice is 12!/(2!)6 × (1/6)12 = 7,484,400/612 = 0.003438.

from math import comb

def multinomial(*counts):
    result, total = 1, 0
    for c in counts:          # product of binomials: no huge intermediates
        total += c
        result *= comb(total, c)
    return result

print(multinomial(1, 4, 4, 2))         # 34650  (MISSISSIPPI)
print(multinomial(2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2) / 6**12)   # 0.003438...

8. Inclusion–exclusion principle

The addition rule requires disjoint sets. When sets overlap, adding their sizes counts elements in the overlap more than once. Inclusion–exclusion corrects this by alternately subtracting and adding back the sizes of intersections.4,5

Two and three sets|A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|
|A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| − |A∩B| − |A∩C| − |B∩C| + |A∩B∩C|
General form|A1 ∪ ⋯ ∪ Am| = ∑i|Ai| − ∑i<j|Ai∩Aj| + ∑i<j<k|Ai∩Aj∩Ak| − ⋯ + (−1)m+1|A1∩⋯∩Am|

Why it works. Take an element lying in exactly t ≥ 1 of the sets. It is counted C(t, 1) times in the first sum, C(t, 2) times in the second, and so on, for a net count of C(t, 1) − C(t, 2) + ⋯ ± C(t, t) = 1 − (1 − 1)t = 1, by the alternating-sum identity of Section 6.5 The same formula holds with probabilities in place of counts.1,2

Example: divisibility

How many integers from 1 to 1000 are divisible by 2, 3 or 5? Let A, B, C be the multiples of 2, 3, 5. Multiples of both 2 and 3 are multiples of 6, and so on, and the number of multiples of d up to 1000 is ⌊1000/d⌋:

|A ∪ B ∪ C| = (500 + 333 + 200) − (166 + 100 + 66) + 33 = 1033 − 332 + 33 = 734

The remaining 1000 − 734 = 266 integers share no factor with 30. A brute-force count in Python agrees.

Example: derangements

A derangement is a permutation with no fixed point: nobody gets their own hat back, no letter goes in its own envelope. The question is classical and is known as the problème des rencontres.1,13 Let Ai be the permutations that fix item i. Any j specified items can be fixed in (n − j)! ways, and there are C(n, j) ways to specify them, so inclusion–exclusion gives the number of permutations with no fixed point as4,5

Derangement numberDn = ∑j=0n (−1)j C(n, j)(n − j)! = n! ∑j=0n (−1)jj!

The sum is a partial sum of the series for e−1, so Dn/n! → 1/e ≈ 0.3679, and in fact Dn is the integer nearest to n!/e for every n ≥ 1.13 The sequence also satisfies Dn = nDn−1 + (−1)n and Dn = (n − 1)(Dn−1 + Dn−2).13 The surprising consequence is that the chance of no match barely depends on n: with 10 hats or 10 million, it is about 37%.11

Example: surjections and the coupon collector

How many functions from an n-set onto a k-set hit every element of the target? Let Ai be the functions that miss target i. Missing j given targets leaves (k − j)n functions, so the number of onto functions is4,5

SurjectionsS(n, k) = ∑j=0k (−1)j C(k, j)(k − j)n

Rolling a die 10 times is a function from 10 rolls to 6 faces. The probability that all six faces appear is S(10, 6)/610 = 16,435,440/60,466,176 = 0.2718.

Example: complements and voids

“At least one” events are usually easiest through the complement. The probability that a five-card hand contains at least one ace is 1 − C(48, 5)/C(52, 5) = 0.3412. For “at least one of several overlapping things” the complement alone is not enough, and inclusion–exclusion takes over: the probability that a 13-card bridge hand is void in at least one suit is

[4 C(39, 13) − 6 C(26, 13) + 4 C(13, 13) − 0] / C(52, 13) = 32,427,298,180 / 635,013,559,600 = 0.0511
from math import comb, factorial

def derangements(n):
    d = 1
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        d = k * d + (-1) ** k
    return d

def surjections(n, k):
    return sum((-1)**j * comb(k, j) * (k - j)**n for j in range(k + 1))

print([derangements(n) for n in range(11)])  # [1, 0, 1, 2, 9, 44, 265, 1854, 14833, 133496, 1334961]
print(surjections(10, 6) / 6**10)            # 0.2718...
print(sum(1 for i in range(1, 1001) if i % 2 == 0 or i % 3 == 0 or i % 5 == 0))  # 734

9. Counting with replacement

Sampling with replacement means each chosen item is returned before the next draw, so the same item can be chosen again. Equivalently, we select from n types with unlimited supply. There are two cases.1,14

Ordered, with replacement: nk

Each of the k draws has n possible results regardless of the others, so the multiplication rule gives nk sequences.1 PINs, passwords, sequences of die rolls (64 = 1,296 for four rolls), and the 365n birthday assignments in Section 4 are all of this kind. itertools.product(items, repeat=k) generates them.8

Unordered, with replacement: multisets and stars and bars

Multisets of size k from n typesC(n + k − 1, k) = C(n + k − 1, n − 1)

An unordered selection with repetition is a multiset: all that matters is how many of each type are chosen. Write the selection as k stars (the items) separated by n − 1 bars into n compartments (the types). Every arrangement of k stars and n − 1 bars in a row is a different multiset, and every multiset gives one arrangement, so the count is the number of ways to choose which k of the n + k − 1 positions hold stars.1,4 Feller used this stars-and-bars representation to count the distributions of indistinguishable particles among cells, the setting of Bose–Einstein statistics in physics.1

  • Ice cream. 12 scoops from 5 flavours: C(16, 4) = 1,820.
  • Dice outcomes ignoring order. Four dice thrown together, recording only how many of each face: C(9, 4) = 126 outcomes, compared with 1,296 ordered sequences.
  • Integer equations. The number of solutions of x1 + x2 + x3 + x4 = 10 in non-negative integers is C(13, 3) = 286. If every xi ≥ 1, give each variable 1 first and distribute the remaining 6: C(9, 3) = 84.

itertools.combinations_with_replacement(items, k) generates the multisets; its documentation gives their number as (n + k − 1)!/(k! (n − 1)!).8

Multisets are not equally likely. When four fair dice are rolled, the 1,296 ordered sequences are equally likely but the 126 multisets are not: “four sixes” arises from one sequence, while “1, 2, 3, 4” arises from 4! = 24. Classical probability must be applied to the ordered outcomes (or the multiset counts must be weighted by their multinomial coefficients).1,14 Treating the unordered outcomes as equally likely is exactly the Bose–Einstein model, which is right for photons but wrong for dice.

10. Counting without replacement

Without replacement, each item can be chosen at most once, and the pool shrinks after every draw. The two cases are the permutations and combinations already met:2,14

  • Ordered: P(n, k) = n!/(n − k)! sequences.
  • Unordered: C(n, k) subsets.

Unlike the with-replacement case, the ordered and unordered models here are interchangeable for probability: every subset corresponds to exactly k! equally likely orders, so a probability computed with ordered draws equals the same probability computed with subsets. Choose whichever makes the event easier to count.2

The hypergeometric distribution

A lot of N = 20 items contains K = 5 defectives. An inspector draws n = 4 without replacement. The number of samples with exactly k defectives is C(K, k) C(N − K, n − k) (choose the defectives, then the good items), out of C(N, n) equally likely samples. This is the hypergeometric distribution:2,3

Hypergeometric probability mass functionP(X = k) = C(K, k) C(N − K, n − k)C(N, n)

The identity ∑k C(K, k) C(N − K, n − k) = C(N, n), which guarantees the probabilities sum to 1, is Vandermonde's identity from Section 6. If the inspector instead replaced each item before the next draw, every draw would be defective with probability p = K/N = 0.25, independently, and X would be binomial.

When the sample is a small fraction of the population, the correction is close to 1 and the binomial is a good approximation to the hypergeometric.3 This is why survey samples drawn without replacement from large populations are routinely analysed as if drawn with replacement.

Simulating both schemes

Python's random.sample draws without replacement and random.choices draws with replacement.15 A simulation is a useful independent check on any counting argument. With 200,000 trials (seed 2026) the simulated frequencies agree with the exact values to within simulation error:

11. Choosing the right formula

Most counting errors come from applying a correct formula to the wrong model. Before computing, answer these questions in order:2,5

  1. What exactly is one outcome? Write two or three outcomes down explicitly. If they are not equally likely, classical probability cannot be applied to them directly.
  2. Does order matter? Swap two chosen items. Same outcome means unordered.
  3. Can an item repeat? If yes, you are sampling with replacement (or from types with unlimited supply).
  4. Are some objects identical? Then divide by the factorials of the multiplicities (multinomial coefficient).
  5. Is the event “at least one”, “none”, or “every”? Try the complement first; if the complement is a union of overlapping events, use inclusion–exclusion.
  6. Check on a small case. Shrink the problem until you can enumerate it with itertools, and confirm your formula matches.

12. Complete Python module

The module below collects every function used in the article. It uses only the standard library (Python 3.8 or later), returns exact integers or Fractions where possible, and was run with Python 3.13 to produce every figure and table above. Generating combinatorial objects efficiently, rather than counting them, is a large subject of its own; Knuth's treatment is the standard reference.16

"""counting.py: exact counting functions for the Counting and Combinatorics article.

All functions return Python ints (arbitrary precision) unless noted.
"""
from math import comb, perm, factorial, prod, lgamma, log, pi, sqrt, e, exp
from fractions import Fraction


def multiply_rule(*stage_sizes):
    """Fundamental counting principle: number of outcomes of a k-stage process."""
    return prod(stage_sizes)


def stirling(n):
    """Stirling's approximation sqrt(2*pi*n) * (n/e)**n (float)."""
    return sqrt(2 * pi * n) * (n / e) ** n


def log_factorial(n):
    """ln(n!) via the log-gamma function: ln Gamma(n + 1)."""
    return lgamma(n + 1)


def circular(n):
    """Arrangements of n distinct objects around a circle (rotations identified)."""
    return factorial(n - 1) if n > 0 else 1


def multinomial(*counts):
    """n! / (n1! n2! ... nk!) with n = sum(counts)."""
    result, total = 1, 0
    for c in counts:          # product of binomials avoids huge intermediates
        total += c
        result *= comb(total, c)
    return result


def derangements(n):
    """Permutations of n objects with no fixed point, D(n) = n*D(n-1) + (-1)**n."""
    d = 1
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        d = k * d + (-1) ** k
    return d


def surjections(n, k):
    """Onto functions from an n-set to a k-set, by inclusion-exclusion."""
    return sum((-1) ** j * comb(k, j) * (k - j) ** n for j in range(k + 1))


def multisets(n, k):
    """k-element multisets from n types (unordered, with replacement): C(n+k-1, k)."""
    return comb(n + k - 1, k)


def count_samples(n, k, ordered, replacement):
    """The four basic sampling counts for drawing k items from n distinct items."""
    if replacement:
        return n ** k if ordered else multisets(n, k)
    return perm(n, k) if ordered else comb(n, k)


def birthday(n, days=365):
    """P(at least two of n people share a birthday), uniform birthdays."""
    return 1 - Fraction(perm(days, n), days ** n)


def binom_pmf(k, n, p):
    """Binomial probability: k successes in n draws with replacement."""
    return comb(n, k) * p ** k * (1 - p) ** (n - k)


def hypergeom_pmf(k, N, K, n):
    """Hypergeometric probability: k successes in n draws without replacement
    from N items of which K are successes."""
    return Fraction(comb(K, k) * comb(N - K, n - k), comb(N, n))


def multinomial_pmf(xs, ps):
    """Multinomial probability of counts xs with cell probabilities ps."""
    return multinomial(*xs) * prod(p ** x for p, x in zip(ps, xs))

13. Exercises with solutions

Exercise 1. Multiplication rule with a restriction

How many 4-digit numbers (1000 to 9999) have all digits different? How many of them are even?

Show solution

All different: the first digit cannot be 0 (9 choices), then 9, 8, 7 for the rest: 9 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 4,536.

Even: the last digit must be 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8, and the first digit must not be 0, so the choices interact. Split by cases (addition rule). Last digit 0: the first digit has 9 choices, then 8 and 7: 9 × 8 × 7 = 504. Last digit 2, 4, 6 or 8 (4 ways): the first digit cannot be 0 or the last digit, 8 choices, then 8 and 7: 4 × 8 × 8 × 7 = 1,792. Total 2,296. Choosing the most constrained position first and splitting into cases is the usual fix when the number of choices at a stage depends on earlier choices.

Exercise 2. Permutation or combination?

A club of 12 members must (a) choose a president, secretary and treasurer, and (b) choose a 3-person social committee. Count each.

Show solution

(a) The roles are distinct, so order matters: P(12, 3) = 12 × 11 × 10 = 1,320. (b) Committee members are interchangeable: C(12, 3) = 1,320/3! = 220.

Exercise 3. Stirling in practice

Use Stirling's formula to estimate the number of decimal digits of 1000!, and compare with the exact count.

Show solution

log10(1000!) ≈ ½ log10(2π · 1000) + 1000 log10(1000/e) = 1.8991 + 1000 × (3 − 0.43429) = 1.8991 + 2565.7055 = 2567.6046. The number of digits is ⌊2567.6046⌋ + 1 = 2,568, which matches len(str(math.factorial(1000))). The Robbins correction 1/(12 000) shifts the logarithm by only 3.6 × 10−5.9

Exercise 4. Committees with a constraint

From 6 women and 5 men, how many 4-person committees have at least one woman? How many have exactly 2 of each?

Show solution

At least one woman, by the complement: C(11, 4) − C(5, 4) = 330 − 5 = 325. Exactly two of each: C(6, 2) × C(5, 2) = 15 × 10 = 150.

A tempting wrong answer to the first part is “choose one woman (6 ways), then any 3 of the remaining 10 (120 ways), giving 720.” This counts a committee with two women twice, once for each woman designated as “the” woman, and so on. Overcounting of this kind is what the complement or inclusion–exclusion avoids.

Exercise 5. Binomial coefficients

(a) Find the coefficient of x3 in (2x − 1)7. (b) Show that ∑k k C(n, k) = n 2n−1 by counting committees with a chair.

Show solution

(a) The term with x3 is C(7, 3)(2x)3(−1)4 = 35 × 8 x3, so the coefficient is 280.

(b) Count pairs (committee, chair) from n people, where the committee is any non-empty subset and the chair is one of its members. Grouped by committee size k: C(n, k) committees, each with k possible chairs, so ∑ k C(n, k). Choosing the chair first: n ways, then any subset of the other n − 1 people joins: 2n−1. Both count the same set. For n = 10 both sides equal 5,120. Dividing by 2n gives the mean of the Binomial(n, ½) distribution, n/2.

Exercise 6. Multinomial coefficients

(a) How many distinct arrangements of the letters of BOOKKEEPER are there? (b) In how many of them are the three E's together?

Show solution

(a) BOOKKEEPER has 10 letters: B 1, O 2, K 2, E 3, P 1, R 1. Count = 10!/(1! 2! 2! 3! 1! 1!) = 3,628,800/24 = 151,200.

(b) Glue the three E's into one block “EEE”. Now arrange 8 units (B, O, O, K, K, EEE, P, R) with O and K each repeated twice: 8!/(2! 2!) = 10,080.

Exercise 7. Inclusion–exclusion

In a group of 40 students, 22 study French, 18 Spanish and 10 German; 8 study French and Spanish, 5 French and German, 4 Spanish and German, and 2 all three. How many study none of the three?

Show solution

|F ∪ S ∪ G| = (22 + 18 + 10) − (8 + 5 + 4) + 2 = 50 − 17 + 2 = 35. None: 40 − 35 = 5.

Exercise 8. Secret Santa

Eight friends put their names in a hat and each draws one at random. What is the probability that nobody draws their own name? That exactly one person does?

Show solution

A draw is a uniformly random permutation of 8 names. Nobody draws their own: D8/8! = 14,833/40,320 = 0.3679.

Exactly one fixed point: choose the person (8 ways) and derange the other 7 (D7 = 1,854): 8 × 1,854/40,320 = 14,832/40,320 = 0.3679. The two answers differ by only 1/40,320. In general, the number of fixed points of a random permutation is approximately Poisson with mean 1.1,11

Exercise 9. Stars and bars

A bakery sells 6 kinds of doughnut. (a) How many different boxes of 10 doughnuts are possible? (b) How many if each box must contain at least one of each kind?

Show solution

(a) Multisets of size 10 from 6 types: C(10 + 6 − 1, 10) = C(15, 5) = 3,003.

(b) Put one of each kind in first (6 doughnuts), then choose the remaining 4 freely: C(4 + 6 − 1, 4) = C(9, 5) = 126.

Exercise 10. With or without replacement?

An urn holds 6 red and 4 blue balls. Three are drawn. Find the probability of exactly 2 red (a) without replacement and (b) with replacement. Which is larger, and why?

Show solution

(a) Hypergeometric: C(6, 2) C(4, 1)/C(10, 3) = 15 × 4/120 = 0.5.

(b) Binomial with p = 0.6: C(3, 2)(0.6)2(0.4) = 3 × 0.36 × 0.4 = 0.432.

Without replacement the result is pulled toward the urn's own proportions: drawing a red makes the next red less likely, which suppresses the extremes (0 or 3 red) and puts more probability on the middle values. This is the same effect as the finite population correction in the variance.3

Exercise 11. Programming

Write a function that counts the 5-card hands containing at least one card of every suit, using inclusion–exclusion, and verify it by brute force with itertools.combinations.

Show solution 
from math import comb
from itertools import combinations

def all_suits(hand=5):
    # subtract hands missing at least one suit
    return sum((-1)**j * comb(4, j) * comb(52 - 13*j, hand) for j in range(5))

deck = [(r, s) for r in range(13) for s in range(4)]
brute = sum(1 for h in combinations(deck, 5) if len({s for _, s in h}) == 4)
print(all_suits(), brute, all_suits() / comb(52, 5))
