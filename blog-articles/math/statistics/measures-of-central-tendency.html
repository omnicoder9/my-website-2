1What “centre” means

A measure of central tendency, also called a measure of location, summarises a set of numbers with a single typical value. For a symmetric, unimodal distribution such as the normal, the mean, median and mode coincide, and the choice hardly matters1. Real data are often skewed, multimodal or contaminated by gross errors, and then the various measures disagree. Choosing among them is a modelling decision.

Three questions guide that choice:

  • What is the scale of measurement? The mode works for nominal categories, the median needs only an ordering, and means need interval or ratio scales. The geometric and harmonic means need strictly positive ratio-scale data.
  • What loss do you want to minimise? The mean minimises the sum of squared deviations and the median minimises the sum of absolute deviations2,3. Each is the “best single number” under a different definition of error.
  • How much should one wild value be allowed to move the answer? This is the question of robustness, made precise by the breakdown point4,5 and the influence function6.

2The running example

Most sections use one small, illustrative data set: twelve simple reaction times in milliseconds from a single participant. Eleven trials are ordinary; on one trial the participant was distracted and responded after 940 ms.

3Arithmetic mean

Definition. For observations x1, …, xn, the sample (arithmetic) mean is x̄ = 1n ∑i=1n xi(1) and the population mean of a random variable X is its expectation, μ = E[X]2.

Properties

  • Balance point. Deviations from the mean sum to zero, ∑(xi − x̄) = 0, so x̄ is the centre of mass of the data2.
  • Least squares. x̄ is the unique value of c that minimises ∑(xi − c)²2. Expanding gives ∑(xi − c)² = ∑(xi − x̄)² + n(x̄ − c)², which is smallest at c = x̄.
  • Unbiased, with known variance. For independent observations with mean μ and variance σ², E[x̄] = μ and Var(x̄) = σ²/n2. Under normality x̄ is the minimum-variance unbiased estimator of μ10.
  • Linear and algebraically convenient. The mean of a + bX is a + bx̄, and group means combine through the weighted mean (Section 4).

Weaknesses

Every observation enters with equal weight and no bound, so a single value can carry the mean anywhere: its finite-sample breakdown point is 1/n, which tends to 03,5. In the running example one lapse moves the mean 57 ms above the median. For heavy-tailed distributions the problem is worse. The Cauchy distribution has no mean, and the sample mean of Cauchy data has the same distribution as a single observation, so averaging more data does not help1. Tukey showed that even a few per cent of contamination by a wider normal distribution can make the mean less efficient than simple alternatives9.

Example 3.1

For the reaction times, ∑xi = 3654, so x̄ = 3654 / 12 = 304.5 ms. Without the lapse, the eleven ordinary trials sum to 2714 and average 246.7 ms.

Numerical note Summing floating-point numbers naively accumulates round-off error. Python’s math.fsum returns a correctly rounded sum, and statistics.fmean uses it internally11. For very long streams, Welford-style running updates x̄k = x̄k−1 + (xk − x̄k−1)/k avoid storing the data.

4Weighted mean

Definition. With non-negative weights wi, not all zero, x̄w = ∑ wi xi∑ wi(2) The ordinary mean is the special case of equal weights.

Weights arise for three distinct reasons, and the interpretation of the result differs in each:

  1. Frequency or importance weights. A grade computed from components worth 20%, 30% and 50%; a mean computed from a frequency table where wi counts how often xi occurs.
  2. Combining group means. The overall mean of groups of sizes nj with means x̄j is ∑njx̄j / ∑nj. Averaging the group means without weights is a frequent error.
  3. Sampling weights. In survey sampling each sampled unit is weighted by the inverse of its probability of selection, so that the weighted mean estimates the population mean without bias12.
  4. Precision weights. When measurements have different variances σi², weighting by wi = 1/σi² gives the minimum-variance unbiased linear combination; this is the basis of inverse-variance (fixed-effect) meta-analysis2.
Example 4.1 · Course grade

Homework 85 (weight 0.20), midterm 72 (0.30), final 90 (0.50): x̄w = 0.20·85 + 0.30·72 + 0.50·90 = 17 + 21.6 + 45 = 83.6. The unweighted mean of the three scores is 82.3.

Every other estimator in this article can be seen as a weighted mean with data-dependent weights: trimmed means give weight zero to extremes, the median gives all weight to the middle order statistic(s), and M-estimators (Section 12) choose weights iteratively from the residuals.

5Geometric mean

Definition. For strictly positive xi, GM = (∏ xi)1/n = exp(1n ∑ ln xi)(3) It is the arithmetic mean on the log scale, transformed back.

The geometric mean is the natural centre for quantities that combine by multiplication: growth factors, ratios, concentrations and other data whose logarithms are roughly symmetric. For log-normally distributed data, the population geometric mean eμ is also the population median7. Limpert, Stahel and Abbt argue that many biological, environmental and economic measurements are better summarised by the geometric mean and a multiplicative standard deviation than by the arithmetic mean and standard deviation7.

Example 5.1 · Average growth rate

An investment returns +50%, −20% and +10% in three years. The growth factors are 1.5, 0.8 and 1.1, whose product is 1.32. The constant annual factor giving the same result is 1.321/3 = 1.0970, an average return of 9.70% per year. The arithmetic mean of the returns, 13.33%, would predict 1.1333³ = 1.456 and overstate the final value by 10%.

The geometric mean has a useful invariance: the GM of ratios xi/yi equals GM(x)/GM(y), so the ranking of systems by the GM of normalised scores does not depend on which reference system is used for normalisation. Fleming and Wallace used this property to argue that normalised benchmark results should be summarised with the geometric mean, never the arithmetic mean13.

CaveatsThe GM is undefined for zero or negative values. Adding an arbitrary constant before taking logs (“log(x + 1)”) changes the answer and should be reported explicitly. Compute through logs, as in equation (3), because the raw product overflows or underflows for long series.

6Harmonic mean

Definition. For strictly positive xi, HM = n∑ 1/xi,   weighted: HMw = ∑ wi∑ wi/xi(4) It is the reciprocal of the mean of reciprocals.

The harmonic mean is the right average for rates when the quantity held fixed is in the rate’s numerator: speeds over equal distances, prices per unit when equal amounts of money are spent, or throughput over equal amounts of work14. In information retrieval, the F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, which penalises a classifier that does well on one and badly on the other15.

Example 6.1 · Average speed

A car covers 60 km at 60 km/h and then 60 km at 40 km/h. Total time is 1 h + 1.5 h = 2.5 h for 120 km, so the average speed is 48 km/h, the harmonic mean 2/(1/60 + 1/40). The arithmetic mean, 50 km/h, would be correct only if equal times were spent at each speed.

The Pythagorean means and their inequality

The arithmetic, geometric and harmonic means are the classical “Pythagorean means”. For positive data they always satisfy min xi ≤ HM ≤ GM ≤ AM ≤ max xi(5) with equality throughout if and only if all xi are equal14,16. All three are members of the power-mean family Mp = (∑xip/n)1/p with p = 1, 0 (as a limit) and −1, and Mp is non-decreasing in p16. For two numbers, GM² = AM × HM14. In the reaction-time data, HM = 261.2 ≤ GM = 275.0 ≤ AM = 304.5, as required.

7Median

Definition. Sort the data to get the order statistics x(1) ≤ … ≤ x(n). The sample median is med = x((n+1)/2)  (n odd),    med = ½ (x(n/2) + x(n/2+1))  (n even)(6) A population median m is any value with P(X ≤ m) ≥ ½ and P(X ≥ m) ≥ ½2.
  • Least absolute deviations. The median minimises ∑|xi − c|2,3. Moving c one unit away from the middle increases the distance to more than half the points and decreases it to fewer than half.
  • Maximal robustness. At least half the observations must be moved to make the median arbitrarily large, so its breakdown point is 50%, the highest possible for a translation-equivariant estimator3,5.
  • The price: efficiency at the normal. For normal data the median’s asymptotic variance is πσ²/(2n), so its efficiency relative to the mean is 2/π ≈ 0.6373,10. A median from 157 normal observations is about as precise as a mean from 100.
  • Equivariance. The median commutes with any monotone transformation: med(log X) = log med(X). The mean does not.
  • Distance from the mean. For any distribution with finite variance, |μ − m| ≤ σ17.
Example 7.1

The reaction-time sample has n = 12, so the median is the average of the 6th and 7th values: (244 + 251)/2 = 247.5 ms. Replacing 940 by 9400, or by 290, leaves it unchanged.

The median is the 0.5 quantile. Software packages compute sample quantiles in at least nine different ways, which agree at the median for most definitions but differ for quartiles18. This matters for the quartile-based estimators in Section 12. Python’s statistics.quantiles, for example, defaults to the “exclusive” method11, and median_low / median_high return an actual data value when n is even, which is useful for ordinal data that cannot be averaged.

8Mode

Definition. The mode of a discrete or categorical sample is the most frequent value. For a continuous distribution it is a point where the density reaches a local (the modal) or global maximum.

The mode is the only measure in this article that applies to nominal data (“the most common blood type”). It is always an actual attainable value. It is also the least stable: a sample may have no repeated values, several tied values (bimodal, multimodal), or a mode that jumps when one observation changes. Since Python 3.8, statistics.mode returns the first mode encountered when there are ties, and statistics.multimode returns all of them11.

For continuous data the sample mode must be estimated. The standard approach smooths the data with a kernel density estimate and takes its maximum; Parzen proved this estimator is consistent19. Grouped data are sometimes summarised by the midpoint of the modal class, a crude version of the same idea.

Example 8.1

Shoe sizes sold in a morning: 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10. Both 8 and 9 appear twice, so the data are bimodal with modes {8, 9}. statistics.mode returns 8, statistics.multimode returns [8, 9]. The twelve reaction times are all distinct, so every value ties for most frequent and the mode carries no information.

For moderately skewed unimodal distributions, Karl Pearson’s empirical rule states that mean − mode ≈ 3(mean − median); Doodson gave a derivation for a family of skewed frequency curves20. It is an approximation, not a law, and the related rule that the median lies between the mode and the mean fails surprisingly often8.

9Trimmed mean

Definition. For a trimming proportion 0 ≤ α < ½, let g = ⌊αn⌋. The α-trimmed mean discards the g smallest and g largest observations and averages the rest: x̄α = 1n − 2g ∑i=g+1n−g x(i)(7) α = 0 gives the mean; α → ½ approaches the median. The 25% trimmed mean is Tukey’s midmean1,21.

Trimming is the simplest way to interpolate between the efficiency of the mean and the robustness of the median. Its breakdown point is α3. At the normal distribution the asymptotic efficiency relative to the mean is about 0.94 for α = 0.10 and 0.87 for α = 0.20 (computed from the asymptotic variance in3), while under heavy tails it can be far more efficient than the mean22. The Princeton robustness study compared a large number of location estimators by simulation and found trimmed means competitive across a wide range of distributions23. Stigler compared eleven estimators on real historical data: determinations of the solar parallax (1761), the mean density of the earth (1798) and the speed of light (around 1880), judged against modern values of those constants. Light trimming improved on the sample mean, although the mean held up well against many newer proposals24. Wilcox recommends 20% trimming as a default for inference with skewed or heavy-tailed data22.

Example 9.1

Reaction times, n = 12. With α = 0.10, g = ⌊1.2⌋ = 1: drop 212 and 940, average the remaining ten: 2502/10 = 250.2 ms. With α = 0.20, g = ⌊2.4⌋ = 2: drop 212, 225, 284, 940, average the middle eight: 1993/8 = 249.125 ms.

Conventions differSome texts define g by rounding rather than flooring αn, and some allow fractional trimming by giving the boundary observations partial weight. scipy.stats.trim_mean floors, as here25. Always state α and the rule used. Trimmed means estimate the population trimmed mean, which equals the mean only for symmetric distributions.

10Winsorized mean

Definition. With g = ⌊αn⌋ as before, replace each of the g smallest values by x(g+1) and each of the g largest by x(n−g), then take the ordinary mean: x̄w,α = 1n [ g·x(g+1) + ∑i=g+1n−g x(i) + g·x(n−g) ](8)

Winsorizing is named after the biostatistician Charles P. Winsor, who proposed it; Dixon studied the resulting estimators and Tukey popularised the name26,27. Where trimming deletes extremes, winsorizing clamps them, so the sample size is kept and the extremes still count as “large” or “small”, just not by how much. The breakdown point is again α. The winsorized sample is also the basis of the winsorized variance, which is the correct ingredient for the standard error of a trimmed mean22.

Example 10.1

Reaction times, α = 0.20, g = 2: replace 212 and 225 by 231, and 284 and 940 by 270. The sum becomes 2·231 + 1993 + 2·270 = 2995, so the 20% winsorized mean is 2995/12 = 249.58 ms.

11Midrange

Definition. MR = ½ (x(1) + x(n))(9)

The midrange is the average of the two most extreme points, the opposite of the median. It uses only those two points and is therefore the least robust estimator here: its breakdown point is 1/n and one outlier moves it by half the outlier’s distance1. In the reaction-time data it is 576 ms, a value no trial came close to.

It nonetheless has a legitimate use. For short-tailed distributions, where extremes are the most informative observations, the midrange beats the mean. For a uniform distribution on (θ − ½, θ + ½), the variance of the midrange is 1/(2(n+1)(n+2)), of order 1/n² rather than the 1/n of the mean10,28. The simulation in Section 13 shows a ninefold efficiency gain over the mean for uniform samples of 50, and near-total failure for every heavier-tailed case.

12Robust measures of location

Robust statistics asks for estimators that perform nearly as well as the best classical method when the model is right and degrade gracefully when it is slightly wrong3,29. Two tools make “gracefully” measurable.

Breakdown point and influence function

  • Breakdown point ε*: the smallest fraction of observations that, replaced by arbitrary values, can carry the estimate to infinity4,5. Mean and midrange: 0. α-trimmed and α-winsorized means: α. Median: ½.
  • Influence function IF(x): the effect on the estimate of adding a small amount of contamination at the point x6. A robust estimator has a bounded influence function. For the mean, IF(x) = x − μ, which is unbounded.

Quartile-based L-estimators

L-estimators are linear combinations of order statistics; the median, trimmed and winsorized means and the midrange are all L-estimators. Two more come from Tukey’s exploratory data analysis, using the lower and upper quartiles, which Tukey calls hinges21,30:

Midhinge = ½ (Q1 + Q3),    Trimean = ¼ (Q1 + 2Q2 + Q3)(10)

Both have breakdown point 25%. The trimean blends the median’s resistance with information from the quartiles. For the reaction times, Tukey’s hinges are Q1 = 234.5 and Q3 = 266.5, giving a midhinge of 250.5 and a trimean of (234.5 + 495 + 266.5)/4 = 249.0 ms. Other quartile definitions18 give slightly different values.

The Hodges–Lehmann estimator (an R-estimator)

Form all n(n+1)/2 Walsh averages (xi + xj)/2 for i ≤ j; the Hodges–Lehmann estimator is their median31. It is derived by inverting the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. At the normal distribution its efficiency relative to the mean is 3/π ≈ 0.955, it is never less than 0.864 for any symmetric distribution, and it can be arbitrarily more efficient for heavy tails31. Its breakdown point is 1 − 1/√2 ≈ 0.2933. For the reaction times there are 78 Walsh averages and their median is 250.25 ms.

M-estimators: Huber and Tukey’s biweight

Huber generalised maximum likelihood by choosing the location T that solves ∑ ψ(xi − Ts) = 0(11) where s is a robust scale estimate and ψ is a chosen function32. ψ(u) = u gives the mean and ψ(u) = sign(u) gives the median. The influence function of an M-estimator is proportional to ψ, so the shape of ψ is the shape of its sensitivity to each observation6,29.

  • Huber’s ψ is linear near zero and clipped at ±k: ψ(u) = max(−k, min(k, u)). Huber showed it is minimax-optimal for a normal distribution with a fraction of arbitrary contamination32. With k = 1.345 it has 95% efficiency at the normal33.
  • Tukey’s biweight (bisquare) ψ(u) = u(1 − (u/c)²)² for |u| ≤ c and 0 beyond34. It is redescending: observations far enough away are ignored entirely. c = 4.685 gives 95% normal efficiency33.

The usual scale for s is the normalised median absolute deviation, MADN = 1.4826 · med|xi − med(x)|; the constant makes it consistent for σ under normality, and its breakdown point is 50%6,35. Equation (11) is solved by iteratively reweighted means: start at the median, give each point weight wi = ψ(ui)/ui, take the weighted mean, and repeat until it settles33. For the reaction times, MADN = 23.72 ms, the Huber estimate is 249.99 ms and the biweight estimate is 246.52 ms; the biweight gives the 940 ms lapse zero weight.

Which robust estimator?No single estimator is best for every distribution23. Reasonable defaults: the median when you need maximal resistance or have ordinal data; a 10–20% trimmed mean when you want something simple that is easy to explain and to use in tests22,24; a Huber M-estimator when you want high efficiency with bounded influence; the biweight when gross outliers are expected and should be rejected, started from a high-breakdown point such as the median because redescending estimators can have several solutions33.

A Monte Carlo experiment

Asymptotic results describe large samples from one model. A short simulation shows what happens at n = 50 under four distributions, all symmetric about 0 so every estimator targets the same value: the standard normal, a uniform (short tails), Student’s t with 3 degrees of freedom (heavy tails), and a contaminated normal where each observation has a 10% chance of coming from N(0, 10²), the model Tukey used9. Each cell is the mean squared error of the mean divided by that of the estimator, over 20 000 samples; values above 1 beat the mean. The code is in Section 14 and uses NumPy and SciPy25,36.

The pattern matches theory. At the normal, the median keeps about two thirds of the mean’s efficiency, close to 2/π, and Huber and Hodges–Lehmann lose only 5%. 14Complete source code

The module below implements every estimator in this article using only the Python standard library, so the logic is visible. In production code, prefer the library routines listed in Table 4, which are tested and vectorised.

central.py

"""central.py: measures of central tendency, standard library only."""
import math
from collections import Counter
from itertools import combinations_with_replacement


def arithmetic_mean(x):
    x = list(x)
    if not x:
        raise ValueError("empty data")
    return math.fsum(x) / len(x)          # fsum avoids round-off build-up


def weighted_mean(x, w):
    x, w = list(x), list(w)
    if len(x) != len(w) or not x:
        raise ValueError("x and w must be non-empty and equal length")
    if any(wi < 0 for wi in w) or math.fsum(w) == 0:
        raise ValueError("weights must be non-negative, not all zero")
    return math.fsum(wi * xi for xi, wi in zip(x, w)) / math.fsum(w)


def geometric_mean(x):
    x = list(x)
    if any(v <= 0 for v in x):
        raise ValueError("geometric mean needs strictly positive data")
    return math.exp(math.fsum(math.log(v) for v in x) / len(x))  # log form avoids overflow


def harmonic_mean(x):
    x = list(x)
    if any(v <= 0 for v in x):
        raise ValueError("harmonic mean needs strictly positive data")
    return len(x) / math.fsum(1 / v for v in x)


def median(x):
    s = sorted(x)
    n = len(s)
    if n == 0:
        raise ValueError("empty data")
    m = n // 2
    return s[m] if n % 2 else (s[m - 1] + s[m]) / 2


def modes(x):
    """All values sharing the highest frequency (multimodal-safe)."""
    c = Counter(x)
    top = max(c.values())
    return sorted(v for v, k in c.items() if k == top)


def trimmed_mean(x, prop=0.1):
    """Drop g = floor(prop*n) points from EACH end, average the rest."""
    s = sorted(x)
    n = len(s)
    g = int(prop * n)
    if 2 * g >= n:
        raise ValueError("trimming removes all data")
    return arithmetic_mean(s[g:n - g])


def winsorized_mean(x, prop=0.1):
    """Replace the g lowest/highest points by the nearest kept value."""
    s = sorted(x)
    n = len(s)
    g = int(prop * n)
    if 2 * g >= n:
        raise ValueError("winsorizing removes all data")
    lo, hi = s[g], s[n - g - 1]
    return arithmetic_mean([lo] * g + s[g:n - g] + [hi] * g)


def midrange(x):
    return (min(x) + max(x)) / 2


def quartiles(x):
    """Tukey hinges (median of each half, middle point included if n odd)."""
    s = sorted(x)
    n = len(s)
    h = (n + 1) // 2
    return median(s[:h]), median(s), median(s[n - h:])


def midhinge(x):
    q1, _, q3 = quartiles(x)
    return (q1 + q3) / 2


def trimean(x):
    q1, q2, q3 = quartiles(x)
    return (q1 + 2 * q2 + q3) / 4


def mad(x, normalize=True):
    """Median absolute deviation; scaled by 1.4826 to estimate sigma under normality."""
    m = median(x)
    d = median([abs(v - m) for v in x])
    return 1.482602218505602 * d if normalize else d


def hodges_lehmann(x):
    """Median of all Walsh averages (x_i + x_j)/2 with i <= j. O(n^2)."""
    x = list(x)
    return median([(a + b) / 2 for a, b in combinations_with_replacement(x, 2)])


def huber_location(x, k=1.345, tol=1e-9, max_iter=100):
    """Huber M-estimate of location with MAD scale, by iteratively reweighted means."""
    x = list(x)
    mu, s = median(x), mad(x)
    if s == 0:
        return mu
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        w = [1.0 if abs((v - mu) / s) <= k else k / abs((v - mu) / s) for v in x]
        new = weighted_mean(x, w)
        if abs(new - mu) < tol * s:
            return new
        mu = new
    return mu


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rt = [212, 225, 231, 238, 240, 244, 251, 256, 263, 270, 284, 940]
    rows = [
        ("arithmetic mean", arithmetic_mean(rt)),
        ("geometric mean", geometric_mean(rt)),
        ("harmonic mean", harmonic_mean(rt)),
        ("median", median(rt)),
        ("10% trimmed mean", trimmed_mean(rt, 0.10)),
        ("20% trimmed mean", trimmed_mean(rt, 0.20)),
        ("20% winsorized mean", winsorized_mean(rt, 0.20)),
        ("midrange", midrange(rt)),
        ("midhinge", midhinge(rt)),
        ("trimean", trimean(rt)),
        ("Hodges-Lehmann", hodges_lehmann(rt)),
        ("Huber M (k=1.345)", huber_location(rt)),
    ]
    for name, val in rows:
        print(f"{name:<22}{val:10.2f}")

"""sim.py: Monte Carlo comparison of location estimators (numpy + scipy)."""
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(20261026)
N, REPS = 50, 20_000


def huber(x, k=1.345, iters=50):
    mu = np.median(x, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    s = stats.median_abs_deviation(x, axis=1, scale="normal")[:, None]
    for _ in range(iters):
        u = np.abs(x - mu) / s
        w = np.where(u <= k, 1.0, k / np.maximum(u, 1e-12))
        mu = (w * x).sum(1, keepdims=True) / w.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    return mu.ravel()


def hodges_lehmann(x):
    i, j = np.triu_indices(x.shape[1])
    return np.median((x[:, i] + x[:, j]) / 2, axis=1)


def winsor_mean(x, prop):
    s = np.sort(x, axis=1)
    g = int(prop * x.shape[1])
    s[:, :g] = s[:, [g]]
    s[:, -g:] = s[:, [-g - 1]]
    return s.mean(1)


ESTIMATORS = {
    "Mean": lambda x: x.mean(1),
    "Median": lambda x: np.median(x, 1),
    "10% trimmed": lambda x: stats.trim_mean(x, 0.1, axis=1),
    "20% trimmed": lambda x: stats.trim_mean(x, 0.2, axis=1),
    "20% winsorized": lambda x: winsor_mean(x, 0.2),
    "Midrange": lambda x: (x.min(1) + x.max(1)) / 2,
    "Hodges-Lehmann": hodges_lehmann,
    "Huber (k=1.345)": huber,
}

DISTRIBUTIONS = {
    "Normal": lambda: rng.standard_normal((REPS, N)),
    "Uniform": lambda: rng.uniform(-1, 1, (REPS, N)),
    "t (3 df)": lambda: rng.standard_t(3, (REPS, N)),
    "10% contaminated": lambda: np.where(rng.random((REPS, N)) < 0.1,
                                          10 * rng.standard_normal((REPS, N)),
                                          rng.standard_normal((REPS, N))),

# All distributions are symmetric about 0, so MSE = variance of the estimator.
table = {}
for dname, draw in DISTRIBUTIONS.items():
    x = draw()
    mse = {e: np.mean(f(x) ** 2) for e, f in ESTIMATORS.items()}
    ref = mse["Mean"]
    table[dname] = {e: ref / m for e, m in mse.items()}   # efficiency vs. the mean

print(f"{'':<17}" + "".join(f"{d:>18}" for d in DISTRIBUTIONS))
for e in ESTIMATORS:
    print(f"{e:<17}" + "".join(f"{table[d][e]:>18.2f}" for d in DISTRIBUTIONS))

15Exercises with solutions

  1. For the data 4, 8, 15, 16, 23, 42, compute the mean, median, midrange and mode.

    Show solution

    Sum = 108, so the mean is 108/6 = 18. With n = 6 the median is (15 + 16)/2 = 15.5. Midrange = (4 + 42)/2 = 23. Every value occurs once, so there is no informative mode. The mean exceeds the median because 42 is far from the rest.

  2. A student earns grades of 3.7, 3.0 and 4.0 in courses worth 3, 4 and 2 credits. Compute the grade-point average.

    Show solution

    Weighted mean with credits as weights: (3·3.7 + 4·3.0 + 2·4.0)/(3 + 4 + 2) = (11.1 + 12 + 8)/9 = 31.1/9 = 3.456. The unweighted mean, 3.567, ignores that the 3.0 course carried the most credit.

  3. A fund gains 20% one year and loses 20% the next. What is its average annual return? Explain why the arithmetic mean of 0% is misleading.

    Show solution

    The growth factors are 1.2 and 0.8. GM = √(1.2 × 0.8) = √0.96 = 0.9798, an average return of −2.02% per year. After two years, $100 is worth $96. The arithmetic mean treats returns as additive, but returns compound multiplicatively; by inequality (5), the arithmetic mean of the factors (1.0) always overstates the geometric mean unless all returns are equal.

  4. An investor spends $100 on shares in each of three months, at prices $20, $25 and $50 per share. What is the average price paid per share?

    Show solution

    Shares bought: 5 + 4 + 2 = 11 for $300, so the average price is 300/11 = $27.27. This is the harmonic mean 3/(1/20 + 1/25 + 1/50) = 3/0.11. Equal money is spent each time, and price is money per share, so the harmonic mean applies. The arithmetic mean of the prices, $31.67, would be correct only if an equal number of shares were bought each time.

  5. A cyclist rides up a 10 km hill at 15 km/h and back down at 45 km/h. What is the average speed for the round trip?

    Show solution

    Times: 10/15 = 0.667 h and 10/45 = 0.222 h, total 0.889 h for 20 km, so 22.5 km/h. This is the harmonic mean 2/(1/15 + 1/45). The arithmetic mean, 30 km/h, overstates it because more time is spent at the lower speed.

  6. For the sample 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 13, 14, 60, compute the mean, median, midrange, 20% trimmed mean and 20% winsorized mean.

    Show solution

    Mean = 135/10 = 13.5; median = (8 + 9)/2 = 8.5; midrange = (3 + 60)/2 = 31.5. With g = ⌊0.2 × 10⌋ = 2, the trimmed mean averages 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 13: 53/6 = 8.83. Winsorizing gives 6, 6, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 13, 13, 13, sum 91, mean 9.10. See Figure 4.

  7. For the twelve reaction times, how many observations can be replaced by arbitrarily large values before each of these estimators can be made arbitrarily large: mean, midrange, 10% trimmed mean, 20% trimmed mean, median?

    Show solution

    Mean: 0 (one bad value is enough). Midrange: 0. 10% trimmed: g = 1, so 1 can be absorbed and 2 break it. 20% trimmed: g = 2, so 2 are absorbed. Median: with n = 12 it averages the 6th and 7th order statistics, so 5 large values leave both untouched; with 6 the 7th becomes arbitrary. The finite-sample breakdown points are therefore 1/12, 1/12, 2/12, 3/12 and 6/12.

  8. Compute the Hodges–Lehmann estimate for the sample 1, 2, 3, 10 by hand. Compare it with the mean and median.

    Show solution

    The 10 Walsh averages (i ≤ j) are 1, 1.5, 2, 5.5, 2, 2.5, 6, 3, 6.5, 10. Sorted: 1, 1.5, 2, 2, 2.5, 3, 5.5, 6, 6.5, 10. Their median is (2.5 + 3)/2 = 2.75. The mean is 4 and the median is 2.5. HL sits close to the median because the Walsh averages involving 10 are in the upper tail.

  9. Prove that c = x̄ minimises S(c) = ∑(xi − c)², and that any median minimises A(c) = ∑|xi − c|.

    Show solution

    Mean. S′(c) = −2∑(xi − c) = −2(nx̄ − nc), which is zero only at c = x̄, and S″ = 2n > 0, so it is the unique minimum.

    Median. A is convex and piecewise linear. Away from data points, A′(c) = #{xi < c} − #{xi > c}. This slope is negative while fewer than half the points lie below c and positive once more than half do, so the minimum is reached where the counts balance: at the middle order statistic when n is odd, and anywhere between the two middle ones when n is even. The conventional median (their average) is one of these minimisers.

  10. Show that for two positive numbers, GM² = AM × HM. Verify with a = 8, b = 2.

    Show solution

    AM × HM = ((a + b)/2) × (2ab/(a + b)) = ab = GM². For 8 and 2: AM = 5, HM = 3.2, product 16 = 4² = GM². This is the similar-triangles relation in Figure 3.

  11. Shoe sizes 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10 were sold. What does statistics.mode return, and what is a better summary?

    Show solution

    Since Python 3.8 it returns 8, the first of the tied values encountered11. The data are bimodal, so report both modes with statistics.multimode, which returns [8, 9].

  12. Programming: adapt huber_location in central.py to compute Tukey’s biweight location with c = 4.685, and apply it to the reaction times. Why does the 940 ms value get exactly zero weight?

    Show solution 
    from central import median, mad, weighted_mean


def biweight_location(x, c=4.685, tol=1e-9, max_iter=100):
    """Tukey biweight M-estimate of location (MAD scale held fixed)."""
    x = list(x)
    mu, s = median(x), mad(x)
    if s == 0:
        return mu
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        u = [(v - mu) / (c * s) for v in x]
        w = [(1 - ui * ui) ** 2 if abs(ui) < 1 else 0.0 for ui in u]
        new = weighted_mean(x, w)
        if abs(new - mu) < tol * s:
            return new
        mu = new
    return mu


rt = [212, 225, 231, 238, 240, 244, 251, 256, 263, 270, 284, 940]
print(round(biweight_location(rt), 2))

    Output: 246.52. The biweight weight is w(u) = (1 − u²)² for |u| < 1 with u = (x − T)/(c·s), and 0 otherwise. With MADN s = 23.72, the cut-off is c·s ≈ 111 ms from the estimate, and 940 ms is about 690 ms away, so it is rejected. The estimate is slightly below the median because 284 ms, the largest ordinary value, is also down-weighted.
