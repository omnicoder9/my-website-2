Every statistical analysis rests on decisions made before any formula is applied: which group of things we want to learn about, which of those things we actually observed, what we recorded about each one, and how well the recorded numbers reflect what we meant to capture. Errors at this stage cannot be repaired by sophisticated modelling later. This article sets out the core concepts of data and measurement in the order an analyst meets them, from defining a population to diagnosing missing values and measurement error.

1Populations and samples

A population is the complete set of units about which we want to draw conclusions. A sample is the subset of that population we actually observe [1]. Survey statisticians separate the target population (the group we want to describe, such as all adults living in a country on a given date) from the sampling frame (the list or procedure from which the sample is actually drawn, such as a register of addresses). Units in the target population that are missing from the frame create undercoverage, a source of bias that a larger sample does not fix [1].

Populations can be finite and enumerable (the 2,400 invoices issued by a firm last year) or conceptual and effectively infinite (all future items a production process could make). In the second case the "population" is better thought of as the probability distribution that generates the data, and inference is about the parameters of that distribution [2].

Why sampling design matters

Only a probability sample, in which every unit in the frame has a known, non-zero chance of selection, supports design-based inference with quantifiable error [1]. Simple random sampling gives every subset of size n the same chance; stratified, cluster and multistage designs trade simplicity for precision or cost. Convenience samples, such as volunteers who answer an online poll, have unknown selection probabilities, so their error cannot be quantified from the design alone [1].

Example

A hospital wants the mean length of stay of all patients discharged in 2025 (target population: every 2025 discharge). It draws 300 records at random from its discharge database (the frame). If day-case patients were never entered into that database, they are outside the frame. The sample mean will then overstate the population mean however large n becomes.

2Observations and experimental units

An observational unit (or case) is the entity on which measurements are taken, and one observation is the set of values recorded for one unit at one occasion. In the tidy data convention, each observation forms a row and each variable forms a column [3].

In a designed experiment the experimental unit is the smallest entity to which a treatment is independently assigned [4,5]. It need not be the same as the observational unit. If a fertiliser is applied to whole field plots and yield is measured on ten plants per plot, the plot is the experimental unit and the plants are observational units, sometimes called subsamples. Treating the plants as independent replicates inflates the apparent sample size. This error is called pseudoreplication, and it produces standard errors that are too small [4].

3Variables, attributes, and parameters

A variable is a characteristic that can take different values across units or over time, such as age, diagnosis or income. The word attribute is used in two ways. In database and machine-learning usage it is a synonym for variable (a column, or "feature"). In classical quality control and sampling, "sampling by attributes" means recording a qualitative characteristic, typically whether an item conforms or not, as opposed to "sampling by variables", where a numerical measurement is recorded [4]. Context decides which meaning applies.

A parameter is a numerical characteristic of the population or of the probability model that generated the data: the population mean μ, the variance σ², a proportion π, or a regression coefficient β [2]. A parameter describes the population as a whole. A variable describes individual units.

Keep these apart

Variable: "systolic blood pressure" takes one value for each patient. Parameter: "the mean systolic blood pressure of all adults in the region" is a single fixed number. Statistic: "the mean systolic blood pressure of the 200 adults we measured" is a single number that would change if we drew a different 200.

4Statistics versus parameters

Formally, a statistic is any function of the sample data that does not depend on unknown parameters [2]. When a statistic is used to estimate a parameter it is called an estimator, and the value it takes in a particular sample is an estimate. By convention, Greek letters denote parameters and Latin letters or "hatted" symbols denote statistics.

Because a statistic depends on which units happened to be sampled, it has a sampling distribution. Its standard deviation is the standard error. For the sample mean of independent observations the standard error is σ/√n, estimated by s/√n [2]. The divisor n − 1 in s² makes it an unbiased estimator of σ² [2].

Python · simulate a parameter and its statistic 
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
population = rng.normal(loc=170, scale=8, size=100_000)   # heights (cm)
mu, sigma = population.mean(), population.std()           # parameters
sample = rng.choice(population, size=50, replace=False)
xbar, s = sample.mean(), sample.std(ddof=1)               # statistics
print(f"mu = {mu:.2f}, sigma = {sigma:.2f}")
print(f"xbar = {xbar:.2f}, s = {s:.2f}, SE = {s/np.sqrt(50):.2f}")

# repeat many samples: the statistic varies, the parameter does not
means = [rng.choice(population, 50, replace=False).mean() for _ in range(2000)]
print(f"mean of 2000 sample means = {np.mean(means):.2f}, sd = {np.std(means):.2f}")

xbar = 170.68, s = 7.73, SE = 1.09

The spread of the 2,000 sample means (1.11) matches the theoretical standard error σ/√n = 7.98/√50 ≈ 1.13 closely, and their average sits on μ. This is the unbiasedness of x̄ seen empirically.

5Types of variables

Variables are first divided by whether their values are categories or numbers. The division determines which summaries, graphs and models are appropriate [6].

Categorical variables: nominal and ordinal

A categorical variable places each unit into one of a set of categories. If the categories have no natural order (blood group, species, country of birth), the variable is nominal. If the categories have a natural order but the distances between them are not defined (cancer stage I to IV, "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree"), it is ordinal [6]. Ordinal data support medians, percentiles and rank-based methods, and they can be modelled directly with cumulative-logit (proportional-odds) models, which use the ordering without pretending the categories are equally spaced [6].

Numerical variables: discrete and continuous

A numerical variable takes values for which arithmetic is meaningful. It is discrete if it can take only a finite or countably infinite set of values, usually integers, and continuous if it can in principle take any value in an interval [2]. Every recorded value is rounded to some resolution, so "continuous" describes the underlying quantity rather than the stored digits. Height recorded to the nearest centimetre is still treated as continuous.

Binary variables

A binary (dichotomous) variable has exactly two possible values, such as success or failure, or exposed or unexposed. Coded 0/1, its mean equals the proportion of 1s, which is why a binary variable can be treated as categorical or numerical depending on the purpose. The natural probability model is the Bernoulli distribution, and its regression counterpart is logistic regression [6]. Collapsing a continuous variable into a binary one (for example "high" versus "normal" blood pressure) discards information and should be justified on substantive grounds.

Count variables

A count variable records the number of times an event occurs in a fixed unit of time, space or exposure. Counts are non-negative integers, often right-skewed, and their variance usually grows with the mean. The Poisson distribution is the reference model, with variance equal to the mean. Real counts are frequently overdispersed (variance greater than mean), which motivates negative binomial and other models [7]. When exposure varies between units (person-years at risk, kilometres driven), it enters the model as an offset so that the analysis is about rates rather than raw counts [7].

6Measurement scales

Stevens proposed in 1946 that measurement is "the assignment of numerals to objects or events according to rules" and classified scales by the transformations that leave their meaning unchanged [8]. The four levels form a hierarchy: each adds a property to the one below it, and the statistics that are meaningful on a scale are those whose conclusions survive its permissible transformations.

Table 3. Properties of the four scales, after Stevens [8]

Interval versus ratio in practice

The test is whether zero means "none of the quantity". At 0 °C there is still thermal energy, so 20 °C is not "twice as hot" as 10 °C. In kelvin, whose zero is absolute, the same two temperatures differ by a ratio of only about 1.035:

Python · ratios need a true zero 
import numpy as np
c = np.array([10.0, 20.0])
k = c + 273.15
print("Celsius ratio:", c[1]/c[0], " Kelvin ratio:", round(k[1]/k[0], 3))
Celsius ratio: 2.0  Kelvin ratio: 1.035

Differences, by contrast, are meaningful on an interval scale. A rise from 10 °C to 20 °C is the same size as a rise from 20 °C to 30 °C, and the coefficient of variation (s/x̄) is meaningful only on a ratio scale, because shifting the zero changes it.

Limits of the typology

Stevens' scheme is a useful checklist but not a complete rulebook for choosing methods. Velleman and Wilkinson argue that the scale type is not a fixed property of the data. It depends on the question being asked, and many real variables (counts, percentages, bounded scores) do not fit any of the four types cleanly [9]. Hand distinguishes representational measurement, where numbers mirror empirical relations among objects, from pragmatic measurement, where the measurement procedure itself defines the attribute, as with many psychological and economic indices [10]. In practice, analysts often treat summed Likert scales with many items as approximately interval. This is defensible when the conclusion would not change under plausible monotone rescalings, and it should be checked rather than assumed.

7Cross-sectional, time-series and panel data

The same variables can be arranged along two axes: which units and which times. The arrangement determines which questions the data can answer and which dependence structures a model must handle [11].

Cross-sectional data

Cross-sectional data record many units at a single point or period of time, such as wages and education for 5,000 workers surveyed in one year. Under random sampling the observations can usually be treated as independent, and the ordering of rows carries no information [11]. Cross-sections describe how variables relate across units at one moment, but they cannot separate change within a unit from differences between units. They also cannot by themselves establish which of two associated variables came first.

Time-series data

Time-series data record one unit (a country, a firm, a sensor) at successive points in time, usually equally spaced: GDP by quarter, daily closing prices, hourly electricity demand. Order is essential. Neighbouring observations are typically correlated (autocorrelation), and series often show trend, seasonality and cycles [12,13]. Many standard procedures assume independent observations and give misleading standard errors when applied to autocorrelated series without adjustment. Time-series methods model the dependence directly, for example through autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models [13].

Longitudinal and panel data

Panel data (econometrics) and longitudinal data (biostatistics and the social sciences) both mean repeated measurements on the same units over time [14,15]. They differ from a pooled cross-section, in which a fresh independent sample is drawn at each time [11]. A panel is balanced if every unit is observed at every time, and unbalanced otherwise. Unbalanced panels are common because of attrition.

The main advantage of following the same units is the ability to study change within units. Each unit serves as its own control, so stable unobserved characteristics (ability, genetics, management quality) can be removed, for example with fixed-effects or first-difference estimators [11,15]. The cost is dependence: measurements on the same unit are correlated, and the analysis must account for it through mixed-effects models, generalised estimating equations or cluster-robust standard errors [14].

8Structured versus unstructured data

Structured data have a predefined schema: a fixed set of fields with declared types, stored in tables, spreadsheets or relational databases, and queryable directly. Everything discussed so far assumes this form. Unstructured data lack such a schema. Examples include free text (clinical notes, reviews, emails), images, audio and video. Between the two sit semi-structured data such as JSON, XML and log files, which carry tags or keys but no rigid table layout [16].

Unstructured sources are large and growing. Gandomi and Haider, citing industry estimates, report that structured data make up only about 5% of all existing data [16]. Statistical analysis of unstructured data almost always starts by measuring it, that is, converting it into structured variables. Text becomes word counts, topic proportions or sentiment scores. Images become pixel intensities or extracted features, and audio becomes spectral summaries. Each conversion is a measurement procedure in its own right, with its own validity and reliability to establish.

Example

A hospital's discharge summaries are unstructured text. An analyst who wants the smoking prevalence among discharged patients must define a rule or classifier that maps each note to a structured variable smoker ∈ {current, former, never, unknown}. The resulting variable is nominal. Its accuracy should be checked against a manually coded subsample, for example with the agreement statistics in Section 9.

Python · from unstructured text to a structured variable 
import re
import pandas as pd

notes = pd.Series([
    "Pt is a current smoker, 10 cigarettes/day.",
    "Former smoker, quit 2015.",
    "Denies tobacco use.",
    "No info on smoking history.",
])

def code_smoking(text):
    t = text.lower()
    if re.search(r"\bformer smoker\b|\bquit\b", t):
        return "former"
    if re.search(r"\bcurrent smoker\b|cigarettes/day", t):
        return "current"
    if re.search(r"denies (tobacco|smoking)|never smoked", t):
        return "never"
    return "unknown"

smoker = pd.Categorical(notes.map(code_smoking),
                        categories=["never", "former", "current", "unknown"])
print(pd.Series(smoker).value_counts())

Rules like these are brittle. "Never smoked, but former vaper" or "quit trying to cut down" would be misclassified, which is why the derived variable needs its own validation.

9Data quality, validity, and reliability

Dimensions of data quality

Data quality is multidimensional. In a widely cited study of data consumers, Wang and Strong identified 15 dimensions grouped into four categories: intrinsic quality (accuracy, objectivity, believability, reputation), contextual quality (relevance, value added, timeliness, completeness, appropriate amount), representational quality (interpretability, ease of understanding, consistency, concise representation) and accessibility (access and security) [17]. A data set can be accurate and still unfit for its purpose if it is out of date or covers the wrong population.

Python · a first-pass data quality audit 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

def audit(df, rules):
    """rules: dict of column -> function returning a boolean Series of valid values"""
    report = pd.DataFrame({
        "dtype":     df.dtypes.astype(str),
        "missing_%": df.isna().mean().mul(100).round(1),
        "n_unique":  df.nunique(),
    })
    report["invalid_n"] = [
        int((~rules[c](df[c].dropna())).sum()) if c in rules else None
        for c in df.columns
    ]
    print(f"Duplicate rows: {df.duplicated().sum()}")
    return report

rules = {
    "age":    lambda s: s.between(0, 120),
    "sex":    lambda s: s.isin(["F", "M"]),
    "sbp":    lambda s: s.between(60, 260),     # systolic BP, mmHg
}
df = pd.DataFrame({"age": [34, 51, -2, 51], "sex": ["F", "M", "X", "M"],
                   "sbp": [120, np.nan, 400, np.nan]})

Validity

Validity is the degree to which a measurement captures the construct it is intended to capture, and therefore whether the inferences drawn from it are justified. Messick's influential account treats validity as a single, unified concept centred on construct validity, supported by several kinds of evidence: content, substantive, structural, generalisability, external and consequential [18]. In applied work the traditional labels remain useful:

  • Content validity: the items cover the domain, so a depression scale should not omit sleep and appetite.
  • Criterion validity: scores agree with a gold standard measured at the same time (concurrent) or later (predictive).
  • Construct validity: scores relate to other variables as theory predicts. They should correlate with measures of related constructs (convergent) and not with unrelated ones (discriminant).

Validity also applies to whole studies. Internal validity concerns whether an observed association reflects a causal effect in the study sample. External validity concerns whether it generalises to other people, settings and times [19].

Reliability

Reliability is the consistency of a measurement: the extent to which repeated measurements of the same unit under the same conditions agree. In classical test theory an observed score X is the sum of a true score T and random error E, and reliability is the share of observed variance due to true scores:

X = T + E,    reliability = σ²T / σ²X = σ²T / (σ²T + σ²E)

Different designs estimate different facets of reliability:

Table 4. Common reliability coefficients
FacetQuestionStatistic
Internal consistencyDo the items of a scale measure the same thing?Cronbach's α [20]
Inter-rater (categorical)Do two raters assign the same category beyond chance?Cohen's κ [21]
Inter-rater or test–retest (numerical)Do repeated measurements of a unit agree?Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) [22,23]
Method comparisonCan a new instrument replace an old one?Bland–Altman limits of agreement [24]

Cronbach's alpha for a scale of k items is

α = [k / (k − 1)] · [1 − Σσ²i / σ²total]

where σ²i are the item variances and σ²total is the variance of the summed score [20]. Cohen's kappa corrects raw agreement po for the agreement pe expected by chance from the raters' marginal frequencies: κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe) [21]. Landis and Koch's often-quoted benchmarks label 0.41–0.60 "moderate" and 0.61–0.80 "substantial" agreement [25]. For the ICC, Koo and Li suggest values below 0.5, 0.5–0.75, 0.75–0.9 and above 0.9 indicate poor, moderate, good and excellent reliability, and stress that the ICC form (model, type and definition) must always be reported [23].

Python · Cronbach's alpha and Cohen's kappa from first principles 
import numpy as np

def cronbach_alpha(items):
    """items: n_respondents x k_items array"""
    items = np.asarray(items, dtype=float)
    k = items.shape[1]
    item_var = items.var(axis=0, ddof=1).sum()
    total_var = items.sum(axis=1).var(ddof=1)
    return k / (k - 1) * (1 - item_var / total_var)

def cohen_kappa(r1, r2):
    r1, r2 = np.asarray(r1), np.asarray(r2)
    cats = np.union1d(r1, r2)
    p_o = np.mean(r1 == r2)
    p_e = sum(np.mean(r1 == c) * np.mean(r2 == c) for c in cats)
    return (p_o - p_e) / (1 - p_e)

survey = [[4, 5, 4, 4], [2, 3, 2, 3], [5, 5, 4, 5],
          [3, 3, 3, 2], [1, 2, 1, 2], [4, 4, 5, 4]]
print(f"Cronbach's alpha = {cronbach_alpha(survey):.3f}")

rater_a = ["pos","pos","neg","neg","pos","neg","pos","pos","neg","pos"]
rater_b = ["pos","neg","neg","neg","pos","neg","pos","pos","pos","pos"]
print(f"Raw agreement = {np.mean(np.array(rater_a) == np.array(rater_b)):.2f}")
print(f"Cohen's kappa = {cohen_kappa(rater_a, rater_b):.3f}")

Raw agreement of 80% looks high, but because both raters call most cases "pos", much of that agreement would occur by chance. Kappa of 0.58 is "moderate" on the Landis–Koch scale. In R, psych::alpha(), irr::kappa2() and irr::icc() provide these statistics with confidence intervals.

How reliability and validity relate

Random error limits how strongly a measure can correlate with anything else. Spearman showed that the observed correlation between two measures is attenuated by their unreliability, and that the correlation between the underlying true scores can be estimated as rxy / √(rxx ryy), where rxx and ryy are the reliabilities [26].

10Missing data and measurement error

Missing-data mechanisms

Rubin's framework classifies missing data by how the probability that a value is missing (the missingness indicator R) relates to the data [27,28]:

  • Missing completely at random (MCAR): missingness is unrelated to any data, observed or unobserved. A test tube dropped by accident is an example.
  • Missing at random (MAR): given the observed data, missingness does not depend on the unobserved values. If older patients skip a follow-up visit more often, but within each age group skipping is unrelated to the outcome, the outcome is MAR given age.
  • Missing not at random (MNAR): missingness depends on the unobserved value itself, even after conditioning on what is observed. People with very high incomes declining to report income is a common example.

The mechanism determines which methods give unbiased answers. Complete-case analysis (deleting incomplete rows) is unbiased for many estimands under MCAR, though it wastes information. Under MAR it can be biased, while likelihood-based methods and multiple imputation using the variables that predict missingness are valid [28,29]. Under MNAR no method that relies only on the observed data is guaranteed to be unbiased. The analysis then requires explicit assumptions about the missing values and a sensitivity analysis that varies them [28]. MAR cannot be verified from the observed data alone, so the plausibility of the assumption must be argued from subject-matter knowledge [30].

Python · how the mechanism biases a simple mean 
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 10_000
age = rng.uniform(20, 80, n)
bp = 100 + 0.6 * age + rng.normal(0, 10, n)      # systolic BP
print(f"True mean BP: {bp.mean():.1f}")

mcar = rng.random(n) < 0.30                      # 30% missing, at random
p_mar = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(age - 50) / 8))        # depends on observed age
mar = rng.random(n) < p_mar * 0.6
p_mnar = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(bp - 140) / 8))       # depends on BP itself
mnar = rng.random(n) < p_mnar * 0.8

for name, miss in [("MCAR", mcar), ("MAR", mar), ("MNAR", mnar)]:
    cc = bp[~miss].mean()                          # complete-case mean
    b1, b0 = np.polyfit(age[~miss], bp[~miss], 1)  # impute from age
    imputed = bp.copy()
    imputed[miss] = b0 + b1 * age[miss]
    print(f"{name}: {miss.mean():.0%} missing | complete-case mean {cc:.1f} "
          f"| age-based imputation {imputed.mean():.1f}")
True mean BP: 130.0
MCAR: 30% missing | complete-case mean 130.2 | age-based imputation 130.2
MAR: 30% missing | complete-case mean 127.0 | age-based imputation 130.2
MNAR: 24% missing | complete-case mean 126.1 | age-based imputation 127.8

Under MCAR both approaches recover the true mean of 130. Under MAR the complete cases under-represent older, higher-pressure patients, so their mean is biased by about 3 mmHg, and imputing from age removes the bias. Under MNAR, imputing from age helps only partly, because the missingness depends on blood pressure itself. Single regression imputation is used here only to show the bias. It understates uncertainty, and for real analyses multiple imputation, which adds appropriate random variation and combines results across imputed data sets with Rubin's rules, is preferred [29,30].

Measurement error

Measurement error is the difference between a recorded value and the true value of the quantity measured. Systematic error (bias) shifts measurements in a consistent direction, as with a mis-calibrated scale or an interviewer who leads respondents. Random error varies unpredictably from one measurement to the next and averages out over many repetitions. The distinction maps onto the targets in Figure 5: systematic error damages validity and random error damages reliability.

Under the classical error model, the observed value is W = X + U, where the error U has mean zero and is independent of the true value X. When such a W is used as a predictor in a simple linear regression, the estimated slope is biased toward zero by the reliability ratio [31]:

E(β̂W) ≈ λ βX,    λ = σ²X / (σ²X + σ²U)

This regression dilution means that a noisily measured exposure understates its true effect. Error in the outcome variable, if it is classical, does not bias the slope but does widen its confidence interval. Error in confounders leaves residual confounding. In multivariable and nonlinear models the direction of bias is not always toward zero [31].

Python · regression dilution under classical measurement error 
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 5_000
x_true = rng.normal(0, 1, n)                 # true exposure, variance 1
y = 2.0 * x_true + rng.normal(0, 1, n)       # true slope = 2

for err_sd in [0.0, 0.5, 1.0]:
    x_obs = x_true + rng.normal(0, err_sd, n)    # classical error
    slope = np.polyfit(x_obs, y, 1)[0]
    lam = 1 / (1 + err_sd**2)                    # reliability ratio
    print(f"error sd {err_sd}: slope {slope:.2f} | theory 2 x {lam:.2f} = {2*lam:.2f}")
error sd 0.0: slope 2.01 | theory 2 x 1.00 = 2.00
error sd 0.5: slope 1.62 | theory 2 x 0.80 = 1.60
error sd 1.0: slope 1.02 | theory 2 x 0.50 = 1.00

When the error variance equals the true-score variance, half of the effect disappears. If the reliability ratio can be estimated from replicate measurements or a validation substudy, the simplest correction divides the naive slope by λ̂. More general methods include regression calibration and simulation–extrapolation (SIMEX) [31]. For comparing two instruments that measure the same quantity, a correlation coefficient is the wrong tool, because two methods can be highly correlated and still disagree systematically. Bland–Altman analysis instead plots the difference against the mean of each pair and reports the mean difference ± 1.96 standard deviations of the differences as the limits of agreement [24].

Exercise 3. Is the ratio meaningful?

On Monday the temperature was 40 °F and on Tuesday it was 80 °F. A news report says Tuesday was "twice as hot". Convert both temperatures to Celsius and kelvin and explain why the claim is wrong.

Show solution

°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9, so 40 °F = 4.4 °C and 80 °F = 26.7 °C, a ratio of about 6.0. In kelvin: 277.6 K and 299.8 K, a ratio of 1.08. The Fahrenheit and Celsius ratios disagree because both are interval scales with arbitrary zeros, so ratios are not invariant under their permissible transformation ax + b. Only on the kelvin (ratio) scale is a ratio of temperatures physically meaningful, and there Tuesday is about 8% "hotter" in absolute thermal terms.

Exercise 4. Statistic, parameter and standard error

In a simple random sample of 400 registered voters, 208 support a ballot measure. (a) Which number is a statistic and which unknown quantity is the parameter? (b) Estimate the standard error of the sample proportion.

Show solution

(a) The statistic is p̂ = 208/400 = 0.52. The parameter is π, the proportion of all registered voters who support the measure.

(b) SE(p̂) ≈ √[p̂(1 − p̂)/n] = √(0.52 × 0.48 / 400) = √0.000624 ≈ 0.025, or 2.5 percentage points. An approximate 95% confidence interval is 0.52 ± 1.96 × 0.025, roughly 0.47 to 0.57, so the sample does not establish majority support.

Exercise 6. Compute Cohen's kappa

Two radiologists independently classify 100 X-rays as "fracture" or "no fracture". Both say fracture for 40; both say no fracture for 45; radiologist A says fracture and B says no fracture for 10; A says no fracture and B says fracture for 5. Compute the raw agreement and Cohen's κ, and interpret it.

Show solution

Observed agreement po = (40 + 45)/100 = 0.85. A says fracture for 50/100 = 0.50 of films and B for 45/100 = 0.45. Chance agreement pe = 0.50 × 0.45 + 0.50 × 0.55 = 0.225 + 0.275 = 0.50. So κ = (0.85 − 0.50)/(1 − 0.50) = 0.70, which is "substantial" agreement on the Landis–Koch scale [25].

Exercise 7. Name the missing-data mechanism

For each situation, give the most plausible mechanism (MCAR, MAR, MNAR) and one consequence for the analysis: (a) a lab freezer fails and destroys a random batch of samples; (b) in a depression trial, patients whose mood worsens drop out before their final assessment, and their worsening was not recorded; (c) in an income survey, response to the income question is lower among younger respondents, and age is recorded for everyone.

Show solution

(a) MCAR. Complete-case analysis is unbiased, but power is lost. (b) MNAR, because dropout depends on the unrecorded outcome. Complete-case and standard MAR-based imputation will both make the treatment groups look better than they are, so a sensitivity analysis (for example, pattern-mixture or delta-adjusted imputation) is needed [28]. If interim mood scores were recorded and fully explain dropout, the situation moves toward MAR. (c) MAR given age, provided that within age groups response is unrelated to income. Imputation models must include age. Whether higher earners within an age group also refuse more is untestable from these data, which is why MAR should be argued rather than assumed [30].

Exercise 8. Correct for regression dilution

A study regresses systolic blood pressure on self-reported daily salt intake and finds a slope of 1.2 mmHg per gram. A validation substudy estimates the reliability ratio of self-reported salt intake at λ = 0.6. Assuming classical, non-differential error, estimate the corrected slope. What happens to its standard error?

Show solution

Corrected slope ≈ 1.2 / 0.6 = 2.0 mmHg per gram. The correction increases the point estimate and also inflates its standard error, because uncertainty in λ̂ must be propagated, for example by the delta method or bootstrapping over the validation substudy [31]. The naive analysis understated the effect by 40%.

Exercise 10. Experimental unit

A researcher randomises 8 cages of mice, 4 cages to a new diet and 4 to control, with 5 mice per cage. She analyses the 40 mice with a two-sample t-test. What is wrong, and what is the effective sample size?

Show solution

The diet is assigned to cages, so the cage is the experimental unit. Mice in the same cage share the environment and are not independent replicates. Analysing 40 mice is pseudoreplication and understates the standard error [4]. The effective sample size for the treatment comparison is 8 cages (4 per arm). A valid analysis compares cage means with a t-test on 6 degrees of freedom, or fits a mixed model with a random cage effect.
