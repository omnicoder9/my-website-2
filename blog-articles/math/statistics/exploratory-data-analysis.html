Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is the work of examining data to find out what it contains before, or alongside, testing hypotheses about it. It asks open questions. What is the shape of each variable? Which variables move together, and how? Are there groups, trends, cycles, or values that do not belong? Would the data be easier to describe on another scale? The answers decide which models are sensible, which assumptions need checking, and which questions are worth asking at all [1,2].

The examples below use small, well-documented public datasets that ship with statsmodels and scikit-learn [3,4], so every number in the article can be reproduced: Brownlee's stack loss plant data [5], Engel's 1857 Belgian household budgets [6], Anscombe's quartet [7], Fisher's iris measurements [8,9], the annual flow of the Nile [10] and the Mauna Loa carbon dioxide record [11]. 1Tukey's exploratory data analysis

John W. Tukey argued in 1962 that data analysis is a science in its own right, broader than mathematical statistics, and that it should be judged by how well it helps people learn from data rather than by the optimality of its procedures under assumed models [12]. His 1977 book Exploratory Data Analysis turned that argument into a toolkit built for pencil, paper and the eye [1]. He described the activity as "detective work": looking for clues in the data, while confirmatory analysis plays the role of the court that weighs the evidence. He later stressed that the two are complements and that a field needs both [13].

The NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods sums up the difference as a change in the order of the steps [2]. A classical analysis imposes a model straight after collecting the data and then analyses the model's parameters. EDA analyses the data first, using mostly graphical techniques, and lets the data suggest which model is appropriate.

Four principles

Velleman and Hoaglin organise Tukey's approach around four themes, often called the four Rs [14]:

  • Resistance. Summaries should not be dragged far by a small part of the data. The median resists a wild value; the mean does not.
  • Residuals. Fit a simple description, subtract it, and look at what is left. Data = fit + residual, and the residuals often hold the interesting structure.
  • Re-expression. Change the scale (logs, roots, reciprocals) when that makes the data more symmetric, the spreads more equal, or the relationships straighter.
  • Revelation. Use displays that show the data, so that the eye can find what no summary was designed to detect. In Tukey's words, "the greatest value of a picture is when it forces us to notice what we never expected to see" [1].

The tools Tukey introduced

Many of the displays and summaries now built into statistical software come from Tukey's book: the stem-and-leaf display, the five-number summary built on hinges, the box-and-whisker plot with its fences for flagging outside values, running-median smoothers, the three-group resistant line, median polish for two-way tables, and the "ladder" of power re-expressions [1,15]. Each is designed to be resistant and easy to compute by hand.

Tukey locates values by depth, the rank counted from the nearer end of the sorted batch. For n values the median has depth (n + 1)/2, and each hinge has depth (⌊depth of median⌋ + 1)/2. Hinges are close to, but not always identical with, the quartiles computed by software, which use various interpolation rules. The H-spread is the distance between the hinges. Values more than 1.5 H-spreads beyond a hinge lie outside the inner fences and are called "outside"; values beyond 3 H-spreads (the outer fences) are "far out" [1].

2Univariate analysis

Univariate analysis describes one variable at a time: its location (where the values centre), spread (how far they range), shape (symmetry, tail weight, number of modes) and any unusual values. For a categorical variable the summary is a frequency table and a bar chart; for a numerical variable it is a set of summaries plus at least one display of the distribution.

Sample skewness and kurtosis have several competing definitions. Joanes and Gill compare them; the adjusted Fisher–Pearson coefficient G₁ and the corresponding G₂ for excess kurtosis are the versions used by most statistical packages [16]. Both are moment-based and therefore sensitive to the very tail values they are meant to describe, so they should be read alongside a plot.

A histogram's appearance depends on the bin width. Freedman and Diaconis showed that a width of h = 2 · IQR · n−1/3 is a good choice for estimating a density, and because it uses the IQR it is not inflated by outliers [17]. A quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plot sorts the data and plots them against the matching quantiles of a reference distribution. If the data come from that distribution (up to location and scale) the points lie near a straight line; curvature reveals skewness, and S-shapes reveal heavy or light tails [18].

The 4-plot

For measurements taken in sequence, the NIST handbook recommends checking four assumptions at once (fixed location, fixed variation, randomness and a fixed distribution) with a "4-plot": a run-sequence plot, a lag plot, a histogram and a normal probability plot [2]. Many univariate surprises are time effects in disguise.

3Bivariate analysis

Bivariate analysis looks at two variables together, asking whether and how one varies with the other. The right display depends on the variable types: a scatterplot for two numerical variables, side-by-side boxplots or strip plots for a numerical variable across categories, and a contingency table with a mosaic or stacked bar chart for two categorical variables.

The usual numerical summaries of association are the Pearson correlation r, which measures linear association; Spearman's ρ, the Pearson correlation of the ranks, which measures monotone association [19]; and Kendall's τ, based on the balance of concordant and discordant pairs [20]. None of them replaces the scatterplot. Anscombe made the point in 1973 with four small datasets that share their means, variances, correlation and least-squares line, yet look nothing alike [7].

Questions to ask of every scatterplot

  1. Form: is the relationship straight, curved, or absent? Does it change direction?
  2. Strength: how tightly do the points follow the form?
  3. Spread: is the vertical scatter constant, or does it fan out as x grows (heteroscedasticity)?
  4. Unusual points: are there points far from the rest in y (outliers) or in x (leverage points)?
  5. Clusters: are there separate groups that a third variable might explain?

4Multivariate exploration

With more than two variables, the analyst cannot look at everything at once, so multivariate EDA combines three strategies: look at all pairs, condition on some variables while looking at others, and project the data onto a few informative directions.

  • Scatterplot matrices and correlation matrices show every pair. They are the first look at a multivariate dataset, but they show only two-dimensional margins [22].
  • Conditioning plots (coplots) and small multiples show the relation between two variables separately within slices of a third, which reveals interactions [22].
  • Colour, shape and facets by group add a categorical variable to any display.
  • Parallel coordinates draw each observation as a line across parallel vertical axes, one per variable, so that many dimensions fit on one page [23].
  • Principal component analysis (PCA) finds orthogonal linear combinations of the variables with the largest variances. Plotting the first two component scores is the best two-dimensional linear view of the data in the least-squares sense [24].

5Detecting trends and patterns

When data are ordered in time or space, the first questions are whether the level drifts (a trend), whether there are regular cycles (seasonality), whether the level jumps (a change point), and whether the variability changes. Raw sequence plots are noisy, so EDA relies on smoothers, which separate a slowly varying signal from rough residuals: data = smooth + rough [1,14].

  • Moving averages replace each value with the mean of its neighbours. They are simple but are pulled by single spikes [26].
  • Running medians replace each value with the median of a window of 3, 5 or more values. They remove isolated spikes completely and preserve genuine steps. Tukey combined them into compound smoothers, re-smoothing the result and then smoothing the residuals ("twicing") [1,14].
  • LOWESS / LOESS fits a weighted straight line (or quadratic) in a moving window, then repeats the fit with robustness weights that down-weight points with large residuals. Cleveland introduced it for smoothing scatterplots [27]. The span (the fraction of the data in each window) controls smoothness.
  • CUSUM, the cumulative sum of deviations from a reference level, changes slope where the mean changes, so its turning point marks a shift [28].
  • STL (seasonal-trend decomposition using LOESS) splits a seasonal series into trend, seasonal and remainder components by iterating LOESS smoothers, with an optional robust mode [29].
Python · smoothing the Nile, decomposing CO₂

The annual flow of the Nile at Aswan from 1871 to 1970 is a classic change-point example: Cobb analysed the apparent drop in level near 1898 [10]. The LOWESS curve and the running medians both show a high level in the 1870s and 1880s and a lower level after the turn of the century; the CUSUM peaks at 1898, and the means of the two segments are 1098 and 850 (in units of 10⁸ m³). A smoother blurs a sudden step into a gradual slope, so a step model fits this series better than a smooth trend, which is a modelling choice the plot makes visible.

The Mauna Loa carbon dioxide record shows both a trend and a seasonal cycle. STL on monthly means from 1958 to 2001 recovers a trend from about 315 to 372 ppm and a stable annual cycle with a peak in May, a trough in October and an amplitude of about 6 ppm. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos define the strength of trend as FT = max(0, 1 − Var(R)/Var(T + R)) and the strength of seasonality analogously with S in place of T [26]. Both are close to 1 here, meaning almost all the variation is structure, not noise.

Other pattern detectors

A lag plot (each value against the previous one) shows autocorrelation as a tilted cloud and cycles as loops [2]. The autocorrelation function quantifies the same thing lag by lag. A seasonal subseries plot draws each month's values as their own mini-series, so that changes in the seasonal pattern stand out [26].

6Identifying anomalies

An anomaly or outlier is an observation that appears inconsistent with the rest of the data. It may be a recording error, a member of a different population, or the most informative point in the dataset. EDA's job is to find such points and ask why they are there; deleting them is a separate decision that needs a reason [30].

Univariate rules

  • The z-score rule, |x − x̄| / s > 3, is the most common and the least reliable. The mean and standard deviation are themselves distorted by the outliers, so a cluster of bad values can hide itself. This is called masking [31].
  • Tukey's fences flag values more than 1.5 IQR beyond the quartiles [1]. For normal data about 0.7% of values fall outside the inner fences, so in large samples some flags are expected even with no anomaly at all.
  • The modified z-score of Iglewicz and Hoaglin replaces the mean and SD with the median and MAD: Mi = 0.6745 (xi − median) / MAD, flagging |Mi| > 3.5 [30]. Leys and colleagues recommend a median ± 2.5 scaled-MAD rule as a default in the behavioural sciences [32].

Multivariate rules

A point can be ordinary on every axis and still be unusual in combination, such as a tall person with a very low weight. The Mahalanobis distance di = √[(xi − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (xi − μ)] measures distance from the centre in units that account for the correlation structure; for multivariate normal data d² follows approximately a chi-squared distribution with p degrees of freedom. With the ordinary mean and covariance it suffers from masking just as z-scores do. The minimum covariance determinant (MCD) estimator instead uses the mean and covariance of the subset of observations, typically about half, whose covariance matrix has the smallest determinant [33]; the FAST-MCD algorithm makes it practical [34].

7Detecting nonlinear relationships

The Pearson correlation measures only how well a straight line fits. A strong but curved relationship can have r near zero, and a monotone curve can have a modest r. Nonlinearity is found with displays first and with coefficients second:

  • Scatterplot smoothers such as LOWESS trace the shape of the relationship without assuming a functional form [27].
  • Residual plots: fit a straight line, then plot the residuals against x or against fitted values. A curve in the residuals means the line has missed structure [22].
  • Rank correlations (Spearman, Kendall) capture any monotone relationship, linear or not [19,20].
  • Distance correlation is zero if and only if the variables are independent (given finite first moments), so it also detects non-monotone dependence [35].
  • The maximal information coefficient (MIC) was proposed for screening many variable pairs for associations of any shape [36].
Python · correlation coefficients across shapes 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from statsmodels.nonparametric.smoothers_lowess import lowess

def dcor(x, y):
    """Distance correlation of Szekely, Rizzo and Bakirov (2007)."""
    def centred(a):
        d = np.abs(a[:, None] - a[None, :])
        return d - d.mean(0) - d.mean(1)[:, None] + d.mean()
    A, B = centred(x), centred(y)
    return np.sqrt((A * B).mean() / np.sqrt((A * A).mean() * (B * B).mean()))

Straightening a curve

Tukey's "bulging rule" chooses a re-expression by the direction of the bulge: if the curve bulges towards high x and high y, move x or y up the ladder of powers (squares); if it bulges towards low values, move down (roots, logs) [37]. A straightened relationship is easier to describe, to model, and to check for outliers.

8Exploring group differences

Comparing a numerical variable across groups is one of the most common exploratory tasks. The comparison covers location, spread and shape. Two groups can have equal medians but different spreads, and a difference in means can be the effect of a few extreme values in one group.

  • Side-by-side boxplots compare medians, spreads, skewness and outside values at a glance. McGill, Tukey and Larsen added notches at median ± 1.58 · IQR/√n: if two notches do not overlap, that is roughly a 5% test that the medians differ. They also proposed widths proportional to √n to show group size [38].
  • Strip plots, dot plots and violin plots show every value or a density estimate; they are better than boxplots for small groups or for multimodal distributions.
  • Empirical CDFs overlaid for each group compare entire distributions.
  • Effect sizes put the difference on an interpretable scale: Hedges' g, the difference in means divided by the pooled SD with a small-sample correction [39], and Cliff's δ, the probability that a value from one group exceeds a value from the other minus the reverse probability [40].
  • Rank-based tests such as Kruskal–Wallis give a quick resistant check across several groups [41]. In EDA they are guides, not verdicts: a hypothesis suggested by exploring the data should be confirmed on new data [13].

9Data transformations

Tukey's term for a transformation is re-expression: the same data on a different scale. A good scale can make a distribution symmetric, make spreads similar across groups, straighten a relationship, and turn multiplicative effects into additive ones [1,15]. These goals often agree: data with right skew, spread that grows with level, and curvature are frequently all improved by the same log transformation.

The ladder of powers

Tukey arranged the power transformations xp in a ladder: …, x², x, √x, log x (which takes the place of p = 0), −1/√x, −1/x, … . The minus signs keep the order of the values. Moving down the ladder pulls in a long right tail; moving up pulls in a long left tail [1,37]. The transformations need positive data.

Box–Cox and Yeo–Johnson

Box and Cox made the ladder continuous and estimable. For y > 0,

y(λ) = (yλ − 1) / λ for λ ≠ 0, and y(λ) = log y for λ = 0,

with λ chosen by maximum likelihood under a normal model [42]. Yeo and Johnson proposed a related family that is defined for zero and negative values as well [43].

Spread versus level

When several groups are compared, plot the log of each group's spread (for example its IQR) against the log of its median. If the slope is b, the power p = 1 − b approximately equalises the spreads: slope 1 suggests logs, slope 0.5 suggests square roots [15].

10Robust exploratory methods

A procedure is robust when moderate departures from its assumptions, including a small fraction of gross errors, change its results only moderately [44,45]. Tukey's emphasis on resistance in EDA and the theory of robust statistics developed side by side [15]. Two ideas make robustness measurable:

  • The breakdown point is the smallest fraction of the data that, if replaced by arbitrary values, can carry the estimate arbitrarily far [46].
  • The influence function describes the effect on the estimate of a small amount of contamination at a point x. A robust estimator has a bounded influence function [45].

Location and scale

The median and the MAD, the median of absolute deviations from the median, are the basic resistant pair. Multiplying the MAD by 1.4826 makes it a consistent estimator of the standard deviation for normal data. Its drawback is low efficiency; Rousseeuw and Croux proposed alternatives (Sn and Qn) with the same 50% breakdown point and better efficiency [47]. Between the mean and the median lie trimmed means, which drop a fixed fraction from each end (Wilcox recommends 20% as a general default [48]); Huber M-estimators, which treat small residuals quadratically and large ones linearly [44]; and the Hodges–Lehmann estimator, the median of all pairwise averages [49].

Lines

Tukey's resistant line splits the data into thirds by x and passes a line through the medians of the outer thirds, with the intercept set from all three thirds [1,14]. The Theil–Sen slope is the median of the slopes between all pairs of points [50,51]. Siegel's repeated median takes, for each point, the median of its slopes to all other points and then the median of those, which raises the breakdown point to 50% [52]. Least median of squares and least trimmed squares regression also reach 50% [33,31].

A practical rule

Compute both a classical and a robust version of each key summary. When they agree, report the classical one with confidence. When they disagree, the disagreement is itself a finding: some observations are driving the classical result, and they deserve a closer look [31,15].

Exercise 11. Code: a reusable outlier screen

Write a Python function that returns a Boolean mask of values with |modified z| > 3.5, and apply it to Engel income on the raw scale and on the log scale. Explain the difference.

Show solution 
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

def flag_outliers(x, k=3.5):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    med = np.median(x)
    mad = np.median(np.abs(x - med))
    return np.abs(0.6745 * (x - med) / mad) > k

income = sm.datasets.engel.load_pandas().data["income"].to_numpy()
print(flag_outliers(income).sum(), np.sort(income[flag_outliers(income)]).round(0))
print(flag_outliers(np.log(income)).sum())
7 [2293. 2341. 2432. 2498. 2552. 2823. 4958.]
1

On the raw scale seven high incomes are flagged, but they are mostly the natural right tail of a skewed distribution. On the log scale, where the distribution is roughly symmetric, only one household (income 4958) stands out. Choose the scale first, then screen for outliers: a rule tuned for symmetric data applied to skewed data mistakes the tail for anomalies.
