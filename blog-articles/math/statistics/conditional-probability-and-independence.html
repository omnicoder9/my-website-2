Conditional Probability and Independence

Conditional probability, the multiplication rule, the law of total probability, Bayes' theorem, independence (pairwise, mutual and conditional), odds and likelihood ratios, and Bayesian updating, with worked examples, Python code and exercises.

Summary. Conditional probability is how probability responds to information: once we learn that an event B has occurred, outcomes outside B are ruled out and the remaining probabilities are rescaled. Almost everything else in this article follows from that one definition. The multiplication rule and the law of total probability are rearrangements of it; Bayes' theorem combines the two; independence is the special case where the information changes nothing; and Bayesian updating is Bayes' theorem applied repeatedly. Each idea is stated precisely, worked by hand, checked in Python with exact fractions, and drawn. Every non-obvious claim carries a numbered reference in Vancouver style, listed at the end.

1. Setting and notation

We work in Kolmogorov's framework: a sample space Ω of outcomes, a collection of events (subsets of Ω), and a probability measure P that assigns each event a number in [0, 1], gives P(Ω) = 1, and is additive over disjoint events.1 We write A ∩ B (or simply AB) for "both A and B", A ∪ B for "at least one", and Ac for "not A". When all outcomes in a finite Ω are equally likely, P(A) = |A| / |Ω|, the classical counting rule used in many of the examples below.2

Two running examples appear throughout. The first is a pair of fair dice, with 36 equally likely ordered outcomes. The second is a screening test for a disease with prevalence 1%, sensitivity 80% and false-positive rate 9.6%, the figures used in Gigerenzer and Hoffrage's well-known study of how people reason about such tests.3

2. Conditional probability

Definition P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)P(B),   provided P(B) > 0.

Read P(A | B) as "the probability of A given B". The definition is due in this form to Kolmogorov, who took it as the elementary definition of conditional probability for events of positive probability.1 Its meaning is geometric: learning that B has occurred removes every outcome outside B, so B becomes the new sample space; the part of A that survives is A ∩ B; and dividing by P(B) rescales so the new probabilities again sum to one.4

For a fixed B, the function A ↦ P(A | B) satisfies all of Kolmogorov's axioms, so it is itself a probability measure.2,4 Consequently every rule you know for ordinary probabilities holds conditionally too, for instance P(Ac | B) = 1 − P(A | B). This is the reason conditioning can be layered: conditioning on B and then on C is the same as conditioning on B ∩ C.

Worked example: two dice

Let A = "the sum is 8" and B = "the first die is even". Unconditionally, five of the 36 outcomes give a sum of 8, so P(A) = 5/36 ≈ 0.139. Given B, only the 18 outcomes in the even columns remain, and three of them, (2,6), (4,4) and (6,2), sum to 8:

P(A | B) = 3/3618/36 = 318 = 16 ≈ 0.167.

Learning that the first die is even has raised the probability of a sum of 8. Reversing the roles gives a different number: P(B | A) = 3/5, because three of the five outcomes summing to 8 have an even first die.

P(A | B) is not P(B | A). The two conditional probabilities share a numerator but have different denominators, and they can differ enormously. Confusing them in court, treating the probability of a forensic match given innocence as the probability of innocence given a match, is known as the prosecutor's fallacy.5 The same confusion underlies most misreadings of diagnostic tests (Section 5).

A frequency reading

If an experiment is repeated many times, P(A | B) is approximately the proportion of trials in which A occurred among those trials in which B occurred.6 This is also how conditional probabilities are estimated from data: a row or column percentage in a contingency table is an estimated conditional probability.

from fractions import Fraction as F
from itertools import product

def prob(event, space):
    return F(sum(1 for w in space if event(w)), len(space))

def cond_prob(a, b, space):
    pb = prob(b, space)
    if pb == 0:
        raise ValueError("P(B) = 0: conditional probability undefined")
    return prob(lambda w: a(w) and b(w), space) / pb

dice = list(product(range(1, 7), repeat=2))
sum8 = lambda w: w[0] + w[1] == 8
first_even = lambda w: w[0] % 2 == 0
print(cond_prob(sum8, first_even, dice))   # 1/6
print(cond_prob(first_even, sum8, dice))   # 3/5
Conditioning on events of probability zero. The ratio definition fails when P(B) = 0, for example "given that a continuous measurement equals exactly 1.7". Such conditional probabilities are defined in measure-theoretic probability through conditional expectations and densities rather than by the elementary ratio.1,4 This article stays with events of positive probability.

3. Multiplication rule

Multiplying the definition through by P(B) gives a rule for the probability that two events both occur:

Multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(B) P(A | B) = P(A) P(B | A).

Applied repeatedly it becomes the chain rule (general multiplication rule) for any number of events:2,6

Chain rule P(A1A2 ⋯ An) = P(A1) P(A2 | A1) P(A3 | A1A2) ⋯ P(An | A1 ⋯ An−1).

The chain rule is most useful when an experiment unfolds in stages and the conditional probability of each stage, given what has already happened, is easy to write down. Every path through a probability tree is an instance of it: the probability of a leaf is the product of the branch probabilities leading to it.

Worked example: three hearts in a row

Draw three cards without replacement from a standard 52-card deck. The probability that all three are hearts is

1352 × 1251 × 1150 = 11850 ≈ 0.01294.

Each factor is a conditional probability: after one heart is gone, 12 of the remaining 51 cards are hearts, and so on. The same answer follows from counting, C(13,3)/C(52,3) = 286/22100 = 11/850, which is a useful check (see the previous article in this series on counting).

4. Law of total probability

A collection of events H1, …, Hk is a partition of Ω if the events are pairwise disjoint and their union is Ω: exactly one of them occurs. Any event E then splits into the disjoint pieces E ∩ Hi, and adding their probabilities, each written with the multiplication rule, gives:2,4

Law of total probability P(E) = Σi P(E | Hi) P(Hi).

In words: the overall probability of E is a weighted average of its conditional probabilities in each scenario, weighted by how likely each scenario is. The simplest case uses the partition {H, Hc}: P(E) = P(E | H)P(H) + P(E | Hc)P(Hc). The law also holds conditionally on any further event C, since conditional probability is itself a probability measure. The partition may be countably infinite.6

Worked example: three suppliers

A factory buys a component from three suppliers. Supplier A provides 50% of the stock with a 2% defect rate, B provides 30% with a 3% defect rate, and C provides 20% with a 5% defect rate. A component is drawn at random. Then

P(defective) = 0.50×0.02 + 0.30×0.03 + 0.20×0.05 = 0.010 + 0.009 + 0.010 = 0.029.

The overall rate, 2.9%, lies between the smallest and largest supplier rates, as any weighted average must.

5. Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem reverses the direction of conditioning. Writing P(H ∩ E) in two ways with the multiplication rule and dividing by P(E), then expanding the denominator with the law of total probability, gives:2,4

Bayes' theorem P(Hj | E) = P(E | Hj) P(Hj)Σi P(E | Hi) P(Hi).

The terms have standard names. P(Hj) is the prior probability of hypothesis j, P(E | Hj) is the likelihood of the evidence under that hypothesis, and P(Hj | E) is the posterior.7 The result is named after Thomas Bayes, whose essay on inferring an unknown binomial probability was published posthumously in 1763 by Richard Price.8

For the suppliers, given that a component is defective, P(A | defective) = 0.010/0.029 = 10/29 ≈ 0.345, P(B | defective) = 9/29 ≈ 0.310 and P(C | defective) = 10/29 ≈ 0.345. Supplier C supplies only 20% of parts but accounts for about a third of the defects.

Worked example: a screening test

A disease has prevalence 1%. A test detects it in 80% of people who have it (sensitivity) and gives a false positive in 9.6% of people who do not (so specificity is 90.4%). What is the probability that a person who tests positive has the disease? This quantity is the test's positive predictive value (PPV).9

P(D | +) = 0.80 × 0.010.80 × 0.01 + 0.096 × 0.99 = 0.0080.10304 ≈ 0.0776.

Fewer than 8% of positives have the disease. Most positives are false positives because the healthy group, although each member rarely tests positive, is 99 times larger. Gigerenzer and Hoffrage found that physicians and students reached the correct answer far more often when the same information was presented as natural frequencies (counts out of a fixed population) rather than as percentages,3 which is exactly what the tree below does.

A simulation of one million people confirms the calculation:

import random
rng = random.Random(2026)
prev, sens, fpr = 0.01, 0.80, 0.096
pos = tp = 0
for _ in range(1_000_000):
    d = rng.random() < prev
    t = rng.random() < (sens if d else fpr)
    if t:
        pos += 1
        tp += d
print(tp, pos, tp / pos)
7986 102674 0.07778...
Predictive values depend on prevalence. Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test, but the PPV also depends on the prior probability. With the same test and a prevalence of 10% the PPV rises to 0.481 (Exercise 4). Reporting a PPV without the prevalence it applies to is incomplete.9

The Monty Hall problem

A car is behind one of three doors. You pick door 1; the host, who knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you did not pick, opens door 3. Should you switch to door 2? This puzzle was posed in a letter to The American Statistician in 1975.10 Bayes' theorem settles it. With priors 1/3 each, the probability the host opens door 3 is 1/2 if the car is behind door 1 (the host chooses at random between 2 and 3), 1 if behind door 2, and 0 if behind door 3. The posterior for door 1 is (1/3 × 1/2) / (1/3 × 1/2 + 1/3 × 1) = 1/3, so switching wins with probability 2/3. A simulation of 100,000 games in the module below gives 0.3354 for staying and 0.6646 for switching. The answer depends on the host's rule; a host who opens a door at random and happens to reveal a goat leaves the two doors at 1/2 each.4

6. Independence of events

Definition A and B are independent if P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B).

When P(B) > 0 this is equivalent to P(A | B) = P(A): learning that B occurred does not change the probability of A.2,6 The product form is preferred as the definition because it is symmetric in A and B and makes sense even when a probability is zero. If A and B are independent, so are A and Bc, Ac and B, and Ac and Bc (Exercise 5).2

Worked example: is it independent?

With two dice, let E = "first die even". The event "sum is 7" is independent of E: P(sum 7) = 6/36 = 1/6, and P(sum 7 ∩ E) = 3/36 = 1/2 × 1/6. Whatever the first die shows, exactly one value of the second die makes 7. The event "sum is 8" is not independent of E, since P(sum 8 | E) = 1/6 ≠ 5/36 (Section 2). Independence is a numerical property of P, not something one can always see from the description of the events.

Independent is not the same as mutually exclusive. If A and B are disjoint and both have positive probability, then P(A ∩ B) = 0 ≠ P(A)P(B), so they are dependent: learning that A occurred tells you for certain that B did not.2

Independence is usually an assumption justified by the physical set-up (separate coins, separate patients, a well-shuffled deck reshuffled between draws) rather than something verified from data. When it holds for repeated trials, the multiplication rule simplifies to a product of unconditional probabilities: the probability of five heads in five tosses of a fair coin is (1/2)5 = 1/32.6

7. Pairwise versus mutual independence

For more than two events there are two non-equivalent notions. Events A1, …, An are pairwise independent if every pair satisfies the product rule. They are mutually (or jointly, or completely) independent if the product rule holds for every sub-collection:2,6

Mutual independence P(Ai1 ∩ ⋯ ∩ Aik) = P(Ai1) ⋯ P(Aik)   for every 2 ≤ k ≤ n and every choice of distinct indices.

That is 2n − n − 1 equations: 4 for three events, 11 for four. Mutual independence implies pairwise independence, but not conversely.

Bernstein's example

Toss a fair coin twice. Let A = "first toss heads", B = "second toss heads" and C = "the two tosses agree". Each event has probability 1/2, and each pairwise intersection is a single outcome with probability 1/4 = 1/2 × 1/2, so the three events are pairwise independent. But A ∩ B ∩ C = {HH} has probability 1/4, not 1/8. Any two of the events determine the third, so they are not mutually independent. Examples of this kind are attributed to S. N. Bernstein.11,6

The converse failure is also possible: the triple product rule P(ABC) = P(A)P(B)P(C) can hold while some pair is dependent, which is why the definition demands every sub-collection.11

from itertools import combinations

def mutually_independent(events, space):
    for k in range(2, len(events) + 1):
        for sub in combinations(events, k):
            joint = prob(lambda w: all(e(w) for e in sub), space)
            prod = F(1)
            for e in sub:
                prod *= prob(e, space)
            if joint != prod:
                return False
    return True

coins = list(product("HT", repeat=2))
A = lambda w: w[0] == "H"
B = lambda w: w[1] == "H"
C = lambda w: w[0] == w[1]
print(mutually_independent([A, B, C], coins))   # False, though every pair passes
Why it matters. Pairwise independence is enough for some results, such as additivity of variances of sums, which only involves pairs. Arguments that multiply three or more probabilities together, such as the probability that every component of a system works, require mutual independence.4

8. Conditional independence

Definition A and B are conditionally independent given C (with P(C) > 0) if P(A ∩ B | C) = P(A | C) P(B | C).

Dawid introduced the notation A ⫫ B | C and argued that conditional independence is a unifying concept across statistical theory, covering sufficiency, ancillarity and causal reasoning.12 Neither independence nor conditional independence implies the other, and the two examples below show each failure.

Conditionally independent but dependent

A bag holds a fair coin and a biased coin that shows heads 90% of the time. One coin is chosen at random and tossed twice. Given which coin was chosen, the tosses are independent. Unconditionally they are not:

P(H1) = ½(0.5) + ½(0.9) = 0.7,   P(H1H2) = ½(0.25) + ½(0.81) = 0.53,   P(H2 | H1) = 53/70 ≈ 0.757 ≠ 0.7.

A first head is evidence that the biased coin was chosen, which makes a second head more likely. The common cause (which coin) induces dependence between its effects; conditioning on the cause removes it.

Independent but conditionally dependent

Toss two fair coins independently, and let C = "at least one head". Then P(first H | C) = P(second H | C) = 2/3, but P(both H | C) = 1/3 ≠ 4/9. Given C, learning that the first coin was tails forces the second to be heads. Conditioning on a common effect creates dependence between independent causes, a pattern Pearl called "explaining away".13

Simpson's paradox

Because conditioning can create or remove association, an association in aggregated data can reverse within every subgroup. Simpson described this in 1951.14 A frequently cited example comes from a 1986 comparison of kidney-stone treatments:15

TreatmentSmall stonesLarge stonesAll stones
Open surgery81/87 = 93.1%192/263 = 73.0%273/350 = 78.0%
Percutaneous nephrolithotomy234/270 = 86.7%55/80 = 68.8%289/350 = 82.6%

Open surgery has the higher success rate for small stones and for large stones, yet the lower rate overall. Each overall rate is a weighted average of the subgroup rates (the law of total probability), and the weights differ: open surgery was used mostly on large stones, which are harder to treat. Which comparison is appropriate depends on how patients came to receive each treatment, which the probabilities alone do not reveal.14

Conditional independence as a modelling assumption

Much of applied probability rests on conditional independence assumptions. Repeated diagnostic tests are often treated as conditionally independent given disease status, which is what justifies multiplying their likelihood ratios (Section 9). Observations in a Bayesian model are typically assumed conditionally independent given the parameter (Section 10).7 Graphical models encode collections of such assumptions in a diagram.13 When the assumption is false, for example two tests that tend to fail on the same patients, multiplying likelihood ratios overstates the evidence.

9. Odds and likelihood ratios

The odds of an event are O(A) = P(A) / (1 − P(A)), and conversely P = O / (1 + O). A probability of 0.2 is odds of 0.25, often written 1:4. Writing Bayes' theorem for H and for Hc and dividing, the denominator P(E) cancels:

Bayes' theorem in odds form P(H | E)P(Hc | E) = P(E | H)P(E | Hc) × P(H)P(Hc),   i.e.   posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds.

The middle factor is the likelihood ratio (LR). It isolates the strength of the evidence from the prior. For a diagnostic test the positive and negative likelihood ratios are16

LR+ = sensitivity1 − specificity,    LR− = 1 − sensitivityspecificity.

For the screening test, LR+ = 0.80/0.096 ≈ 8.33. The prior odds are 0.01/0.99 ≈ 0.0101, the posterior odds 0.0842, and the posterior probability 0.0842/1.0842 ≈ 0.0776, the same PPV as before. LR− = 0.20/0.904 ≈ 0.221, so a negative result lowers the probability of disease from 1% to about 0.22%. Deeks and Altman note that likelihood ratios above 10 or below 0.1 are generally regarded as strong evidence for ruling a diagnosis in or out.16 Fagan's nomogram performs the odds-form calculation graphically.17

Log odds: evidence adds

Taking logarithms turns the product into a sum: log posterior odds = log prior odds + log LR. Good called the log likelihood ratio the weight of evidence.18 On a log-odds scale, each piece of conditionally independent evidence shifts belief by a fixed distance, regardless of where you start.

A second positive result from an independent repeat of the test (assumed conditionally independent given disease status) multiplies the odds by 8.33 again: 0.0842 × 8.33 ≈ 0.70, a probability of 0.412.

Bayes factors

When the two hypotheses are statistical models, the likelihood ratio is called the Bayes factor. Kass and Raftery, adapting Jeffreys, suggested reading a Bayes factor of 1 to 3 as "not worth more than a bare mention", 3 to 20 as positive, 20 to 150 as strong, and above 150 as very strong evidence.19 These labels are conventions, not thresholds with a formal justification.

def to_odds(p): return p / (1 - p)
def to_prob(o): return o / (1 + o)

def post_test(prior, sens, spec, positive=True):
    lr = sens / (1 - spec) if positive else (1 - sens) / spec
    return lr, to_prob(to_odds(prior) * lr)

lr, p1 = post_test(0.01, 0.80, 0.904)    # 8.3333, 0.0776
_,  p2 = post_test(p1,   0.80, 0.904)    # 0.4123 after a second positive

10. Bayesian updating

Bayesian updating is Bayes' theorem applied as data arrive: the posterior after one observation becomes the prior for the next. If the observations are conditionally independent given the hypothesis, updating one observation at a time gives exactly the same posterior as updating on all the data at once, because the likelihood of the whole data set is the product of the individual likelihoods.7

Sequential form P(H | x1, …, xn) ∝ P(xn | H) × P(H | x1, …, xn−1).

Worked example: fair or biased coin?

A coin is either fair or lands heads with probability 0.75, and we start at 1/2 for each. Tosses give H, H, T, H, H. After each toss the posterior probability of the biased coin is:

After tossResultLR (biased : fair)P(biased)
0prior–0.5000
1H1.50.6000
2H1.50.6923
3T0.50.5294
4H1.50.6279
5H1.50.7168

A single update using the likelihoods of the whole sequence, (1/2)5 and (3/4)4(1/4), gives 81/113 ≈ 0.7168, the same as the last row. The order of the tosses does not matter either.

Continuous hypotheses: the beta–binomial model

If the probability of heads θ can be any value in [0, 1], the prior is a density. With a Beta(a, b) prior and s heads in n tosses, the posterior is Beta(a + s, b + n − s): the beta family is conjugate to the binomial likelihood, so updating just adds counts.7 Bayes' 1763 essay treated essentially this problem with a uniform prior.8 Starting from Beta(1, 1) (uniform) and observing 7 heads in 10 tosses gives Beta(8, 4), with posterior mean 8/12 ≈ 0.667 and mode 0.7.

Two features of updating are visible in the figure. The posterior mean (a + s)/(a + b + n) is a compromise between the prior mean and the observed proportion, and as n grows the data dominate and the posterior concentrates near the observed proportion, whatever reasonable prior was used.7

12. Complete Python module

The module below uses only the standard library. Exact answers use fractions.Fraction, which represents rational numbers exactly and avoids floating-point rounding,20 and simulations use a seeded random.Random so the output is reproducible. It was run with Python 3; the output follows.

"""Conditional probability and independence: worked examples.

Exact answers use fractions.Fraction; simulations use a seeded
random.Random so the output is reproducible.
"""
from fractions import Fraction as F
from itertools import product
import random


# ---------- 1. Finite sample spaces --------------------------------------
def prob(event, space):
    """P(event) for equally likely outcomes in a finite sample space."""
    return F(sum(1 for w in space if event(w)), len(space))


def cond_prob(a, b, space):
    """P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B); undefined when P(B) = 0."""
    pb = prob(b, space)
    if pb == 0:
        raise ValueError("P(B) = 0: conditional probability undefined")
    return prob(lambda w: a(w) and b(w), space) / pb


dice = list(product(range(1, 7), repeat=2))
first_even = lambda w: w[0] % 2 == 0
sum8 = lambda w: w[0] + w[1] == 8
sum7 = lambda w: w[0] + w[1] == 7


# ---------- 2. Multiplication rule ---------------------------------------
def chain(*factors):
    """P(A1 ... An) = P(A1) P(A2|A1) ... P(An|A1...An-1)."""
    out = F(1)
    for f in factors:
        out *= f
    return out


# ---------- 3. Law of total probability and Bayes' theorem ---------------
def total_probability(priors, likelihoods):
    """P(E) = sum_i P(E | H_i) P(H_i) over a partition {H_i}."""
    return sum(p * l for p, l in zip(priors, likelihoods))


def bayes(priors, likelihoods):
    """Posterior P(H_i | E) for every hypothesis in a partition."""
    pe = total_probability(priors, likelihoods)
    return [p * l / pe for p, l in zip(priors, likelihoods)]


# ---------- 4. Diagnostic test: odds and likelihood ratios ---------------
def to_odds(p):
    return p / (1 - p)


def to_prob(o):
    return o / (1 + o)


def post_test(prior, sens, spec, positive=True):
    lr = sens / (1 - spec) if positive else (1 - sens) / spec
    return lr, to_prob(to_odds(prior) * lr)


# ---------- 5. Independence ----------------------------------------------
def independent(a, b, space):
    return prob(lambda w: a(w) and b(w), space) == prob(a, space) * prob(b, space)


def mutually_independent(events, space):
    """Check the product rule for every sub-collection of size >= 2."""
    from itertools import combinations
    for k in range(2, len(events) + 1):
        for sub in combinations(events, k):
            joint = prob(lambda w: all(e(w) for e in sub), space)
            prod = F(1)
            for e in sub:
                prod *= prob(e, space)
            if joint != prod:
                return False
    return True


coins = list(product("HT", repeat=2))
A = lambda w: w[0] == "H"            # first toss heads
B = lambda w: w[1] == "H"            # second toss heads
C = lambda w: w[0] == w[1]           # tosses agree


# ---------- 6. Bayesian updating -----------------------------------------
def sequential_update(prior, lik_heads, flips):
    """Discrete-hypothesis Bayesian updating, one flip at a time."""
    post = list(prior)
    history = [tuple(post)]
    for f in flips:
        lik = [p if f == "H" else 1 - p for p in lik_heads]
        post = bayes(post, lik)
        history.append(tuple(post))
    return history


def main():
    print("1. Two dice")
    print("   P(sum=8)              =", prob(sum8, dice))
    print("   P(sum=8 | first even) =", cond_prob(sum8, first_even, dice))
    print("   P(first even | sum=8) =", cond_prob(first_even, sum8, dice))

    print("\n2. Multiplication rule: three hearts in a row without replacement")
    p3 = chain(F(13, 52), F(12, 51), F(11, 50))
    print("   P =", p3, "=", round(float(p3), 6))

    print("\n3. Three suppliers (shares 0.5, 0.3, 0.2; defect rates 2%, 3%, 5%)")
    priors = [F(1, 2), F(3, 10), F(1, 5)]
    defect = [F(2, 100), F(3, 100), F(5, 100)]
    pd = total_probability(priors, defect)
    print("   P(defective) =", pd, "=", float(pd))
    for name, q in zip("ABC", bayes(priors, defect)):
        print(f"   P({name} | defective) = {q} = {float(q):.4f}")

    print("\n4. Screening test: prevalence 1%, sensitivity 80%, false-positive rate 9.6%")
    prev, sens, spec = 0.01, 0.80, 1 - 0.096
    ppv = bayes([prev, 1 - prev], [sens, 1 - spec])[0]
    print(f"   PPV by Bayes' theorem     = {ppv:.4f}")
    lr, p1 = post_test(prev, sens, spec)
    print(f"   LR+ = {lr:.4f}; prior odds = {to_odds(prev):.4f}; "
          f"posterior odds = {to_odds(prev) * lr:.4f}; P = {p1:.4f}")
    _, p2 = post_test(p1, sens, spec)
    print(f"   after a 2nd independent positive: P = {p2:.4f}")
    lrn, pneg = post_test(prev, sens, spec, positive=False)
    print(f"   LR- = {lrn:.4f}; P(disease | negative) = {pneg:.5f}")
    rng = random.Random(2026)
    n, pos, tp = 1_000_000, 0, 0
    for _ in range(n):
        d = rng.random() < prev
        t = rng.random() < (sens if d else 1 - spec)
        if t:
            pos += 1
            tp += d
    print(f"   simulation (n={n:,}): {tp}/{pos} = {tp / pos:.4f}")

    print("\n5. Independence")
    print("   first even vs sum 7 independent?", independent(first_even, sum7, dice))
    print("   first even vs sum 8 independent?", independent(first_even, sum8, dice))

13. Exercises with solutions

Numerical answers were checked with the functions above.

Exercise 1 · Conditioning on a sum

Two fair dice are rolled and the sum is at least 9. What is the probability that at least one die shows a six?

Solution

The outcomes with sum ≥ 9 are (3,6), (4,5), (4,6), (5,4), (5,5), (5,6), (6,3), (6,4), (6,5), (6,6): ten equally likely outcomes. Seven contain a six. So the answer is 7/10. Unconditionally, P(at least one six) = 11/36 ≈ 0.31, so the information more than doubles it.

Exercise 2 · Chain rule

An urn holds 5 red and 3 blue balls. Three are drawn without replacement. Find the probability of red, red, blue in that order.

Solution

By the chain rule, 5/8 × 4/7 × 3/6 = 60/336 = 5/28 ≈ 0.179.

Exercise 3 · Total probability and Bayes

Forty percent of email is spam. The word "free" appears in 25% of spam and 2% of legitimate email. Find P(free), and the probability that a message containing "free" is spam.

Solution

P(free) = 0.25 × 0.4 + 0.02 × 0.6 = 0.100 + 0.012 = 0.112. Then P(spam | free) = 0.100/0.112 ≈ 0.893. In odds form: prior odds 0.4/0.6 = 2/3, LR = 0.25/0.02 = 12.5, posterior odds 8.33, probability 8.33/9.33 ≈ 0.893.

Exercise 4 · Prevalence and predictive value

Use the screening test of Section 5 (sensitivity 0.80, false-positive rate 0.096) in a clinic where prevalence is 10%. What is the PPV?

Solution

PPV = 0.08 / (0.08 + 0.096 × 0.9) = 0.08 / 0.1664 ≈ 0.481. The same test is nearly six times as informative about a positive patient here as in the 1% population, purely because of the prior.

Exercise 5 · Complements of independent events

Prove that if A and B are independent, so are A and Bc.

Solution

A is the disjoint union of A ∩ B and A ∩ Bc, so P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B) = P(A) − P(A)P(B) = P(A)(1 − P(B)) = P(A)P(Bc). Applying the result twice gives independence of Ac and Bc.

Exercise 6 · Pairwise but not mutual

Roll two dice. Let A = "first die odd", B = "second die odd", C = "sum odd". Are A, B, C pairwise independent? Mutually independent?

Solution

Each event has probability 1/2. A ∩ C means first odd and second even, probability 1/4; likewise B ∩ C and A ∩ B have probability 1/4. So the events are pairwise independent. But if both dice are odd the sum is even, so P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = 0 ≠ 1/8. They are not mutually independent; this is Bernstein's example in disguise.

Exercise 7 · Explaining away

Two fair coins are tossed independently and you are told that at least one is heads. Compute P(first H | C), P(second H | C) and P(both H | C). Are the tosses conditionally independent given C?

Solution

C = {HH, HT, TH}. P(first H | C) = 2/3, P(second H | C) = 2/3, P(both H | C) = 1/3 ≠ 4/9. They are not conditionally independent, although they are unconditionally independent.

Exercise 8 · Likelihood ratios

A test has sensitivity 0.95 and specificity 0.90. The pre-test probability is 0.20. Find LR+, LR−, and the post-test probability after a positive and after a negative result.

Solution

LR+ = 0.95/0.10 = 9.5 and LR− = 0.05/0.90 ≈ 0.0556. Prior odds are 0.25. After a positive: odds 2.375, probability 2.375/3.375 ≈ 0.704. After a negative: odds ≈ 0.01389, probability ≈ 0.0137.

Exercise 9 · Beta–binomial updating

With a Beta(2, 2) prior for a success probability, you observe 3 successes in 12 trials. Give the posterior and its mean. Then show that updating on the first 6 trials (1 success) and then the last 6 (2 successes) gives the same result.

Solution

Posterior Beta(2 + 3, 2 + 9) = Beta(5, 11), mean 5/16 = 0.3125. Sequentially: Beta(2, 2) → Beta(3, 7) after 1 of 6 → Beta(5, 11) after 2 of 6. Same answer, because conjugate updating just adds counts.

Exercise 10 · Monty Hall by Bayes' theorem

Repeat the Monty Hall calculation for a host who opens one of the two unchosen doors at random and happens to reveal a goat behind door 3. Should you switch?

Solution

Now P(host opens 3 and shows a goat | car behind 1) = 1/2, P(… | car behind 2) = 1/2, P(… | car behind 3) = 0. Posteriors: door 1 and door 2 each (1/3 × 1/2)/(1/3) = 1/2. Switching neither helps nor hurts. The data are the same as in the standard version; only the likelihood (how the host behaves) differs.

Exercise 11 · Programming

Use mutually_independent from the module to verify your answer to Exercise 6, and write a function that returns which of the 2n − n − 1 product conditions fail.

Solution 
from itertools import combinations
A = lambda w: w[0] % 2 == 1
B = lambda w: w[1] % 2 == 1
C = lambda w: sum(w) % 2 == 1

def failures(events, names, space):
    out = []
    for k in range(2, len(events) + 1):
        for idx in combinations(range(len(events)), k):
            joint = prob(lambda w: all(events[i](w) for i in idx), space)
            prod = F(1)
            for i in idx:
                prod *= prob(events[i], space)
            if joint != prod:
                out.append("".join(names[i] for i in idx))
    return out

print(failures([A, B, C], "ABC", dice))            # ['ABC']
