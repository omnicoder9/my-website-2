1. Two complementary views of a vector

In elementary linear algebra, a vector is usually treated algebraically as an ordered finite list of scalars, most often real numbers. Thus an \(n\)-vector is an element \[ \mathbf v=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)\in\mathbb R^n. \] Boyd and Vandenberghe use precisely this coordinate viewpoint: a vector is an ordered finite list of numbers, and its entries are its components. This representation is the natural one for computation and for general dimensions. [1]

In two and three dimensions, the same object also has a geometric interpretation as a directed displacement: an arrow with a magnitude and direction. Equivalent directed segments—segments having the same length and direction—represent the same free vector, even if drawn at different locations. [2] These two viewpoints are compatible: the coordinate vector records the signed displacement along the chosen coordinate axes.

Important distinction. A point and a vector may use the same numerical coordinates but play different roles. The point \(P=(2,3)\) denotes a location; the vector \(\mathbf v=\langle2,3\rangle\) denotes a displacement. In Euclidean coordinates, the position vector of \(P\) is the vector from the origin to \(P\). [2]

This article focuses on real vectors. Most algebraic statements extend directly to vectors over other fields such as \(\mathbb C\), although the complex inner product requires conjugation and therefore differs from the real dot product discussed here.

2. Vector Fundamentals

2.1 Scalars versus vectors

A scalar is a single number drawn from the underlying field, here \(\mathbb R\). A vector contains multiple ordered scalar components and obeys vector addition and scalar multiplication. In geometric applications, scalars such as mass or temperature carry magnitude without an intrinsic spatial direction, whereas displacement, velocity, acceleration, and force are modeled by vectors because direction is essential. [2]

2.2 Coordinate vectors

Fix the standard basis of \(\mathbb R^n\), \[ \mathbf e_1=(1,0,\ldots,0),\quad \mathbf e_2=(0,1,\ldots,0),\quad\ldots,\quad \mathbf e_n=(0,\ldots,0,1). \] Every vector \(\mathbf v=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)\) has the unique expansion \[ \mathbf v=v_1\mathbf e_1+\cdots+v_n\mathbf e_n. \] Thus the coordinates \(v_i\) are exactly the scalar weights of \(\mathbf v\) relative to the standard basis. Linear combinations of vectors are central throughout linear algebra. [1,3]

2.3 Row vectors and column vectors

Linear algebra usually represents vectors as columns. An \(n\times1\) matrix is a column vector, \[ \mathbf v= \begin{bmatrix} v_1\\v_2\\\vdots\\v_n \end{bmatrix}, \] while a \(1\times n\) matrix is a row vector, \[ \mathbf v^{T}= \begin{bmatrix} v_1&v_2&\cdots&v_n \end{bmatrix}. \] Transposition converts one orientation into the other. The orientation matters in matrix algebra because matrix dimensions determine which products are defined. Boyd and Vandenberghe explicitly distinguish \(1\times n\) row vectors from \(n\times1\) column vectors. [1]

Example 1 — Orientation. If \[ \mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\\4\end{bmatrix}, \] then \(\mathbf x\) is \(3\times1\), whereas \[ \mathbf x^T=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&4\end{bmatrix} \] is \(1\times3\). The scalar \(\mathbf x^T\mathbf x\) is defined and equals \(2^2+(-1)^2+4^2=21\). The product \(\mathbf x\mathbf x^T\), by contrast, is a \(3\times3\) matrix.

2.4 Vector equality

Definition. Two vectors in \(\mathbb R^n\) are equal exactly when all corresponding coordinates are equal: \[ \mathbf u=\mathbf v \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u_i=v_i\quad\text{for every }i=1,\ldots,n. \] In the geometric interpretation, equal free vectors have the same magnitude and direction. [1,2]

Equality is therefore stronger than equality of magnitude. For example, \(\langle1,0\rangle\) and \(\langle0,1\rangle\) both have norm \(1\), but they are different vectors because their components—and directions—differ.

2.5 The zero vector

The zero vector in \(\mathbb R^n\) is \[ \mathbf0=(0,\ldots,0). \] It is the additive identity: \[ \mathbf v+\mathbf0=\mathbf v. \] Its Euclidean magnitude is \(0\); conversely, the Euclidean norm of a vector is zero only for the zero vector. [1,2]

2.6 Negative vectors

The additive inverse of \(\mathbf v=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)\) is \[ -\mathbf v=(-v_1,\ldots,-v_n), \] and it is characterized by \[ \mathbf v+(-\mathbf v)=\mathbf0. \] Geometrically, \(-\mathbf v\) has the same magnitude as \(\mathbf v\) and the opposite direction. [2]

3. Vector Operations

3.1 Vector addition

For \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^n\), addition is componentwise: \[ \mathbf u+\mathbf v = (u_1+v_1,\ldots,u_n+v_n). \] The operation is commutative and associative: \[ \mathbf u+\mathbf v=\mathbf v+\mathbf u,\qquad (\mathbf u+\mathbf v)+\mathbf w=\mathbf u+(\mathbf v+\mathbf w). \] Together with the zero vector and additive inverses, these are part of the vector-space structure of \(\mathbb R^n\). [1,2]

Geometrically, addition can be visualized with the head-to-tail rule or the parallelogram rule. If \(\mathbf v\) is placed with its tail at the head of \(\mathbf u\), then \(\mathbf u+\mathbf v\) runs from the tail of \(\mathbf u\) to the head of the translated \(\mathbf v\). [2]

3.2 Vector subtraction

Subtraction is defined through the additive inverse: \[ \mathbf u-\mathbf v=\mathbf u+(-\mathbf v) =(u_1-v_1,\ldots,u_n-v_n). \] For points \(P\) and \(Q\) with position vectors \(\mathbf p\) and \(\mathbf q\), the displacement from \(P\) to \(Q\) is \(\mathbf q-\mathbf p\). [2,4]

3.3 Scalar multiplication

For a scalar \(\alpha\in\mathbb R\), \[ \alpha\mathbf v=(\alpha v_1,\ldots,\alpha v_n). \] The length scales by \(|\alpha|\); positive \(\alpha\) preserves direction, negative \(\alpha\) reverses direction, and \(\alpha=0\) yields the zero vector. [1,2]

Scalar multiplication distributes over both vector addition and scalar addition: \[ \alpha(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)=\alpha\mathbf u+\alpha\mathbf v, \qquad (\alpha+\beta)\mathbf v=\alpha\mathbf v+\beta\mathbf v, \] and satisfies \[ \alpha(\beta\mathbf v)=(\alpha\beta)\mathbf v,\qquad 1\mathbf v=\mathbf v. \] [2]

3.4 Linear combinations

Definition. Given vectors \(\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_k\in\mathbb R^n\) and scalars \(c_1,\ldots,c_k\), a linear combination is any vector of the form \[ c_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_k\mathbf v_k. \] The set of all such combinations is the span of the vectors. Linear combinations are one of the organizing ideas of linear algebra: matrix-vector multiplication can be understood as forming a linear combination of the columns of a matrix. [1,3]
Example 2 — A linear combination. Let \[ \mathbf a=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}0\\3\\4\end{bmatrix}. \] Then \[ 2\mathbf a-3\mathbf b = \begin{bmatrix}2\\4\\-2\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}0\\9\\12\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\-5\\-14\end{bmatrix}. \]

3.5 Componentwise operations

Vector addition, subtraction, and scalar multiplication are inherently componentwise. In numerical computing one also encounters additional elementwise operations such as the Hadamard product \[ \mathbf u\odot\mathbf v = (u_1v_1,\ldots,u_nv_n). \] This produces another \(n\)-vector, but it is not the same operation as the dot product, which sums the componentwise products and produces a scalar. It is also distinct from an outer product, which produces a matrix. Elementwise operations are common in numerical software, but they are additional operations rather than part of the minimal vector-space axioms. [1]

Example 3 — Three different “products”. For \(\mathbf u=(1,2,3)^T\) and \(\mathbf v=(4,5,6)^T\): \[ \mathbf u\odot\mathbf v=(4,10,18)^T, \] \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=1(4)+2(5)+3(6)=32, \] while \[ \mathbf u\mathbf v^T= \begin{bmatrix} 4&5&6\\ 8&10&12\\ 12&15&18 \end{bmatrix}. \]

4. Geometry of Vectors

4.1 Vectors in \(\mathbb R^2\)

A vector in the plane has the form \[ \mathbf v=\langle v_1,v_2\rangle. \] Using the standard unit vectors \[ \mathbf i=\langle1,0\rangle,\qquad \mathbf j=\langle0,1\rangle, \] it can be written as \[ \mathbf v=v_1\mathbf i+v_2\mathbf j. \] In standard position, the tail is at the origin and the head is the point \((v_1,v_2)\). [2]

4.2 Vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\)

A vector in three-dimensional Euclidean space is \[ \mathbf v=\langle v_1,v_2,v_3\rangle =v_1\mathbf i+v_2\mathbf j+v_3\mathbf k, \] where \[ \mathbf i=\langle1,0,0\rangle,\quad \mathbf j=\langle0,1,0\rangle,\quad \mathbf k=\langle0,0,1\rangle. \] The coordinate model extends the planar treatment by adding a third mutually perpendicular coordinate axis. [4]

4.3 Position vectors

If \(P=(x,y,z)\), its position vector relative to the origin \(O=(0,0,0)\) is \[ \overrightarrow{OP}=\langle x,y,z\rangle. \] This identifies points in Euclidean space with vectors only after an origin has been chosen. The distinction matters conceptually: points denote locations, while vectors denote displacements. [2,4]

4.4 Displacement vectors

If \[ P=(x_1,y_1,z_1),\qquad Q=(x_2,y_2,z_2), \] then the displacement from \(P\) to \(Q\) is \[ \overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle x_2-x_1,\;y_2-y_1,\;z_2-z_1\rangle. \] Its norm is exactly the Euclidean distance between \(P\) and \(Q\). [4]

4.5 Magnitude: the Euclidean norm

Definition. For \(\mathbf v=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)\in\mathbb R^n\), the Euclidean norm is \[ \|\mathbf v\|_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2+\cdots+v_n^2}. \] When there is no ambiguity, it is written simply \(\|\mathbf v\|\). [1,2]

The Euclidean norm satisfies the characteristic norm properties: \[ \|\mathbf v\|\ge0,\qquad \|\mathbf v\|=0\iff\mathbf v=\mathbf0, \] \[ \|\alpha\mathbf v\|=|\alpha|\|\mathbf v\|, \qquad \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|\le\|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\|. \] The last inequality is the triangle inequality. [1]

Example 4 — Magnitude. For \(\mathbf v=\langle3,-4,12\rangle\), \[ \|\mathbf v\| = \sqrt{3^2+(-4)^2+12^2} = \sqrt{169} =13. \]

4.6 Direction

For a nonzero vector, direction can be encoded by its unit vector \(\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|\). In the plane, a direction can also be parameterized by an angle \(\theta\) from the positive \(x\)-axis: \[ \mathbf v = \|\mathbf v\|\langle\cos\theta,\sin\theta\rangle. \] The magnitude \(\|\mathbf v\|\) and direction angle together determine a nonzero planar vector. [2]

4.7 Unit vectors

Definition. A vector \(\mathbf u\) is a unit vector if \(\|\mathbf u\|=1\). If \(\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0\), its normalization is \[ \widehat{\mathbf v} = \frac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}. \] This vector has unit magnitude and the same direction as \(\mathbf v\). [2]
Example 5 — Normalization. For \(\mathbf v=\langle-5,12\rangle\), \[ \|\mathbf v\|=13, \qquad \widehat{\mathbf v} = \left\langle-\frac5{13},\frac{12}{13}\right\rangle. \] Indeed, \[ \left\|\widehat{\mathbf v}\right\| = \sqrt{\frac{25}{169}+\frac{144}{169}}=1. \]

5. The Dot Product

5.1 Definition

Definition. For \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^n\), \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = \mathbf u^T\mathbf v = \sum_{i=1}^{n}u_iv_i. \] The result is a scalar. [1,5]
Example 6 — Dot product. If \[ \mathbf u=\langle2,-1,3\rangle,\qquad \mathbf v=\langle4,5,-2\rangle, \] then \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v =2(4)+(-1)(5)+3(-2) =8-5-6 =-3. \]

5.2 Algebraic properties

For vectors \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\) and scalar \(c\), \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u \] (symmetry), \[ \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v+\mathbf w) = \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v+\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w \] (distributivity), \[ (c\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf v = c(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v) = \mathbf u\cdot(c\mathbf v), \] and \[ \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v=\|\mathbf v\|^2. \] [5]

Proof of \(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v=\|\mathbf v\|^2\). By the coordinate definitions, \[ \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v = \sum_{i=1}^{n}v_i^2, \] while \[ \|\mathbf v\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_i^2}. \] Squaring the norm gives the dot product with itself. \(\square\)

5.3 Geometric interpretation

For nonzero vectors \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\), if \(\theta\in[0,\pi]\) is the angle between them, then \[ \boxed{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = \|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta.} \] This identity connects the coordinate definition of the dot product to Euclidean geometry. [5]

Derivation from the law of cosines. The vector \(\mathbf u-\mathbf v\) closes the triangle whose other sides have lengths \(\|\mathbf u\|\) and \(\|\mathbf v\|\). Hence \[ \|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2 = \|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2 -2\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta. \] Algebraically, \[ \|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2 = (\mathbf u-\mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf u-\mathbf v) = \|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2-2\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v. \] Equating the two expressions yields the formula. [5]

The sign of the dot product encodes the qualitative angle: \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v>0 \Rightarrow \theta<90^\circ, \] \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0 \Rightarrow \theta=90^\circ \quad\text{for nonzero vectors}, \] \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v<0 \Rightarrow \theta>90^\circ. \] This follows immediately from the sign of \(\cos\theta\) on \([0,\pi]\). [5]

5.4 Angle between vectors

Solving the geometric dot-product identity for \(\theta\) gives \[ \boxed{ \theta = \arccos\left( \frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v} {\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|} \right) } \qquad(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0). \] [5]

Example 7 — Angle. Let \[ \mathbf u=\langle1,1,0\rangle,\qquad \mathbf v=\langle1,0,1\rangle. \] Then \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=1,\qquad \|\mathbf u\|=\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt2, \] so \[ \cos\theta=\frac{1}{2}, \qquad \theta=60^\circ. \]

5.5 Orthogonality

Definition. Vectors \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) are orthogonal when \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0. \] For nonzero Euclidean vectors, this is equivalent to their meeting at a right angle. The zero vector is algebraically orthogonal to every vector because \(\mathbf0\cdot\mathbf v=0\). [5]

5.6 Cauchy–Schwarz and the existence of the angle

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality states \[ |\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v| \le \|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|. \] Therefore, for nonzero vectors, \[ -1\le \frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v} {\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|} \le1, \] which guarantees that the arccosine formula for the angle is well-defined. Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz occurs exactly when the vectors are linearly dependent. [1]

6. Projection

6.1 Scalar projection

The signed scalar component of \(\mathbf v\) in the direction of a nonzero vector \(\mathbf u\) is \[ \operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v = \frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|}. \] If \(\widehat{\mathbf u}=\mathbf u/\|\mathbf u\|\), then this is simply \[ \operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v = \mathbf v\cdot\widehat{\mathbf u}. \] [5]

6.2 Vector projection

Definition. The orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf v\) onto the line spanned by \(\mathbf u\ne\mathbf0\) is \[ \boxed{ \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v = \frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v} {\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\mathbf u = \frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v} {\|\mathbf u\|^2}\mathbf u. } \] [5]

The residual \[ \mathbf r = \mathbf v-\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v \] is orthogonal to \(\mathbf u\). Indeed, \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf r = \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v - \frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|^2} (\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u) =0. \] Thus every vector decomposes into a component parallel to \(\mathbf u\) and a component orthogonal to \(\mathbf u\). This one-dimensional projection is the prototype for orthogonal projection onto higher-dimensional subspaces. [3,5]

Example 8 — Projection. Let \[ \mathbf u=\langle2,1\rangle,\qquad \mathbf v=\langle3,4\rangle. \] Then \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=10,\qquad \|\mathbf u\|^2=5, \] so \[ \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v = \frac{10}{5}\mathbf u = 2\langle2,1\rangle = \langle4,2\rangle. \] The orthogonal residual is \[ \mathbf r = \langle3,4\rangle-\langle4,2\rangle = \langle-1,2\rangle, \] and \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf r=2(-1)+1(2)=0. \]

7. Work and Other Geometric Applications of the Dot Product

7.1 Work done by a constant force

If a constant force \(\mathbf F\) causes displacement \(\mathbf d\), the mechanical work associated with the force along that displacement is \[ \boxed{ W=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d =\|\mathbf F\|\,\|\mathbf d\|\cos\theta, } \] where \(\theta\) is the angle between the force and displacement. [5]

This formula captures only the component of force parallel to the motion. A force perpendicular to the displacement has zero dot product and therefore contributes zero work in this constant-force model. [5]

Example 9 — Work. Suppose \[ \mathbf F=\langle40,10\rangle\text{ N}, \qquad \mathbf d=\langle6,2\rangle\text{ m}. \] Then \[ W = 40(6)+10(2) = 260\text{ J}. \]

7.2 Distance from a point to a line

Let a line pass through point \(A\) with direction vector \(\mathbf u\ne\mathbf0\), and let \(P\) be another point. Set \[ \mathbf v=\overrightarrow{AP}. \] The part of \(\mathbf v\) orthogonal to the line is \[ \mathbf v_\perp = \mathbf v-\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v. \] Therefore the point-to-line distance is \[ d(P,L)=\|\mathbf v_\perp\|. \] This follows directly from orthogonal projection and the fact that the shortest segment from a point to a Euclidean line is perpendicular to the line. [3,5]

8. The Cross Product

8.1 Cross product in \(\mathbb R^3\)

The standard cross product is a binary operation specific to three-dimensional Euclidean vector algebra: \[ \times:\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^3. \] For \[ \mathbf u=\langle u_1,u_2,u_3\rangle,\qquad \mathbf v=\langle v_1,v_2,v_3\rangle, \] it is defined by \[ \boxed{ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v = \left\langle u_2v_3-u_3v_2,\; u_3v_1-u_1v_3,\; u_1v_2-u_2v_1 \right\rangle. } \] [6]

A common mnemonic writes this as the formal determinant \[ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\ u_1&u_2&u_3\\ v_1&v_2&v_3 \end{vmatrix}, \] expanded along the first row. [6]

Example 10 — Computing a cross product. Let \[ \mathbf u=\langle1,2,3\rangle,\qquad \mathbf v=\langle4,-1,2\rangle. \] Then \[ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v = \left\langle 2(2)-3(-1),\; 3(4)-1(2),\; 1(-1)-2(4) \right\rangle = \langle7,10,-9\rangle. \]

8.2 Right-hand rule and orientation

The cross product has both magnitude and an orientation-dependent direction. For nonparallel \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\), \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\) is perpendicular to the plane they span, and the choice between the two possible normal directions is determined by the right-hand rule. Reversing the operands reverses the direction: \[ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v = -(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u). \] Thus the cross product is anticommutative, not commutative. [6]

For the standard right-handed basis, \[ \mathbf i\times\mathbf j=\mathbf k,\qquad \mathbf j\times\mathbf k=\mathbf i,\qquad \mathbf k\times\mathbf i=\mathbf j, \] and reversing any pair changes the sign. [6]

8.3 Orthogonality

Theorem. For all \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^3\), \[ \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)=0, \qquad \mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)=0. \] Hence, whenever the cross product is nonzero, it is orthogonal to both factors. [6]
Coordinate verification. Using the component formula, \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v) &= u_1(u_2v_3-u_3v_2) +u_2(u_3v_1-u_1v_3) +u_3(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)\\ &=0 \end{aligned} \] because the six terms cancel in pairs. The second identity follows similarly. \(\square\)

8.4 Magnitude and the sine formula

If \(\theta\in[0,\pi]\) is the angle between \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\), then \[ \boxed{ \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\| = \|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta. } \] Consequently, \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\mathbf0\) exactly when at least one vector is zero or the two vectors are parallel/antiparallel. [6]

Together with the dot-product formula, \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = \|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta, \] the cross product provides the sine-based complement to the dot product’s cosine-based geometric information. [5,6]

8.5 Algebraic properties

For \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\in\mathbb R^3\) and \(c\in\mathbb R\), \[ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=-(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u), \] \[ \mathbf u\times(\mathbf v+\mathbf w) = \mathbf u\times\mathbf v+\mathbf u\times\mathbf w, \] \[ (c\mathbf u)\times\mathbf v = c(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v) = \mathbf u\times(c\mathbf v), \] \[ \mathbf v\times\mathbf v=\mathbf0. \] The cross product is bilinear and anticommutative, but it is not associative in general. [6]

8.6 Area of a parallelogram

If adjacent sides of a parallelogram are \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\), its area is \[ \boxed{ A_{\mathrm{parallelogram}} = \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|. } \] Indeed, if the base has length \(\|\mathbf u\|\), then the altitude relative to that base is \(\|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta\), giving \[ A = \|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta. \] [6]

8.7 Area of a triangle

A triangle determined by the same two side vectors has half the parallelogram’s area: \[ \boxed{ A_{\triangle} = \frac12\|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|. } \] [6]

Example 11 — Triangle area from three points. Let \[ A=(1,0,0),\quad B=(3,1,0),\quad C=(2,4,2). \] Then \[ \overrightarrow{AB}=\langle2,1,0\rangle,\qquad \overrightarrow{AC}=\langle1,4,2\rangle. \] Their cross product is \[ \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = \langle2,-4,7\rangle, \] so \[ A_{\triangle} = \frac12\sqrt{2^2+(-4)^2+7^2} = \frac{\sqrt{69}}{2}. \]

9. Scalar Triple Product and Volume

9.1 Definition

Definition. For \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\in\mathbb R^3\), the scalar triple product is \[ \boxed{ \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w). } \] It is a scalar. [6]

The scalar triple product equals the determinant of the \(3\times3\) matrix whose rows (or, consistently, whose columns) are the three vectors: \[ \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w) = \det \begin{bmatrix} u_1&u_2&u_3\\ v_1&v_2&v_3\\ w_1&w_2&w_3 \end{bmatrix}. \] It is invariant under cyclic permutation, \[ \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w) = \mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf w\times\mathbf u) = \mathbf w\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v), \] and changes sign when two vectors are exchanged. [6]

9.2 Geometric interpretation and volume

The cross product \(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w\) is normal to the base parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf v\) and \(\mathbf w\), with magnitude equal to the base area. Taking its dot product with \(\mathbf u\) multiplies this base area by the signed height of \(\mathbf u\) in the normal direction. Therefore the volume of the parallelepiped generated by \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\) is \[ \boxed{ V = \left| \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w) \right|. } \] [6]

The absolute value is necessary because the scalar triple product is signed: its sign records orientation, whereas geometric volume is nonnegative. [6]

9.3 Coplanarity test

Three vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) are coplanar exactly when \[ \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)=0. \] Geometrically, zero triple product means the associated parallelepiped has zero volume; algebraically, it means the determinant of the component matrix is zero. [6]

Example 12 — Volume. Let \[ \mathbf u=\langle1,0,2\rangle,\quad \mathbf v=\langle2,1,0\rangle,\quad \mathbf w=\langle0,3,1\rangle. \] First, \[ \mathbf v\times\mathbf w = \langle1,-2,6\rangle. \] Then \[ \mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w) = 1(1)+0(-2)+2(6) = 13. \] Hence the parallelepiped volume is \[ V=|13|=13. \]

10.1 Conceptual hierarchy

The fundamental operations are addition and scalar multiplication. They generate linear combinations and therefore spans, subspaces, bases, and matrix-vector transformations. The Euclidean dot product adds metric structure: lengths, angles, orthogonality, and projections. In \(\mathbb R^3\), the cross product adds an orientation-dependent construction that produces a normal vector and encodes area. Combining dot and cross products produces the scalar triple product and oriented volume. This progression mirrors the standard development of elementary vector and linear algebra. [1,3,5,6]

Common mistakes to avoid.
  • Confusing a point \((x,y,z)\) with a vector \(\langle x,y,z\rangle\).
  • Assuming two vectors with equal length are equal.
  • Using the angle formula when one vector is zero; the direction of the zero vector is undefined.
  • Confusing the dot product (scalar) with componentwise multiplication (vector).
  • Assuming the cross product is commutative; in fact, reversing the order changes its sign.
  • Forgetting the factor \(1/2\) when using a cross product to compute triangle area.
  • Forgetting the absolute value when converting a scalar triple product into geometric volume.
