01

The one fact

Everything that follows is a consequence

A process sends a message and waits. The reply does not come. There are many reasons why: the peer crashed; the peer is alive but its garbage collector has stopped the world for nine seconds; the request was dropped by a switch; the reply was dropped by a different switch; the peer processed the request successfully and then died before answering; the peer is fine and the network between you is being reconfigured. From where the caller sits, these are the same event. There is no observation the caller can make, and no protocol it can run, that distinguishes a peer that is slow from a peer that is dead.

This is not an engineering shortfall waiting to be fixed by better monitoring. It is a property of asynchronous message passing, and it was proved to be fundamental in 1985: in an asynchronous system where even one process may fail by crashing, there is no deterministic algorithm that always reaches agreement.1 The proof turns on exactly this ambiguity. An algorithm that waits forever for a possibly-dead process is not live; an algorithm that gives up on a possibly-slow process may proceed without it and be wrong. The literature calls this the impossibility of consensus in asynchronous systems, but the operational statement is blunter: you cannot tell slow from dead.

Almost every technique in this article exists to manage that single ambiguity. Timeouts pick a side of it. Retries compensate for picking wrong. Idempotency keys clean up after the retries. Quorums arrange for the answer to be correct even when some participants are on the wrong side of it. Consensus protocols make a group of processes agree on which side to take. Circuit breakers stop you from taking it too often. Sagas accept that you will sometimes act on a wrong guess and plan the apology in advance. The mechanisms look unrelated in a textbook table of contents. They are one family.

The second consequence follows immediately. Because you cannot tell slow from dead, you cannot have partial failure without ambiguity, and partial failure is the defining feature of a distributed system. Leslie Lamport's often-quoted definition — a distributed system is one in which the failure of a computer you did not know existed can render your own computer unusable — is funny because it is a statement about dependency, but the sharper version is about knowledge. In a single process, a function call either returns or the process dies with it; there is no third outcome. Across a network there is always a third outcome, and it is the common one.

This is why the 1994 note from Sun's laboratories arguing against unifying local and remote objects has aged so well: the differences between a local call and a remote one are not differences of latency and syntax that a good abstraction can hide, but differences of partial failure and concurrency that no abstraction can hide.23 Thirty years of RPC frameworks have not made that claim less true. They have made it easier to forget.

A note on what this article is for. It is organised around the concept list that engineers actually get handed — scalability, replication, partitioning, consensus, CAP, fault tolerance, retries, sagas, delivery semantics — because that is how the subject is taught and how interviews are conducted. But it refuses to treat those as twenty separate topics. Each one is introduced as a response to the ambiguity above, and each is filed on a numbered assumption plate that records what it assumes, what it buys, what it gives up, how it fails, and what you will see in production when it is failing. The plates are collected into a ledger at the end. If you read nothing else, read the ledger: it is the article's actual argument, which is that no mechanism here removes a problem. Each one moves a problem somewhere you would rather have it.

02

How the field learned this

1975 to now, in one spine

The problem announced itself before the vocabulary existed. In 1975 a paper on network communication design described what became known as the Two Generals problem: two parties can never become certain, through an unreliable channel, that they have agreed on anything, because the last acknowledgement is always unacknowledged.4 The formal treatment of what a distributed process can be said to know arrived later and confirmed the intuition: common knowledge is unattainable over an unreliable channel.5

The founding technical result is Lamport's 1978 paper, which does three things in eleven pages. It observes that in the absence of a shared clock the only ordering you can defend is causal — the happens-before relation. It gives an algorithm, the logical clock, for producing timestamps consistent with that relation. And it shows that a total order built from those timestamps is enough to implement state machine replication.6 Every subsequent replicated log, from Paxos to Raft to Kafka, is downstream of that observation. Jim Gray's Notes on Data Base Operating Systems in the same year gave the transactional half of the vocabulary, including two-phase commit and the recognition that the commit protocol is the hard part.7

The 1980s supplied the impossibility results and the failure taxonomy. The Byzantine Generals paper established what can be achieved when components lie rather than merely stop, and fixed the 3f+1 bound for arbitrary faults.8 FLP followed in 1985.1 Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer then showed the way out: consensus is solvable under partial synchrony, where the system is eventually timely even though you never know when "eventually" started.9 Chandra and Toueg completed the picture by factoring the timing assumption into a separate module, the failure detector, and classifying exactly how weak that module can be while still permitting consensus.1011 The standard graduate texts develop all four in depth.12131415 The standard graduate texts develop all four in depth.12131415 These four results are the load-bearing theory. Everything practical is an engineering response to them.

The practical era begins with quorums — Gifford's weighted voting and Thomas's majority-consensus protocol, both 1979, which introduced the intersection argument that still governs replicated reads1617 — and continues through Schneider's state-machine tutorial,18 Herlihy and Wing's formalisation of linearizability,19 Birman's virtual synchrony,20 and the Xerox PARC work on epidemic replication that gave us gossip.21 Lamport's Paxos, submitted in 1990 and published in 1998, supplied a consensus protocol that was correct and almost universally misunderstood, prompting a second paper in 2001 that tried to explain it plainly.2223

Then the web arrived and the theory met an operations budget. Google's published stack — GFS, MapReduce, Chubby, Bigtable, and later Spanner — showed what the ideas cost at scale.2425262728 Amazon's Dynamo paper made the opposite argument, trading consistency for availability in a shopping cart where a lost write was worse than a conflict.29 Brewer's CAP conjecture, offered as a keynote in 2000 and formalised by Gilbert and Lynch in 2002, gave the trade a name and, for a decade, a great deal of confusion.3031 The clarifying correction came from Brewer himself in 2012 and from Abadi's PACELC, which pointed out that the more consequential trade happens when the system is healthy.3233

The most recent chapter is not theoretical. It is the recognition, formalised in 2021, that large systems have a second stable operating point — functioning, consuming resources, producing nearly no useful work — and that the sustaining loop which holds them there is usually made of retries.3435 The two largest internet incidents of late 2025 were both textbook instances: a latent race condition emptied a DNS record at AWS, and the resulting lease-renewal stampede kept the region degraded for ten hours after the original fault was fixed;36 and an oversized configuration file propagated to every Cloudflare edge machine and crashed the proxy that was supposed to read it.37 Neither was a consensus bug. Both were failures of the surrounding assumptions.

04

Scalability is a shape, not a number

Throughput, latency, and the cost of talking

"Scalable" is usually used to mean "fast", which is why it causes so much trouble. Scalability is a statement about a derivative: how throughput responds when you add resources. A system that serves ten thousand requests per second and cannot be made to serve eleven thousand for any amount of money is fast and not scalable. A system that serves two hundred and can be taken to two hundred thousand linearly is slow and scalable. These are different properties with different remedies.

Amdahl's law gives the first bound: if a fraction of the work is inherently serial, speedup saturates at the reciprocal of that fraction, no matter how many processors you add.40 Gustafson's rejoinder is that in practice the problem size grows with the machine, so the serial fraction shrinks in relevance — which is why batch analytics scales better than a single request.41 But neither captures what actually happens to distributed services, because both assume that adding a worker costs nothing but the worker.

Gunther's Universal Scalability Law adds the missing term. Throughput is modelled with two penalties: contention, the serial fraction, and coherency, the cost of keeping participants consistent with each other. Contention flattens the curve. Coherency, because it grows with the number of pairs of participants rather than the count, eventually bends the curve downward.4243

The retrograde region on the right of that figure is not a theoretical curiosity. It is what a chatty service mesh does at four hundred instances; what a gossip protocol does when the membership list gets large enough that anti-entropy traffic dominates useful traffic; what a distributed lock does when enough clients contend for it. The practical reading is that the question "how many nodes should this run on?" has an answer that is not "as many as possible", and that the answer is determined by the coherency term — how much the nodes must know about each other — rather than by the work itself.

Two other quantities deserve to be separated from throughput. Latency generally gets worse as you scale out, because work that used to be a function call becomes a network round trip, and because queueing delay rises steeply as utilisation approaches one.44 It is also why raw utilisation is a poor health metric on its own: two systems at 70% can sit on opposite sides of a knee.45 Availability usually gets worse too, for reasons covered in §16, unless the added nodes are genuinely redundant rather than merely additional. It is entirely possible to scale a system successfully and make it slower and less available in the same quarter, and to be surprised by this.

05

Vertical scaling, and the case for it

The option that has no distributed-systems problems

Vertical scaling means running the same program on a larger machine. It is unfashionable and frequently correct. Its decisive advantage is that it introduces no coordination: there is no partition to tolerate, no replica to be stale, no consensus to reach, no clock to skew, and no partial failure. A local transaction is atomic because the machine either does it or stops. Every mechanism in the rest of this article is unnecessary at this scale, and every one of them has a defect rate.

The ceiling is real but higher than most estimates. Contemporary cloud instances offer hundreds of vCPUs and multiple terabytes of memory in a single image, and a single well-indexed relational database on such hardware will serve workloads that most organisations will never exceed. Stack Overflow's public architecture posts are the standard citation here, but the general observation holds independently: a large fraction of systems that were rebuilt as distributed systems for scale reasons were rebuilt before they needed to be, and inherited a permanent complexity cost in exchange for headroom they did not use.

The honest arguments against vertical scaling are two, and neither is throughput. The first is blast radius: one machine is one failure domain, and its loss is total. The second is discontinuity — capacity arrives in steps whose sizes you do not control, and the step above the largest available instance does not exist. When either of those becomes the binding constraint, the decision has been made for you. Until then, the question to ask is not "will this scale?" but "what is the smallest number of machines that meets the availability requirement?", which is usually two or three rather than thirty.

Horizontal scaling and the shared-nothing bet

Capacity that grows with spend

Horizontal scaling means adding machines. It works when the work partitions — when requests can be routed to any of N interchangeable workers, or when data can be split so that most operations touch one piece. Stonebraker's 1986 case for shared-nothing architecture set the terms that still apply: partition the data, put the compute next to its partition, and avoid any shared component that all nodes must traverse, because that component becomes both the bottleneck and the failure domain.4647

The bet has two halves and they fail differently. Stateless horizontal scaling — web servers, API front ends, stream processors with external state — is close to free, which is why it is the first thing anyone does. Add instances behind a load balancer and throughput rises roughly linearly until some shared dependency saturates. That shared dependency is almost always the database, the cache, or a coordination service, and the scaling problem has therefore been moved rather than solved. Cluster managers exist to make this half routine: Borg, Omega and Kubernetes all treat a fleet of interchangeable workers as the unit of allocation.4849 The microservice style is largely a statement about organising that fleet along team boundaries.50

Stateful horizontal scaling is where the difficulty lives, because splitting data across nodes destroys exactly the properties that made the single-machine version simple. A foreign key that spanned two rows now spans two machines. A transaction that touched three tables now needs an atomic commitment protocol. A query that scanned an index now scatters to every shard and gathers. Each of those has a distributed solution, and each solution appears on a plate later in this article with its price attached.

One structural warning. Horizontal scaling makes it very easy to add dependencies without noticing, because adding a service is cheap and calling it is one line. The arithmetic in §16 is unforgiving about this: a request path that traverses thirty services at three nines each cannot be better than about 97% available, before any of them has an actual bug.

Partitioning, sharding, and where the keys go

The routing decision that everything else inherits

Partitioning (the term of art in the literature) and sharding (the term of art in operations) are the same thing: a rule that maps each key to one owner. The rule is chosen once and is extremely difficult to change later, because it is baked into every client's routing logic and every operator's mental model. Three families are in common use.

Range partitioning assigns contiguous key ranges to nodes. It keeps range scans cheap — WHERE created_at BETWEEN touches one or two partitions — and it is what HBase, Bigtable and CockroachDB use.2751 Its failure mode is the hot range: if the key is a timestamp, every write goes to the last partition, and you have built a distributed system with a single active node.

Hash partitioning applies a hash to the key and takes the result modulo the partition count. Load is even by construction, which is why DynamoDB and Kafka default to it.5253 Two costs follow. Range scans now touch every partition, converting a cheap query into a scatter-gather with the tail behaviour described in §18. And changing the partition count moves nearly every key, which makes resizing a migration project rather than an operation.

Consistent hashing fixes the resizing problem. Nodes and keys are hashed onto the same ring, and a key belongs to the next node clockwise; adding a node therefore takes over only the arc between it and its predecessor, moving roughly 1/N of the keys.54 The original formulation had poor balance, which is why every production implementation uses virtual nodes: each physical node claims many small tokens, so ownership variance falls with the token count. Dynamo, Cassandra and Riak all work this way.2955 Later refinements bound the load explicitly56 or avoid the ring entirely, as in jump consistent hashing.57

Three practical points that the diagrams do not show. First, hot keys defeat every scheme. A celebrity account, a single popular product, a monotonically increasing counter — these live on one partition by definition, and the remedy is application-level (key salting, request coalescing, a dedicated cache) rather than topological. Second, secondary indexes have to choose: a local index is partitioned like the data and requires a scatter-gather to query; a global index is partitioned by the indexed value and requires a distributed write on every update. Third, rebalancing is a live operation on a system under load, and the mechanism for moving a partition without losing writes is itself a small distributed protocol.58 Vitess exists largely to run this operation safely for MySQL,59 and the survey literature is a useful map of which stores made which choice.60 The peer-to-peer lineage of the ring — Chord and its successors — is where the routing arguments were first worked out.61

Replication: where writes are allowed to land

Three topologies, three failure modes

Partitioning splits data so each piece lives somewhere. Replication copies each piece so it lives in more than one place. The two are orthogonal and almost always combined: a partition is replicated, and the replicas of different partitions are spread across nodes so that no single loss takes out a whole partition.

Replication buys three distinct things that are worth separating, because they justify different designs. It buys durability (the data survives a machine); availability (the service survives a machine); and locality (a reader in Frankfurt does not pay a transatlantic round trip). A design tuned for the first may be wrong for the third.

Single-leader replication designates one replica as the only one that accepts writes; followers apply the leader's log. This is PostgreSQL streaming replication, MySQL replication, MongoDB replica sets, Kafka partitions, and the internals of most managed relational services. Cloud-native designs push the idea further by replicating the log rather than the pages, as Aurora does in shipping redo records to a quorum of storage nodes.62 Its great virtue is that the leader is a natural serialisation point, so concurrency control is a single-machine problem. Its costs are that writes are unavailable during failover, and that the failover itself is a consensus problem in disguise — you must not end up with two leaders.

Whether followers are updated synchronously or asynchronously is the sharpest single knob in this whole subject. Synchronous replication means the write is not acknowledged until a follower has it, so a leader crash loses nothing; it also means the write is as slow as the slowest required follower, and unavailable if that follower is down. Asynchronous replication is fast and lossy: a leader crash discards whatever had not yet shipped. Semi-synchronous configurations — one synchronous follower, the rest asynchronous — are the common compromise, and the reason most managed databases can promise both low write latency and a bounded recovery point.

Multi-leader replication allows writes at more than one replica, which is the only way to get local write latency in several regions at once. It converts an availability problem into a conflict-resolution problem: two regions can modify the same row concurrently and both accept it, and something must reconcile them afterwards. Everything in §15 about conflict resolution applies, and the honest summary is that multi-leader is straightforward for data that is naturally partitioned by user or region and treacherous for anything with a global invariant.

Leaderless replication — Dynamo's model, inherited by Cassandra and Riak — has clients write to several replicas directly and read from several, using quorum arithmetic to guarantee overlap.29 There is no failover because there is no leader, which removes an entire class of incident. The price is that reads must reconcile divergent replicas at read time, and that the system's guarantees are now a function of client-side parameters that application developers can, and do, set wrongly.

Failover deserves its own warning. The 2018 GitHub incident is the canonical public example: a 43-second network partition between coast-side data centres triggered an automated failover, after which both sides had accepted writes, and reconciling them took twenty-four hours of degraded service.63 The mechanism worked as designed. The design assumed the detector was right.

Quorums: buying intersection from both sides

R + W > N, and what it does not cover

A quorum system replaces "ask the leader" with "ask enough replicas that you cannot have missed anything". The arithmetic is Gifford's and Thomas's, from 1979: if every write is acknowledged by W replicas and every read consults R replicas, and R + W > N, then every read set intersects every write set, so at least one replica in every read has seen the most recent write.1617 The reader then takes the highest version it sees.

The grid above is measured rather than asserted: 4,000 trials per cell, N=5, with a random minority of replicas hidden from the writer. Every cell above the staircase reads 0.0% stale, and the cells below it degrade smoothly toward 79% for W=1, R=1. The second number in that figure is the one usually left out of the textbook version: W=4 made the write fail outright in 32% of trials and W=5 in 66%, because a write quorum larger than the number of reachable replicas is simply unavailable. Intersection is not free, and you can buy it from the read side or the write side but you must buy it.

Three qualifications matter in production. First, R + W > N is a statement about a fixed N. If membership changes — a node is replaced, a replica is rebuilt from an old snapshot, an operator adds a data centre — the intersection argument silently stops holding during the transition, which is why membership changes in quorum systems are themselves run through a consensus protocol. Even the election itself needs care under churn: etcd added a pre-vote phase precisely so that a node returning from a partition cannot disrupt a healthy leader by incrementing its term.64

Second, quorums do not give you linearizability by themselves. Two concurrent writes can each reach a quorum with neither observing the other, and a read that lands after both must pick one. Dynamo's answer was to return both to the client with a version vector and let the application merge.29 Cassandra's default answer is last-write-wins by timestamp, which is convenient and silently discards data.

Third, real leaderless systems relax the arithmetic deliberately. Sloppy quorums accept W writes from any W reachable nodes rather than the W designated for that key, storing a hinted handoff to be delivered when the real owner returns. This raises write availability and abandons the intersection guarantee for the duration.29 Divergence is then repaired by two background mechanisms: read repair, which fixes replicas that were caught out during a read, and anti-entropy, which compares Merkle trees of key ranges between replicas so that only differing subtrees are exchanged.65 Gossip carries the membership and failure information those mechanisms need, using the epidemic protocols from PARC2166 or the SWIM protocol that separates failure detection from dissemination.67

Load balancing is failure detection wearing a hat

Placement, health checks, and the feedback loop nobody drew

A load balancer answers a routing question — which backend gets this request? — and a health question — which backends are eligible? The first is a performance problem with elegant theory. The second is the slow-versus-dead problem again, and it is where load balancers cause outages.

On placement, the theory is unusually satisfying. Assigning each request to a uniformly random backend leaves a peak load noticeably above the mean. Sampling two backends at random and sending to the lighter of the two reduces the peak dramatically — the "power of two choices" result, which improves the expected maximum load from logarithmic to log-logarithmic in the number of bins.6869 Global least-loaded is marginally better and requires state that a distributed fleet of balancers does not have.

That measured gap is why least_request with two-choice sampling is Envoy's default policy and why the same idea appears in schedulers such as Sparrow.7071 The practical lesson is that a small amount of local information beats none by a wide margin and beats perfect global information by very little — which is a good summary of distributed systems generally.

Layer matters for what you can decide on. L4 balancing routes connections by IP and port, is stateless per packet, and scales to enormous throughput; Google's Maglev is the reference design, using consistent hashing so that connections survive changes in the balancer fleet.72 L7 balancing terminates the request and can route on path, header or method, retry idempotent requests, and enforce per-route timeouts — all of which require it to understand the protocol, and therefore to be a stateful, more expensive hop. Client-side balancing, as in gRPC and service meshes, removes the hop entirely and pushes the placement decision into every caller.73

The health question is where the interesting failures are. A health check is a failure detector with all of the properties described in §11: too aggressive and it removes healthy backends, too lax and it keeps sending traffic into a black hole. The AWS incident of October 2025 shows the sharp edge. Newly launched EC2 instances were brought into service before their network state had propagated, so health checks against them alternated between passing and failing. The oscillation increased load on the health-checking subsystem, which degraded, which delayed checks further, which triggered automatic availability-zone DNS failover and removed working capacity from service.36 Engineers resolved it by disabling automatic health check failover — that is, by turning off the safety mechanism, because the mechanism had become the fault.

Two design rules follow. Health checks should be hysteretic — require several consecutive failures to eject and several successes to restore — so that a flapping backend does not flap the fleet. And ejection should be velocity-limited: no single signal should be allowed to remove more than a small fraction of capacity, because a health check that can remove everything will eventually do so. AWS's own remediation for the 2025 event was exactly this, adding a control on how much capacity one load balancer may withdraw.36 Envoy's outlier detection ships the same idea as max_ejection_percent.74

Consensus, and why it is expensive on purpose

Agreement despite the ambiguity

Consensus is the problem of getting a group of processes to agree on one value, such that the value was proposed by someone, everyone who decides decides the same thing, and every correct process eventually decides. FLP says you cannot have all three deterministically in an asynchronous system with one possible crash.1 Every real protocol therefore takes the same escape route: it keeps safety unconditionally and sacrifices liveness during bad periods. A Raft cluster with an unstable network does not produce two conflicting decisions; it produces no decisions until the network settles.

Chandra and Toueg made this precise by separating the timing assumption into a failure detector module and asking how weak it can be. The answer, ◇S — a detector that eventually suspects all crashed processes and eventually stops suspecting at least one correct process — is the weakest that solves consensus given a majority of correct processes.1011 In practice you implement ◇S with a heartbeat and a timeout, which is why every consensus system has an election timeout in its configuration and why tuning it is a real operational activity.

Paxos is the classical protocol.22 Its reputation for difficulty is partly deserved and partly a consequence of the original presentation; the paper's second attempt at explanation is four pages,23 and the literature contains a genre of papers whose purpose is to make it implementable.7576 Raft was designed explicitly for understandability, decomposing the problem into leader election, log replication and safety, and constraining elections so that only a candidate with an up-to-date log can win.7778 Later analysis established that the two are closer than the framing suggests: Raft is essentially Multi-Paxos with a particular leader-election rule and a contiguity constraint on logs.79

Variants exist for particular pressures. Viewstamped Replication predates Paxos and reaches similar conclusions from a replication rather than an agreement angle.8081 ZAB, ZooKeeper's protocol, is optimised for a primary-backup workload with high read volume.82 EPaxos removes the leader bottleneck by committing non-interfering commands in one round trip.83 KRaft, the metadata quorum that replaced ZooKeeper in Kafka 4.0, stores cluster metadata as an append-only log rather than a tree.84 When participants may lie rather than merely stop, you need Byzantine protocols — PBFT and its descendants, of which HotStuff's linear message complexity underpins most modern BFT systems8586 — or Nakamoto's probabilistic alternative, which trades determinism for open membership.87

The engineering point that matters more than the protocol choice: consensus is for metadata, not for the data path. Chubby's designers said so explicitly — it is a lock service and a small file store, used to elect leaders and publish configuration, not to hold your records.26 ZooKeeper, etcd and Consul occupy the same role.8889 Every write to a majority-quorum system waits for a majority of disks, in the worst case across availability zones, and that latency belongs on the control plane where it happens rarely, not on the request path where it happens constantly.

Leader election, leases, and split brain

Exclusion in a system with no shared clock

Leader election is consensus applied to a single question: who is in charge? The naive implementation — a lock in a database, a key in etcd — is correct only if you are careful about a detail that the API hides. A lock held by a process that has become unreachable must eventually be releasable, or the system stops. But a lock that is released while the holder is still running produces two holders. This is slow-versus-dead in its purest form.

The standard answer is the lease: a lock with an expiry that the holder must renew.90 If the holder vanishes, the lease lapses and someone else takes over. The correctness of this depends on a clock assumption — that the grantor's and holder's notion of elapsed time do not diverge by more than the safety margin. A holder that is paused for longer than its lease (a long garbage-collection pause, a virtual machine migration, a stopped container) may wake up believing it still holds a lease that has been granted to someone else.

The remedy is a fencing token: a monotonically increasing number issued with each lease grant, presented on every write to the protected resource, and rejected by the resource if it is lower than the highest seen. This converts a timing assumption into an ordering check, and it is the difference between a lock that is usually right and a lock that is safe.91 Storage systems that do not support fencing cannot be protected by a distributed lock, however good the lock service is.

Kubernetes implements leader election on top of a first-class Lease object in the coordination API group, with holderIdentity, leaseDurationSeconds and renewTime fields; the scheduler and controller-manager use it for high availability, and node heartbeats use it as well.92 More recent releases add coordinated leader election, in which candidates advertise themselves and a deterministic strategy picks among them — motivated by version-skew constraints during upgrades rather than by safety.93

The AWS October 2025 incident contains an instructive lease story on a much larger scale. EC2's DropletWorkflow Manager holds a lease on every physical server it manages, and each lease requires a periodic state check that depends on DynamoDB. When DynamoDB became unreachable, those checks failed and leases across the fleet began timing out. When DynamoDB recovered, every droplet in the region needed a new lease at once — and the work took long enough that leases timed out again before they could be established, queueing more work behind them. AWS's own description is that the subsystem "had entered a state of congestive collapse and was unable to make forward progress".36 The lease mechanism was correct. Its recovery path had never been exercised at regional scale.

CAP, PACELC, and the trade you make every day

The rare choice and the constant one

Brewer's conjecture, presented in 2000 and formalised by Gilbert and Lynch in 2002, states that a distributed data store cannot simultaneously provide consistency, availability and partition tolerance.3031 The formal version is narrow and precise: consistency means linearizability, availability means every request to a non-failed node returns a non-error response, and the theorem says that in the presence of a partition you cannot have both.

The popular version — "pick two of three" — is wrong in a way that has cost the industry a great deal of confused architecture. Partitions are not a design choice; they are a property of networks. The system does not get to decline them. So the real statement is conditional: when a partition occurs, you must choose between consistency and availability, and "CA" describes a system that has simply not decided what it will do when the network splits. Brewer said as much in his 2012 retrospective, adding that the choice is not global or permanent — it can be made per operation, and a well-designed system detects partitions, enters an explicit degraded mode, and runs a recovery process afterwards.32 Kleppmann's critique goes further, arguing that CAP's definitions are narrow enough that the theorem's practical guidance is limited and that the richer vocabulary of consistency models is more useful.94

Abadi's PACELC is the more useful formulation because it names the trade you actually make every day. If Partitioned, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, choose Latency or Consistency.33 The second clause is the one that governs your p99 on a healthy Tuesday. A majority-quorum write costs a round trip to a majority of replicas whether or not anything is broken. Spanner pays that cost — plus a commit-wait bounded by clock uncertainty — in exchange for external consistency.28 DynamoDB's default eventually-consistent read does not pay it, and returns a possibly-stale answer at single-digit-millisecond latency.

The frequency asymmetry is worth internalising. Partitions inside a well-managed region are rare — weeks or months apart. Requests are constant. A system tuned entirely around the P clause, at the cost of the E clause, has optimised for the uncommon case. This is the most common architectural error in the space, and it usually shows up as a global consensus round on a read path that could have been served from a local replica.

Consistency models, from strict to eventual

A lattice, and one line across it

"Strong" and "eventual" are the two words most engineers have, and there are roughly a dozen useful models between them. The hierarchy is a lattice: each model permits every behaviour permitted by the models below it, so a stronger model is a smaller set of allowed histories and therefore a larger set of forbidden anomalies.9596

Linearizability is the strongest single-object model: every operation appears to take effect instantaneously at some point between its invocation and its response, and that point respects real time. Herlihy and Wing's formulation makes it composable, which is why it is the standard for registers and for coordination services.19 It is what etcd gives you on a quorum read, and what makes "read your own write" free rather than something you have to arrange.

Sequential consistency requires a single total order that respects each process's program order but not real time.97 Two clients can both be told an operation happened, and disagree about whether it happened before or after an operation a third client observed on their wall clock. This distinction sounds pedantic and is the entire content of the phrase "strict serializable" versus "serializable".

Causal consistency requires only that operations related by happens-before are seen in that order by everyone; concurrent operations may be seen in any order.698 This is enough to prevent the class of anomaly users actually notice — a reply appearing before the message it replies to, a photo visible before the album it was added to — and it is attainable without coordination. Adya's formalism of isolation levels as cycles in a dependency graph is the corresponding vocabulary on the transactional side, and is what modern checkers actually test against.99

The session guarantees from the Bayou project sit below that and are per-client rather than global: read-your-writes, monotonic reads, monotonic writes, and writes-follow-reads.100101 They are cheap, they are usually what a user means by "consistent", and they are frequently implementable by sticky routing plus a version token in a cookie.

The horizontal line in the figure is the most important structural fact on it. Bailis and colleagues established which models are achievable by a system that remains totally available during a partition: causal consistency, the session guarantees, and a substantial set of transactional isolation levels — read committed, monotonic atomic view, and several others — are all available.102 Anything requiring a global order, and any invariant that requires comparing state on both sides of the split, is not. The line is not a matter of implementation quality. It is the CAP boundary drawn at the right resolution.

Two systems illustrate what the top of the lattice costs. Spanner achieves external consistency by giving every transaction a timestamp from TrueTime, an interval-valued clock with bounded uncertainty, and then waiting out that uncertainty before committing — trading a few milliseconds of latency for a guarantee that timestamp order matches real order.28 Aurora DSQL takes a different route: optimistic concurrency control with strong snapshot isolation, so transactions hold no locks while executing and coordinate across regions only at commit time; conflicting transactions are aborted and retried by the client.103104 Both use tightly synchronised physical clocks; hybrid logical clocks are the general technique for combining physical time with causal tracking.105106107

Eventual consistency in practice

Convergence, and the conflicts it hides

Eventual consistency promises that if writes stop, replicas converge. It is the weakest useful guarantee and the most widely deployed one. DNS is eventually consistent, and the entire internet is built on it. CDN caches are eventually consistent. Search indexes, materialised views, analytics pipelines, and every read replica with asynchronous replication are eventually consistent.108

The trouble is not the convergence; it is what convergence means when two replicas hold different values. Something must choose, and the choice is where data disappears.

Last-write-wins resolves by timestamp. It is the default in Cassandra and it is a silent lost-update machine: two concurrent updates to different fields of the same row become one update, and the loser is discarded with no error and no record. Worse, it depends on clock synchronisation across writers, so a node with a fast clock wins arguments it should have lost.55

Version vectors detect concurrency rather than resolving it, returning both values as siblings and asking the application to merge — Dynamo's approach, and the honest one, because the application is the only layer that knows whether two shopping carts should be unioned or one should win.29109110

CRDTs — conflict-free replicated data types — remove the conflict by construction. If every pair of concurrent operations commutes, then replicas that have seen the same set of operations, in any order, hold the same state, and merge is a mathematical join rather than a policy.111112 Counters, sets, registers, sequences and JSON documents all have CRDT formulations, and the technology is production reality: Automerge and Yjs power local-first collaborative editors, and Figma's multiplayer engine is built on a related model.113114115

The limitation is precise and easily glossed over. CRDTs give you convergence, not correctness. A grow-only counter converges; a counter with a constraint that it must never go below zero does not, because "check the balance and then decrement" is not commutative. Any invariant that spans replicas — uniqueness of a username, a seat sold once, a non-negative balance — requires coordination, and no amount of algebra removes that requirement. What CRDTs do is let you identify precisely which parts of your data model need coordination and confine it there.

Fault tolerance and the arithmetic of nines

Redundancy helps; dependencies do not

Fault tolerance begins with a failure model, because a mechanism that tolerates crashes does not tolerate corruption. The standard hierarchy runs: crash-stop (a process halts and stays halted); crash-recovery (it halts and comes back, possibly with stale state); omission (messages are lost); timing (responses are late enough to be indistinguishable from lost); and Byzantine (arbitrary behaviour, including lies).1168 Costs rise sharply along that list: crash faults need 2f+1 replicas to tolerate f failures, Byzantine faults need 3f+1 and several rounds of message exchange. Most production systems choose crash-recovery with omission and treat corruption as a checksum problem rather than a consensus problem.

Redundancy is the base technique, and it comes in two flavours with different economics. Replication stores N full copies; tolerating two failures costs 3× the storage. Erasure coding splits an object into k data fragments plus m parity fragments, tolerating m losses at a storage overhead of (k+m)/k — 1.5× for a 10-of-15 code rather than 3× — at the cost of reconstruction reads that touch many nodes, which makes it right for cold data and wrong for hot small objects.117118 A third option exists where the data is derived rather than authoritative: record the computation and recompute on loss, which is what Spark's resilient distributed datasets do with lineage.119

The left panel of that figure is the one that ruins availability targets. Availability composes multiplicatively across serial dependencies: a request that must traverse thirty services, each independently available 99.9% of the time, is available about 97.0% of the time. Nobody designs that number; it accumulates one reasonable decision at a time. Every service call, every DNS lookup, every sidecar, every token-validation round trip and every remote config fetch is a term in that product.

The right panel is the good news, and it comes with a condition. Independent replicas compose the other way — three replicas at 99% each give 99.9999% if their failures are independent. The condition does almost all the work. Failures stop being independent the moment replicas share a config push, a control plane, a certificate expiry, a deployment pipeline, a clock source, or a dependency. The Cloudflare event of November 2025 is the pure form: a single generated configuration file was propagated to every machine in a global network, and every machine failed the same way at the same time.37 There was no shortage of redundancy. There was a shortage of independence.

Three structural techniques address exactly this. Cell-based architecture partitions the whole system — not just the data — into independent cells with their own capacity and control plane, so that a fault is bounded by cell size.120 Shuffle sharding assigns each customer a random subset of workers rather than a contiguous shard, so that two customers rarely share the same full set and a single bad tenant degrades a small, calculable fraction of others.121 Static stability designs the data plane to keep working with the state it already has when the control plane is unavailable — the property whose absence turned an AWS DynamoDB failure into an EC2 failure, because instance launches required a control-plane path that had gone away.12236

Resilience: the part that is not about correctness

Degrading well, and the loops that stop you

Fault tolerance keeps a system correct when a component fails. Resilience is the broader property of continuing to deliver something useful when conditions are outside the design envelope — including conditions nobody enumerated. The distinction matters because most large outages are not caused by a component failing in a way the design anticipated. They are caused by the system's response to a small fault being worse than the fault.

Bronson and colleagues gave this shape a name in 2021.34 A metastable failure occurs in an open system — one whose load arrives regardless of its health. Such a system has a stable regime, in which a transient perturbation decays, and a metastable failure regime, in which the system is busy, consuming resources, and producing almost no useful work. What holds it there is a sustaining effect: a feedback loop, usually made of retries, that keeps offered load above capacity even after the original trigger has gone. Removing the trigger does not restore the system. The follow-up study found the pattern in 22 publicly documented incidents across 11 organisations, including four each at AWS, Google Cloud and Azure.35

The simulation accompanying this article reproduces it in a hundred lines. Arrivals are Poisson at 480 requests per second against a server whose capacity is 600. At t=30 s capacity drops to 200 for thirty seconds, then returns.

The result is the sharpest finding in the lab. Three retry policies — no backoff, exponential backoff, and exponential backoff with full jitter — produce identical outcomes: goodput reaches zero during the dip and stays at zero for the remaining two minutes, long after capacity has been restored. They are identical because they have identical work amplification, 2.52 attempts sent per request that arrived. Backoff and jitter change when retries arrive. They do not change how many. The policy that recovers is the one that caps the total: a retry budget of 10% holds amplification at 1.00 and returns to 99.2% of pre-dip goodput.

This is not an argument against jitter, which solves a real and different problem — synchronised clients arriving in a herd, the case AWS's guidance addresses directly.123124 It is an argument that jitter is not a defence against metastability, and that many production systems have deployed it believing that it is.

The second finding concerns headroom. Sweeping the steady arrival rate while holding everything else constant locates the boundary between the two regimes.

Below roughly 400 requests per second — about 67% of nominal capacity — the same dip is absorbed and the system recovers completely. At 420 and above it collapses and stays collapsed. Nothing about the trigger changed across that sweep. The only variable was how much headroom the system had when the trigger arrived. Utilisation targets are usually justified in terms of latency; this is the stronger justification.

Everything else in the resilience toolkit is a way of breaking or weakening a sustaining loop. Load shedding rejects work at the edge so that the work admitted can be completed; a fast rejection is cheap and a slow timeout is not.125 Backpressure propagates that signal upstream instead of buffering. Bulkheads — separate thread pools, connection pools or queues per dependency — stop one slow dependency from consuming all of a caller's concurrency.126 Prioritisation ensures that when you shed, you shed the retries and the batch traffic before the interactive requests. And graceful degradation — serving a stale cache, a default recommendation, an unpersonalised page — turns a failure into a lower-quality success.

The last component is not technical. Large systems are kept running by people who hold a model of them, and incidents are resolved by that model rather than by the architecture diagram. Cook's summary of how complex systems fail — that they run in a degraded mode continuously, that catastrophe requires multiple failures, and that the practitioners at the sharp end are simultaneously the last defence and the most convenient thing to blame — remains the most useful two pages in the literature,127 and the STELLA report is its modern continuation.128

Because none of this can be validated by reasoning alone, the field has converged on injecting the faults deliberately: chaos engineering as formalised at Netflix,129 lineage-driven fault injection which uses the structure of successful executions to search for the failures that matter,130 deterministic simulation of the kind FoundationDB built its correctness argument on,131 semantic-aware model checking that explores deep interleavings without enumerating all of them,132 and formal specification with TLA+ where the protocol is subtle enough to warrant it.133134 The empirical case for testing is strong: an analysis of 198 production failures in distributed data-intensive systems found that most catastrophic outcomes were triggered by error-handling code that was itself untested, and that a large fraction could have been caught by unit tests of the error paths alone.135136137

Timeouts: the primal control

Choosing which way to be wrong

A timeout is where the ambiguity of §1 becomes a decision. Because you cannot tell slow from dead, you set a threshold and declare that beyond it you will act as though the peer is dead. The threshold does not remove the ambiguity; it allocates the cost of being wrong. Too short and you abandon healthy work and generate load through retries. Too long and you hold resources — a thread, a connection, a lock, a customer — waiting for something that will never arrive.

Choosing the value is an empirical exercise, not a round number. The defensible method is to set it from the observed latency distribution of the specific dependency, at a high percentile plus a margin — commonly p99.9 plus some multiple of the standard deviation — and to revisit it when the distribution moves. A single global timeout applied to every dependency is always wrong for most of them, because a same-rack call and a cross-region call do not share a distribution. TCP's own retransmission timer is computed this way, from a smoothed round-trip estimate and its variance, rather than being configured.138

Two structural rules matter more than the value.

Deadlines must propagate and shrink. A request should carry an absolute deadline, not a per-hop duration, and each hop should pass on the time remaining rather than starting a fresh budget. gRPC makes this a first-class part of the protocol.139 Without it, a chain of five services each with a "reasonable" 10-second timeout can hold a client for fifty seconds, and every hop below the one that gave up computes an answer that will be discarded on arrival — which is precisely the wasted work that pushes a system into the metastable regime.

Fan-out multiplies rare events into common ones. Dean and Barroso's account of tail latency at Google is the canonical statement: if one request in a hundred is slow at a leaf, a request that must contact a hundred leaves and wait for all of them is slow 63% of the time.140 The computation is elementary and its consequences are not. It means the p99 of a leaf sets the p50 of the root, that adding a shard makes every request slightly worse, and that a service which looks healthy by its own metrics can be the cause of an unacceptable experience two layers up.

The countermeasures are also from that paper. Hedged requests send a second copy to another replica after a short delay — say, at the p95 — and take the first response, which removes almost all of the tail for a few percent extra load; the simulated curve above shows a fall from 63% to under 5% at fan-out 100. Tied requests go further, cancelling the twin when one starts executing. Micro-partitioning and selective replication of hot partitions reduce the chance that any one leaf is the straggler. All of these require the operation to be safely repeatable, which is the connection to §19.

Retries, and the arithmetic of amplification

The most dangerous three lines in any client library

A retry is the correct response to a transient failure and the mechanism by which a small fault becomes a large outage. Both statements are true and the difference between them is entirely a matter of bounds.

The first bound to understand is depth. If every service in a call chain retries three times, and the chain is four services deep, a single user request can produce 3⁴ = 81 attempts at the bottom of the stack. The service that is already struggling receives the product of everyone else's good intentions. The standard remedy is to retry at one layer only — usually the one closest to the user, which knows the request's business value — and to have deeper layers fail fast and propagate.

The second bound is rate. AWS's guidance is the standard reference and it is specific: retry with exponential backoff, and add jitter, because unjittered backoff synchronises clients into waves that arrive together.123124 Full jitter — sleeping a uniform random interval between zero and the current backoff — performs best in their measurements, and the reasoning generalises to any situation where many clients react to the same signal at the same time.

But as §17 established, jitter does not bound total work. The mechanism that does is a retry budget: a token bucket refilled as a fraction of successful requests, from which each retry must draw. When the dependency is broadly unhealthy, successes stop, the bucket empties, and retries stop with it — exactly when continuing to retry would be most harmful. In the simulation this was the difference between total collapse and 99.2% recovery. gRPC implements this as a first-class retry throttling policy; Envoy exposes it as a retry budget; the concept appears in the SRE literature as a client-side retry budget.141142

Three further rules, all learned expensively:

  • Retry only what is safe to retry. A GET is safe. A POST that creates a charge is not, unless it carries an idempotency key — see §24. Retrying a non-idempotent operation after a timeout is how duplicate orders happen, and the timeout is exactly the case where you cannot know whether the first attempt succeeded.
  • Respect the server's opinion. HTTP's Retry-After tells you when to come back;143 rate-limit headers tell you how much budget remains.144 A client that ignores them is generating load the server has explicitly said it cannot serve.
  • Distinguish retryable from terminal. A 503 and a 400 are both errors; retrying the second is pure waste. Client libraries that retry on any non-2xx are common and are a source of amplification that nobody intended.

Circuit breakers and their successors

A device for not sending

A circuit breaker watches the outcomes of calls to one dependency. While failures are below a threshold it is closed and calls pass through. When the failure rate crosses the threshold it opens and calls fail immediately without touching the network. After a cooldown it goes half-open, admitting a small number of probes; success closes it, failure re-opens it. The pattern was popularised by Nygard and made ubiquitous by Netflix's Hystrix.126145

The measured behaviour is worth stating plainly, because the usual justification for a breaker is protecting the downstream service, and the larger benefit is usually to the caller. In the simulation, adding a breaker to a client that already had jitter and a retry budget raised goodput during the capacity dip from 198 to 281 requests per second, and cut the fraction of server effort spent producing answers nobody was waiting for from 10.3% to 1.4%. Refusing to send increased throughput.

Breakers have well-known weaknesses. They are coarse: one threshold for a whole dependency, when the failure may be confined to one shard or one endpoint. They are slow to react at low traffic, because the failure rate over a small window is noisy. And a fleet of independently-tripping breakers can produce a sawtooth in which the dependency is repeatedly re-loaded by every caller's probes arriving together — the thundering-herd problem again, one level up, which is why probe admission should itself be jittered and rate-limited.

The industry's direction of travel is instructive. Netflix put Hystrix into maintenance mode in 2018, and said explicitly that its focus had moved to adaptive mechanisms that react to measured application performance rather than to pre-configured thresholds.145146 Adaptive concurrency limits are the successor idea: rather than counting failures, infer the concurrency the dependency can sustain from observed latency, using the same control-theoretic logic as TCP congestion control, and let the limit move continuously.147 Resilience4j is the maintained library in the Java ecosystem, offering breakers alongside bulkheads, rate limiters and time limiters;148 at the infrastructure layer, Envoy's outlier detection ejects individual hosts rather than whole dependencies.74

Distributed transactions and the blocking window

Atomicity across machines that share no disk

A transaction on one machine is atomic because one component — the log — decides. Across machines there is no such component, and creating one is the whole problem. Jim Gray named the pieces in 1978 and the vocabulary has not changed much since.7149150

Two-phase commit is the base protocol. A coordinator asks every participant to prepare; a participant that votes yes has durably promised it can commit and may not unilaterally change its mind. If all vote yes the coordinator writes a commit decision and tells everyone. It is safe. It is not live: between voting yes and hearing the decision, a participant is in doubt and cannot release its locks. If the coordinator dies in that window, participants block — potentially indefinitely.

Three-phase commit adds a round to make the protocol non-blocking, at the cost of assuming synchrony — which, per §1, is exactly the assumption you cannot make.151 The engineering refinements of the era — presumed abort, presumed commit, and the read-only optimisation that drops a participant from phase two entirely — came out of IBM's R* project and are still what commercial implementations do.152 The practical fix is to stop treating the coordinator as a single point: Paxos Commit replaces the coordinator's log with a consensus group, so the decision survives coordinator failure and the blocking window closes.153 This is why modern distributed SQL databases layer 2PC on top of a replicated log rather than a single coordinator.

The standardised cross-vendor form is X/Open XA, implemented by most relational databases and message brokers.154 Its reputation in microservice architectures is poor and largely deserved: it requires every participant to support the protocol, holds locks across network round trips, and couples the availability of each service to all the others. Helland's 2007 paper is the standard argument for avoiding it — that at scale you should treat each service's data as its own and build workflows out of idempotent, retryable messages instead.155156

That is not the end of the story, because the systems built since have made distributed transactions genuinely cheaper. Percolator layered snapshot-isolation transactions over Bigtable using a client-driven 2PC with a timestamp oracle.157 Calvin removed the commit protocol entirely by deterministically ordering transactions before executing them, so no agreement is needed at commit time.158 Spanner combines 2PC with Paxos-replicated participants and TrueTime timestamps to give external consistency across continents.28 CockroachDB follows a similar design with hybrid logical clocks instead of atomic ones.51 Aurora DSQL takes the optimistic route: no locks during execution, conflict detection at commit, cross-region coordination only at commit time, and a serialisation error the client is expected to retry.103104

The decision rule that survives all of this: use a distributed transaction when the invariant genuinely spans systems and the business cannot tolerate a window of inconsistency — a ledger's double entry, a seat sold once. Use the next section's approach when it can.

Sagas: buying availability with apologies

Compensation is not rollback

Garcia-Molina and Salem's 1987 paper addressed long-lived transactions in a single database: a transaction that holds locks for hours is intolerable, so split it into a sequence of short transactions, each with a compensating transaction that semantically undoes it. If step four fails, run the compensations for steps three, two and one in reverse.159 The idea transplanted to microservices almost unchanged and is now the standard alternative to 2PC across services.160

Two coordination styles are in use. Orchestration puts a single component in charge of the sequence: it calls each service, records progress durably, and drives compensation on failure. This is what Temporal, Cadence and AWS Step Functions provide, and it is easier to reason about, test and observe, at the cost of a component that knows the whole workflow.161162 Choreography has each service react to events and emit its own, with no central brain — more decoupled, and considerably harder to answer "why is this order stuck?" about.

The costs are real and are usually understated in introductions.

  • You give up isolation. Intermediate states are visible. Between "payment taken" and "courier booked", an observer sees a paid order with no shipment, and reports it. Countermeasures — semantic locks, a PENDING status the UI understands, commutative updates — are application design, not framework features.
  • Compensation is not rollback. A refund is a different event from a charge that never happened: it appears on a statement, it may cost a fee, it may arrive days later, and the customer has already seen the email. Some effects cannot be compensated at all — a notification sent, a physical item dispatched, a price quoted to a competitor.
  • Every step must be idempotent, because every step will be retried, and so must every compensation.

The transactional outbox is the pattern that makes each step safe. Writing to the database and publishing to a broker are two systems and cannot be made atomic without 2PC; instead, write the business change and an outbox row in one local transaction, and have a separate relay read the outbox and publish. The publish is at-least-once, which is why consumers need §24's deduplication, but no message is ever lost and none is ever published for a transaction that rolled back.163

Message ordering, and what a clock can carry

There is no global order to observe

Two events happened on two machines. Which came first? In the absence of a shared clock the question often has no answer, and the correct model is a partial order rather than a sequence. Lamport's happens-before relation defines it: a precedes b if they are on the same process and a came first, or if a is a send and b is its receive, or transitively. Events not related either way are concurrent, and concurrency is not a measurement failure — it is a fact about the execution.6

Lamport clocks — one counter per process, incremented on each event and advanced to max(local, received) + 1 on receipt — give timestamps consistent with happens-before, so if a → b then L(a) < L(b). The converse does not hold, which is the whole limitation: equal or ordered counters tell you nothing about whether the events were actually related. Vector clocks carry one counter per process and recover the full partial order, at a cost of O(N) metadata per message.109110 Hybrid logical clocks combine a physical timestamp with a logical component, giving values that are close to wall time, comparable to it, and still consistent with causality.105

Above these sits total order broadcast — every correct process delivers the same messages in the same order — which is equivalent to consensus. If you have one you can build the other, so the price of a global order is the price of consensus, permanently, on every message that needs it.16418

Which is why production systems buy order in the smallest unit that works. Kafka guarantees order within a partition and nothing across partitions; the partition is chosen by a key, so events for the same key are ordered and events for different keys are not.53165 Amazon SQS offers FIFO queues with ordering scoped to a message group ID.166 The design decision is therefore the choice of ordering key, and the rule is that the key must cover the scope of the invariant. If "account credited" and "account debited" must be applied in order, they must share a key. If they are keyed by transaction ID, they will be processed in parallel and the invariant is gone.

This has a direct throughput consequence: order and parallelism are traded against each other by the same choice. A coarse key gives strong ordering and limits concurrency to the number of distinct keys; a fine key gives parallelism and no cross-event ordering. There is no setting that gives both.

The related tool for reasoning about global state is the consistent cut — a set of events closed under happens-before, so no message is received in the cut without its send. Chandy and Lamport's snapshot algorithm computes one without stopping the system, and it is the direct ancestor of the checkpointing that gives stream processors their exactly-once guarantees.167168

Exactly-once is a property of effects, not of messages

At-most-once, at-least-once, and the third thing

A sender transmits and waits for an acknowledgement. It does not arrive. Either the message was lost, or it was delivered and the acknowledgement was lost. The sender cannot tell — §1 again — and has exactly two options. Do not resend, and risk losing the message: at-most-once. Resend, and risk delivering it twice: at-least-once. There is no third option at the level of message delivery, and the Two Generals result is the proof.4169 The end-to-end argument predicts where the fix must live: a guarantee the application needs can only be implemented completely at the application's own endpoints, so no amount of reliability in the transport removes the receiver's obligation.3

The simulation makes the cost of each concrete. Two thousand credits of +1 are sent over a channel that loses 15% of messages and duplicates 10% of deliveries.

At-most-once ends 5.8% short: real money missing. At-least-once ends 30.4% over: real money invented. The third row is not a stronger delivery guarantee — it is the identical at-least-once channel with a receiver that records which request identifiers it has already applied and ignores repeats. That memory suppressed 609 redundant applications and produced exactly the right answer.

This is the whole of "exactly-once" as it exists in practice. Exactly-once delivery is impossible; exactly-once effect is routine, and it is bought with an idempotency key plus receiver-side state. The pattern in HTTP APIs is a client-generated Idempotency-Key header, which Stripe pioneered and which is now being standardised;170171 the server stores the key with the response and replays the stored response on a repeat rather than re-executing.

Three ways the pattern breaks, all seen in production:

  • The window expires. Deduplication state is finite, so keys are retained for hours or days. A message stuck in a dead-letter queue and replayed a week later arrives after its key has been forgotten, and is applied again. Retention must exceed the longest plausible retry, including manual replays.
  • The key is wrong. A key derived from the request body deduplicates two genuinely distinct identical payments. A key regenerated on each client retry deduplicates nothing. The key must be generated once, by the client, per logical operation.
  • The effect escapes the ledger. Deduplication protects the database write. If the handler also sent an email before the duplicate check, the customer got two emails.

Stream processors achieve the same property differently, by making the state transactional rather than the message. Kafka's exactly-once semantics combine an idempotent producer (sequence numbers per producer and partition, so the broker discards duplicates) with transactions that atomically commit both the output records and the consumer offsets — so a reprocessed batch overwrites rather than adds.172 The mechanism has been stable across the 4.x line, which dropped ZooKeeper entirely and moved metadata into the same log abstraction.173174 Flink takes the checkpointing route, using Chandy–Lamport-style barriers to snapshot operator state consistently and resuming from the snapshot on failure.175168167 Both are correctly described as exactly-once processing, and both still deliver messages at least once underneath.

The practical default for most systems: assume at-least-once everywhere, make every consumer idempotent, and stop looking for a broker setting that removes the requirement.

What this means on Monday

The short version

A working checklist, in rough order of how often it prevents an incident.

  1. Every remote call has a timeout, and the value came from the dependency's latency distribution — not from a default, and not from the same constant as every other call.
  2. Deadlines propagate and shrink. Each hop passes on the time remaining. Work whose deadline has passed is cancelled, not completed.
  3. Retries happen at one layer, with jitter and a budget. Jitter for the herd; the budget for the amplification. Without the budget you have not addressed metastability.
  4. Every write carries an idempotency key, and the receiver retains keys for longer than the longest replay you can imagine.
  5. Steady-state utilisation leaves real headroom. The collapse threshold in the lab was 67% of capacity. Yours is a number you can measure and should. Set the target as an explicit SLO with an error budget rather than as folklore.176
  6. Health checks are hysteretic and ejection is velocity-limited. No single signal may remove more than a bounded fraction of capacity.
  7. The data plane keeps working when the control plane does not. Static stability is the property that turns a control-plane outage into a non-event.
  8. Consensus is on the control plane, not the request path. Elect leaders and store configuration with it; do not put a majority round trip in front of every read.
  9. The consistency model is written down per operation, in the vocabulary of §14 rather than as "strong" or "eventual", and the ones below the availability line are marked as such.
  10. Distributed locks carry fencing tokens, and the protected resource actually checks them.
  11. Failure independence is audited, not assumed. List what your replicas share: config pipeline, control plane, certificate, clock, image. Each shared item is a correlated failure waiting for its turn. The reliability pillar of the AWS Well-Architected Framework is a serviceable checklist for this if you have none of your own.177
  12. The failure paths are tested. Most catastrophic outages begin in error-handling code that was never executed.135

And one closing observation. Every item above is a way of taking the ambiguity from §1 and deciding, in advance and in writing, how it will be resolved. A distributed system does not become correct by removing the uncertainty. It becomes correct by choosing, deliberately, what to believe when the evidence runs out — and by making sure that whatever it believes, the damage is bounded when it is wrong.
