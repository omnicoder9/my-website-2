01

Three adjectives and no claim

the thesis

On 19 July 2024 a configuration file went out to Windows machines running a widely deployed endpoint sensor. The file had passed a validator. The sensor had passed a release process. The vendor had passed audits. Within hours, airlines, hospitals, banks and broadcasters were reading the same blue screen, and recovery required somebody to physically touch each machine.

The published root-cause analysis is unusually clear about the mechanism. A new template type defined twenty-one input parameter fields; the integration code that invoked the content interpreter supplied twenty. For four months this did not matter, because every instance shipped so far had used a wildcard for the twenty-first field. On 19 July an instance arrived that did not, the interpreter read one element past the end of its input array in kernel mode, and roughly 8.5 million machines stopped.[1,2]

Nothing in that sentence is exotic. It is an off-by-one. What makes it worth opening with is not the bug but the paperwork around it: at every gate the software passed, and every gate was measuring something real. The validator checked the file. The stress test checked the template. The staged deployments checked the earlier instances. What no gate checked was the proposition that actually mattered — the interpreter never reads beyond the inputs the template type generates — because nobody had written that proposition down in a form that could fail.

This is the ordinary condition of software quality. Ask a team whether their system is reliable and they will say yes. Ask whether it is fast, secure, maintainable, and they will say yes to those too, and mean it. Every one of those answers is an adjective, and an adjective is not a claim. It has no subject, no circumstance, no threshold, and above all no way of being wrong. Compared to what? is not a rhetorical jab; it is the question that converts a mood into a proposition.

The argument of this article is a single move applied twelve times. A quality attribute is not a property of code. It is a conditional claim about behaviour under stated circumstances, and it becomes engineering only at the moment it names the circumstance, the threshold, and the observation that would refute it. Everything else — the models, the standards, the dashboards, the maturity levels — is scaffolding around that move, useful in proportion to how much it helps you make it.

Two corollaries follow, and both are unwelcome. The first is that quality is plural and the plural does not sum. There is no scalar behind the twelve attributes, no single number that a system has more or less of, because the claims are addressed to different people under different circumstances and are traded against one another in the same finite budget of effort, structure and complexity. Raising one lowers others. The second corollary is that most of what the industry measures is chosen for being measurable rather than for being the thing at stake. Line coverage is easy; whether the suite would notice a fault is hard. Uptime at the load balancer is easy; whether a nurse could give a drug is hard.

Both corollaries have a price tag. The most-cited estimate puts the cost of poor software quality in the United States at roughly $2.41 trillion in 2022, with accumulated technical debt near $1.52 trillion — figures that should be read as order-of-magnitude rhetoric rather than measurement, since they are assembled from surveys and incident tallies with wide error bars, but which are not wrong about the direction.[3]

To keep the discussion concrete, every claim in this article is written against one system. Call it Meridian: a bedside medication administration platform. A nurse scans a patient wristband and a drug barcode on a handheld or cart-mounted tablet; Meridian checks the scan against the pharmacy order, warns about interactions, and writes an administration record. It runs on ward Wi-Fi in buildings with thick walls, on devices bought in three different procurement cycles, in hospitals that will not tolerate downtime at 03:00, under a regulator that treats it as a medical device.[4] It is deliberately unglamorous, and it exercises every attribute on the list, because a system that computes the wrong dose quickly is worse than useless and a system that computes the right dose after the patient has been moved is merely useless.

The examples around it are drawn from wherever the evidence is best: flight software, compilers, database engines, kernels, trading systems, browsers, and the accounting package that put British postmasters in prison. The attributes do not change between domains. Only the thresholds do.

What quality meant before it meant software

1924–1979

The vocabulary software borrowed came from manufacturing, and it arrived with a disagreement already inside it.

Walter Shewhart’s contribution at Bell Labs in the 1920s was not a definition of quality but a way of separating two kinds of variation: the noise a process makes when it is behaving, and the signal that something has changed. The control chart made that distinction operational, and in doing so it established the pattern every quality regime since has copied — you do not manage the thing, you manage a measure of the thing against a limit, and you argue about whether the measure is the thing.[5] W. Edwards Deming and Joseph Juran carried this into post-war industry with two different emphases that are still visible in every engineering organisation. Juran defined quality as fitness for use, which puts the customer’s purpose at the centre and makes quality irreducibly relative.[6] Deming attacked inspection itself: you cannot inspect quality into a product, you have to build the process that produces it, and most defects are the system’s fault rather than the worker’s.[7] Philip Crosby then pushed the other way, defining quality as conformance to requirements and insisting that it is free, because prevention costs less than rework.[8]

Crosby’s definition is the one software engineering absorbed most deeply and the one that causes the most trouble. Conformance to requirements is a perfectly good definition when the requirements are right. It has nothing to say when they are wrong, and in software they are routinely wrong — not through carelessness, but because the requirement is a statement about a world that the system will change as soon as it enters it.[9]

David Garvin’s 1984 taxonomy is the cleanest map of the disagreement. He identified five views of quality that people hold simultaneously without noticing: the transcendent (you know it when you see it), the product-based (a measurable quantity of some attribute), the user-based (fitness for purpose), the manufacturing-based (conformance to specification), and the value-based (quality at an acceptable price).[10] Most arguments about whether a system is any good are two people in different cells of that table. An architect defending a design is usually arguing product-based; a support lead is arguing user-based; a finance director is arguing value-based; and an auditor is arguing manufacturing-based. All four can be correct at once.

Gerald Weinberg’s formulation is the one that survives best because it refuses to hide the politics: quality is value to some person. The sentence looks like a shrug and is in fact a demand — name the person. Weinberg had spent the previous two decades arguing that the social structure of programming determines its output more than its tools do, which is why The Psychology of Computer Programming reads as a quality book in disguise.[11,12] The moment you name the person, the adjective starts to acquire a subject, and the subject starts to imply a circumstance. That is rung two of the ladder.

One more inheritance is worth naming, because software keeps rediscovering it. Marilyn Strathern’s compression of Goodhart’s law — when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure — was written about university audit, not software, and it describes with precision what happens to coverage percentages, story-point velocity, defect counts and availability dashboards the moment they are attached to compensation.[13] Everything in this article that recommends a measure is subject to it.

03

The enumeration era, and what a model is for

1968–2023

The NATO conference at Garmisch in October 1968 is conventionally dated as the moment the discipline named its own problem. Reading the report now, what strikes you is not the phrase “software crisis” but how much of the discussion is about quality specifically: reliability, maintainability, the impossibility of testing large systems adequately, the gap between what was promised and what was delivered.[14] The Rome follow-up a year later was gloomier and more honest about how little anyone knew.[15] Dijkstra’s contribution to the vocabulary — program testing can be used to show the presence of bugs, but never to show their absence — is the single most consequential sentence in the field’s history of quality, because it forecloses the obvious approach.[16,17]

What followed was two decades of enumeration. Barry Boehm and colleagues built the first characteristics tree in 1976, decomposing general utility into as-is utility, maintainability and portability, and then decomposing those into primitives that could in principle be measured.[18] Jim McCall’s model for the US Air Force the following year organised eleven factors into three uses — product operation, product revision, product transition — and connected each factor to criteria and then to metrics, which is where the ambition began to outrun the evidence.[19] Hewlett-Packard’s FURPS added the practitioner’s insight that every project also carries constraints that are not attributes at all.[20] Geoff Dromey’s later contribution was to point out that most of these models float free of the artefact: they list desirable properties without saying which structural features of a program produce them, and a model that cannot be traced to code cannot be checked in code.[21]

The lineage then became standards. ISO/IEC 9126 in 1991, revised in 2001, fixed six characteristics as an international vocabulary.[22] ISO/IEC 25010:2011 replaced it with eight, and split product quality from quality in use.[23] The 2023 revision is the current baseline and the changes are worth knowing precisely, because they reflect two decades of argument. Safety was added as a top-level characteristic with its own subcharacteristics, which is a concession that safety is not a species of reliability. Usability was renamed interaction capability and portability was renamed flexibility; inclusivity and self-descriptiveness were added under interaction capability, resistance under security, and — usefully for this article — scalability was added explicitly as a subcharacteristic of flexibility. The quality-in-use model moved out to ISO/IEC 25019 and the overview to ISO/IEC 25002.[24,25]

It is worth being clear about what these models can and cannot do, because a great deal of disappointment comes from expecting the wrong thing. A quality model is a vocabulary and a checklist for omissions. It is genuinely valuable for that: run a list of nine characteristics past a design review and you will find two nobody had thought about. What it is not is a measurement instrument. No standard in the lineage tells you the threshold for your system, and none can, because the threshold is a claim about your users and your consequences. The standard supplies the noun; you have to supply the number.

The one attempt to close that gap deserves attention. ISO/IEC 5055:2021, developed by CISQ and adopted from an OMG specification, defines automated measures of four characteristics — security, reliability, performance efficiency and maintainability — by counting occurrences of specific structural weaknesses in source code, drawn from the Common Weakness Enumeration.[26,27] It is the first ISO measure taken from the internal structure of software rather than from its operational behaviour, and it is genuinely useful for comparing codebases or writing acceptance criteria into a contract. It is also, by construction, a measure of the presence of known bad patterns rather than of the presence of the property you want, which is a different thing and a weaker one. A codebase with zero counted weaknesses can still compute the wrong dose.

04

The scenario, and the shape of a plate

stimulus · environment · measure

The technique that actually closes the gap between the adjective and the number came out of the software architecture community in the late 1990s, and it is embarrassingly simple. A quality attribute requirement is written as a six-part scenario: a source generates a stimulus that arrives at an artefact in a stated environment, producing a response, which is captured by a response measure.[28] The Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method built an evaluation process around collections of such scenarios, and its most useful output is not a verdict but two lists: sensitivity points, where a design decision strongly affects one attribute, and tradeoff points, where a single decision affects several attributes in opposite directions.[29,30]

The six parts matter individually. Without a source you cannot tell an attack from a load spike. Without an environment you get the number that holds at 03:00 on a Tuesday and not the one that holds during the morning drug round. Without a response measure you have a story. The discipline is unnatural at first and then becomes hard to give up, because you notice how many requirements in your backlog cannot survive the transcription.

The twelve plates that follow add two fields to the scenario, both of which come from operational experience rather than from the literature. The first is paid for by. Every attribute you raise is funded from another one, and writing the funding source next to the claim makes the reviewer confront the withdrawal at the moment of the deposit. The second is the tell: the observation you will actually encounter first when the claim has quietly become false. Tells are not measures. They are the things people notice before the dashboard does — the laminated cheat sheet on the cart, the module only one person edits, the reconciliation job with a tolerance. Every experienced engineer has a private list of them, and they are almost never written down, which is why the same failure gets rediscovered in every organisation.

Plates are grouped into five bands by whom the claim is addressed to, because that turns out to predict more than the usual groupings do. Claims about the answer (correctness, reliability, availability) are addressed to the person who depends on the output. Claims about what the answer costs (performance, scalability) are addressed to whoever pays for capacity. Security is alone in its band because it is the only claim written against an intelligent opponent who reads your mitigations. Usability is alone because it is the only one whose measure requires observing a human being. And the last five — readability, testability, maintainability, extensibility, portability — are addressed to nobody outside the building. They are claims about the cost of the next change, made to engineers, on behalf of a future that has not yet arrived and cannot complain.

That last band is where quality work most often dies, for a structural reason worth stating plainly. The beneficiaries of the first seven attributes can complain today. The beneficiaries of the last five are next year’s team. Naming the payer is the only defence, which is why every plate in band V has a measure that a manager can be shown this quarter.

05

Correctness

Q01

Correctness is the only attribute with a formal definition, and the definition is the source of all the trouble: a program is correct with respect to a specification if every execution satisfying the precondition terminates in a state satisfying the postcondition.[31,32] Every word of that is doing work, and the load-bearing phrase is with respect to a specification. Correctness is not a relation between a program and reality. It is a relation between a program and a document.

The gap between the specification and the need is where the expensive failures live. The British Post Office prosecuted around 700 subpostmasters between 1999 and 2015 on the strength of shortfalls reported by the Horizon accounting system; the High Court found in 2019 that the system was capable of producing apparent shortfalls that had no counterpart in reality, and the public inquiry’s first volume in 2025 found that senior people at the Post Office and its supplier knew, or should have known, that the software could produce illusory gains and losses, while the organisation “maintained the fiction that its data was always accurate”.[33,34] Every one of those transactions was, in the narrow sense, processed as specified. The specification simply did not include the proposition that the branch balance corresponds to money.

Where correctness can be established rather than sampled, it is worth an extraordinary amount. The seL4 microkernel carries a machine-checked proof that its C implementation refines its abstract specification.[35] CompCert is a C compiler with a proof that the compiled code preserves the semantics of the source; when a large random-testing campaign found hundreds of bugs across mainstream compilers, CompCert was the one in which the campaign found no wrong-code errors in the verified middle end.[36,37] Amazon has documented using TLA+ on core services and finding design defects in protocols that had survived review and testing, including bugs requiring long, specific sequences of events that no test suite would have generated.[38,39]

Between proof and hope sits the technique most teams should be using more of. Property-based testing states a proposition over a domain and lets a generator attack it, which is a qualitatively different activity from writing examples.[40,41] Its power comes from the small scope hypothesis: most faults have a counterexample within a small bound, so exhausting a small domain finds much of what an unbounded search would.[42] But generators are not neutral, and this is the finding from the lab that most surprised the author. The seeded fault in Meridian’s ceiling-dose check — an alert raised above the maximum rather than at or above it — was missed by all 8 hand-written example tests, and missed by all 200,000 draws of a uniform random generator over a continuous weight range, because the failing set has measure zero in a continuum. Over a discrete grid of 1,000 plausible orders it appears in 4 of them, and random search needs about 257.8 draws to stumble into one. A generator that deliberately includes boundary values found it in 4 of 15 cases. Boundary-value analysis is not a quaint precursor to random testing; it targets exactly the region random testing cannot reach.[43]

Reliability

Q02

Reliability is correctness with time and probability attached: the probability that the system performs without failure for a stated period under stated conditions. The shift from a logical property to a statistical one changes what evidence counts. You cannot prove a reliability figure; you estimate it, and the estimate has a confidence interval that most organisations never report.[44]

The most important result in the field is a negative one. Littlewood and Strigini showed that validating ultra-high dependability claims by testing is infeasible in principle: to gain confidence that a system fails less often than once in 109 hours, you would need to observe it for something on the order of 109 failure-free hours, and no test campaign can afford that.[45] Butler and Finelli reached the same conclusion for life-critical real-time software.[46] This is why aviation and rail certify processes rather than probabilities for software: nobody can measure the number the regulation would otherwise want.[47,48]

Jim Gray’s taxonomy from Tandem remains the practical frame. Hard faults that recur deterministically get found in test; the failures that reach production are dominated by transient conditions — timing, load, resource exhaustion, unusual interleavings — which is why so much of production reliability is achieved by process pairs and restarts rather than by fixing the underlying defect.[49,50] Ariane 501 is the canonical case of a fault that was not a fault: a conversion from a 64-bit float to a 16-bit integer overflowed in inertial reference software that had been reused from Ariane 4, where the trajectory made the overflow impossible. The code was correct for its original environment. Reliability is not transitive across environments.[51] Therac-25 remains the case study that should be read in full by anyone writing clinical software: the failures came from a concurrency defect that had been present in an earlier machine and masked by hardware interlocks that the newer design removed.[52,53]

Reliability engineering has an uncomfortable interaction with measurement, and SQLite documents it better than any paper. The project maintains 100% branch and MC/DC coverage of its core, and describes the tension explicitly: MC/DC discourages defensive code with unreachable branches, while fuzzing rewards exactly that defensive code, so the two disciplines pull against one another and the project runs both.[54] The general lesson is that a reliability technique which cannot be reconciled with your coverage metric is not thereby wrong; your metric may be.

Availability

Q03

Availability is the fraction of demand that the system serves. It is the attribute most often quoted, most often quoted wrongly, and the one where arithmetic does the most work.

Three arithmetic facts should be on the wall of every team that publishes a number. First, availability composes downward through serial dependencies. In the lab, forty independent dependencies each at 99.9% yield 96.077% — 1,694 minutes a month, about twenty-eight hours — without any of them having broken a promise. Second, an error budget is the useful representation of the target because it is spendable: 99.9% is 43.2 minutes a month and 99.99% is 4.32, which is the difference between a change freeze and a release cadence.[55,56] Third, redundancy delivers its advertised gain only under independence. Two 99% replicas give four nines if they fail independently; introduce a 10% chance of a shared cause and the pair drops to 99.891%, turning 4.3 minutes of monthly downtime into 47.1.

The correlation term is where real outages live. A study of network-partitioning failures in cloud systems found that the majority led to catastrophic effects such as data loss or unavailability, that many were triggered by partial partitions rather than clean splits, and that a substantial share could have been caught by unit tests of the code that handles the failure.[57] The 2024 endpoint-sensor outage was a perfect correlated failure: the same content file arrived at millions of machines within minutes, which is what a global configuration channel is for.[1] Any mechanism that makes a fleet consistent also makes it simultaneously wrong.

Availability is also the attribute most vulnerable to measuring at the wrong boundary, and to leaving unsaid what is being counted. The arithmetic above counts time, which is the convention inherited from operations; Meridian’s plate counts attempts, which is what a ward actually cares about. These are not interchangeable. A system can burn very little clock while failing the few requests that mattered, and a percentage of attempts converts to minutes only if you assume demand is spread evenly — which, on a ward with an 08:00 round, it emphatically is not. Stating the unit is not pedantry; it is the difference between two claims with different falsifiers.

The boundary matters as much as the unit. Meridian’s plate insists on measuring at the device, because the honest question is not whether the service returned 200 but whether a nurse could give a drug. A load balancer cannot see a ward tablet that has lost Wi-Fi, and the fleet-wide number will be reassuring while a floor is dark. This is the general form: the measurement point encodes whose availability you care about, and choosing it is the substantive decision, not a detail of instrumentation.

Finally, availability trades directly against correctness, and pretending otherwise is how systems acquire their worst defects. Meridian’s offline mode is a deliberate purchase of availability with consistency: two writers, a reconciliation, and a window in which the system of record disagrees with the ward. That trade is defensible. What is not defensible is making it implicitly, in a cache configuration, without anyone clinical being told.

Performance

Q04

Performance is the only attribute whose folk measure — the average — is actively misleading, and the lab makes the point numerically. In a simulated single-server system with realistically variable service times, the mean sojourn time sits at the 72.5th percentile at 30% utilisation and the 63.2th at 95%. A mean is a number that most requests are faster than. It tells you almost nothing about the experience that generates complaints.

The second fact is that latency is a function of utilisation, and the function is not linear. Queueing theory has said so since Kingman’s heavy-traffic approximation: waiting time grows roughly as ρ/(1−ρ), so the last few percent of utilisation are catastrophically expensive.[58,59] In the lab, moving from 70% to 95% utilisation multiplies p50 by 9.5 and p99 by 4.3, while the same hardware, the same code and the same test suite report no change whatsoever. This is why a performance claim without a stated utilisation is not a claim, and why capacity and latency requirements cannot be written by different people.

The third fact is fan-out. If a user-visible operation touches twenty independent services, each with a well-behaved 99th percentile, the probability that at least one of them lands in its tail is 18.2%. Dean and Barroso’s account of this at Google scale is still the clearest statement of why tail tolerance has to be an architectural property rather than an optimisation: at high fan-out, the tail is the median.[60]

What should the threshold be? For interactive systems the anchors are older than the web. Robert Miller’s 1968 taxonomy identified roughly 0.1 second as the boundary of perceived instantaneity, one second as the limit for uninterrupted flow of thought, and ten seconds as the point where attention is lost.[61,62] Google’s Core Web Vitals thresholds are recognisably descended from those numbers: Largest Contentful Paint at or under 2.5 seconds, Interaction to Next Paint at or under 200 milliseconds, Cumulative Layout Shift at or under 0.1, assessed at the 75th percentile of real visits, with INP having replaced First Input Delay in March 2024 precisely because measuring only the first interaction flattered everyone.[63,64]

Knuth’s warning about premature optimisation is quoted so often that its second half has worn away; the point of the passage is that the 3% of code that matters should be identified by measurement and then optimised aggressively.[65] The modern equivalent of that discipline is a profile before a patch, and a workload that resembles the morning drug round rather than an empty ward.[66]

Scalability

Q05

Scalability is not performance, and conflating them produces some of the most expensive architecture in the industry. Performance is how fast one unit of work completes. Scalability is what happens to throughput and latency as you add load and capacity. A system can be fast and unscalable, or slow and perfectly scalable, and the second is often the better business.[67]

Amdahl’s law gives the first ceiling: if a fraction σ of the work is serialised, speedup cannot exceed 1/σ however much hardware you buy.[68] With the lab’s contention parameter of 0.03 that ceiling is 33.3×, and a naive capacity plan drawn from Amdahl alone predicts a curve that flattens and stays flat.

The Universal Scalability Law adds the term that matters in practice: a coherency cost κ that grows with the number of pairs of workers that must agree, producing a curve that peaks and then turns down.[69,70] In the lab, with σ=0.03 and κ=0.0005, throughput peaks at concurrency 44 at 13.6× a single unit, and at four times that concurrency has fallen to 8.12×. Retrograde scalability is not a pathology; it is the default behaviour of any system whose workers coordinate, and the only question is whether the peak lies inside or outside your planned envelope. This is the origin of the operational experience that adding servers made it slower, which sounds like nonsense and is arithmetic.

Two consequences for how the claim gets written. First, a scalability requirement must name the dimension of growth. Meridian scaling from one hospital to twelve is a different problem from Meridian scaling from 700 to 9,000 devices in one hospital: the first multiplies the formulary service’s read load and the audit store’s write load, and the second multiplies Wi-Fi contention and the size of every fan-out. Systems routinely scale on one axis and collapse on another. Second, the measure has to include the coordination points explicitly, because they are where κ lives — the shared sequence, the global lock, the leader election, the cross-region transaction, the nightly reconciliation that must see everything.

It is also worth noticing what the 2023 ISO revision did here: it added scalability as a subcharacteristic of flexibility rather than of performance efficiency.[24] That is a defensible taxonomic choice and a slightly misleading one for engineers, because the mechanisms that buy scalability — partitioning, statelessness, asynchrony — are the same mechanisms that spend latency, and the trade is a performance trade whatever the standard files it under.

Security

Q06

Security is the one attribute whose environment includes somebody who has read your specification. Every other claim in this article is made against nature, which is indifferent; this one is made against an opponent who will study your response measure and aim at the space it does not cover. That is why security requirements cannot be written as a threshold alone and have to be written against a threat model: named actors, named assets, named capabilities.[71]

The design principles have been stable for fifty years and remain the best available checklist: economy of mechanism, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation, open design, separation of privilege, least privilege, least common mechanism, psychological acceptability.[72] The last of those is the one most often skipped, and it is the one Meridian will fail first. A shared ward tablet with an aggressive lock timeout produces a shared password on tape under the cart, which is a security control converting itself into a security defect through the usability channel.

Security is also the attribute with the best evidence that whole classes of defect can be eliminated structurally rather than found individually. Microsoft reported that around 70% of its assigned CVEs were memory-safety issues.[73] Android’s team reported that as new code shifted to memory-safe languages, memory-safety defects fell from 76% of Android vulnerabilities in 2019 to 24% in 2024, with the mechanism being that vulnerability density is concentrated in new code, so changing what new code is written in bends the curve without rewriting the past.[74] A joint government advisory now recommends published memory-safe roadmaps.[75] It is the clearest example in the field of a quality attribute improved by changing the class of mistake that is expressible, rather than by testing harder.

The regulatory ground is moving underneath this attribute faster than any other. The EU Cyber Resilience Act entered into force on 10 December 2024; its reporting obligations apply from 11 September 2026 — actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents must be notified with an early warning within 24 hours, a full notification within 72 hours, and a final report within 14 days of a corrective measure becoming available for an actively exploited vulnerability — with the main obligations following on 11 December 2027.[76,77] For a product like Meridian this converts a security property into a documented, time-bounded process obligation, which is a different kind of engineering artefact: the measure is no longer “are we secure” but “can we detect, decide and notify inside 24 hours,” and that is rehearsable.[78,79]

Usability

Q07

Usability is the only attribute whose measurement requires a person. ISO 9241-11 defines it as the extent to which a system can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use — a definition built almost entirely out of the word specified, for the same reason this article keeps insisting on circumstances.[80] The 2023 revision of ISO/IEC 25010 renamed the product-side characteristic interaction capability and added inclusivity and self-descriptiveness beneath it, reserving usability proper for the quality-in-use model.[24,25]

The measures are more mature than engineers usually assume. The System Usability Scale is a ten-item questionnaire from 1986 that has been validated across hundreds of studies and produces a comparable score.[81] Heuristic evaluation with a handful of trained evaluators finds a large share of severe problems cheaply, which is why it survives in an era of telemetry.[82] Norman’s framing of affordances, signifiers, mapping and feedback is still the most useful diagnostic vocabulary for saying why a screen fails.[83]

Clinical software supplies the field’s most instructive usability failure, and it is a quality failure in the strict sense of this article: a system that satisfies every functional requirement while producing a worse outcome. Computerised prescriber order entry with drug-safety alerting reduces certain classes of error, and clinicians override the great majority of the alerts they receive — override rates of roughly 80% and above are reported repeatedly in the literature.[84] An alert that is overridden 90% of the time is not a safety control; it is a click. Meridian’s plate therefore makes override rate a first-class measure alongside task completion, because it is the number that reveals whether the safety mechanism is being used or endured.

Accessibility is the part of usability that has hardened into law, and the dates are worth knowing. WCAG 2.2 became a W3C Recommendation in October 2023 and was approved as ISO/IEC 40500:2025 in October 2025.[85,86] The European Accessibility Act became enforceable on 28 June 2025 for a wide range of consumer-facing products and services, with EN 301 549 as the harmonised standard auditors work from.[87,88] Note that these are not the same requirement: EN 301 549 still carries WCAG 2.1, so the version with ISO standing and the version with legal force in the EU are one revision apart, and a team that says “we conform to WCAG” has not yet said which of those it means. For a hospital system none of this is a compliance footnote: a nurse with low vision on a night shift is exactly the specified user in the specified context, and a contrast ratio is a response measure in the same sense that a latency percentile is.

Readability

Q08

Readability is the attribute engineers argue about most and measure least, which is odd given how much of the working day it consumes. Field studies of professional developers consistently find that comprehension — reading code, navigating it, reconstructing intent — occupies more time than writing it, with estimates around and above half of active development time.[89,90]

Peter Naur’s argument explains why this is not a tooling problem. Programming, on his account, is theory building: the program text is a residue of a theory held by the people who built it, and the theory cannot be recovered from the text alone.[91] Readability, in that light, is the degree to which the text lets a newcomer rebuild enough of the theory to act safely. This is why the useful measure in the plate is a stopwatch on a real newcomer rather than a static score, and why the most valuable comment is almost never a description of what the code does. It is a record of why the alternative was rejected, which is precisely the part of the theory the text cannot contain.

There is a genuine science here, and it is more modest than the style guides suggest. Buse and Weimer trained a model of perceived readability from human judgements and found it correlated with defect density and code churn, which at least establishes that the intuition tracks something.[92] Knuth’s literate programming took the strongest possible position — that the primary audience of a program is a human reader — and it has never been widely adopted, which is itself evidence about the incentives.[93] The practitioner literature converges on decomposition, naming and the elimination of surprise, and disagrees loudly on nearly everything else.[94,95,96]

Two things are worth saying plainly against the grain of that literature. First, readable code is usually more code and sometimes slower code, and pretending the trade does not exist damages the argument for it. Second, readability is measured against a reader, and teams almost never say which one. Code that is beautifully readable to the three people who wrote the domain model is opaque to the on-call engineer at 02:00, and the on-call engineer is the reader whose failure costs money. Meridian’s plate names them explicitly for that reason.

The tell for this attribute is the most reliable in the article. A module that only one person has ever modified is not a well-factored module; it is a module with an owner and a moat, and its readability has never been tested because it has never had a stranger.

Testability

Q09

Testability is the attribute that determines the price of every other attribute’s evidence, which makes it the highest-leverage item on the list and the one least likely to appear in a requirements document. It decomposes into two properties: controllability, the ability to drive the system into a chosen state, and observability, the ability to see what state it reached.[97] Legacy code is hard to test not because it is old but because it fuses decisions with side effects, leaving nowhere to inject and nothing to inspect.[98,99]

The lab puts a number on observability. Localising a single fault in a twelve-stage pipeline takes 5 runs on average when only the final output is visible, and 1 when every stage reports its intermediate state — a 5× difference in debugging cost that no amount of test-writing recovers. That ratio compounds every time anyone touches the system, which is why instrumentation usually repays itself before the end of the quarter in which it lands.

The lab also puts a number on what test suites are actually worth, and this is the result practitioners should argue with hardest. Two suites over the same module, running the identical set of 7 calls, both achieve 100% statement coverage. One asserts only that nothing threw and that the result is a non-negative float; the other asserts the expected value. Against 31 generated mutants, of which 6 turn out to be behaviourally indistinguishable across an 840-point sweep and therefore unkillable by anything, the smoke suite kills 4% of the killable mutants and the asserting suite kills 64%. Identical coverage; sixteen-fold difference in fault detection.

This is not a toy artefact. Inozemtseva and Holmes found that coverage is correlated with test suite effectiveness when suite size is ignored, and that the correlation is weak once suite size is controlled for — that is, bigger suites find more faults, and coverage is largely a proxy for size.[100] Mutation analysis has better evidence behind it: coupling between mutant detection and real fault detection has been demonstrated empirically.[101,102,103] Google runs mutation testing at scale by surfacing a small number of mutants during code review rather than reporting a score, which is the design that makes the technique affordable.[104,105]

One structural finding deserves to be better known: an analysis of catastrophic failures in distributed data-intensive systems found that a large majority were triggered by events that unit tests of the error-handling code could have caught, and that in many cases the error handler itself contained the defect.[106] The untested code is disproportionately the code that runs when things are already going wrong.

Maintainability

Q10

Maintainability is the cost of the next change, and it is the attribute where the industry’s metrics are furthest from the thing they claim to measure. Cyclomatic complexity counts linearly independent paths through a procedure and is a genuine, well-defined number.[107] The Maintainability Index combines it with Halstead volume and lines of code into a single score.[108,109] Both are computed at the level of the function, and the cost of change is overwhelmingly determined at the level of the system.

The lab demonstrates this directly. Two module graphs with sixty modules and 100 dependency edges each: one layered and acyclic, one unconstrained. Mean blast radius — the number of modules that must be rebuilt and retested when one module changes — is 10.98 in the layered graph and 35.75 in the tangled one, a 3.26× difference in the cost of every future change. The tangled graph contains a strongly connected component of 37 modules, which is a polite way of saying that a third of the system is one module wearing thirty-seven hats. Every function in both graphs could have identical cyclomatic complexity.

The theory is old and correct. Parnas’s criterion for decomposition — modules hide decisions likely to change, not steps in a flowchart — predicts blast radius directly, because a module boundary drawn around a volatile decision terminates the propagation of changes to that decision.[110] Lehman’s laws add the dynamic: a system in use must change or become less useful, and as it changes its complexity increases unless work is done to reduce it.[111] Ward Cunningham’s original debt metaphor was narrower and sharper than its current usage: shipping code that does not match your current understanding of the domain is borrowing, and the interest is paid in every subsequent change made against the wrong model.[112,113]

What can actually be measured and defended in a budget meeting is delivery behaviour. The DORA programme’s throughput and stability measures — lead time for change, deployment frequency, change failure rate, failed deployment recovery time, and more recently rework rate — are proxies for maintainability with a decade of survey data behind them.[114,115] They are proxies, and they are gameable, and they remain the best available instrument for the claim “we can still change this thing.” The honest version of the maintainability plate pairs them with a structural measure like blast radius, because delivery metrics can be held flat for a surprisingly long time by heroics.

Extensibility

Q11

Extensibility is maintainability’s forward-looking cousin and a much riskier bet, because it requires guessing which changes are coming. Parnas set out the discipline in 1979: design for extension and contraction by identifying the subsets and extensions you intend to support, and structuring the program so that those specific variations are additions rather than edits.[116] The critical word is specific. A design is not extensible in general; it is extensible along the axes someone anticipated, and rigid along every other axis, often more rigid than a plain design would have been.

This is why the plate’s measure is a written list of anticipated changes with a demonstration for each, rather than an adjective. Meridian’s list is short and concrete: a new dose-banding rule, a regional label format, a paediatric weight-band protocol, an additional formulary source. Each one is either implementable by adding a module and a configuration entry, or it is not, and that is checkable today. The ratio of added to modified lines across the last ten variations shipped is the retrospective version of the same measure, and it is the one that catches an abstraction that has quietly stopped working.

The costs are real and frequently understated. Every extension point is a place where code that the core team has not reviewed determines a clinical outcome, which makes it simultaneously a correctness risk and an attack surface. Extension points also degrade readability, because control flow that used to be visible in a function is now distributed across a registry, and degrade testability, because the set of configurations that must be verified is now a product rather than a sum. Machine-learning systems are the extreme case of this: the code is a small part of the system, and configuration, data dependencies and feedback loops account for most of the accumulated debt, with entanglement meaning that changing anything changes everything.[117,118]

The most useful discipline here is to date the bet. An extension point added because a customer asked for a variation next quarter is a forecast with a horizon; an extension point added because someone might want something someday is a permanent tax with no named payer. Feature flags that were never removed are the most common physical evidence of the second kind.

Portability

Q12

Portability is the claim that the software will behave the same somewhere it has not run, and it is almost always false in a specific, discoverable way. The useful reframing: “portable” names the layer below which you stopped testing.

Standardisation genuinely works, within limits. POSIX made a large body of system code movable between Unix variants by writing down the interface rather than the implementation.[119] Managed runtimes moved the boundary further up, at the price of inheriting the runtime’s own portability problems. WebAssembly is the most recent serious attempt at a portable, fast, formally specified compilation target, and its design paper is explicit that a precise semantics is what makes portability checkable rather than aspirational.[120]

The bugs live in what the abstraction does not standardise. Integer widths and signedness of char, floating-point evaluation order, endianness, the memory model, path and locale rules, time zone data, the behaviour of a full disk. SQLite’s testing documentation is again the best practical treatment: because undefined and implementation-defined behaviour in C is non-portable by definition, the project runs its suites under trapping and sanitising builds, with both signed and unsigned char, on 32-bit and 64-bit and big- and little-endian machines, and adds test cases specifically designed to provoke undefined behaviour.[54] Kernighan and Pike’s rule of thumb still holds: portability is achieved by avoiding the questionable construct entirely, not by conditionally compiling around it.[121]

For Meridian the substantive decision is not technical but administrative. A supported-target list that is explicit and dated, with a named owner who runs the conformance suite on each target before release, converts a vague adjective into a maintained claim. The 2019 Android handheld still in a storeroom is either on the list, in which case somebody runs the suite on it, or it is not, in which case the ward needs to know. The failure mode is a “known differences” page in the release notes, which is a portability claim being retracted one bullet at a time.

The 2023 ISO revision renamed this characteristic flexibility and gathered adaptability, installability, replaceability and scalability beneath it.[24] The name is arguably better — the underlying property is the ability to work in circumstances other than the ones you were built for — but the engineering measure is unchanged: a list of circumstances, and evidence for each.

Nothing here is free

the surface

The single most useful thing a quality model does not tell you is which attributes fight. The matrix below is a judgement, not a measurement — it records, for each attribute, what happens to the others when you push it hard in a real system — but the shape of it is not controversial to anyone who has done the work.

Three readings are worth making explicit.

The band V attributes are the only ones that mostly reinforce. Readability, testability, maintainability and portability push each other up and push almost nothing down except performance and delivery speed. That is the entire economic case for internal quality, and it is why the argument for it is always an argument about time horizon rather than about engineering. Conversely, performance is the most predatory row in the matrix: pushing it hard costs something in almost every other column, which is why performance work should be gated by a measured threshold rather than pursued as a virtue.

Availability and correctness are structurally opposed, and no amount of cleverness dissolves the opposition. A system that must answer when a dependency is unreachable must answer from something other than the truth. Meridian’s offline mode is the concrete case: it converts an availability requirement into a reconciliation problem with a clinical consequence. The correct engineering response is not to eliminate the trade but to make it explicit, bounded and visible — a stated staleness window, a reconciliation with an alarm rather than a tolerance, and a screen that tells the nurse which mode they are in.

Security is the only attribute that raises correctness while lowering nearly everything else. Least privilege, complete mediation and fail-safe defaults genuinely make a system more likely to do the right thing, and they also cost latency, availability, usability and extensibility simultaneously. This is why security is best introduced as a constraint on the solution space at design time rather than as an attribute to be optimised later: it is cheap when it shapes a structure and ruinous when it has to be threaded through one.

The Pareto experiment in the lab shows what this looks like at the resolution of a single configuration decision.

Five candidate cache TTLs for Meridian’s order lookup, scored on mean latency, mean staleness and the number of distinct freshness states a test suite must cover. Not one option dominates another: every step that buys latency sells freshness and enlarges the verification burden. The Pareto front is the entire option set. No further measurement can decide it, because the decision is how stale a medication record may be, and that is a clinical judgement wearing an engineering costume. The value of getting this far is not that the tool picks; it is that the choice has been surfaced to someone entitled to make it, with the consequences priced. That is what an architecture tradeoff analysis is for, and it is most of what it produces.[29]

            n += 1
            w, r = rng.choice(grid)
            if disagrees(w, r):
                draws_to_first.append(n)
                break
            if n >= 20000:
                draws_to_first.append(n)
                break
    mean_draws = sum(draws_to_first) / len(draws_to_first)

    # (d) Random search over a CONTINUOUS weight domain: the failing set has
    #     measure zero, so uniform sampling never reaches it.
    cont_hits = 0
    for _ in range(200000):
        w = rng.uniform(1.0, 200.0)
        r = rng.choice(RATES)
        if disagrees(w, r):
            cont_hits += 1

    # (e) A boundary-biased generator (what Hypothesis/QuickCheck shrink toward):
    #     include, for each rate, the weight that lands exactly on the ceiling.
    biased = []
    for r in RATES:
        w = MAX_SINGLE_DOSE_MG / r
        biased.extend([(w - 1, r), (w, r), (w + 1, r)])
    biased_hits = sum(1 for w, r in biased if disagrees(w, r))

    RESULTS["e1"] = {
        "example_cases": len(examples),
        "example_detections": example_hits,
        "grid_cases": len(grid),
        "grid_failures": len(grid_failures),
        "failing_fraction_pct": round(100 * p, 3),
        "failing_inputs": [[w, r] for w, r in grid_failures],
        "random_grid_mean_draws": round(mean_draws, 1),
        "random_grid_expected_draws": round(1 / p, 1),
        "continuous_draws": 200000,
        "continuous_detections": cont_hits,
        "biased_cases": len(biased),
        "biased_detections": biased_hits,

class Mutator(ast.NodeTransformer):
    """Applies exactly one mutation, identified by index."""

    CMP = {ast.Lt: ast.LtE, ast.LtE: ast.Lt, ast.Gt: ast.GtE,
           ast.GtE: ast.Gt, ast.Eq: ast.NotEq, ast.NotEq: ast.Eq}
    BIN = {ast.Mult: ast.Div, ast.Div: ast.Mult,
           ast.Add: ast.Sub, ast.Sub: ast.Add}

    def __init__(self, target):
        self.target = target
        self.count = 0

    def _hit(self):
        self.count += 1
        return self.count - 1 == self.target

    def visit_Compare(self, node):
        self.generic_visit(node)
        if len(node.ops) == 1 and type(node.ops[0]) in self.CMP:
            # relational operator replacement
            if self._hit():
                node.ops = [self.CMP[type(node.ops[0])]()]
            # boundary shift: x < k  ->  x < k + 1
            if self._hit():
                node.comparators = [
                    ast.BinOp(left=node.comparators[0], op=ast.Add(),
                              right=ast.Constant(value=1))]
        return node

    def visit_BinOp(self, node):
        self.generic_visit(node)
        if type(node.op) in self.BIN:
            if self._hit():
                node.op = self.BIN[type(node.op)]()
        return node

    def visit_Call(self, node):
        self.generic_visit(node)
        if isinstance(node.func, ast.Name) and node.func.id == "int":
            if self._hit():
                node.func = ast.Name(id="round", ctx=ast.Load())
        return node

    def visit_Constant(self, node):
        if isinstance(node.value, float):
            # scalar perturbation, and sign flip of a scaling factor
            if self._hit():
                return ast.copy_location(
                    ast.Constant(value=node.value + 1.0), node)
            if self._hit():
                return ast.copy_location(
                    ast.Constant(value=node.value * 2.0
                                 if node.value else 1.0), node)
        return node

Measuring without lying

GQM · SLO · DORA

Every measure in this article is corruptible, and the corruption is not a moral failure but a predictable response to incentives.[13] The defence is not better measures; it is a small set of structural habits.

Derive the measure from a question, and the question from a goal. The Goal–Question–Metric method is thirty years old and still the only widely taught procedure that makes the derivation explicit, which means it makes an orphaned metric visible: if you cannot state the question a number answers, deleting it costs nothing.[122] Most engineering dashboards would lose half their panels to this test and improve.

Prefer measures that can fail. A service level objective is a threshold with an error budget attached, which makes it a claim that can be violated and therefore a claim that can be managed. The error budget is what turns reliability from an aspiration into a currency: a team that is under budget has earned the right to ship faster, and a team that is over it has already spent the argument.[55,56]

Measure outcomes at the boundary the user cares about. This is the single most common error in practice and it is nearly always a measurement-point error rather than a statistical one — latency at the load balancer, availability at the service, coverage at the line. The corresponding discipline for developer productivity is the SPACE framework’s insistence that no single dimension suffices and that any serious measure combines satisfaction, performance, activity, communication and efficiency, precisely because each one alone is trivially gamed.[123]

Pair every proxy with a structural check. DORA’s delivery metrics are the best available proxies for maintainability, with a decade of data behind them.[114,115] They can also be held flat by a heroic team on top of a rotting codebase for a surprisingly long time. Pairing them with a structural measure — blast radius, dependency cycles, the ISO 5055 weakness counts — catches the divergence.[26]

Two anti-patterns are worth naming because they are so widespread that they read as best practice.

The first is the coverage target. A number like 80% attached to a build gate produces tests written to reach 80%, which are exactly the tests E2 shows to be worthless: they execute lines without asserting anything about them. Google’s published approach is instructive — they treat coverage as information for the reviewer rather than as a threshold, and use mutation testing by surfacing a handful of surviving mutants during review rather than by publishing a score.[104,105] The measure is aimed at a decision a human makes, not at a gate a build makes.

The second is flakiness tolerance. A test suite with intermittent failures does not degrade gracefully; it stops being evidence, because a red build no longer means anything and the organisation learns to re-run rather than investigate.[124,125] Flakiness is the one quality problem with a genuinely zero-tolerance answer, since its cost is not the failed run but the destruction of every other signal in the pipeline.

Where the folklore outruns the evidence

honesty

An article that recommends measurement owes the reader a section on how bad much of the field’s evidence is.

Start with the most-quoted number in software quality: that a defect found in production costs one hundred times more to fix than one found in requirements. Laurent Bossavit traced the citation chains behind this and several other load-bearing claims and found that they decay, as you follow them backwards, into a small number of studies with narrow scope, inconsistent definitions and sample sizes that would not support the generality routinely claimed for them.[126] The underlying phenomenon is real — late defects usually do cost more, and a design defect discovered after certification really can cost a fortune — but the specific multiplier is folklore wearing a citation. The same analysis applies to the “10x programmer” and to several widely repeated figures about requirements churn.

The problem is not confined to old chestnuts. Shepperd, Bowes and Hall re-analysed a large body of defect-prediction studies and found that the research group conducting the study was a stronger predictor of the reported result than the classifier being evaluated — a finding about the literature rather than about classifiers, and a sobering one for anyone selecting a technique from published comparisons.[127]

Controlled experiments in this field are hard for structural reasons, and the most instructive recent example concerns AI assistance. METR ran a randomised controlled trial in which sixteen experienced open-source developers completed 246 real tasks in repositories they knew well, with AI tools randomly allowed or disallowed per task. Developers forecast a 24% speedup, reported a 20% speedup afterwards, and were measured to be 19% slower with the tools allowed — with a confidence interval running from 2% to 39% slower.[128] The result is striking, and what happened next is more instructive still.

METR began a second study in August 2025 with 57 developers, 143 repositories and more than 800 tasks. In February 2026 they published, not the headline, but the reasons the headline could not be trusted. Developers were increasingly declining to take part rather than work without AI; between 30% and 50% of them said they were choosing not to submit certain tasks because they did not want those tasks assigned to the no-AI condition; the pay rate had dropped from $150 to $50 an hour; and time measurement had become unreliable for developers running several agents at once. The raw numbers had flipped direction — an estimated 18% speedup for the returning developers, confidence interval 38% faster to 9% slower, and 4% for the newly recruited ones, interval 15% faster to 9% slower — but both intervals cross zero, and every selection effect pushes the same way, so METR characterised their own estimate as a lower bound and announced they were redesigning the study.[129] This is not a retraction of the 2025 finding, and it is not a confirmation of it either. It is a team declining to report a number they could no longer defend.

That is what good evidence practice looks like, and it is worth dwelling on because the same selection effects operate quietly inside every organisation’s internal metrics. The teams that adopt a new practice are not a random sample of teams. The modules that get refactored are not a random sample of modules. The incidents that get written up are not a random sample of incidents. None of this is a reason to stop measuring; it is a reason to state, next to every number, what would have to be true for it to be misleading. A measure published without its validity conditions is a claim without a falsifier, which is where this article started.

Three observations about the pattern.

First, every mature regime scales evidence to consequence rather than demanding uniform rigour. DO-178C assigns software levels A to E by the severity of the failure condition, and only Level A — failures that would be catastrophic — requires modified condition/decision coverage, the criterion that demands each condition in a decision be shown to independently affect the outcome.[47,131] ISO 26262 does the same with ASIL A to D, and IEC 62304 with software safety classes A to C.[4,130] The engineering content of the standard is mostly the mapping from consequence to required evidence, which is exactly the mapping that unregulated teams make implicitly and inconsistently.

Second, the regimes certify processes and artefacts because, as Littlewood and Strigini showed, the probabilities they would prefer to certify cannot be measured.[45] This is frequently mistaken for bureaucratic capture. It is a rational response to an impossibility result, and it has a real cost: a process-certified system can be defective in ways the process was not designed to catch, which is roughly the history of every certified system that failed.[53]

Third, the newest regimes are converging on process obligations with clocks rather than product thresholds. The Cyber Resilience Act’s reporting duties — 24 hours, 72 hours, 14 days — are not statements about how secure a product must be, but about how fast a manufacturer must notice and tell.[76,77] The European Accessibility Act works differently, pointing at a harmonised standard that contains testable success criteria.[87,88] Both patterns are available to teams with no regulator at all: a rehearsed disclosure clock and a harmonised checklist are useful engineering artefacts independent of whether anyone is compelling you to have them.

The general lesson for the unregulated majority is not to adopt DO-178C. It is that the threshold question has a defensible answer only once someone has written down the consequence of being wrong, and that writing down the consequence is the part organisations skip.

Quality when most of the code is generated

2025–2026

The composition of the code entering these systems has changed faster than the practices around it, and the honest summary of the evidence as of mid-2026 is that the effect on quality is strongly conditional on what the code lands in.

DORA’s 2024 report found that rising AI adoption was associated with small decreases in delivery throughput and larger decreases in delivery stability, and attributed the mechanism to batch size: it becomes easy to produce more code per change, and larger changes have always failed more.[115] The 2025 report, based on nearly five thousand respondents, reframed the conclusion around amplification — AI does not fix a team, it magnifies the system it lands in, so organisations with small batches, strong platforms and fast feedback gain, and organisations without them accelerate their existing problems.[132] That framing is more useful than either optimism or alarm, because it is actionable: it says the prerequisite work is the band V work.

The METR work, discussed above, is the other pole of the evidence: a careful randomised design found a slowdown for experienced developers on mature codebases in early 2025, and a year later the same team could not obtain a clean measurement, because working without AI had become unpalatable enough to participants that the developers and tasks with the most to gain selected themselves out of the sample.[128,129] Their own reading is that late-2025 tools probably did accelerate these developers and that their estimate is a floor rather than a finding. Anyone quoting either study as settled — in either direction — is overreaching.

What does not change is the structure of this article’s argument, and it is worth being blunt about why. Generated code shifts the bottleneck from production to verification. The scarce resource becomes the ability to state what the code must do and to notice when it does not — which is to say, correctness claims with falsifiers, assertions rather than coverage, seams and observability, and a review process that can actually absorb the volume arriving at it. Every one of those is a band V attribute, and every one of them is what the DORA amplification finding says determines the outcome.

Three practical consequences follow. Assertions matter more than they did, because E2’s sixteen-fold gap is exactly the gap between code that was executed by a generated test and code that was checked by one. Property-based and boundary-directed testing matter more, because they encode the specification rather than reproducing the implementation’s assumptions — a generated test derived from generated code inherits its errors, and a property does not.[40,41] And review latency becomes a first-class quality metric, because a review queue that cannot keep pace with production is a quality gate that has been removed without anyone deciding to remove it.

The oldest advice in the field survives this transition unchanged, and Dijkstra’s version is the one to keep: testing shows the presence of defects, never their absence.[16] The provenance of the code does not alter the logic. It only alters how much code arrives per unit of attention.

Writing a requirement that cannot lie

before / after

Everything in this article reduces to a rewriting exercise that takes about ten minutes per requirement and is almost never done. Here is the whole method on one clause from Meridian’s specification.

as written

The system shall be highly available and responsive.

7 words. Nothing in it can be false.
as a claim

A nurse administering a medication on a ward tablet, during the 08:00 round when every device on the floor is active, and with the ward access point degraded to a single radio, shall receive a verification verdict within 1.0 s at the 99th percentile and 250 ms at the median, measured on the device from barcode decode to rendered verdict. 99.95% of administration attempts shall complete, counted at the device, over a rolling 28 days; an attempt completed in offline mode counts as a success only if its record reconciles within 15 minutes of reconnection. This is funded by correctness: offline mode admits a second writer, and the reconciliation alarm — not a tolerance — is the control. It is refuted by any 28-day window in which more than 0.05% of attempts fail, by a p99 above 1.0 s in the peak hour, or by a single unreconciled record. Watch for nurses scanning twice.

Person, circumstance, number, percentile, measurement point, funding source, falsifier, tell.

The rewritten version is longer, which is the usual objection, and it is worth answering directly. The extra length is not ceremony: every added clause corresponds to a decision that someone was going to make anyway, silently, in code. Who is the user? What are they doing? Under what conditions? Measured where? Against what number? At which percentile? Failing how? The original version leaves all seven to be answered by whoever writes the implementation, at the moment they write it, with no record. The rewrite moves those decisions to the people entitled to make them and to a moment when changing them is cheap.

A short procedure that works in review, in roughly the order the questions bite:

  1. Name the person. Not “the user” — the agency nurse on a first shift, the on-call engineer at 02:00, the auditor in eighteen months. If nobody can be named, the requirement is decoration.
  2. Name the circumstance. The worst realistic environment, not the average one. Peak hour, degraded network, cold cache, the device bought in 2019.
  3. Name the number and its percentile. A mean is not a requirement. Neither is a maximum without a measurement window.
  4. Name the measurement point. Whose clock, whose boundary. This is where most requirements silently change meaning.
  5. Name the funding source. Which attribute pays for this one. If the answer is “nothing,” the requirement is not yet understood.
  6. Name the falsifier. What observation would refute the claim, and which gate would see it. A requirement with no gate is a wish.
  7. Name the tell. What you will actually notice first when this quietly stops being true. Write it in the runbook, not the specification.

Two failure modes to expect. The first is that some requirements will not survive the exercise at all: they turn out to be preferences, or restatements of a design decision, or someone’s anxiety. Deleting them is a gain. The second is that the numbers you first write down will be wrong, often by an order of magnitude, and you will find out during the first load test. That is also a gain, and it arrives roughly a year earlier than it otherwise would.

Finally, keep the ledger. The twelve plates in this article are a format, not a canon; the value is in having one page per attribute, current, with a named payer and a named falsifier, that a new engineer can read in twenty minutes and an auditor can read in an hour. Most organisations have this information. It is distributed across a wiki, three dashboards, a runbook, two people’s heads and an incident channel, which is another way of saying they do not have it.

Where to read next

a short shelf

A deliberately short list, chosen for what each one does that the others do not.

For the scenario technique and the tradeoff vocabulary: Bass, Clements and Kazman remains the standard reference, and the ATAM papers are worth reading in the original for the distinction between sensitivity and tradeoff points.[28,29,30]

For the standards: read ISO/IEC 25010:2023 for the vocabulary and the 2023 change list for what two decades of argument settled; read ISO/IEC 5055 if you need a measure you can put in a contract.[24,26] Do not read either expecting thresholds.

For testing that finds things: the SQLite testing document is the best free description of a serious testing strategy in existence, including its own internal tensions; Inozemtseva and Holmes for what coverage does not tell you; Just and colleagues for why mutants are a defensible proxy for real faults.[54,100,101]

For reliability and operations: the two SRE books for error budgets and the practice around them; Cook’s four-page “How complex systems fail” for the framing that survives contact with an actual incident; Leveson for why safety is not a species of reliability.[53,55,56,133]

For performance and scale: Gunther for the Universal Scalability Law and the discipline of fitting it to your own data; Dean and Barroso for tail behaviour; Gregg for the methodology of measurement.[60,66,69]

For the internal attributes: Parnas’s two papers are still the clearest statements of why modularity pays and what it pays for; Feathers for what to do when the seams are missing; Ousterhout for a short, opinionated and unusually honest treatment of design tradeoffs.[96,98,110,116]

For scepticism: Bossavit on how folklore becomes fact, and Shepperd and colleagues on researcher bias, read together as a course in reading software engineering literature.[126,127]

For the failures: Leveson and Turner on Therac-25, the Ariane 501 board report, the SEC order on Knight Capital, the CrowdStrike root cause analysis, and volume one of the Post Office Horizon inquiry. Five documents, five decades, one pattern: the system passed every check that had been written down.[1,34,51,52,134]

For proving rather than sampling: Meyer’s “Applying design by contract” is the shortest route into specification as an executable object; King and, two decades later, Cadar and colleagues for symbolic execution, which is the technique that actually finds the boundary cases E1 could not; Holzmann for model checking as practised rather than as advertised.[135,136,137,138]

For the tools that changed what testing can reach: Serebryany and colleagues on sanitizers, which converted a class of undefined behaviour from invisible to loud, and Yuan and colleagues’ study of distributed-system failures, whose finding that most catastrophic failures come from unchecked errors in rarely-run handlers is the strongest argument for testing your error paths that anyone has assembled.[106,139] Kyle Kingsbury’s Jepsen reports are the working demonstration that vendors’ consistency claims are claims.[140] Gray and Reuter for what a transaction actually promises before you decide to give one up.[50]

For coding standards, if you are obliged to have one: MISRA C is the most carefully argued of them, and its rule rationales are worth reading even if you write no C, because each one is an explicit theory about which mistakes a language invites.[141]

For review as a measurement instrument rather than a ritual: Fagan’s original inspection paper, which specified rates, roles and rework and reported defect data, sets a bar the modern pull request rarely clears.[142] Read it beside Weinberg on egoless programming.[11]

For process, read historically and in one sitting: Royce’s paper, which proposed the single-pass model in order to say it does not work; Boehm’s spiral as the first serious attempt to make the risk explicit; Mills on cleanroom, Humphrey and Paulk on the maturity model, and the agile manifesto. Five positions, forty years, one recurring argument about where to put the feedback.[143,144,145,146,147,148] Beck for the practice that made test-first ordinary.[149]

For the essays that have not aged: Brooks on essence and accident and on the tenth-anniversary reconsideration; Conway’s paper, which is four pages and one idea; Lampson’s hints, which is the closest thing the field has to a list of things worth knowing; Knuth on literate programming, which is a claim about readability disguised as a tool.[93,150,151,152,153] Gawande on checklists, for the discipline of writing down the check you keep meaning to remember.[154]

For what oversight bodies conclude when they look: the GAO’s Patriot report on a clock drift that killed twenty-eight people, the Mars Climate Orbiter board on an interface unit mismatch, and Koopman’s expert report on unintended acceleration, which is the most detailed public account of what a real safety-critical codebase looks like when someone is finally allowed to read it.[155,156,157]

And for the historical record, the Garmisch report repays a slow read. The problems it names in 1968 — that large systems cannot be adequately tested, that maintenance dominates cost, that the gap between promise and delivery is structural — are the problems this article is still trying to make checkable fifty-eight years later.[14]
