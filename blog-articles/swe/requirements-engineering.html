Abstract. This article treats requirements engineering as a single discipline rather than two feuding ones. It sets out the world–machine frame that underlies the field, traces the method from the 1968 Garmisch conference through tabular specification, use cases, goal models, user stories and behaviour-driven development to the EU AI Act, and then works through thirteen artefacts — stakeholder analysis, elicitation, business rules, functional and non-functional requirements, personas, stories, use cases, acceptance criteria, prioritisation, MVP, scope management and traceability — asking of each what it can carry and where it is blind.

A single case runs throughout: a public electric-vehicle charging platform, chosen because it is genuinely multi-stakeholder, genuinely regulated, and spans firmware, payments, grid capacity and physical ergonomics. Clause 23 runs a dependency-free requirements checker over that case's requirement set at build time and reports what it found, including the eight defects present in the first draft.

§ 11The question every requirement answers

There is one question that separates a requirement from a wish, and it is not what should the system do? It is how would we know? A sentence that cannot fail a test cannot pass one either. Everything else in this discipline — the elicitation techniques, the story formats, the prioritisation arithmetic, the traceability matrices that certification authorities demand and nobody enjoys maintaining — is scaffolding erected around that single question.

Requirements engineering is the part of building software where you are still allowed to be wrong cheaply. After it, wrongness has to be paid for in code, in schema migrations, in retraining, in recalls. The standard that governs the field defines it as an interdisciplinary function mediating between the domains of the acquirer and the supplier, concerned with discovering, eliciting, developing, analysing, verifying, validating, communicating, documenting and managing requirements.[1] That is a fair description of the activity and a poor description of the difficulty. The difficulty is that the people who know what is needed cannot say it, the people who can say it precisely do not know it, and the thing being described does not exist yet, so nobody can point at it.

The field has been aware of this for half a century. In 1976 Thomas Bell and Thomas Thayer opened a paper at the second International Conference on Software Engineering with a question that reads as rhetorical and was not: do requirements arise naturally from an obvious need, or do they come about only through diligent effort — and even then contain problems? They analysed two very different projects and found the problems were of the same shape in both.[2] Forty years later the NaPiRE surveys, run across dozens of countries and hundreds of companies, found practitioners still naming the same problem first: incomplete or hidden requirements, followed by moving targets, underspecified requirements, and communication flaws between team and customer.[3,4]

What follows is a working account of the discipline: where it came from, what its artefacts actually do, where each one is blind, and how they compose into something you can hand to an auditor, a compiler, or a new engineer on their second day. A single worked example runs through it — a public electric-vehicle charging platform — and at the end a program checks that example's requirement set and reports what it finds, including the parts this article got wrong on the first pass.

§ 22Scope: the boundary that keeps moving

Requirements engineering has a boundary problem with design, and the boundary has never held still. The traditional formulation — requirements say what, design says how — collapses the moment you look at it. "The system shall present a QR code" is a how at the level of the driver's experience and a what at the level of the firmware team. Every statement is a what to the layer below it and a how to the layer above.

David Parnas and Paul Clements resolved this honestly in 1986 by conceding that the rational, top-down design process is a fiction nobody has ever followed, and then arguing that you should produce the documentation as if you had — faking the rational process, because the artefact is useful even when the history that produced it was not orderly.[5] That remains the most practical position available. The requirements document is not a record of how the team thought; it is the description you wish you had been given at the start, written after you understood enough to write it.

Where the boundary does hold is in whose vocabulary the sentence is written in. A requirement is stated in terms of phenomena the stakeholder can observe and cares about. A design decision is stated in terms of the solution's internal structure. The charging platform's driver does not have an opinion about whether the tariff engine is a service or a library; she has a very firm opinion about whether the price on the screen is the price on her statement. Both sentences are legitimate engineering artefacts. Only one of them is a requirement.

Two things are consistently out of scope and consistently smuggled in anyway. The first is implementation bias: a requirement that names a vendor, a protocol, or a data structure has pre-decided a design question, and unless a higher-level constraint forced that choice, it should not be there.[1] The second is the reverse failure — the FBI's Virtual Case File programme was later described by a Government Accountability Office official as a case where requirements were written as how certain things should look rather than what the system should do, which meant every redefinition cascaded through work already built.[6]

§ 33Definitions: the world, the machine, and three sentences

The cleanest formal account of what requirements engineering is about comes from Pamela Zave and Michael Jackson's 1997 paper on the four dark corners of the discipline. Their argument is deceptively simple: every description involved in requirements engineering is a description of the environment, not of the software.[7] The software is a means. The environment is the subject.

They separate three kinds of statement, and almost every requirements argument you will ever have is a confusion between two of them.

R — the requirement
A condition on the world that the stakeholder wants to be true. Stated entirely in the world's vocabulary. The driver is billed for the energy that entered her battery. Note that no computer appears in that sentence.
D — the domain properties
Things that are true of the world whether or not you build anything. Batteries accept charge at a rate that falls with state of charge. Copper has a current limit. Cards are issued by banks that can be unreachable. These are not decisions; they are facts you inherit, and they are the least documented part of most projects.
S — the specification
A condition on the phenomena shared between world and machine — the interface. When the acquirer returns an approval code, the charge point unlocks the connector within 8 seconds. This is the only one of the three that a programmer can implement directly.

The relationship between them is an entailment: D, S ⊨ R. Given what is true of the world, a specification the machine satisfies must guarantee the requirement.[7,8] Jackson later built the Problem Frames method on top of this, treating the structuring of the world — not the internals of the machine — as the real design work.[9]

The practical payoff is diagnostic. When a system fails in the field, the frame tells you which of three investigations to open. If R was wrong, you built the right thing for the wrong goal — a validation failure. If S did not entail R, your specification had a gap — a verification failure. If D was false, an assumption about the world rotted, which is the failure mode that kills people, because nothing in the code is broken.

Two more definitions, because the field uses them inconsistently. Verification asks whether the system meets its specification; validation asks whether the specification meets the need.[1,10] Both are cheap to say and expensive to skip. And a stakeholder is anyone with an interest in the outcome, which is a broader category than "user" — the regulator who will read your logs is a stakeholder who will never touch your interface.[11]

§ 44Prior art: a short history of asking first

The phrase software engineering was chosen provocatively for the 1968 NATO conference at Garmisch, to suggest that software might one day be built on a theoretical footing the way other engineering disciplines were.[12] The conference report is largely a record of practitioners describing projects that had failed for reasons nobody could name precisely. Requirements are all over it, unlabelled.

Winston Royce's 1970 paper supplied the diagram that the next thirty years misread. Royce drew a sequential model, said in the accompanying text that it was risky and invited failure, and proposed iteration and a preliminary programme as correctives.[13] The industry kept the picture and discarded the argument. Bell and Thayer named requirements as a first-class problem in 1976.[2] Within a year the first methods arrived: Douglas Ross and Kenneth Schoman's structured analysis for requirements definition,[14] and Mack Alford's Software Requirements Engineering Methodology with its Requirements Statement Language and the REVS toolset built to check it.[15,16]

The most influential artefact of the era was not a method but a document. In 1978 a Naval Research Laboratory team led by Kathryn Heninger, working with David Parnas, rewrote the requirements for the A-7E aircraft's flight software — roughly twelve thousand assembler instructions on a computer with sixteen kilobytes of memory — using tabular notations designed to be precise, concise, unambiguous, and checkable for completeness and consistency.[17,18] The A-7E document is still the reference example of what a fully worked requirements specification looks like, and its descendant, the Software Cost Reduction method, produced tooling that automated the consistency checks.[19,20]

Then the field split, roughly along the line between people who had to certify aircraft and people who had to ship web applications, and the two halves stopped reading each other. The certification half formalised: goal-oriented methods such as KAOS and i* modelled intent and its refinement into responsibilities;[21,22,23] the NFR Framework gave non-functional requirements a treatment as first-class softgoals to be satisficed rather than satisfied;[24] the standards consolidated into IEEE 830 and eventually ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148.[1,25] The shipping half compressed: use cases from Ivar Jacobson,[26] then user stories from Extreme Programming,[27] then personas,[28] then behaviour-driven development,[29] then the minimum viable product.[30,31]

Both halves were right about something. The formal half was right that a requirement you cannot check is not a requirement. The agile half was right that a requirement you cannot revise is a liability, and that most requirements documents were written to survive a review meeting rather than to be true. The useful practitioner today reads both literatures and notices they are arguing about the cost of being wrong, not about the nature of requirements.

A statistic worth handling carefully The claim that requirements defects cost a hundred times more to fix after delivery traces to Barry Boehm's 1981 data from sixty-three projects.[32] It is repeated everywhere, including in NASA's own guidance.[33] It is also the subject of a sustained critique: Laurent Bossavit's audit of the primary sources argues the evidence does not meet a reasonable standard for the claim's confidence.[34] Boehm and Victor Basili themselves revised the picture in 2001, noting the curve is markedly flatter for small projects with fast feedback.[35] The defensible version is narrow and still useful: the cost of a requirements error scales with how much work has been built on top of it before anyone notices. Continuous integration flattens the curve by shortening that interval; it does not repeal it.

§ 55The subject system: one case, carried throughout

Abstract requirements advice is worth very little. Everything below is grounded in one system, chosen because it is genuinely multi-stakeholder, genuinely regulated, and spans firmware, cloud services, payments, and physical ergonomics without any of them being optional.

A charge point operator runs a network of public electric-vehicle chargers. It is building the platform that sits between four worlds that do not share a vocabulary: the drivers who arrive at a bay, the mobility providers whose customers roam onto the network under someone else's contract, the distribution system operator whose capacity the site borrows, and the regulator who audits the bill. The system has firmware in the charge point, services in a cloud region, a payment terminal governed by card-scheme rules, and a steel enclosure that a person in a wheelchair has to be able to reach.

Three constraints on this system are not negotiable and were not invented by anyone on the project. Under the EU's Alternative Fuels Infrastructure Regulation, publicly accessible recharging points deployed from 13 April 2024 must permit ad-hoc recharging with a payment instrument widely used in the Union; points rated at 50 kW or above must offer a card reader or a contactless payment device, and a QR code alone will not do.[36] The same regulation requires ad-hoc price components to be presented to the end user, in a specified order, before the session begins.[36] And only energy measured by a legally verified meter may appear on an invoice — a metrology rule that predates the platform by roughly a century.

Throughout, tagged items refer to this case: STK-1 for a stakeholder, NEED-4 for a stakeholder need, BR-3 for a business rule, FR-05 and NFR-01 for requirements, AC-06 for an acceptance criterion, VER-04 for a verification artefact. Clause 23 runs a program over the whole set and reports what it finds. Clause 24 prints the resulting traceability matrix.

§ 66Stakeholder analysis

Nearly every catastrophic requirements failure is, on inspection, a stakeholder who was not in the room. The London Ambulance Service's computer-aided despatch system is the canonical case: the 1993 inquiry found that neither the system nor its users were ready for the full implementation of 26 October 1992, that the software was incomplete and not fully tested, that fall-back paths had not been exercised, and that scepticism about the vehicle location data had never been resolved.[37] Anthony Finkelstein and John Dowell's later analysis is blunter about the underlying cause: the requirements reflected a management model of how despatch ought to work rather than the crews' model of how it did.[38]

Stakeholder identification received far less attention than the techniques that depend on it, which Helen Sharp, Anthony Finkelstein and Galal Galal-Edeen observed directly when they proposed a procedure for it in 1999. Their method starts from a set of baseline stakeholders and expands outward: suppliers who feed the baseline group information or work, clients who consume its products, and satellites who interact with it in other ways, with legislators treated separately.[39] The move that matters is procedural: you derive stakeholders from interactions rather than from a brainstorm, so the list is auditable.

Two orthogonal tools help once the list exists. Ronald Mitchell, Bradley Agle and Donna Wood's salience model classifies stakeholders by three attributes — power, legitimacy and urgency — and predicts that managers attend to those holding more of them, which explains why legitimate-but-not-urgent claims are reliably deferred.[40] And the power-interest grid, though crude, is useful precisely because placing a dot forces an argument. In the charging case the argument is about STK-8: an advocacy group with no contractual relationship, no budget authority, and a statutory instrument behind it that will eventually make its position expensive to have ignored.

§ 77Requirements elicitation

Elicitation is not collection. Requirements are not lying around waiting to be picked up; the metaphor of "gathering" them is, as Donald Gause and Gerald Weinberg argued, the single most misleading thing said about the activity.[41] What people can tell you is a small and biased subset of what you need. Joseph Goguen and Charlotte Linde's survey of techniques makes the point sociologically: interviews produce accounts of work that are shaped by the interview, and expert knowledge is often precisely the knowledge its holder can no longer articulate.[42]

The empirical evidence about which technique to use is weaker than the literature's confidence implies. Oscar Dieste and Natalia Juristo's systematic review and aggregation of elicitation experiments concluded that structured interviews perform well and consistently across studies, and that many comparative claims rest on thin evidence.[43] Neil Maiden and Gordon Rugg's ACRE framework offers the more useful framing: choose the technique from the properties of the knowledge you are after — its accessibility, its internal structure, whether observation is even possible.[44] Contextual inquiry, Hugh Beyer and Karen Holtzblatt's apprenticeship model, exists because for some knowledge there is no substitute for watching someone work and asking why in the moment.[45]

In the charging case, three findings came from three different quadrants. The latency budget in NFR-01 came from an interview: drivers said the wait at the terminal felt interminable. The number came from telemetry: session abandonment rose sharply past eight seconds. And NEED-5 — deciding from the office whether a fault needs a van — came from riding along with a field technician for a day and noticing that three of five call-outs ended in a remote reset that could have been issued from a desk. No one had complained about this. It was simply how the job was done.

The other half of elicitation is negative: eliciting what the system must not do. Obstacle analysis in KAOS systematises this by asking, for each goal, what conditions would obstruct it, and then refining the obstacles until they are concrete enough to design against.[22] The security and safety communities arrived at the same practice from the other direction, as misuse cases and hazard analysis.[46] The EARS unwanted-behaviour pattern exists because this class of requirement is systematically under-elicited.[47]

§ 88Business rules

A business rule is a constraint the organisation or its regulator imposes on how the world may be, independent of any system. It is not a requirement, and conflating the two is one of the more expensive category errors in the field. Requirements change when the product strategy changes; business rules change when the law, the contract, or the accounting standard changes, and they apply to every system the organisation builds, including the ones written on paper.

The Business Rules Manifesto states the position sharply: rules are a first-class requirement in their own right, they are built on facts and terms rather than on process, and they should be expressed declaratively in natural language, for business people, separately from the processes that enforce them.[48] The Object Management Group formalised that vocabulary in SBVR and later gave decision logic its own executable notation in DMN, whose decision tables can be checked for completeness and contradiction mechanically.[49,50]

In the charging case the rules are inherited, and each one has a provenance outside the project:

  • BR-1 — AFIR Article 5(1): ad-hoc payment at points deployed from 13 April 2024, with a card reader or contactless device at 50 kW and above.[36]
  • BR-3 — only energy measured by a legally verified meter may be invoiced. The platform's integration of power samples is not a billable quantity, however accurate it happens to be.
  • BR-4 — on a roaming session the driver's contract is with the mobility provider; the operator settles with the provider and never bills the driver directly. This one comes from OCPI's settlement model.[51]
  • BR-5 — site import may never exceed the connection agreement's capacity, including during recovery from a fault. The qualifier is the whole rule.

Two practices follow. First, rules get their own identifiers and their own change control, because their lifecycle is not yours — when AFIR's 2027 retrofit deadline arrives, BR-1 changes and everything tracing to it must be re-examined. Second, rules should be expressed where they can be evaluated rather than scattered through conditionals; the reason DMN exists is that a decision table with a hit policy can be shown to a compliance officer, and an if statement cannot.[50]

§ 99Functional requirements

A functional requirement states an obligation on observable behaviour: given some condition, the system does some thing. It is the most familiar artefact in the discipline and the one most often written badly, because writing it badly is so easy and the badness does not surface until integration.

ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 lists nine characteristics of a well-formed individual requirement — necessary, appropriate, unambiguous, complete, singular, feasible, verifiable, correct and conforming — and separately requires the set to be complete, consistent, affordable, bounded and traceable.[1] These are not aspirations; each one has a corresponding review question and most can be partly automated.

Levels matter as much as wording. A business requirement explains why the money is being spent; a stakeholder need names what a party must be able to do; a solution requirement states what the system must do or be; and a transition requirement covers what is only true while you are changing over — the category that gets forgotten and then dominates the last two months of a programme.[52]

Here is FR-02 from the case, in its final form:

When the acquirer approves the pre-authorisation, the charge point shall unlock the connector and begin energy transfer.

It is singular in obligation, names its trigger, names one responsible system, states an observable response, and traces up to NEED-1 and NEED-2 and down to AC-03. It also, deliberately, says nothing about how long that should take — the timing lives in NFR-01, where an architect can trade it against availability without renegotiating the functional behaviour.

§ 1010Writing them so a machine can object

Natural language is the notation of record in industry, and will remain so: Samuel Fricker and colleagues' industrial survey found only a small minority of projects using formal notations at all.[53] The productive response is not to abandon natural language but to constrain it.

The best-known constraint is EARS, developed by Alistair Mavin and colleagues at Rolls-Royce while analysing the airworthiness regulations that formed the certification basis for an aero-engine control system. Mavin noticed that the requirements that read well all shared an underlying clause structure, and that readability improved further when the clauses always appeared in the same order.[47,54] The ruleset is small: zero or many preconditions, zero or one trigger, exactly one system name, one or many responses. Five patterns fall out of it, plus combinations.[55] Rewriting a requirement set into EARS at Rolls-Royce reduced all eight of the problem types they had catalogued — ambiguity, vagueness, complexity, omission, duplication, wordiness, inappropriate implementation and untestability.[47]

Constraint enables tooling. Henning Femmer and colleagues formalised the idea of a requirements smell — an indicator in the text that something is likely wrong, borrowed from the code-smell tradition — and showed that lightweight detection catches a meaningful share of defects that reviews miss.[56] Daniel Berry and Erik Kamsties' treatment of ambiguity remains the reference for why this is hard: much ambiguity is invisible to the reader, because each reader resolves it silently and consistently in their own favour.[57] Natural-language processing for requirements is now a substantial research area with a mapping study to match.[58]

At the far end sits formal specification. Leslie Lamport's TLA+ and Daniel Jackson's Alloy both let you state properties of a design and search for counterexamples mechanically.[59,60] The industrial case for them was made most persuasively by Chris Newcombe and colleagues at Amazon Web Services, who reported using TLA+ on production systems and finding design bugs in replication and fault-tolerance protocols that had survived deep review, testing and years of operation.[61] The A-7E tabular method is the older cousin of this idea, and its automated consistency checks are the same move.[18,20]

What none of this buys you is correctness of the requirement itself. A perfectly formed, machine-checked, unambiguous specification of the wrong thing is still the wrong thing. Notation improves S. Only stakeholders can tell you about R.

§ 1111Non-functional requirements, and why the name is wrong

The term is a historical accident that has cost the industry a great deal. It implies a residual category — the leftovers after the real requirements are written — when in practice these are the requirements that determine the architecture. You can add a feature to almost any system. You cannot add availability, and you certainly cannot add safety.

Martin Glinz's analysis of the term found the definitions in circulation mutually inconsistent, and proposed classifying by kind of concern — functional, performance, specific quality, constraint — rather than by what a requirement is not.[62] Lawrence Chung and colleagues had already given the category serious theoretical treatment in the NFR Framework, modelling quality concerns as softgoals that are satisficed to a degree rather than satisfied outright, with explicit positive and negative contribution links between them.[24,63] That last detail is the point: quality attributes conflict with each other by construction, and a notation that cannot express the conflict cannot help you resolve it.

The reference vocabulary is ISO/IEC 25010. The 2023 revision expanded the product quality model from eight top-level characteristics to nine, adding safety alongside functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, interaction capability, reliability, security, maintainability and flexibility.[64] The 2011 edition had folded usability and portability differently; the revision renamed them interaction capability and flexibility and redistributed the subcharacteristics.[65] A companion model covers quality in use.[66]

The scenario is what makes it a requirement

"The system shall be highly available" is not a requirement. It cannot fail a test. The remedy from the software architecture community is the quality attribute scenario, which has six parts: a source of stimulus, the stimulus, the artifact stimulated, the environment, the response, and the response measure.[67,68] The sixth is the one that does the work. In the Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method the scenarios are elicited into a utility tree, prioritised, and then used to expose sensitivity points and tradeoff points in the architecture — the places where one decision moves two attributes in opposite directions.[68,69]

Compare the two drafts of NFR-03 in the case. The first read: the platform shall be highly available and shall recover quickly from faults. Two obligations, two unquantified adjectives, no artifact, no environment, no measure. The second reads: if the site controller loses its power-allocation state, then the site controller shall re-establish a safe allocation within 30 seconds while holding aggregate import below the contracted capacity. It names the condition, the responsible element, the bound, and the invariant that must survive the recovery — which is the part BR-5 actually cares about.

§ 1212Personas

Alan Cooper introduced personas to a wide audience in The Inmates Are Running the Asylum, on the basis of a practice he had been refining since 1983, when he began interviewing people about their work and then arguing with a composite of them out loud while designing.[28,70] The central claim is counter-intuitive and mostly holds: designing precisely for one well-specified person produces a better result for a broad population than designing vaguely for everyone, because "the user" is elastic and will be bent to fit whatever you build.

A persona is a design tool, not a market segment. Its content is goals and context, not demographics. John Pruitt and Jonathan Grudin's account of running personas at scale inside Microsoft is the honest one about what it takes to make them work: grounding in real data, sustained visibility, and organisational effort that most teams underestimate.[71] The critique is equally worth reading — Christopher Chapman and Russell Milham argue that personas are unfalsifiable, that there is no way to verify a given persona corresponds to anyone, and that the multivariate statistics make composite "typical" users vanishingly rare in reality.[72]

The reconciliation used in practice: treat a persona as a hypothesis about a segment that must trace to evidence and must be falsifiable by it. In the charging case the persona behind NEED-1 is not "Sarah, 34, drives an estate car." It is: a driver who has never used this network, has no app installed, has fifteen minutes, and will abandon the bay rather than create an account. That is a claim about behaviour, and the telemetry either supports it or does not. The persona behind NEED-8 carries a different kind of weight — a wheelchair user's reach envelope is anthropometric data, not a narrative device, and it converts directly into FR-11's 400–1200 mm bound.

§ 1313User stories

The user story came out of Extreme Programming as a deliberate reduction: a chunk of functionality valuable to the customer, written on a card, whose purpose is to schedule a conversation rather than to replace one.[27,73] Ron Jeffries' three Cs name the parts — the Card is a token, the Conversation is where the requirement actually lives, and the Confirmation is how you will know it is done.[74] The familiar template (As a <role>, I want <goal> so that <benefit>) came later, from Rachel Davies and her team at Connextra, and reached the industry mainly through Mike Cohn's User Stories Applied.[75] Bill Wake's INVEST mnemonic — independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small, testable — is the quality checklist most teams actually apply.[76]

The template earns its keep through the third clause. So that forces a benefit, and a benefit that cannot be stated is a feature that cannot be prioritised. It is also where most teams stop bothering, which is why backlogs fill with items whose value nobody can reconstruct six months later.

Jeff Patton's story mapping addresses the format's structural weakness: a flat backlog destroys the narrative order of the work, so you lose the ability to see whether a release actually lets anyone finish a task end to end.[77] A map arranges stories along the user's journey horizontally and by priority vertically, and slices become releases that each do something whole. This is the same insight that makes vertical slicing the right shape for a minimum viable product.

What a story cannot do is be a contract. It carries no obligation, no bound, and no alternate path. In a regulated context this is disqualifying: no certification authority will accept As a driver, I want to pay by card as the requirement traced by a test. It is not a defect of the format — it is the format working as designed, deferring precision to the conversation and the acceptance criteria.

Verified by

The role is ‘as a developer’ or ‘as the system’.

§ 1414Use cases

Ivar Jacobson had been writing usage scenarios for Ericsson's AXE switching system since the late 1960s; he presented the technique to the object-oriented community at OOPSLA in 1987, and it reached the mainstream with the 1992 OOSE book.[26,78] Alistair Cockburn encountered the 1987 paper while building a methodology for IBM Consulting, went to Stockholm to learn it from Jacobson, and later wrote the book that fixed the industry's templates.[79,80] Both authors subsequently adapted the technique to incremental delivery — Jacobson as Use-Case 2.0, slicing a use case into independently deliverable stories, and Cockburn as agile use cases; in 2023 the two set out the principles they agree on.[81,82]

The structural contribution of a use case is not the ellipse in the diagram. It is the extensions. A use case has a main success scenario — the path everyone agrees on in the room — and then a numbered list of the ways it can deviate, each with its own handling. That list is where the money is.

Compare the four notations available for a single need. Each carries different information and none is a superset of the others.

Cockburn's goal levels are the other durable contribution: a use case can sit at summary level (a business process), user-goal level (something one person completes in one sitting), or subfunction level.[79] Mixing levels in one document is the most common way use cases become unreadable, and the reason many teams abandoned them for stories without noticing that the extensions went too.

§ 1515Acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria are where a requirement becomes falsifiable. Everything before them is intent; they are the operational definition of done for one item, agreed before the work starts rather than negotiated after it.

The dominant format came out of behaviour-driven development. Dan North had been rewriting JUnit as JBehave to replace the vocabulary of testing with the vocabulary of behaviour; in 2004, discussing it with the business analyst Chris Matts, the two realised the same vocabulary applied to analysis, and developed the Given/When/Then structure to capture a story's acceptance criteria in executable form.[29,83] Martin Fowler's summary is the compact one: given is the state of the world before, when is the behaviour being specified, then is the change you expect.[84] Eric Evans' notion of a ubiquitous language was an explicit influence — the point was a shared vocabulary across analysts, testers, developers and the business.[29]

Gojko Adzic's Specification by Example is the best account of what actually makes this work, and it is not the tooling: it is deriving a small set of key examples collaboratively, then automating them so the specification cannot silently diverge from the system.[85,86] Where BDD fails, it usually fails because a single person wrote Gherkin alone, in which case the format has added ceremony and removed nothing.

Not every criterion should be Given/When/Then. In the charging case, AC-12 reads: given the production enclosure drawing, when reach is measured from a seated anthropometric model, then every driver-operated control falls within 400–1200 mm. That is an analysis, not a test, and its verification artefact VER-08 is a reach study. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 recognises four verification methods — test, analysis, inspection and demonstration — and insisting everything be an automated test is a good way to leave the physical and procedural requirements unverified.[1]

One discipline is worth more than the format: write the criterion before the implementation, and write it so that a reasonable engineer could build something that passes it and is wrong. Then fix the criterion. That exercise finds ambiguity faster than any review.

§ 1616Requirements prioritization

Prioritisation is where requirements engineering becomes economics. The problem is not ranking; it is that stakeholders asked to rank will place everything at the top, which is why every method in use is really a device for forcing a scarcity constraint into the conversation.

MoSCoW is the bluntest and most used. Dai Clegg devised it at Oracle UK in 1994 for rapid application development, donated it to the DSDM consortium, and it became a core practice of that framework.[87,88] The four buckets — must, should, could, won't — work because "won't have, this time" is a category the others lack, and because DSDM pairs the labels with a rule most adopters skip: the must-haves should account for a bounded share of the timebox's effort, leaving deliberate slack.[88] Without that constraint, MoSCoW degenerates into a list where everything is a must.

Kano's model is orthogonal and more interesting. Noriaki Kano and colleagues argued in 1984 that the relationship between how fully a feature is implemented and how satisfied the customer is is not linear and not uniform: some attributes are must-be (their absence disgusts, their presence is unremarked), some are one-dimensional (satisfaction scales with fulfilment), and some are attractive (their absence is unnoticed, their presence delights).[89]

Quantitative methods try to make the tradeoff explicit. Joachim Karlsson and Kevin Ryan's cost-value approach uses pairwise comparison to derive value and cost estimates and plots requirements against both, which surfaces the high-value, low-cost items that intuition misses.[90] Karl Wiegers' relative weighting scheme separates benefit from penalty — the harm of not doing something, which is where compliance items live.[91]

The most economically literate method is Donald Reinertsen's. His argument in The Principles of Product Development Flow is that in a capacity-constrained system, sequencing rather than prioritisation determines throughput, and the economically optimal sequence ranks work by cost of delay divided by duration — weighted shortest job first.[92] SAFe adopted it with relative estimation, decomposing cost of delay into user-business value, time criticality, and risk reduction or opportunity enablement.[93] The critique is fair and worth holding: relative proxies for cost of delay are not cost of delay, and a ratio of two guesses is a guess.

Finally, a widely repeated figure deserves care. The claim that 64% of features are rarely or never used comes from a keynote Jim Johnson of the Standish Group gave at XP 2002; Mike Cohn, who checked with Standish directly, reports the figure came from a study of four internal applications.[94] The broader Chaos data has its own methodological critics: Jorge Eveleens and Chris Verhoef's analysis in IEEE Software argues the definitions used make the headline figures misleading,[95] and Magne Jørgensen and Kjetil Moløkken-Østvold question the 1994 overrun numbers directly.[96] The underlying observation — that usage is heavily concentrated in a minority of features — is probably true and worth acting on. The specific number is not evidence.

§ 1717Minimum viable product

MVP is the most abused term in the vocabulary, and its two definitions pull in different directions. Frank Robinson coined it in 2001 at SyncDev, where it was an economic instrument: the right-sized product, big enough to cause adoption, satisfaction and sales, but not so big as to be bloated and risky — maximum return on risk for both vendor and customer.[30] Eric Ries, building on Steve Blank's customer development, redefined it as an epistemic instrument: the version of a new product that allows a team to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort.[31,97]

The gap matters. Robinson's MVP must be sellable. Ries' MVP must be informative, and may not be a product at all — a landing page, a concierge service, a wizard-of-oz prototype. A systematic mapping of the literature by Valentina Lenarduzzi and Davide Taibi found the definitions have continued to proliferate rather than converge.[98] Neither original definition supports the usage that dominates in practice, which is "the smallest thing we can ship before the deadline."

The operational question is not what is the minimum? but what is the riskiest thing we believe? An MVP is an experiment, and an experiment without a hypothesis is just a smaller product. In the charging case, the riskiest belief behind NEED-1 was not technical — card terminals are a solved problem — it was behavioural: will a stranger tap a bank card at an unattended machine on a dark forecourt and trust that the eventual charge will be correct? The first slice was designed to answer that: one site, one tariff, card only, no roaming, no app, manual settlement behind the scenes. It billed real drivers real money on week three.

Amazon's working-backwards process is the most disciplined version of the same instinct in an established company. Before anything is built, the team writes an internal press release and an FAQ describing the finished product from the customer's point of view; if the press release does not describe something worth announcing, the idea is reworked or dropped on paper.[99] It is a requirements technique disguised as a writing exercise: it forces the benefit statement, the target customer, and the objections into text before anyone can hide behind a roadmap.

What an MVP cannot do is discharge non-negotiable obligations. BR-1 applies to any publicly accessible point deployed after April 2024 regardless of how minimal the release is.[36] Safety and legal constraints are not learning opportunities. The scope you can compress is the scope where being wrong is recoverable.

§ 1818Scope management

Scope creep and scope discovery look identical for the first third of a project and are opposites. Discovery is the set converging as you learn what was always true; creep is the set expanding because nobody is enforcing the relationship between new items and stated needs.

The FBI's Virtual Case File is the reference case for creep at scale. After roughly 170 million dollars and five years, the programme was abandoned in 2005.[6,100] The Government Accountability Office had warned as early as 2003 that the bureau was proceeding without an enterprise architecture — without, in effect, a stable frame against which new requirements could be judged.[6] An arbitrator later apportioned fifty-nine identified problems between FBI requirement changes and contractor errors. The successor programme, Sentinel, delivered in 2012 after its own delays and overruns.[101]

Three mechanisms do most of the work in practice.

A baseline with a version
Not to freeze the requirements — that fails — but to make change visible. If you cannot say which version of the set the current estimate was based on, you cannot detect drift.
An admission test for every new item
Which need does this trace to? If none, either a need was missed — add it explicitly, with a stakeholder — or the item is out. In the charging case this test removed FR-07, a driver-efficiency leaderboard nobody could attribute to a stakeholder.
A visible exchange rate
Adding something means removing something or moving the date. DSDM's timebox with a bounded must-have share encodes this structurally.[88] The point is that the trade happens in the open.

The classical framing — the iron triangle of scope, time and cost with quality in the middle — is a useful teaching device and a poor model of reality, because quality is not a dependent variable you can dial down without consequences; it converts into future cost with interest. The more honest formulation is that scope is the only variable most organisations can actually move, which is why prioritisation and scope management are the same activity viewed from two ends.

Blind spot

§ 1919Traceability

Traceability is the property that lets you answer two questions in both directions: why does this exist? and where did this end up? It is widely treated as a compliance tax, and in organisations that treat it that way it becomes one — a matrix produced by an intern the week before an audit, accurate on the day it was printed and wrong thereafter.

Orlena Gotel and Anthony Finkelstein's 1994 study is the paper that reframed it. Working from empirical data across more than a hundred practitioners, they split the problem in two: post-RS traceability tracks a requirement forward from the specification through design, code and test, while pre-RS traceability tracks it backward through the process that produced it — who said it, from what source, superseding what earlier statement. Their finding was that most of the problems attributed to poor traceability were pre-RS problems, and that the tooling of the day addressed almost exclusively the post-RS half.[102] Thirty years later this is still mostly true. Your issue tracker knows which commit closed which ticket. It very rarely knows who asked for the ticket and whether that person still works here.

Bala Ramesh and Matthias Jarke's field study produced reference models for what practitioners actually record, and found a sharp split between low-end users who trace because they are told to and high-end users who trace because it supports their work — the latter capturing rationale, not just links.[103] Jane Cleland-Huang and colleagues' survey of trends sets out the research agenda that followed: automated link recovery, trace quality, and the economics of maintaining links over a system's life.[104,105] There is also direct evidence of benefit: Patrick Mäder and Alexander Egyed's controlled experiment found developers performing maintenance tasks with traceability available were measurably faster and more correct than those without.[106]

Where it is not optional

In certified domains, traceability is the evidence, not a byproduct of it. DO-178C requires bidirectional traceability across the specification hierarchy — system requirements to high-level software requirements to low-level requirements to source code to test cases — with the depth required scaling by design assurance level.[107] IEC 62304 imposes an analogous structure on medical device software,[108] and the FDA's design control regulation requires that design outputs be traceable to design inputs and that the whole be captured in a design history file.[109,110] ISO 26262 does the same for road vehicles and IEC 61508 for programmable safety systems generally.[111,112] The interchange of these link structures between tools is what ReqIF exists to standardise.[113]

The practical minimum for a non-certified team is much smaller than the certified apparatus and delivers most of the value: give every need and every requirement a stable identifier, record the parent link at the moment the child is written, and run a check that fails the build when a requirement has no parent or no acceptance criterion. That is roughly two hundred lines of code, and clause 23 contains a working version of it.

§ 2020Validation, verification, and assumptions that rot

Return to the entailment D, S ⊨ R. Verification asks whether the machine satisfies S. Validation asks whether R was the right condition to want. Neither asks whether D is still true — and D is where the worst failures live, because when a domain assumption rots, every artefact in the chain remains internally consistent.[7,114]

The examples are unusually clear.

Ariane 5, flight 501, 1996
Inertial reference software reused from Ariane 4 raised an operand error converting a horizontal-velocity value that exceeded what a 16-bit integer could hold. The code was correct against its specification. The specification was correct against Ariane 4's trajectory. The domain assumption — the range of that variable — was not re-validated for a launcher with a different flight profile.[115]
Mars Climate Orbiter, 1999
The mishap board identified the root cause as ground software supplying impulse data in pound-force seconds where the navigation software expected newton-seconds. An interface requirement existed on paper. The shared phenomenon — the unit — was assumed rather than verified end to end.[116]
Therac-25, 1985–87
Nancy Leveson and Clark Turner's investigation found that removing the hardware interlocks of earlier models placed the entire safety burden on software whose requirements had never been analysed for the hazards those interlocks had been covering.[46,117]
737 MAX, 2018–19
The Joint Authorities Technical Review found that MCAS design assumptions were not adequately reviewed, updated or validated as the function evolved, that certification deliverables were not updated to reflect the changes, and that evaluating a single worst-case angle-of-attack scenario was inadequate to identify the hazardous effects of single-sensor failures.[118] The congressional report's chapter headings name the theme directly: faulty assumptions.[119] The FAA's return-to-service summary lists the resulting design changes, beginning with the use of a single angle-of-attack input.[120]

What links them is not a coding error. In each case the machine did what its specification said, and the specification had been derived from a description of the world that had quietly stopped being accurate. The practical countermeasure is to write domain assumptions down as first-class items with owners and review dates — the same treatment business rules get — and to re-validate them whenever the operating envelope changes. Almost nobody does this, which is why it keeps happening.

The positive evidence for requirements quality mattering is thinner than one would like but consistent in direction. Hubert Hofmann and Franz Lehner's study of requirements engineering practice across projects identified knowledge, resources and process as the factors distinguishing more successful efforts,[121] and Motoei Kamata and Tetsuo Tamai found a measurable relationship between the quality of specific parts of a requirements specification and project outcome.[122]

§ 2121Requirements for systems that learn

Machine learning breaks a load-bearing assumption of the discipline: that behaviour is specified before it is implemented. A trained model's behaviour is induced from data, which means the specification of what it does is, in part, a specification of the data it was trained on.

Andreas Vogelsang and Markus Borg interviewed data scientists about this directly and found the requirements problems are real and under-served: eliciting what a model should optimise, specifying data quality, and defining what acceptable performance even means for a probabilistic component.[123] Saleema Amershi and colleagues' case study of machine learning teams at Microsoft describes the same gap at organisational scale.[124] D. Sculley and colleagues had earlier catalogued the system-level consequences — entanglement, hidden feedback loops, undeclared consumers — under the heading of technical debt.[125]

Three requirement types become first-class in this setting.

Data requirements
Provenance, coverage, representativeness, labelling policy, exclusion criteria. Datasheets for datasets exist to make these explicit and auditable.[126]
Model behaviour requirements
Not a single accuracy figure but a distribution of performance across subpopulations and conditions, with thresholds. Model cards were proposed precisely to force this reporting.[127]
Operational envelope requirements
Where the model may be used and where it may not, plus what happens at the boundary — abstention, escalation, human review.

Regulation has now made much of this mandatory. The EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems trained on data to be developed on the basis of training, validation and test sets that are relevant, sufficiently representative and, to the extent possible, free of errors and complete for the intended purpose;[128,129] technical documentation under Article 11 and Annex IV must be sufficient to assess conformity, and Article 12 imposes automatic logging.[128] Work is under way to map these obligations onto concrete verification activities, which is itself a requirements engineering problem.[130] The GDPR's data-protection-by-design obligation had already established the pattern of a legal requirement that must be discharged in the architecture rather than in a policy document.[131]

Certification exposes the sharpest version of the problem. DO-178C's traceability objectives assume that behaviour can be traced from low-level requirements to source code; a neural network's behaviour is encoded in learned parameters that cannot be mapped to specific functions, so the objectives are not achievable in the usual way and the assurance argument has to be restructured around architectural mitigation instead.[107,132] That is a genuinely open problem, not a paperwork one.

The same pressure now applies to large language models in products. A prompt is a specification written in the least constrained notation available, and "the assistant shall be helpful" fails all nine of 29148's characteristics.[1] The techniques that transfer are the old ones: name the operational envelope, specify the unwanted behaviours explicitly, define acceptance in terms of measured outcomes over a held-out set rather than impressions, and record which stakeholder asked for each behaviour.

§ 2323Checking the set: this article's own case, run at build time

Everything above is assertion until something checks it. The charging case carries a requirement set — eight stakeholders, ten needs, six business rules, functional and quality requirements, acceptance criteria and verification artefacts — and the program below was run over it while this page was generated. Its output is not illustrative. It is the actual output, and it found defects that were in the set as first drafted.

The checker is dependency-free Python. It builds the trace graph, then asks four questions: does every requirement descend from a need, does every need reach a requirement, does every requirement have an acceptance criterion with a verification artefact behind it, and is each statement singular, bounded and shaped like one of the five EARS patterns. It adds two economic checks: whether any need is realised only by requirements of lower priority than itself, and how the needs rank under weighted shortest job first.

EARS classification and well-formedness heuristics


_EARS = [
    ("unwanted",     re.compile(r"^\s*if\b.+?,\s*then\s+the\b", re.I | re.S)),
    ("state-driven", re.compile(r"^\s*while\b", re.I)),
    ("optional",     re.compile(r"^\s*where\b", re.I)),
    ("event-driven", re.compile(r"^\s*when\b", re.I)),
    ("ubiquitous",   re.compile(r"^\s*the\s+\S+.*\bshall\b", re.I | re.S)),
]

def smell(text):
    """Return a list of (code, detail) well-formedness complaints."""
    out, low = [], text.lower()

    shalls = len(re.findall(r"\bshall\b", low))
    if shalls == 0:
        out.append(("W-OBLIG", "no binding 'shall'"))
    if shalls > 1:
        out.append(("W-SING", f"{shalls} obligations in one statement"))

    # A performance-flavoured statement with no number is not verifiable.
    perf = any(w in low for w in ("within", "latency", "throughput",
                                  "availability", "response time", "uptime",
                                  "concurrent", "per second", "recovery"))
    if perf and not _QUANTITY.search(text):
        out.append(("W-UNVERIF", "performance claim with no measurable bound"))

    if _PASSIVE.search(text) and not _AGENT.search(text):
        out.append(("W-AGENT", "passive voice with no responsible agent"))
    return out

The two directions of trace, and the verification chain


def _backward(self):
    """Every requirement must descend from a NEED (29148: 'necessary')."""
    out = []
    for it in self.of_kind("FR", "NFR"):
        anc = self.ancestors(it.id)
        if not any(self.items[a].kind == "NEED" for a in anc):
            out.append(Finding("error", "T-ORPHAN", it.id,
                               "no backward trace to a stakeholder need"))
    return out

def _forward(self):
    """Every need must reach at least one requirement (completeness)."""
    out = []
    for need in self.of_kind("NEED"):
        desc = self.descendants(need.id)
        if not any(self.items[d].is_requirement for d in desc):
            out.append(Finding("error", "T-UNREAL", need.id,
                               "need is not realised by any requirement"))
    return out

def _verification(self):
    out = []
    for it in self.of_kind("FR", "NFR"):
        acs = [c for c in self.children[it.id] if self.items[c].kind == "AC"]
        if not acs:
            out.append(Finding("error", "V-NOAC", it.id,
                               "no acceptance criterion; not verifiable"))
            continue
        for ac in acs:
            if not [v for v in self.children[ac]
                    if self.items[v].kind == "VER"]:
                out.append(Finding("warn", "V-NOVER", ac,
                                   "criterion has no verification artefact"))
    return out

§ 2626Closing: the essential difficulty

Fred Brooks divided the difficulties of software into the accidental — those attached to the production of software rather than to software itself — and the essential, which he located in the specification, design and testing of the conceptual construct.[154] Nearly forty years of tooling has demolished a great deal of the accidental. The essential difficulty is exactly where he left it, and it is the subject of this article.

Every technique here is a way of making a belief about the world falsifiable before it is expensive. A stakeholder map is a falsifiable claim about who matters. A persona is a falsifiable claim about behaviour. An acceptance criterion is a falsifiable claim about a specific outcome. A response measure is a falsifiable claim about how well. A traceability link is a falsifiable claim about why something exists. The formats differ in cost, precision and audience; they do not differ in purpose.

The requirement is not the sentence. The requirement is the condition in the world that the sentence is trying to describe — and the sentence is only worth writing if someone could show you it was wrong.

Which returns to the opening question, and to why it is worth asking on every line. Not what should the system do? — that question has a comfortable answer for any system, including systems nobody needs. The question is how would we know? When a team cannot answer it, they have not finished writing the requirement. When they can, most of the rest of the engineering follows.
