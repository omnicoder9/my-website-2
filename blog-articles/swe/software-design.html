01

What design is, and what it is not

Software design is the activity of choosing which decisions a future change will be allowed to touch. Everything else in the discipline — the principles, the catalogues, the diagrams, the arguments in review — is downstream of that one act.

This is a narrower definition than the field usually gives itself, and it is worth defending. The obvious definition — design is planning the system before you build it — describes a schedule rather than a skill. It also describes something that has not been true of successful projects for forty years. Real designs are discovered while being built, revised after contact with users, and documented afterwards in a form that implies a rationality nobody experienced. Parnas and Clements said so plainly in 1986: the rational design process is an ideal that cannot be followed, but the documentation it would have produced is still worth having, so you should fake it — write down the design as if it had been derived cleanly, because the reader needs the clean version even though the author never had it.1

The narrower definition survives that. Whether you decide in advance or halfway through, the work is the same: you are partitioning the system so that anticipated changes fall inside one boundary rather than across many. Parnas made this the criterion for decomposition in 1972 and nothing since has replaced it.2 A module, in his formulation, is not a chunk of the flowchart. It is a design decision that has been hidden, along with everything that follows from it, behind an interface that does not mention it.

Design, architecture, implementation

Architecture is design at the scale where decisions are expensive to reverse. Perry and Wolf gave the field its first careful vocabulary in 1992 — elements, form, and rationale — and the standard that eventually followed, ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010, defines architecture in terms of fundamental concepts and properties embodied in elements and relationships.34 The practical distinction most teams use is Fowler's: architecture is the set of decisions you wish you could get right early because changing them later is disproportionately painful. That is a statement about cost, not about altitude on a diagram. Choosing a persistent log instead of a mutable table is architecture. Choosing whether a helper takes a record or three fields is design. The reasoning is identical; the blast radius is not.

Implementation is what remains once the containments are chosen. This is not a demotion. Brooks argued in 1987 that the essential difficulty of software is the construction of the conceptual structures themselves — the invisible, arbitrary, changeable specification — while everything to do with representing those structures in a language is accidental.5 His prediction was that no single tool would deliver an order of magnitude, because the tools attack the accidents. Four decades of tooling, including tools that now write large volumes of correct code from a prompt, have not disturbed that argument. Generation is cheap. Deciding what should exist, and what each part is allowed to know, is not.

Why the activity resists method

Design problems in software have the structure Rittel and Webber called wicked: the problem is not fully knowable until you have tried a solution, there is no stopping rule, and solutions are better or worse rather than true or false.6 Schön's account of professional practice describes the corresponding skill as reflection-in-action — a conversation with the materials, where each move reveals what the next constraint is.7 Curtis, Krasner and Iscoe's field study of seventeen large projects found the same thing empirically: the binding constraint on design quality was not notation or process but the thin supply of people who held an accurate mental model of the whole application domain.8

One more constraint runs through all of it. Brooks argued that conceptual integrity — the sense that a system reflects one set of ideas rather than many good ones bolted together — is the most important consideration in system design, and that it is easier to achieve with fewer designers.9 That is in permanent tension with the throughput of a large team, and most of the machinery in section 22 exists to manage the tension rather than resolve it.

So the honest framing is this. Design cannot be reduced to a procedure, but it can be reduced to a habit of asking a small number of questions, repeatedly, at every scale: what is likely to change, what should not have to know about it, and what will it cost me if I am wrong. The rest of this article is the accumulated vocabulary the field has built for answering those three questions — where each piece came from, what it hides, and where you have already seen it working.

02

1962–1972: the decade that named the problem

Every idea in the modern vocabulary has a first appearance, and the first appearances cluster tightly. The figure below sets them on one line; the sections that follow work through what each one actually claimed.

Near-decomposability, 1962

Herbert Simon's essay on the architecture of complexity supplies the background assumption the whole field rests on: complex systems that survive are hierarchic and nearly decomposable, meaning interactions within a subsystem are strong and interactions between subsystems are weak.10 His parable of the two watchmakers — one who assembles watches from stable sub-assemblies, one who must restart from scratch whenever interrupted — is an argument about the survival value of intermediate stable forms. Software inherits this directly: a system built from parts that make sense on their own can absorb an interruption, a partial rewrite, or a new requirement without being reconstructed.

The 1968 conference, and the law that came with it

The NATO conference at Garmisch in October 1968 gave the field its name and its founding anxiety, recording in unusual candour how routinely large systems missed their budgets and their specifications.11 The follow-up at Rome a year later was less optimistic still, and the two reports remain the best primary source on what practitioners believed the problem was before anyone had a vocabulary for it.12

In the same year Melvin Conway published the observation that organisations which design systems are constrained to produce designs whose structure copies the communication structure of the organisation.13 It reads as an aphorism and is in fact a design constraint with teeth, later tested empirically on matched pairs of products built by co-located and distributed teams and found to hold.14 Section 22 returns to what you can do about it.

Structure inside the program, 1968–1974

Dijkstra's letter about the goto is remembered for the wrong reason. Its argument is not that a jump instruction is immoral; it is that unrestricted jumps destroy the correspondence between the static text of a program and its dynamic progress, which is what makes reasoning possible.15 Knuth's careful reply six years later accepted the reasoning and rejected the absolutism, showing where the disciplined jump still earns its place.16 The exchange matters because it established the actual currency of design argument: not correctness, which both forms achieve, but the reader's ability to hold the thing in mind.

Dijkstra's other 1968 paper, on the THE multiprogramming system, is the first description of a layered operating system in which each level provides an abstract machine to the one above and can be verified against that level alone.17 Wirth's stepwise refinement gave the corresponding procedure for arriving there — decompose a task into subtasks, and treat each subtask's specification as fixed while its realisation is deferred.18 And Dijkstra's later essay coined the phrase the field now uses for the whole idea: separation of concerns, which he described as studying one aspect in isolation not because the others do not matter but because a mind can only be sure of one at a time.19

Parnas, 1972

Then the paper that reframed everything. Parnas took a small program — a KWIC index, which reads lines, produces all circular shifts of each, and prints them alphabetised — and decomposed it twice.2 The first decomposition follows the processing steps, which is what a flowchart suggests and what most people still do by default. The second assigns each module a secret: a decision that might change, which no other module is permitted to know.

Both versions work. Both produce identical output. The difference appears only when a requirement arrives. Change how shifts are represented — store index pairs instead of copied text, a plausible memory optimisation — and in the first decomposition three of the five modules must be edited, because they all reach into the shared representation. In the second, one module changes and no caller notices. Parnas's conclusion was that modularisation should proceed by listing difficult or changeable design decisions first, then designing modules to hide each one. He returned to it in 1979 to argue for designing so that subsets and extensions are cheap,20 and in 1985 with Clements and Weiss to show the method applied to a real avionics system.21

Read the 1972 paper today and it is startling how little has been added. Encapsulation is a language mechanism for what Parnas described as a discipline. Interfaces, contracts, hexagonal ports, microservice boundaries and plugin APIs are all the same move at different scales.

03

1972–1994: hiding, contracts, and a catalogue

Coupling and cohesion get names

In 1974 Stevens, Myers and Constantine published the taxonomy that most code review still runs on, even where nobody can name it.22 They ranked the ways two modules can be joined — from content coupling, where one reaches into the other's internals, down to data coupling, where only needed parameters pass — and the ways a module's contents can belong together, from coincidental to functional. The 1979 book with Yourdon extended it into a full method.23 Michael Jackson's program design work, running in parallel, argued from a different direction that program structure should mirror the structure of the data it processes,24 a line of work he later generalised into problem frames — the discipline of classifying the problem in the world before designing the machine that addresses it.25

What makes the ladders durable is that they are diagnostic rather than prescriptive. Nobody achieves functional cohesion everywhere. But when a module is hard to name, or a change in one file keeps breaking another, the ladders tell you which specific thing has gone wrong, and the fix follows from the diagnosis.

Abstract data types and the meaning of a subtype

Liskov and Zilles's 1974 paper made the abstract data type a first-class construct: a set of operations whose specification is independent of any representation, so that the representation becomes a hidden decision by construction rather than by discipline.26 Simula had already supplied the mechanism — classes, objects, inheritance — as a modelling device for simulation.27 Smalltalk turned it into a philosophy of message-passing between encapsulated entities.28 Reenskaug's Model–View–Controller note from Xerox PARC gave the first widely copied separation of a user interface from the thing it displays.29

Liskov's 1987 keynote, and the 1994 paper with Wing that made it precise, supplied the condition under which a hierarchy is honest: if S is a subtype of T, anything provable about objects of type T must hold for objects of type S.3031 This is a statement about behaviour, not about method signatures, which is why a compiler cannot check it and why the violations are always found at runtime by a caller who trusted the declared type.

Meyer built the corresponding machinery into a language. Design by Contract states each routine's preconditions, postconditions and class invariants as executable assertions, converting the informal promise of an interface into something checkable and inheritable.3233 It also gave the Open–Closed Principle its name: a module should be open for extension and closed for modification — usable by clients as a fixed thing while still being specialisable. Hoare's axiomatic basis had already supplied the logical apparatus underneath.34

Patterns arrive, from architecture

The pattern idea did not come from computing. Christopher Alexander, an architect, argued in Notes on the Synthesis of Form that good design fits a form to a context, and that the designer's real work is diagnosing misfits.35 A Pattern Language then supplied 253 named, cross-referenced solutions — each with a context, a recurring conflict of forces, and a resolution — written so that non-specialists could use them.36 The Timeless Way of Building made the underlying claim: patterns are not a catalogue to be applied but a language to be spoken.37

Beck and Cunningham brought the form to software at OOPSLA in 1987.38 Seven years later Gamma, Helm, Johnson and Vlissides published twenty-three patterns with a uniform template — intent, motivation, applicability, structure, consequences, known uses — and the field acquired a shared vocabulary almost overnight.39 Two things about that book are routinely forgotten. It is substantially a book about the limits of inheritance, and it says so on page 20 in the line that became a principle in its own right: favour object composition over class inheritance. And it is a book of consequences, not recipes; every entry spends as much space on what the pattern costs as on what it buys.

04

1994–today: architecture, agility, distribution

The decade after the catalogue was spent scaling the vocabulary upward. Shaw and Garlan codified architectural styles — pipes and filters, layered, blackboard, event systems — as a discipline with its own design space.40 Kruchten's 4+1 model separated the logical, process, development and physical views, tied together by scenarios, because no single diagram serves every reader.41 The SEI's line of work turned architecture into something that could be evaluated against stated quality attributes rather than admired.4243 Buschmann and colleagues produced the pattern volumes for architecture and for concurrent and networked systems.4445 UML consolidated the notations, and then quietly receded to a whiteboard vocabulary rather than a modelling language.4647

Fielding's 2000 dissertation is the period's most consequential design document. It derives REST by starting from null and adding constraints one at a time — client–server, statelessness, cacheability, uniform interface, layered system, code on demand — and naming what each constraint buys and costs.48 It is worth reading less for REST than as a demonstration of the method: architecture as an explicit accumulation of constraints, each justified by the property it induces.

In the same window Martin assembled the principles that Feathers would later arrange into the SOLID acronym.4950 Extreme Programming pushed design into a continuous activity supported by tests and refactoring rather than an upstream phase.5152 Evans supplied the vocabulary for keeping a large model coherent — ubiquitous language, aggregates, bounded contexts — and, in the bounded context, gave the most useful modularity concept invented since Parnas.53 Fowler catalogued the enterprise layer,54 Hohpe and Woolf the messaging layer.55

The distributed turn followed. Amazon's move to service-owned data and small independent teams, described by Vogels in 2006, is the earliest widely documented version.56 By 2014 the microservices label had crystallised the style,57 and by the second edition of Newman's book the field had a sober account of the costs as well.58 What is striking in hindsight is how little of it was new as design: a service boundary is a module boundary with a network in the middle, subject to the same coupling and cohesion analysis, plus failure modes that in-process modules do not have.5960

The most recent contribution is procedural. Evolutionary architecture argues that if a design property matters — a dependency rule, a latency budget, a bound on cyclic imports — it should be expressed as an automated fitness function that runs in the build, because otherwise it decays.61 That is design treated as a test rather than a document, and it is the practice most likely to survive the current era, in which code volume has stopped being a limiting factor and the scarce good is a stated, checkable intent.

05

Separation of concerns

Separation of concerns is the parent move. Dijkstra's formulation is worth keeping in its original shape: you study one aspect at a time, not because the other aspects are unimportant, but because it is impossible to be sure about several at once, and the separation is what makes local certainty possible.19

Note what it does not say. It does not say the aspects are independent in the finished system — performance and security cut across everything — only that they can be reasoned about separately, and that a design which forces you to think about serialisation while you are thinking about tax rules has failed at something.

The concerns worth separating are not fixed. They are whatever you find yourself changing on different schedules and for different reasons. Some recur so often they have become structural conventions:

The failure mode has a name too. When the same concern is smeared across many modules — a date format handled independently in eleven places, an authorisation check copied into every handler — you have a cross-cutting concern that was never separated. Aspect-oriented programming was one response, resolving it by weaving behaviour at defined join points;63 middleware pipelines and interceptors are the mainstream version of the same idea, and section 17 shows the pattern underneath them.

Abstraction, encapsulation, information hiding

These three get conflated constantly, including in textbooks. They are different, and the differences are load-bearing.

Abstraction is selecting a vocabulary. It is a statement about what matters at this level: a file has a name, a size and a sequence of bytes, and does not have a cylinder or a block group. Abstraction is judged by whether the vocabulary stays true when the details change. POSIX's open, read, write, close has survived spinning disks, network filesystems, process introspection through /proc, and solid-state storage without changing shape.66 That longevity is the measurement.

Encapsulation is a language mechanism: bundling state with the operations on it, and marking some of it inaccessible. It is enforced by a compiler, it is syntactic, and it is neither necessary nor sufficient for good design. A class with private fields and a public getter and setter for each one is fully encapsulated and hides nothing at all.

Information hiding is the design decision that encapsulation implements. It requires you to have identified a specific thing that might change and committed to preventing the rest of the system from depending on it.2 The test is not whether a field is private. It is whether you can name the change the module absorbs.

Deep and shallow

Ousterhout supplies the sharpest working test of an abstraction: value is functionality divided by interface.67 A deep module offers a small interface over substantial machinery. A shallow one offers an interface nearly as large as what it hides, so a reader must learn the wrapper and the thing wrapped. His related warning concerns pass-through methods — a method whose body is a single delegating call — and pass-through layers, which do nothing but re-expose the layer below with different names.

The Unix file interface is the canonical deep module: four calls over a virtual filesystem layer that dispatches to ext4, btrfs, XFS, NFS and procfs, with page caching, permission checks and journalling underneath.68 SQLite's VFS layer is a deliberate, documented example of the same discipline at library scale — the entire operating-system dependency of the database is one struct of function pointers, which is why SQLite ports to unfamiliar platforms in days.69

The counter-example is the wrapper written to satisfy a rule. A UserServiceImpl whose every method forwards to UserRepository with the same signature is a shallow module: it adds a name to learn, a file to open, and a place for a bug to hide, in exchange for a flexibility nobody has asked for. Section 11 takes up when that trade is actually worth making.

07

Modularity, layering, componentization

Modularity

A module is a unit of decomposition with a named boundary and a hidden interior. The word covers a function, a class, a package, a library, a service, and a process, and the analysis does not change with the scale — only the cost of getting it wrong. Baldwin and Clark's economic treatment is the most useful non-software account: modularity is valuable because it creates options, and each independent module is an option to improve or replace one part without renegotiating the whole.70 MacCormack, Rusnak and Baldwin later measured this on real codebases, comparing designs using dependency structure matrices and showing that a widely known redesign — Mozilla's — produced a measurably more modular structure than the proprietary product it came from.71

Layering

A layer is a module whose secret is an entire category of decision, and whose rule is directional: upper layers may depend on lower ones, and never the reverse. Dijkstra's THE system established the form;17 the internet protocol stack is the version everyone uses daily.7273

Layering has a specific pathology worth naming: the leak. When a caller must know what is below to use what is above — when the ORM's laziness dictates transaction boundaries in the controller, when TCP's congestion behaviour forces application-level chunking — the layer has failed to hide its category. Sometimes the leak is unavoidable and should be documented. The end-to-end argument is the classic statement of when not to put a function in a lower layer at all: if the function can only be completely implemented with knowledge held at the endpoints, providing it in the network is at best a performance optimisation.74 That argument has been reused, correctly, to resist middleware that promises to solve a problem it cannot see the whole of.

The most successful component interface ever shipped is worth naming here because it is so small. Unix pipes let any program that reads a byte stream and writes a byte stream compose with any other, with no shared types, no registry and no build-time coupling.75 McIlroy's framing in the Bell System Technical Journal foreword — programs as tools that do one thing and combine with others — is the earliest statement of a component philosophy that later ecosystems have repeatedly rediscovered at greater cost.76 The price is real: the universal interface is untyped, so composition errors are discovered at runtime and structured data has to be re-parsed at every stage.

Componentization

A component is a module with an independent lifecycle: it is versioned, published, depended upon by name, and replaceable without recompiling its consumers. Where a module boundary is a claim about knowledge, a component boundary is additionally a claim about release cadence and ownership.

Interfaces and contracts

An interface is the published half of a module: the set of things a caller may name. A contract is the published half of its behaviour: what must be true before a call, what will be true after, and what will hold throughout.33 You can have an interface without a contract, and most codebases do; the result is that callers discover the contract by experiment and then depend on whatever they found.

Hyrum's Law

Which leads to the most important practical fact about interfaces: with enough consumers, every observable behaviour of your system becomes a dependency, regardless of what you promised.7879 Iteration order of a hash map that was never specified. The exact wording of an error string, parsed by a client. Timing that happened to be fast enough to hide a race. The design response is not to write a longer document; it is to make unspecified behaviour actively unstable where you can — deliberately randomised iteration order, injected latency, chaos testing — so that accidental dependencies fail early instead of at the moment you finally need to change something.

Designing the interface first

Bloch's rule for API design, from twenty years of doing it on the Java platform, is that you should write the client code before the implementation, and write several clients, because the second and third reveal what the first one hid.80 His related warning is that public APIs are forever: you get one chance, and every mistake is paid for by everyone who ever calls it. The corollary he draws is to prefer a small interface that can grow over a complete one that cannot shrink — the same logic as Ousterhout's depth criterion, arrived at from the maintainer's side.

Two mechanical tactics do most of the work. First, accept the narrowest type you can use and return the most specific type you can commit to. Second, name the failure modes in the type system where the language allows — a result type, a checked error, a nullable annotation — because a failure mode that exists only in documentation is a failure mode that exists only in production.

Coupling and cohesion, measured

Most design argument is qualitative, and it need not be entirely. Martin's structural metrics operate on the module dependency graph and answer a specific question: is each module as abstract as its position in the graph requires?81

  • Efferent coupling, Ce — modules this one depends on. How much of the system can break me.
  • Afferent coupling, Ca — modules that depend on this one. How much I break when I change.
  • Instability, I = Ce / (Ca + Ce) — 0 means everything depends on me and I depend on nothing, so I must not move; 1 means nothing depends on me, so I am free.
  • Abstractness, A — the fraction of the module's types that are contracts rather than implementations.
  • Distance from the main sequence, D = |A + I − 1| — the sequence A + I = 1 is the line along which a module is exactly as abstract as its stability demands. Distance from it in one direction is rigidity; in the other, irrelevance.

The two failure zones have names. A module with low abstractness and low instability sits in the zone of pain: concrete, and depended on by everything, so it cannot change and everything is welded to its details. A module with high abstractness and high instability sits in the zone of uselessness: an elaborate interface hierarchy nobody calls.

Two older metrics complement these and answer different questions. McCabe's cyclomatic complexity counts independent paths through a routine and tells you where the testing burden is;83 Chidamber and Kemerer's suite — weighted methods per class, depth of inheritance tree, coupling between object classes, response for a class — was the first attempt to make object-oriented design measurable and is still the ancestor of most tooling in the space.84 None of the three families measures design quality. The right way to use any of this is comparative and directional, never absolute. A fitness function that fails the build when a new cycle appears, or when D rises for a module you are trying to stabilise, converts a metric into a design constraint that survives staff turnover.61 A dashboard of the same numbers, admired quarterly, does nothing. Tornhill's behavioural analysis makes the same point from the version control side: the modules worth fixing are the ones where high complexity and high change frequency coincide, which no static metric alone will tell you.85

SOLID

The five principles were assembled by Martin around 2000 out of earlier work — Meyer's open–closed principle, Liskov's substitution condition, and two of Martin's own C++ Report columns — and arranged into the acronym by Michael Feathers a few years later.49508687 They are worth taking one at a time, because collapsing them into a slogan is how they get misapplied.

Single responsibility

Stated as "a class should have one reason to change", which is more precise than "a class should do one thing". Martin's later gloss sharpens it further: a module should be responsible to one actor. An invoice class that computes tax, formats a PDF and persists a row answers to the finance team, the design team and the DBA. Three actors, three independent change schedules, three reasons the file gets touched, and every touch risks the other two.

The principle is routinely over-applied. Taken literally it produces a codebase of single-method classes with no cohesion between them, which is coincidental cohesion wearing a design principle's clothes.22 The useful reading is the actor test, not the verb count.

Open–closed

Open for extension, closed for modification: you should be able to add a case without editing existing code.32 Plugin systems are the honest version — a PostgreSQL index access method, a Terraform provider, a Babel plugin, a Kubernetes admission webhook all add behaviour to a system whose core does not change.888990 The dishonest version is the abstraction added in advance for extensions nobody requests, which section 11 addresses. Open–closed is a property you should be able to point at after the second real variation arrives, not a permission slip to build a framework before the first.

Liskov substitution

A subtype must be usable wherever its supertype is, without the caller knowing.3031 The canonical violation is a subclass that strengthens a precondition or weakens a postcondition — the read-only collection that throws on add, the square that inherits from rectangle and breaks independent setWidth. Java's Collections.unmodifiableList returns something that is a List by type and throws UnsupportedOperationException from every mutator, so a caller written against the interface can be broken by a value that satisfies it — a reminder that mature platforms carry these scars deliberately.91

Interface segregation

No client should be forced to depend on methods it does not use.87 A sixteen-method service interface implemented by one class and consumed by five callers who each use two methods creates five spurious dependencies: every caller recompiles, and every test double must stub sixteen methods. Go's convention of one- and two-method interfaces declared at the point of use is the strongest mainstream expression of this principle, and it is enforced by the language's structural typing rather than by discipline.

Dependency inversion

High-level modules should not depend on low-level modules; both should depend on abstractions, and the abstraction should belong to the high-level side.86 That last clause is the whole principle and the part most often dropped. Putting an interface in the persistence package and having the domain import it inverts nothing.

Dependency injection is a mechanism for supplying the concrete implementation, not the principle itself; Fowler's article separating the two is still the clearest treatment, and the distinction matters because a container full of concrete classes wired by configuration achieves nothing structurally.9293 The compile-time test is simple: could you delete the entire infrastructure package and still compile the domain? If not, the arrow has not moved.

What SOLID does not cover

All five are class-level, object-oriented, and silent on data, concurrency, failure and time. A system can satisfy every one and still deadlock, lose writes under partition, or fall over when a dependency slows down. The principles in sections 19–20 and the failure-oriented patterns of Nygard cover ground SOLID does not reach.6059

DRY, KISS, YAGNI — and the arguments against them

DRY

The Pragmatic Programmer's formulation is precise and almost always misquoted: every piece of knowledge must have a single, unambiguous, authoritative representation within a system.9495 Knowledge, not text. Two functions that happen to contain the same six lines are duplicate text; if they encode different rules that coincide today, merging them creates a coupling between two things that were never related, and the next requirement will need a boolean parameter to tell them apart.

The counter-argument has hardened into practice over the last decade, sometimes under the label "a little duplication is cheaper than the wrong abstraction". The reasoning is that duplication is a local, visible, cheaply reversible cost, while a premature shared abstraction is a distributed, invisible, expensive one. The reconciliation is the original wording: deduplicate knowledge aggressively, deduplicate coincidence never, and wait for the third occurrence before deciding which you have.

Single source of truth

DRY's structural cousin. Every fact should have one authoritative home, and everything else should derive from it. Codd's relational model is the formal ancestor: normalisation is the systematic elimination of the same fact stored in two places, precisely because two copies can disagree.96 In modern practice it appears as generated code — a protobuf schema producing types in six languages, an OpenAPI document producing clients and server stubs — and as infrastructure defined once in version control rather than adjusted by hand.9789

The nuance is that distributed systems cannot always have one copy, so they arrange for one authority instead. Helland's distinction between data on the inside — mutable, transactionally consistent, owned — and data on the outside — immutable, versioned, a snapshot of what somebody else once said — is the cleanest statement of the design rule: a cached copy is not a second truth if it is explicitly stamped as a past observation of somebody else's truth.98

KISS

Kernighan and Plauger got there first and more usefully: write clearly, do not be too clever, because debugging is harder than writing and you will not be smart enough to debug your cleverest code.99100 The Zen of Python's version — simple is better than complex, complex is better than complicated — preserves the distinction that matters, since some problems are irreducibly complex and the goal is to avoid adding complication on top.101

Hickey's contribution is the sharpest available definition of the word.102 Simple means unentwined — one fold, one role — and is an objective property of a construct. Easy means familiar or near at hand, and is a property of you. The two are routinely confused, and the confusion is expensive, because the tool that is easiest to start with is frequently the one that braids the most concerns together and becomes hardest to change.

YAGNI

You aren't gonna need it, from Extreme Practice.51 Fowler's account gives the cost model that makes it an argument rather than an attitude: building a speculative feature costs the build, plus the carrying cost of maintaining and reading it until it is needed, plus the repair cost if the speculation was wrong — and speculation about future requirements is wrong most of the time.103

The asymmetry Boehm measured is the reason the argument is not symmetric. His cost data showed the expense of correcting a defect rising by roughly an order of magnitude at each later stage of the lifecycle, which is an argument for getting structural decisions right early even while deferring features.104 Modern practice has compressed the stages, but the shape survives: cheap to move a boundary before anything depends on it, expensive afterwards.

The line YAGNI must not be used to cross is the one between features and structure. Skipping a feature nobody asked for is YAGNI. Skipping a boundary between two things that change for different reasons is not thrift; it is deferred cost at a much worse exchange rate, because the boundary is cheap to install now and expensive to retrofit through forty-three call sites.

The figure is the whole argument for doing the design work early, and it is also the argument for doing only the design work whose blast radius you can name. If you cannot describe the change a boundary absorbs, you are not containing a decision; you are adding a layer.

Demeter, least astonishment, and the rest

The Law of Demeter

Formulated at Northeastern University in 1987 during the Demeter project and published the following year, it restricts which objects a method may send messages to: its own fields, its arguments, objects it creates, and itself.105106 The shorthand is "talk only to your immediate friends".

The popular version — count the dots — is a bad approximation. builder.a().b().c() is a fluent interface returning the same object and violates nothing. order.getCustomer().getAddress().getCountry().getTaxRate() violates the law badly, not because of the dots but because the caller now depends on four classes and on the shape of a graph it does not own. Any of those four can break it.

The corresponding refactoring is "tell, don't ask": move the computation to where the data lives and call order.taxRate(). The cost, which the original authors noted, is a proliferation of small forwarding methods on intermediate classes. That trade is worth making at the boundaries between subsystems and rarely worth making inside a cohesive one.

Principle of least astonishment

The clearest primary source is Cowlishaw's account of designing REXX, which describes an explicit astonishment factor as a design criterion: if a feature is misapplied and produces what looks to the user like an unpredictable result, the feature has a high astonishment factor and should be redesigned.107 Note that the standard is the user's model, not the designer's; consistency with what people already expect beats internal elegance.

Concretely, in an API: a method named getX that mutates state; a constructor that opens a network connection; an equals implementation that ignores a field used in hashCode; a setter that silently clamps values. Each is defensible in isolation and each will produce a bug in somebody else's code. Norman's account of affordances and mental models is the same principle in a different medium, and reads well against API design.108

Command–query separation

Meyer's rule: a routine should either return a value or change state, not both.109 It makes calls safely repeatable, makes reasoning local, and makes tests trivial. stack.pop() famously violates it and is kept because the atomic version is worth the exception — which is the correct way to break a rule: knowingly, for a named reason, in a place you document.

Fail fast, and make invalid states unrepresentable

Two rules that belong together. Validate at the boundary and refuse bad input at the point of entry rather than propagating it inward — a parse, not a validate.60 Then choose types that cannot express nonsense: a sum type for an order that is either draft with no payment or placed with one, instead of a single struct with a nullable payment field and a comment. The second rule eliminates the class of bug rather than detecting it, which is the strongest form of design there is.

Least privilege, applied to modules

Give each module the narrowest access that lets it do its job. This is a security principle that pays for itself as a design principle: a module that can only see what it needs cannot accidentally depend on what it does not. Browser process isolation is the same principle at OS scale, with renderer processes deliberately denied filesystem and network authority so that a compromise of the parser cannot become a compromise of the machine.110

Composition over inheritance

The Gang of Four put the recommendation on page 20 of a book largely about inheritance, and the reasoning has held up better than almost anything else in the volume.39 Inheritance is white-box reuse: the subclass sees the parent's internals, is broken by changes to them, and is fixed at compile time. Composition is black-box reuse: the composing object sees only the published interface, can be rewired at runtime, and cannot be broken by a refactoring it cannot see.

The failure mode is the fragile base class. A parent method changed to call another of its own methods — an innocuous internal refactoring — silently changes the behaviour of every subclass that overrode the second method. Bloch's treatment gives the practical rule for the cases where inheritance is still right: design and document for inheritance explicitly, describing exactly which methods call which others, or else prohibit it.111 Most classes should be final, or sealed, or whatever the language offers, and the decision should be deliberate.

Modern platforms have largely settled this. React's documentation states plainly that across thousands of components its authors found no case where a component inheritance hierarchy was the right answer, and recommends containment through children and specialisation through props instead.112 Go has no inheritance at all, offering embedding and interfaces. Rust has traits with default methods and no data inheritance. The one place inheritance still earns its keep is where the supertype is a genuine behavioural contract and the subtype genuinely satisfies Liskov — abstract syntax tree nodes, algebraic shapes, framework extension points designed for it.31

The word "over" is doing real work in the slogan, though. It is a default, not a prohibition. A deep hierarchy of five levels is a design smell; a single abstract base with a documented template method and three implementations is often the clearest thing available.

What a pattern is

A pattern is a named solution to a recurring problem in a context, described together with the forces it resolves and the consequences of using it.3739 All four parts matter. Strip the context and you get a rule applied where it does not belong. Strip the consequences and you get a recipe, which is how patterns acquired their bad reputation in the 2000s.

The value is chiefly linguistic. Saying "put a facade over the vendor SDK and adapt their record to our domain type at the boundary" compresses a paragraph of explanation into a sentence both people understand, and it does so without either party having to open an editor. Alexander's claim that patterns form a language rather than a catalogue is the part software adopted least well and needed most.37

Reading the catalogue as a list of volatilities

The most useful re-reading of the twenty-three patterns is not by structure but by what each one lets vary. Every pattern is a bet that a particular thing will change and the things around it will not.

Read that way the taxonomy stops being arbitrary. Creational patterns vary what gets made and how. Structural patterns vary how things are wired and what surface they present. Behavioural patterns vary who does what, when. And the question to ask before reaching for any of them is the Parnas question: is the thing this pattern lets vary actually going to vary?2

The frame used for each entry below

Sections 15 to 17 walk the catalogue. Each entry gives the intent, what it lets vary, at least one named system where it is load-bearing, and the cost, because a pattern described without its cost is marketing. The cards collect the same information in the form used throughout this article: what is hidden, what is published, and what change the arrangement absorbs.

All five creational patterns exist because the expression new Thing() names a concrete class at the point of use, and that name is a dependency. They differ in what they substitute for it.

Singleton

Ensures one instance with a global access point.39 It is the one entry in the catalogue whose reputation has collapsed, and deservedly.

Structural patterns

Adapter

Observer

When patterns hurt

The catalogue's second decade produced a backlash, and much of it was earned. Four failure modes account for nearly all of it.

Patterns applied without their context. The template gives intent, motivation and applicability first, for a reason. Applying the structure without checking the forces produces a class diagram that resembles the book and a system that has gained indirection without gaining flexibility. Vlissides, one of the four authors, spent a whole follow-up book on exactly this misreading.132

Speculative generality. An interface with one implementation, a factory for a class that will never have a sibling, a strategy parameter always passed the same value. Fowler lists it among the smells to be refactored away, and the cure is deletion.52 This is where YAGNI is genuinely load-bearing: the abstraction costs comprehension every day and pays out only if the predicted variation occurs.103

Patterns as workarounds for missing language features. Norvig's 1996 observation still stands: many of the twenty-three patterns are invisible or much simpler in languages with first-class functions, multiple dispatch, or macros.113 Strategy is a function argument. Command is a closure. Iterator is a generator. Abstract Factory is a module. Recognising this prevents the ceremony from being imported into languages that never needed it.

Pattern density as a proxy for quality. Gabriel's critique cut deepest here: a pattern language in Alexander's sense was meant to serve the inhabitants of the thing built, and software adopted the form while dropping that purpose.13337 Counting patterns in a codebase measures vocabulary, not habitability.

The over-engineering test

Three questions, in order, before adding a layer of indirection:

  1. Name the change. What specific future event does this absorb? If the answer is "requirements might change", you have not named anything.
  2. Name the second implementation. Can you describe it concretely? An interface with one implementation and no plausible second is a rename.
  3. Price the retrofit. If the change arrives in a year, what does adding the boundary then actually cost? Sometimes it is one afternoon, and the honest answer is to wait.

The opposite error is real too, and gets less attention because its costs arrive later. A codebase with no seams cannot be tested without its database, cannot swap a vendor without a rewrite, and cannot be understood one piece at a time. Foote and Yoder's big ball of mud is the empirically dominant architecture precisely because it is what accumulates when nobody makes these calls at all — and their paper is unusually honest that it often ships.134 The discipline is not to avoid indirection; it is to be able to name what each piece of it hides.

Two of these deserve emphasis because they cover ground the original catalogue does not touch at all.

Failure is a design concern, not an operational one. Nygard's stability patterns are the clearest treatment: a timeout is a design decision, a bulkhead is a decomposition, and a circuit breaker is a state machine you put in the design because the alternative — unbounded retry into a degraded dependency — converts a slow dependency into a total outage.60 None of this is visible in a class diagram, and all of it belongs in the design review.

Erlang's OTP takes the same position further than anyone else. Armstrong's thesis argues that since software errors are inevitable, the design should isolate them: a process that fails simply dies, and a supervisor — a separate process whose only job is a restart policy — brings the subsystem back to a known state.139 The supervision tree is a decomposition by failure domain rather than by function, and it is the clearest available demonstration that fault containment is a modularity decision.

Machine learning systems have their own coupling problem. Sculley and colleagues named it precisely: models erode boundaries because the data they consume becomes an input to everything downstream, producing entanglement where changing anything changes everything, plus hidden feedback loops and undeclared consumers of model outputs.137 The design responses — versioned feature definitions, explicit contracts on training data, monitored input distributions — are all recognisably information hiding applied to a new kind of secret.

The same moves in unrelated domains

The strongest evidence that these ideas are structural rather than fashionable is that they were reinvented independently, under different regulatory and physical constraints, by people who were not reading each other's books.

Automotive

AUTOSAR's Classic Platform specifies a layered software architecture in which application software components communicate only through a runtime environment, which sits above basic software services, which sit above a microcontroller abstraction layer.140 The microcontroller abstraction layer exists for exactly Parnas's reason: the silicon is a decision that changes, and changing it should not touch application code. An engine control application compiled against the RTE does not know which microcontroller it runs on.

Avionics

ARINC 653 defines time and space partitioning: applications run in partitions with statically allocated memory and a fixed cyclic schedule, so a fault in one cannot consume the resources of another.141 This is the bulkhead pattern with a certification authority behind it. DO-178C then requires traceability from requirements through design to code and tests, at an assurance level determined by what happens if the software fails.142 It is the most rigorous mainstream answer to the question of what a design document is for.

Robotics

ROS structures a robot as a graph of nodes communicating over typed topics and services; ROS 2 rebuilt this on DDS with explicit quality-of-service policies for reliability, durability and deadline.122121 The design pays off in a specific way: a simulated laser scanner and a real one publish the same message type, so the entire perception and planning stack runs unchanged against either. That is information hiding used to make testing possible, which is one of its most under-advertised uses.

Healthcare and finance

HL7 FHIR defines a set of resources — Patient, Observation, Encounter — with a REST interface and explicit extension points, so that systems built decades apart can exchange data without agreeing on their internal models.143 FIX does the analogous job for trading, and both illustrate a design rule that in-house systems routinely ignore: the integration schema is a published contract with a compatibility policy, and it should not be your internal model.14498

Browsers, databases, version control

Chrome's multi-process architecture puts renderers in low-privilege sandboxed processes because a memory-safety bug in a parser is inevitable and the design goal is to bound its consequences.110 PostgreSQL exposes an index access method interface so that new index types can be added as extensions without patching the core.88 Git's object model is four content-addressed types — blob, tree, commit, tag — over which the entire porcelain is built; the storage format is a hidden decision, which is why the on-disk representation has changed repeatedly without breaking the commands.145

Kubernetes generalises its own control loop into an extension point: the operator pattern packages domain-specific operational knowledge as a controller over a custom resource, so that running a database becomes a reconciliation loop like any other.146 That is Open–Closed applied to a cluster.

Recording and evaluating a design

A design that exists only in the heads of the people who made it decays at the rate those people leave. But the artefact worth keeping is not the diagram; it is the rationale, which Perry and Wolf named as one of the three constituents of architecture and which is invariably the first thing lost.3

Architecture decision records

The lightest useful format, and the one that has actually spread. One short file per decision, in the repository, immutable once accepted: context, decision, status, consequences.147 The reason it works where wiki pages fail is that it is append-only and versioned alongside the code, so a superseding decision links back rather than overwriting, and a reader in three years can reconstruct why the obvious alternative was rejected.

Views, and knowing which one you need

Kruchten's 4+1 exists because a single diagram serves nobody: the logical view answers the domain question, the process view answers concurrency, the development view answers build and ownership, the physical view answers deployment, and scenarios tie them together.41 The C4 model is the modern lightweight descendant — context, containers, components, code — and its main contribution is telling you when to stop: most teams need the first two levels and never the fourth.148 ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010 formalises the underlying idea of viewpoints addressing stakeholder concerns.4

Evaluating against quality attributes

The failure of most design review is that it argues about elegance. The SEI's method does not: ATAM elicits quality-attribute scenarios from stakeholders, maps them to architectural decisions, and surfaces the risks, sensitivity points, and explicit tradeoffs.4342 ISO/IEC 25010 supplies the vocabulary of attributes — performance efficiency, compatibility, usability, reliability, security, maintainability, portability — and the value of the list is that it forces you to say which ones you are sacrificing.149

The scenario is the unit of work, and it must be concrete enough to disagree with: not "the system should be scalable" but "a tenfold increase in device registrations over one hour must not increase p99 ingest latency beyond 400 ms, and the design response is partitioning by device ID, which costs us cross-device queries."

Fitness functions

Anything you can state as a rule, you can assert in the build.61 Layer dependency rules checked by an import analysis. A ban on new cycles, enforced from the analyser in section 9. A cap on public API surface. Latency budgets asserted in integration tests. Coupling metrics tracked as a ratchet that may improve but not regress. The distinction from a linter is that a fitness function encodes a design intention rather than a style preference, and it fails the build.

A review checklist that is actually about design

  1. For each new module: what change does it absorb? If nobody can say, it is a rename.
  2. Which way do the dependencies point, and does anything in the domain import infrastructure?150
  3. What is now observable that we did not intend to promise?78
  4. What happens when the slowest dependency takes ten seconds?60
  5. Which invalid states are still representable?
  6. What did we duplicate, and is it knowledge or coincidence?94
  7. What would this look like to somebody who arrives in eighteen months and reads only the tests?

Design under change

Lehman's laws, derived from studying large systems over decades, are the empirical backdrop: a system in real use must continually change or become progressively less useful, and as it changes its complexity increases unless work is done explicitly to reduce it.151 The second clause is the important one. Complexity growth is the default; holding it flat is an activity somebody must be funded to do.

Technical debt, used correctly

Cunningham's metaphor was about deliberate, strategic borrowing: shipping a design you know is not right for what you have now learned, in order to get feedback, and then refactoring to incorporate what you learned.152 The metaphor has since been stretched to cover ordinary mess, which drains it of meaning. Kruchten, Nord and Ozkaya's reframing is more useful: debt is invisible-to-users work whose cost is paid as reduced velocity later, and it can be prudent or reckless, deliberate or inadvertent — four quadrants that call for four different responses.153

Refactoring as the mechanism

Refactoring is changing structure without changing behaviour, in small verified steps.52 The precondition is a test suite that pins behaviour, which is why legacy code — defined by Feathers as code without tests — requires the seam-finding techniques of his book before any structural work is safe.154155 At system scale the equivalent is the strangler fig: put a facade in front of the old system, route one capability at a time to new code behind it, and delete the old path when traffic reaches zero.118

Conway's law, and doing something about it

Conway's observation is not advice; it is a constraint that operates whether or not you believe in it.13 MacCormack, Baldwin and Rusnak tested the mirroring hypothesis on matched pairs of products and found the organisational structure legible in the coupling of the code.14 The practical consequence, sometimes called the inverse Conway manoeuvre, is that if you want a particular module structure you must arrange the teams to match it, because otherwise the org chart will win. Two teams sharing ownership of one module will produce a module with two personalities and a coordination meeting; one team owning three modules will produce three modules that quietly know about each other.

What to hold still

A final note on the economics. Not everything should be designed for change, because the flexibility has a carrying cost and most predictions are wrong.103 The question is always which axis. Interfaces published to people you cannot contact should be maximally conservative, because you cannot fix them later.80 Internal boundaries between components you deploy together can be loose, because the refactor is an afternoon. The mistake is applying the ceremony appropriate to the first case to the second, and then, having spent the budget, applying none of it to the first.

A worked decomposition

Take a concrete brief. A fleet of industrial sensors reports telemetry. The system must ingest readings, evaluate alert rules against them, and notify people through several channels. It must support two sensor vendors today and more later, and the operations team insists that a slow notification provider must never stop ingestion.

Step 1 — list the decisions likely to change

Not the components. The decisions. This is Parnas's actual instruction and it is the step that gets skipped.2

Each row above becomes a module whose secret it is. The low-volatility row becomes the domain type everything else is written against — the stable centre, which is what makes the arrangement work at all.

The domain declares the ports; adapters implement them; nothing in the domain imports anything vendor-specific.62150 The patterns fall out of the decisions rather than being chosen for their own sake:

This is the step that distinguishes design from decoration. Walk each row of the first table and count what moves.

The last row is not a failure of the design; it is the design working as intended. The stable centre was chosen precisely because changing it is rare, and the cost of changing it is the price paid for everything else being cheap. If that row turned out to fire monthly, the decomposition was built on a wrong prediction and should be redrawn — which is the honest thing to say about all of this. Design is a set of bets about what will change, and the discipline is in naming the bets so you can tell when you have lost one.
