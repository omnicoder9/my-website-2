The question that outlives the fashion

Ask three architects to describe a system and you will get three drawings. Ask them what the drawings forbid and you will find out whether any of them has an architecture at all.

Software architecture has an unusually large vocabulary for a discipline with an unusually small number of underlying moves. The catalogue below runs to sixteen named styles. Strip away the diagrams and the branded terminology and each one turns out to be a rule of the same shape: here is a boundary; here is which direction dependencies are permitted to cross it; here is what happens to anyone who tries to cross it the other way. Hexagonal architecture and onion architecture and clean architecture are three drawings of one rule. Microservices and service-oriented architecture agree about the boundary and disagree about the pipe. A modular monolith and a microservice fleet can enforce identical rules and differ only in whether violating them costs a compile error or a pager alert.

That framing is not a rhetorical simplification. It is the operational definition the field converged on after thirty years of argument. Perry and Wolf, in the 1992 paper that gave the subject its name and its research agenda, defined architecture as elements, form, and rationale — where form is precisely the set of constraints on how elements may relate.1 Garlan and Shaw catalogued the recurring shapes a year later and observed that what distinguishes a style is its vocabulary of components and connectors together with the constraints on their composition.2 The current international standard for architecture description says the same thing in procurement language: an architecture is fundamental concepts or properties of a system embodied in its elements, relationships, and the principles of its design and evolution.3 Elements, relationships, principles. Boxes, arrows, and the rules about arrows.

The practical value of holding the definition that tightly is that it makes the styles comparable. You can ask of any of them the same five questions — where is the seam, which way do the arrows point, what does that buy, what does it cost, and how will you know when you have chosen wrong — and get answers that sit side by side on a page. That is the structure of this article. Each style gets a card. The cards are collected at the end into a single ledger, and the ledger is the thing worth keeping.

One more framing note before the history. Martin Fowler's much-quoted formulation is that architecture is the set of decisions you wish you could get right early because they are so hard to change later.4 That is a good heuristic and a bad definition, because it defines architecture by regret. The better version, and the one that has aged well, is that architecture is the set of decisions that constrain everyone else's decisions. A boundary is a promise made on behalf of engineers who have not been hired yet. Most of the failures catalogued later in this piece are failures to keep that promise cheaply enough that anyone bothers.

How the field got its vocabulary

The intellectual content of software architecture was settled remarkably early, and almost everything since has been a response to changes in the cost of a boundary rather than to new ideas about boundaries.

The founding artefact is Dijkstra's 1968 report on the THE multiprogramming system, which described an operating system built as a strict hierarchy of levels, each providing an abstract machine to the level above and depending on nothing above itself.5 Dijkstra's motivation was not elegance but proof: he wanted to verify the system layer by layer, which is possible only if the dependency graph is acyclic and directed. Layered architecture, hexagonal architecture, and every modern "dependency rule" descend from that report.

The same year, Melvin Conway published the observation that organisations produce designs which copy their own communication structures.6 It was a throwaway line in a paper about committee design that has since been tested empirically across large codebases; the "mirroring hypothesis" holds well enough that organisational structure predicts modular structure across a range of products and firms.7 Conway's law is the reason a section on team topology belongs in an architecture article rather than a management one, and it is why the modern practice of deliberately reshaping teams to induce a desired architecture — the inverse Conway manoeuvre — is a legitimate technical intervention.8

In 1972 David Parnas supplied the criterion that everything else hangs from. Modules should be decomposed around the decisions likely to change, with each module hiding one such decision behind an interface that reveals as little as possible.9 The paper's famous demonstration compares a KWIC index built by decomposing along the steps of the algorithm against one built by hiding representation choices, and shows that the second survives changes that shatter the first. Parnas returned to the theme repeatedly — designing for extension and contraction,10 and later the "uses" hierarchy that makes subsets of a system independently buildable.11 Information hiding is not an object-oriented idea, a microservice idea, or a domain-driven idea. It is the idea, and the rest are packaging.

Brooks contributed the notion that conceptual integrity is the most important single attribute of a system design, and that it is best achieved by concentrating design authority rather than distributing it.12 DeRemer and Kron gave the field the distinction between programming-in-the-small and programming-in-the-large, arguing that the language for wiring modules together is a different language from the one for writing them.13 Lehman's laws established that a program in use must be continually adapted or it becomes progressively less satisfactory, which is the empirical basis for treating architecture as something that evolves rather than something delivered.14

The 1990s formalised all of this. Perry and Wolf proposed architecture as a research field;1 Garlan and Shaw catalogued styles;2,15 Kruchten pointed out that no single diagram serves everyone and proposed the 4+1 view model — logical, process, development, physical, plus scenarios that tie them together;16 the Siemens group published the first serious pattern catalogue;17 and the Software Engineering Institute began turning architecture into an evaluable artefact with the Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method.18,19 Bass, Clements, and Kazman's textbook, now in its fourth edition, remains the canonical statement of the discipline as taught.20

What changed after 2000 was economics, not theory. Cheap virtualisation, then containers, then managed orchestration, drove the marginal cost of an additional deployment unit down by orders of magnitude. Boundaries that had been absurd in 1996 — one process per business capability, each independently released — became merely expensive, and then routine. Every architectural fashion of the last twenty years is legible as a repricing of the same menu. The ideas arrive early; the hardware and the org charts arrive later.

The load-bearing vocabulary

A handful of terms do most of the work. They are worth stating precisely because the styles are defined in terms of them.

Coupling is the amount one element must know about another to function. It has kinds, and the kinds behave very differently. Compile-time coupling means a change to B forces a rebuild of A. Deployment coupling means a change to B forces a redeploy of A. Runtime coupling means an outage in B causes a failure in A. Semantic coupling means a change in what B means breaks A even though every signature still type-checks. Splitting a monolith into services removes the first two, converts the third into a distributed systems problem, and does nothing at all about the fourth — which is why the "distributed monolith" is a real and common outcome rather than a rhetorical warning.21

Cohesion is the degree to which the things inside a boundary change together. High cohesion and low coupling are not two goals; they are the same goal measured from opposite sides of the line. The reliable test is temporal: look at the commit history and ask which files keep appearing in the same change. If the answer routinely spans three modules, the boundary is drawn in the wrong place, whatever the diagram says.

Connector is Perry and Wolf's underrated contribution: the relationship between components is itself a first-class design element with its own properties.1 A function call, a queue, a shared database table, an HTTP request, and a nightly file drop are all connectors, and they differ in latency, failure semantics, ordering guarantees, and who is blamed at 3 a.m. Most architectural mistakes are connector mistakes wearing component clothing.

Quality attributes — availability, latency, modifiability, testability, security, operability — are the requirements architecture actually serves; the ISO product-quality model enumerates a standard set.22 The discipline's central insight is that these trade off against each other and cannot be simultaneously maximised, which is why architecture evaluation methods focus on identifying the trade-off points rather than scoring a design out of ten.18 An architecturally significant requirement is one whose satisfaction constrains structure. "Support 10,000 concurrent users" usually is; "use PostgreSQL" usually is not, though teams frequently swap the two.

Fitness function is the modern operational addition: an automated, objective check that some architectural characteristic still holds, run continuously in the same pipeline as the tests.23 This is what turns a dependency rule from a review comment into a property of the build. A worked example appears later in this article.

Architecture decision record is the corresponding documentation practice — a short, immutable, dated note capturing a decision, its context, and its consequences, stored beside the code.24 Its value is not the decision but the rationale, which is exactly what Perry and Wolf identified as the third component of architecture and exactly what evaporates when the people who made the call leave.1

Reading the cards

Each style below is summarised in a card with five fields. Seam names what the boundary is cut along — a technical concern, a business capability, a data format, a client. Arrow states the dependency rule the style imposes, in the notation of Figure 1. Buys and Costs are the trade, stated in terms of the qualities above rather than in adjectives. Tell is the diagnostic symptom that appears when the style has been adopted in a context that does not support it — the thing you would notice six months in, before anyone admits there is a problem.

The cards are deliberately terse and deliberately opinionated. Everything defensible about a style is in the prose; the card is what you would write on a whiteboard when someone asks why the codebase looks like this.

The monolith

A monolith is a system deployed as a single unit. That is the whole definition, and it is worth defending against the two things it is routinely confused with: a monolith is not necessarily a big ball of mud, and it is not necessarily a single codebase written by people who had not heard of modules. Stack Overflow served a top-100 website for years from a handful of web servers running one ASP.NET application against one SQL Server, a configuration its own engineers documented in detail precisely because it contradicted the prevailing assumption that scale requires fragmentation.25 Basecamp's argument for the "majestic monolith" is that a small team's throughput is dominated by the cost of coordination, and a single deployable removes almost all of it.26

What the monolith buys is that every arrow in Figure 1 is the first one. Calls are function calls: microseconds, no partial failure, no serialisation, no version skew. A transaction spans whatever it needs to span, because there is one database and one connection. Refactoring across a boundary is a compiler-assisted rename rather than a coordinated release of two teams. Debugging is a stack trace instead of a distributed trace. These are not small advantages; they are the advantages that every distributed architecture spends its budget trying to buy back with tooling.27,28

What it costs is that nothing enforces the boundaries. Every module can reach into every other module's internals because the language permits it and the deadline encourages it, and Foote and Yoder's "big ball of mud" paper remains the definitive anthropology of what happens next: structure erodes not through incompetence but through a long sequence of individually reasonable expedients.29 The failure is gradual and then sudden. Test suites slow until nobody runs them locally. A change to shipping breaks billing for reasons no one can reconstruct. Shopify's engineers described exactly this state in a codebase of some 2.8 million lines of Ruby: making an innocuous change could trigger a cascade of unrelated test failures, and simply understanding enough context to make a small change had become expensive.30

The second cost is scaling granularity. A monolith scales as a unit, so the memory-hungry report generator and the CPU-hungry image resizer are provisioned together at the maximum of both. And the third is organisational: a single deployment pipeline is a single queue, and queues have contention. Past a few dozen engineers committing to one artefact, release coordination begins to consume real capacity.

None of that argues for starting elsewhere. Fowler's "monolith first" position — that the successful microservice architectures he had seen almost all began as monoliths that were subsequently split, and the failures almost all began as microservices — remains the best-supported default, because the boundaries you would need to know in advance are precisely the thing a new product does not yet know.31,32

──Monolith
SeamNone enforced. The deployment unit is the only real boundary.
ArrowAnything may call anything, in-process. All four arrow types collapse into the first.
BuysSimplicity, atomic transactions, cheap refactoring, one trace, one deploy, minimal operational surface.
CostsNo structural pressure against entanglement; coarse scaling; a shared release queue.
TellA one-line change requires understanding four unrelated subsystems, and the test suite is the slowest thing in the team's day.

The modular monolith

The modular monolith keeps the single deployment unit and adds enforced internal boundaries. It is the most under-rated position on the menu, largely because it lacks a conference circuit.

Enforcement is the entire point, and it must be mechanical. Shopify's programme — internally called componentization — reorganised code from technical groupings (models, views, controllers) into business domains, then built tooling that raised runtime errors when a component reached into another component's internals or used an undeclared dependency.30,33 Their published reasoning is worth quoting in structure if not in words: microservices were rejected not because they are bad but because the team judged the distribution tax unnecessary for the problem they actually had, which was coupling, not scale.34 Kirsten Westeinde's framing of the "design payoff line" — the point at which poor structure starts costing more than it saves — is a usefully honest way to time the investment.34

Mechanisms differ by ecosystem and all of them exist: Java module declarations and OSGi bundles;35,36 .NET assemblies with internal visibility; Ruby packaging tools of the kind Shopify built; Python import linters; and, in any language, a build-time check on the import graph of the sort demonstrated later in this article. The common thread is that the boundary must fail a build, not a code review, because code review does not scale and reviewers rotate.

The trade is straightforward. You get information hiding, independent reasoning, a genuine option on later extraction — a module with a clean interface and its own tables can be lifted into a service in a way that a tangled one cannot — and you keep in-process calls, single-transaction consistency, and one pipeline. What you do not get is independent deployment, independent scaling, or technology heterogeneity. If those are the constraints that actually bind, the modular monolith is a way station rather than a destination; if they are not, it is the destination, and treating it as a way station is how organisations acquire distributed systems they cannot afford to operate.

The strategic argument is Baldwin and Clark's: modularity creates real options.37 Sullivan and colleagues applied that lens directly to Parnas's KWIC example and showed that information hiding has calculable option value — the right, but not the obligation, to substitute an implementation later.38 A modular monolith is a portfolio of such options bought at low cost. Microservices exercise the options immediately, whether or not the price has moved in your favour.

──Modular monolith
SeamBusiness capability, enforced at build or load time inside one deployable.
ArrowExplicit, checked module graph; cross-module calls only through published interfaces.
BuysInformation hiding without the distribution tax; atomic transactions; a real option on later extraction.
CostsNo independent deploy or scale; enforcement needs tooling and sustained will; one runtime, one language.
TellModules exist in the folder structure but the dependency checker is disabled "temporarily," and has been for a year.

Client–server

Client–server is the oldest distributed style still in daily use and the one most often mistaken for a mere implementation detail. Its defining move is to place a boundary between the party that initiates work and the party that holds the shared resource, then run a request–response protocol across it. Tanenbaum and van Steen's treatment remains the standard reference for the family and its variants.39

The architectural content lies in what the split forces you to confront. Birrell and Nelson's 1984 paper on remote procedure calls established the technique of making a network call look like a local one, and did so with unusual candour about where the illusion breaks: failure modes, latency, and the semantics of "exactly once."40 A decade later, Waldo and colleagues wrote the definitive rebuttal to taking that illusion seriously — local and remote objects differ irreducibly in latency, memory access, partial failure, and concurrency, and any design that papers over the difference will fail in production in one of those four ways.41 That paper is thirty years old and remains the single most useful thing to hand an engineer proposing to "just make it a service call."

The tier vocabulary layered on top is worth disentangling from the layer vocabulary. A tier is a deployment boundary you can point at in a rack or a cloud console; a layer is a rule about which code may call which. A system can have five layers in one tier, and frequently should. Two-tier fat clients gave way to three-tier designs in the 1990s largely because business logic on thousands of desktops could not be patched, and thence to the n-tier arrangements catalogued in Microsoft's application architecture guide and its successors.42,43

The Web is the largest client–server system ever built, and Roy Fielding's dissertation is the definitive analysis of why its particular constraints — client–server, statelessness, cacheability, uniform interface, layered system, optional code-on-demand — produce the properties they do.44,45 REST is genuinely an architectural style in Garlan and Shaw's sense: a named set of constraints, each of which buys a specific property at a specific cost. Statelessness, for instance, buys horizontal scalability and visibility and costs per-request payload size. It is the best worked example in the literature of deriving properties from constraints rather than asserting them.

──Client–server
SeamBetween the initiator of work and the custodian of the shared resource; the seam is a network hop.
ArrowClient depends on server's interface; the server knows nothing of the client. Strictly a peer/request arrow.
BuysCentralised control of shared state, independent client evolution, security enforcement at one perimeter.
CostsLatency, partial failure, and versioning across an interface you cannot change unilaterally.
TellThe client has grown a second copy of the business rules "for responsiveness," and the two copies have diverged.

Layered architecture

The layered style partitions code by technical concern and permits calls in one direction only. Presentation calls application, application calls domain, domain calls persistence. Dijkstra's THE system is the ancestor;5 the enterprise formulation is Fowler's, whose Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture supplies most of the components that populate the layers — service layer, domain model, data mapper, repository, unit of work.46

Two distinctions carry the weight. First, closed versus open layers. A closed layer must be traversed: presentation may not reach past application to the domain. This is what makes a layer substitutable, since the layer below has exactly one caller pattern to satisfy. An open layer may be bypassed, which is faster to write and destroys the substitutability the style exists to provide. Most codebases claiming to be layered have quietly opened a layer or two, and the honest response is to document it as a decision rather than pretend.47

Second, the architecture sinkhole: a request that passes through every layer and is transformed by none of them. A controller that calls a service that calls a repository that calls a table, all five signatures nearly identical, is paying full indirection price for zero abstraction. Richards and Ford's rule of thumb is that if the majority of requests look like that, the layers are ceremony.47 A minority is fine and expected; simple reads should be simple.

Layering's real weakness is the one Parnas identified in 1972 without naming it: technical layers group code by how it works, not by what changes together.9 Adding a field to an order touches the controller, the DTO, the service, the entity, the mapper, and the schema — six files in five layers, all for one business change. High coupling along the axis of change, low cohesion within each layer. That observation is the seed of everything in the next several sections: hexagonal, onion, and clean architecture all keep the idea of layers and rotate the axis so that the innermost layer is a business concept rather than a technical one.

Where layering remains excellent is where the layers genuinely differ in rate of change and in required expertise: operating system kernels, network stacks, compiler front-ends and back-ends, graphics pipelines. The OSI model's persistence as a teaching device, despite its inaccuracy as a description of TCP/IP, reflects how natural the mental model is when the abstraction levels are real.

──Layered
SeamTechnical concern: presentation, application, domain, persistence.
ArrowStrictly downward; closed layers may not be skipped. All arrows are calls within one process.
BuysSubstitutability of each level, an obvious place for every kind of code, a shallow learning curve for new hires.
CostsGrouping by mechanism rather than by change; sinkhole indirection; a business change touches every layer.
TellAdding one field to one concept produces a six-file pull request and everybody considers that normal.

Pipes and filters

The pipes-and-filters style decomposes a computation into independent transformers connected by a uniform data channel. Each filter reads from its input, transforms, writes to its output, and knows nothing about its neighbours except the shape of the data.

The canonical instance is the Unix pipeline, and its origin story is unusually well documented: Doug McIlroy had been agitating since 1964 for a way to screw programs together like garden hose, and the shell pipe operator arrived in Research Unix in 1973, described in the 1974 Communications of the ACM paper that introduced the system to the world.48,49,50 The consequence was a toolkit whose combinatorial power vastly exceeded its component count, because the coupling was to a format — lines of text — rather than to any particular program.

The style recurs wherever a medium is stable enough to be a contract:

  • Compilers. LLVM's design is a pass pipeline over a single intermediate representation; passes are separately compiled, reorderable, and independently testable precisely because they all speak IR.51 The entire modern language ecosystem — Rust, Swift, Julia, Clang — is a consequence of that one contract being good enough to share.
  • Media. GStreamer builds arbitrary audio and video processing graphs from elements connected by pads, with capability negotiation deciding at link time whether two elements can agree on a format.52 The same shape appears in FFmpeg's filter graphs and in every digital audio workstation's effects chain.
  • Scientific computing. Nextflow expresses bioinformatics workflows as processes connected by channels, which is what makes a genomics analysis portable across a laptop, a cluster, and a cloud without editing the science.53
  • Stream processing. The Dataflow model generalises the style to unbounded, out-of-order data, adding windowing and triggers to say when a filter's output is complete enough to emit — the hard part that batch pipelines get to ignore.54
  • Networking and middleware. Servlet filter chains, HTTP middleware stacks, and service-mesh sidecar chains are all pipes and filters with a request as the medium.

What the style buys is exceptional recombination and near-free parallelism: filters with no shared state can run concurrently, and pipeline stages naturally pipeline. What it costs is expressiveness. Anything the medium cannot carry, the pipeline cannot express, which is why Unix text pipelines struggle with structured data and why every generation reinvents a richer channel format. Error handling is the other weak point: a failure four stages in has to be represented in the medium or it escapes the architecture entirely, and the number of shell pipelines that silently swallow a non-zero exit code is difficult to overstate. Interactive and stateful workloads fit badly; the style wants a flow, not a conversation.

──Pipes and filters
SeamAlong the stages of a transformation; the contract is the data format, not the component.
ArrowStrictly forward, one stage to the next. No stage names any other stage.
BuysRecombination without recompilation, natural concurrency, independent testing of each stage.
CostsOnly what the medium can express; awkward error propagation; poor fit for interactive or stateful work.
TellHalf the "filters" have grown a side channel — a shared file, a database, a global — to pass what the pipe cannot.

Plugin architecture

A plugin architecture (Richards and Ford call it microkernel) splits a system into a core that owns lifecycle and a published extension contract, plus an open-ended set of independently written components that implement it.47 The defining property is negative: the core does not know the names of its plugins. Discovery happens through a registry, a manifest, a directory scan, or a service loader — never through an import.

The pattern is old and load-bearing. Emacs was extensible-by-design in 1981, with the editor's own behaviour written in the extension language it exposed, which is why an editor from the Carter administration still has an ecosystem.55 Eclipse adopted OSGi to get dynamic module lifecycle — bundles that can be installed, started, updated, and stopped at runtime, with versioned import and export of packages — and the resulting IDE platform hosted an entire tools industry.36,35 Visual Studio Code took the harder lesson from Eclipse's plugin-crash problem and runs extensions in a separate process, the extension host, so that a badly behaved extension cannot block the editor's UI thread.56 That isolation decision is architectural, not defensive engineering: it converts a call arrow into a peer arrow deliberately, accepting serialisation cost to buy fault containment.

The most consequential contemporary example is Kubernetes, which is best understood not as a container platform but as a plugin architecture with three published contracts and almost no built-in implementations. The Container Runtime Interface lets the kubelet drive any conforming runtime without recompilation;57 the Container Storage Interface lets storage vendors ship drivers without touching core code;58 the Container Network Interface does the same for networking.59 Add custom resource definitions and admission webhooks and the extension surface reaches the control plane's own API. The strategic effect is that vendors compete inside the ecosystem rather than forking it.

The same shape shows up in databases (PostgreSQL extensions, which can add types, operators, index methods, and even foreign data wrappers),60 in web servers (nginx and Apache modules), in CI (Jenkins plugins), in infrastructure tooling (Terraform providers), and in content management, where WordPress's hook system is arguably the most widely deployed plugin architecture on earth.

The costs are real and specific. Contract design is genuinely hard and effectively permanent: a published extension point is a public API with an ecosystem attached, and the deprecation cost is borne by strangers. Versioning across a plugin boundary is a distributed-systems problem in disguise. Security is inverted — you are executing third-party code inside your process unless you have paid for isolation. Debugging spans code you did not write and cannot read. And there is a governance trap: a core that keeps absorbing popular plugins to "make things easier" gradually stops being a core, while a core that absorbs nothing leaves every user assembling a bespoke distribution.

SeamBetween an invariant core and variable capability; the seam is a published extension contract.
ArrowCore → contract only; plugins → contract inward. The core never names a plugin.
BuysExtension without modification, third-party ecosystems, independent release of capability.
CostsContract design is permanent; version skew; third-party code inside your trust and failure boundary.
TellThe core has started shipping special cases for its three most popular plugins.

The inward-pointing family

Three styles — hexagonal, onion, and clean — are usually presented as competitors and are better understood as one rule with three sets of packaging. The rule is the dependency inversion principle applied at architectural scale: high-level policy must not depend on low-level detail; both should depend on abstractions owned by the policy.61,62 Rotate the layered stack until the domain is at the centre, point every source-code dependency inward, and the database becomes a plugin.

The intellectual move is worth stating carefully, because it is subtle. In a layered system, the domain calls the repository, so the domain depends on persistence. In an inward-pointing system, the domain defines the repository interface, and the persistence module implements it. Control flow is unchanged — at runtime the domain still ends up executing SQL. What changes is the direction of the source dependency, and therefore who can be compiled, tested, and reasoned about without the other. That is the second arrow in Figure 1, and it is the only genuinely non-obvious idea in this entire article.

Hexagonal architecture (ports and adapters)

Alistair Cockburn's formulation, published in 2005 and refined over two decades, states the intent as: allow an application to be equally driven by users, programs, automated tests, or batch scripts, and to be developed and tested in isolation from its eventual runtime devices and databases.63,64 The hexagon is deliberately not a meaningful shape — Cockburn chose it to avoid the top-down connotations of a layer diagram and to leave room to draw several ports, and has said as much. There are exactly two kinds of thing outside the application: driving actors that initiate (a UI, a test harness, a message consumer) and driven actors that the application uses (a database, a mail gateway, a payment provider).

A port is an interface expressed in the application's own vocabulary — OrderRepository, NotificationSender — not the technology's. An adapter translates between a port and a specific technology. The test of a good port is that its signature contains no vendor nouns: if the interface mentions rows, topics, or status codes, the abstraction has leaked and substitutability is already gone.

The most valuable property in practice is testability. With ports defined by the application, the entire domain and use-case layer can be exercised with in-memory adapters at unit-test speed, and the integration tests that require a real database shrink to the adapters themselves. Netflix's engineering account of adopting the style for a data-heavy service emphasises exactly this pairing — the ability to swap the persistence technology and to test business logic without it.65

──Hexagonal (ports and adapters)
SeamAround the application; everything outside is either a driving or a driven actor.
ArrowInward. The application defines ports in its own vocabulary; adapters depend on the application.
BuysTechnology substitutability, fast isolated tests, a domain that compiles without a database.
CostsIndirection for every external interaction; interface proliferation; disciplined naming required forever.
TellPorts are named after the technology they front — KafkaPort, JdbcPort — so nothing is actually substitutable.

Onion architecture

Jeffrey Palermo's 2008 formulation adds concentric structure to the same rule: a domain model at the centre, domain services around it, application services around those, and infrastructure, UI, and tests in the outermost ring, with all couplings pointing toward the centre.66 Palermo's motivating complaint was specific and worth remembering: in the conventional layered enterprise application of the day, every layer ultimately depended on the data-access layer, which made the database the most stable thing in the system and the business rules the least.

Compared with hexagonal, onion prescribes more: it tells you there is a distinction between domain services and application services, and it puts the object-relational mapping firmly outside the model. That is more guidance and therefore more opportunity to argue about ring membership. The practical difference in a finished codebase is usually a matter of package naming.

──Onion
SeamConcentric rings by stability: domain model, domain services, application services, infrastructure.
ArrowInward, always. The outer rings may name the inner; never the reverse.
BuysA domain model with no persistence concepts in it; explicit stability gradient; enforceable by package rules.
CostsMore prescribed structure to argue about; ring membership disputes; ceremony in small systems.
TellA "domain" ring full of ORM annotations, which everyone agrees is temporary.

Clean architecture

Robert C. Martin's synthesis, first blogged in 2012 and expanded into a book in 2017, unifies hexagonal, onion, and several contemporaries under a single stated dependency rule: source code dependencies must point only inward, and nothing in an inner circle may know anything at all about an outer circle.67,68 The rings are entities, use cases, interface adapters, and frameworks and drivers. The book's sharpest formulation is that the web, the database, and the framework are all details — decisions that a good architecture defers and that a bad one enshrines in the type signatures of the business rules.

Clean architecture's distinctive contribution is the boundary-crossing discipline: when control flow needs to cross a circle outward, you invert it with an interface owned by the inner circle, and you pass simple data structures across the boundary rather than entities or ORM objects, so that the outer circle cannot become coupled to the inner circle's shape. Microsoft's .NET guidance describes the same arrangement as its recommended structure for non-trivial applications.43

The style attracts more criticism than the other two, and some of it lands. The mapping cost is real: a single business operation may require a request DTO, an input port, an interactor, an output port, a response model, and a view model, and in a CRUD-dominated application that is a lot of typing to move a string. The "defer the database decision" argument is weaker in practice than in principle, since choosing a relational store versus an event log versus a document store has consequences that reach into the domain model whatever the interfaces say. The honest position is that clean architecture is an investment whose return depends on how much genuine business logic exists and how long the system will live. Where there is little logic and a short horizon, it is overhead; where there is a decade of rules and three generations of infrastructure ahead, it is the cheapest thing in the budget.

Making the arrow executable

A dependency rule that lives in a wiki is a suggestion. The useful form is a fitness function: an automated check in the same pipeline as the tests, failing the build when the rule is violated.23 Mature tools exist for this — ArchUnit in the JVM world, NetArchTest in .NET, import-linter in Python, dependency-cruiser in JavaScript — but the mechanism is simple enough to build in an afternoon, and building it once is the fastest way to understand what the rule actually asserts.

The script below is dependency-free Python. It walks a package, extracts the import graph with the ast module, assigns each module to a ring by prefix, reports any import that points outward, and separately reports cycles using Tarjan's algorithm — because a dependency rule with a cycle in it is not a hierarchy at all, which was Dijkstra's original point.5 Note the ALLOWED_EXCEPTIONS set: escape hatches should be explicit, enumerated, and dated, never implicit.

archcheck.pystdlib only · python 3.10+ · exit 1 on violation
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""archcheck - a dependency-direction fitness function in the standard library.

Reads a package tree, builds the import graph with `ast`, assigns every module to
a layer by prefix, and fails the build if an arrow points the wrong way or if the
modules form a cycle. This is the executable form of "the domain may not name the
infrastructure": a rule that a CI job can enforce instead of a review comment.

    python3 archcheck.py <package-root> [--json]

Exit status 0 = clean, 1 = violations found.
"""
from __future__ import annotations

import ast
import json
import os
import sys
from collections import defaultdict

# --- the architecture, as data ------------------------------------------------
# Rings are ordered innermost-first. A module may import from its own ring and
# from any ring strictly inside it. Nothing may import outward. This is the
# dependency rule shared by hexagonal, onion and clean architecture.

RINGS = ["domain", "application", "adapters", "infrastructure"]

PREFIX_TO_RING = {
    "domain": "domain",
    "app": "application",
    "usecases": "application",
    "adapters": "adapters",
    "web": "adapters",
    "cli": "adapters",
    "infra": "infrastructure",
    "db": "infrastructure",
}

# Escape hatches are explicit, dated and reviewed - never implicit.
ALLOWED_EXCEPTIONS: set[tuple[str, str]] = {
    # ("domain.money", "infra.decimal_compat"),  # expires 2026-12-01, ticket ARCH-118
}


def ring_of(module: str) -> str | None:
    head = module.split(".", 1)[0]
    return PREFIX_TO_RING.get(head)


def module_name(root: str, path: str) -> str:
    rel = os.path.relpath(path, root)
    rel = rel[:-3] if rel.endswith(".py") else rel
    parts = [p for p in rel.split(os.sep) if p != "__init__"]
    return ".".join(parts)


def imports_of(path: str, this_module: str) -> set[str]:
    """Every module this file names, resolving `from . import x` relatively."""
    with open(path, "r", encoding="utf-8") as fh:
        tree = ast.parse(fh.read(), filename=path)
    found: set[str] = set()
    pkg = this_module.rsplit(".", 1)[0] if "." in this_module else ""
    for node in ast.walk(tree):
        if isinstance(node, ast.Import):
            for a in node.names:
                found.add(a.name)
        elif isinstance(node, ast.ImportFrom):
            if node.level:                      # relative import
                base = pkg.split(".")
                base = base[: len(base) - (node.level - 1)] if node.level > 1 else base
                target = ".".join([p for p in base if p] + ([node.module] if node.module else []))
            else:
                target = node.module or ""
            if target:
                found.add(target)
    return found


def scan(root: str) -> dict[str, set[str]]:
    graph: dict[str, set[str]] = {}
    for dirpath, _dirs, files in os.walk(root):
        for f in files:
            if not f.endswith(".py"):
                continue
            path = os.path.join(dirpath, f)
            mod = module_name(root, path)
            graph[mod] = {i for i in imports_of(path, mod) if ring_of(i) is not None}
    return graph


def direction_violations(graph: dict[str, set[str]]) -> list[dict]:
    idx = {r: i for i, r in enumerate(RINGS)}
    out = []
    for src, targets in sorted(graph.items()):
        sr = ring_of(src)
        if sr is None:
            continue
        for dst in sorted(targets):
            dr = ring_of(dst)
            if dr is None or (src, dst) in ALLOWED_EXCEPTIONS:
                continue
            if idx[dr] > idx[sr]:
                out.append({"kind": "outward-dependency", "from": src, "from_ring": sr,
                            "to": dst, "to_ring": dr})
    return out


def cycles(graph: dict[str, set[str]]) -> list[list[str]]:
    """Tarjan's strongly connected components; any SCC of size > 1 is a cycle."""
    index: dict[str, int] = {}
    low: dict[str, int] = {}
    stack: list[str] = []
    on: set[str] = set()
    found: list[list[str]] = []
    counter = [0]

    def strong(v: str) -> None:
        index[v] = low[v] = counter[0]
        counter[0] += 1
        stack.append(v)
        on.add(v)
        for w in graph.get(v, ()):
            if w not in graph:
                continue
            if w not in index:
                strong(w)
                low[v] = min(low[v], low[w])
            elif w in on:
                low[v] = min(low[v], index[w])
        if low[v] == index[v]:
            comp = []
            while True:
                w = stack.pop()
                on.discard(w)
                comp.append(w)
                if w == v:
                    break
            if len(comp) > 1:
                found.append(sorted(comp))

    for v in sorted(graph):
        if v not in index:
            strong(v)
    return found


def main(argv: list[str]) -> int:
    if len(argv) < 2:
        print(__doc__)
        return 2
    root = argv[1]
    as_json = "--json" in argv
    graph = scan(root)
    viol = direction_violations(graph)
    cyc = cycles(graph)

    if as_json:
        print(json.dumps({"modules": len(graph), "violations": viol, "cycles": cyc}, indent=2))
    else:
        by_ring: dict[str, int] = defaultdict(int)
        for m in graph:
            by_ring[ring_of(m) or "?"] += 1
        print("archcheck: %d modules  %s" % (
            len(graph), "  ".join("%s=%d" % (r, by_ring[r]) for r in RINGS)))
        for v in viol:
            print("  OUTWARD  %-28s (%s)  ->  %-24s (%s)"
                  % (v["from"], v["from_ring"], v["to"], v["to_ring"]))
        for c in cyc:
            print("  CYCLE    %s" % " -> ".join(c + [c[0]]))
        print("archcheck: %s" % ("PASS" if not viol and not cyc else
                                 "FAIL (%d outward, %d cyclic)" % (len(viol), len(cyc))))
    return 0 if not viol and not cyc else 1


if __name__ == "__main__":
    raise SystemExit(main(sys.argv))

Two features of this are worth transplanting into real pipelines regardless of language. First, the check is on the graph, not on file locations, so moving a file cannot silently satisfy it. Second, cycles are reported separately from direction violations, because they are a different disease: an outward arrow is a design mistake, while a cycle is a statement that two modules are in fact one module and should be merged or split for real.

Service-oriented architecture

SOA decomposes an enterprise into network-addressable services aligned to business functions, each with a published contract, discoverable and composable into higher-level processes. The OASIS reference model defines it as a paradigm for organising and utilising distributed capabilities that may be under the control of different ownership domains — and that last clause is the part that distinguishes SOA from everything before it.69 SOA's problem statement was not decomposition of one application; it was integration across an enterprise where the accounting system, the CRM, and the warehouse system were bought from three vendors in three decades.

Its technical ancestry runs through CORBA and the distributed object movement of the early 1990s, which established interface definition languages, brokers, and location transparency,70 and through the middleware category that Bernstein characterised as a model for distributed system services.71 The 2000s canon standardised on XML: SOAP for the envelope,72 WSDL for the contract,73 and a large family of WS-* specifications for security, transactions, and reliable messaging. Thomas Erl's textbooks defined the orthodox principles — standardised contracts, loose coupling, abstraction, reusability, autonomy, statelessness, discoverability, composability.74

The failure mode that damaged the term's reputation was the enterprise service bus. Given a central component responsible for routing, transformation, and orchestration, organisations put business logic in it, because that is where the integration team sat. The bus then became a shared deployment unit, a shared release queue, and a single point of failure, which is to say a monolith with worse latency and a licence fee. This is a Conway's law outcome rather than a technology failure: a central integration team produces central integration logic.6,7

The counter-example is instructive. Amazon executed the same decomposition without the bus, and Werner Vogels described the result publicly in 2006 in terms that are startlingly close to what would be called microservices eight years later: services owning their own data with no direct database access from outside, teams responsible for their service end to end, and the operating slogan "you build it, you run it."75 The published architecture of Dynamo two years later shows what service autonomy demands of the storage layer — per-service availability and consistency choices rather than one enterprise database standard.76 Revisiting the conversation fourteen years on, Vogels connected that early structure directly to the organisational patterns that made it survivable.77

SOA is not obsolete. Where an organisation genuinely spans ownership domains — bank to clearing house, hospital to insurer, airline to global distribution system — its concerns are exactly the live ones: negotiated contracts, versioning across parties who cannot be forced to upgrade, discovery, and federated identity. What has changed is that the smart pipe is gone, replaced by dumb transport and, where mediation is genuinely needed, by an API gateway or a service mesh that handles cross-cutting concerns without owning business logic.78

Microservices

The term was settled at a workshop of software architects in 2011 and given its canonical definition by James Lewis and Martin Fowler in 2014: an approach to developing a single application as a suite of small services, each running in its own process and communicating with lightweight mechanisms, built around business capabilities, independently deployable by fully automated machinery, with minimal centralised management and possible heterogeneity of language and storage.79 The article's most durable slogan — smart endpoints and dumb pipes — is an explicit repudiation of the ESB.

Everything that follows is downstream of one design choice: each service owns its data, and no other service may touch that store. This is the constraint that makes independent deployment real, because a shared database is a shared schema and a shared schema is a shared release. It is also the constraint that generates every difficulty in the style. Once data is partitioned across services, a business operation spanning two services can no longer be a database transaction. The standard replacement is the saga: a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions for rollback, a technique first described by Garcia-Molina and Salem in 1987 for long-lived transactions and rediscovered wholesale by the microservice generation.80,81 Compensation is not rollback; some things — an email sent, a package shipped — cannot be undone, only apologised for. That is a business conversation, not a technical one.

The second consequence is that queries spanning services must be composed rather than joined, via API composition or a maintained read model. The third is that consistency becomes a designed property rather than an inherited one; the CAP result formalises what you must give up under partition,82 and Brewer's own retrospective makes the point that the interesting engineering is in choosing what to degrade rather than in picking two letters.83 Pat Helland's distinction between data on the inside — private, mutable, transactionally consistent — and data on the outside — immutable, versioned, and always somewhat stale on arrival — is the cleanest available framing of what crossing a service boundary does to information.84

The operational tax is substantial and non-optional: service discovery, load balancing, retries with backoff and jitter, circuit breakers and bulkheads,85 distributed tracing,27 centralised logging, per-service CI/CD, container orchestration,86 and a testing strategy that copes with the fact that no developer can run the whole system. Netflix, whose seven-year migration to the cloud was completed in early 2016, built much of the public tooling for this and also pioneered deliberately injecting failure to verify that the resilience actually works.87,88 Fowler's "microservice premium" is the standard caution: the style buys productivity at scale by imposing a fixed cost that smaller systems cannot amortise.32

Empirical studies of migrations report a consistent pattern: the motivations are maintainability and scalability, and the reported pains are dominated by decomposition difficulty, distributed data management, and operational complexity rather than by the technology of services as such.89,90 The recurring, expensive failure is the distributed monolith: services that must be released together, because a shared library, a shared database, or an implicit call chain reintroduced deployment coupling across a network. This is strictly worse than a monolith, since it has all the coordination cost plus latency and partial failure.21,91

The corrective that the industry converged on is granularity discipline. Uber, having reached roughly 2,200 critical microservices, published domain-oriented microservice architecture in 2020: services are grouped into domains, domains are fronted by gateways so that consumers depend on a domain rather than on individual services, and layers constrain which domains may call which.92 It is explicitly a correction rather than a repudiation — the observation being that a microservice architecture with no higher-order structure is just a monolith whose function calls have been replaced by network calls and whose module boundaries have been deleted. The most useful single heuristic remains: a service boundary should be a boundary you would defend in a business conversation, not one that appeared because a class got large.

Event-driven architecture

An event-driven system is one in which components communicate by announcing that something happened rather than by requesting that something be done. The inversion is total: the producer of an event has no dependency on its consumers and, in the strict form, no knowledge that they exist. That is the fourth arrow in Figure 1, and it is the only one that removes a dependency entirely rather than redirecting it.

Fowler's warning is the right place to start: "event-driven" names at least four distinct patterns that people routinely conflate.93 The distinctions have consequences:

  • Event notification. A thin message — an identifier and a type — announcing a change. Consumers call back for detail. Minimal coupling to payload shape, maximal chattiness, and no way to reconstruct history from the stream.
  • Event-carried state transfer. The message carries enough state that consumers need not call back, at the price of coupling every consumer to the producer's data shape and accepting duplicated, stale copies everywhere.
  • Event sourcing. The event log is the system of record. Treated separately below.
  • CQRS. Frequently paired with events, but a separate decision, and also treated below.

Topology is the other axis. In a broker topology, events are published to a channel and consumers subscribe; the workflow is emergent, no component owns it, and adding a consumer requires changing nothing. In a mediator topology, an orchestrator receives an event and directs a sequence of steps; the workflow is explicit, named, and observable in one place. Broker topologies are maximally decoupled and minimally comprehensible: nobody can answer "what happens when an order is placed?" without reading every subscriber. Mediator topologies reintroduce a central component that knows the process, which is a coupling you may want — order fulfilment with compensations is a workflow, and pretending otherwise produces a distributed system nobody can debug.47,94 Hohpe and Woolf's messaging patterns remain the reference vocabulary for everything in this space; the catalogue predates the current wave by two decades and has needed almost no revision.94

The substrate matters more here than in most styles. Traditional message queues deliver and delete; a log-structured broker retains an ordered, replayable sequence with per-consumer offsets. Kafka's original design paper describes exactly this choice and its consequences for throughput and for multiple independent consumers,95 and Jay Kreps's longer argument treats the log as a unifying abstraction for data integration, since a durable ordered log is simultaneously a message bus, a replication mechanism, and a source of truth.96 Kleppmann's treatment of the same territory is the best single explanation of why stream processing and database replication turn out to be the same problem.97 On the interoperability side, CloudEvents standardises the metadata envelope so that events can cross platform boundaries without bespoke translation.98

Cross-domain instances make the shape concrete. In finance, LMAX's retail trading platform processed six million orders per second on a single business-logic thread by feeding it events through lock-free ring buffers, an architecture Fowler documented in detail and whose Disruptor component was released as open source.99,100 In telemetry and IoT, MQTT's publish-subscribe model over constrained networks is event-driven architecture as a wire protocol.101 In telecoms, Erlang's actor model made message passing between isolated processes the unit of both concurrency and fault tolerance, an argument Joe Armstrong developed into a general theory of building reliable systems from unreliable parts.102 In retail and logistics, order-placed and inventory-reserved events fan out to pricing, fraud, fulfilment, and analytics consumers that the checkout team has never met.

The costs are ordering, duplication, and comprehension. Global ordering across partitions is not available at scale, so systems settle for per-key ordering and design handlers to be commutative or idempotent. Exactly-once delivery in the general case is not achievable; effectively-once processing is, through idempotency keys and deduplication, and every serious deployment implements it. Debugging requires correlation identifiers threaded through every hop and a tracing system to reassemble them.27 And schema evolution becomes the central governance problem, because a retained log means old events are read forever by code written later.

CQRS

Command Query Responsibility Segregation separates the model used to change state from the model used to read it. Its ancestor is Bertrand Meyer's command–query separation, a method-level discipline holding that a routine either changes state or returns a value, never both.103 Greg Young raised the idea to the architectural level around 2010,104 and Fowler's summary is the most-cited short statement of it — along with his caution that for most systems CQRS adds risky complexity.105 The Microsoft patterns group produced the most thorough field report, including an unusually honest account of what went wrong during the journey.106

The motivation is a genuine asymmetry that a single model handles badly. Writes need invariants, validation, and a normalised shape that makes illegal states unrepresentable. Reads need denormalised, pre-joined, screen-shaped data, often at a hundred times the volume. A single ORM-mapped model serving both ends up either with a write model contorted by display concerns or a read path that assembles a dashboard from eleven joins. Splitting them lets each be shaped by its own pressure, and lets them scale independently — read replicas, caches, and search indexes for one side, a small consistent store for the other.

Levels of commitment vary, and conflating them is the most common source of unnecessary pain. The lightest form is two sets of objects against one database and one transaction: nearly free, and often sufficient. The middle form is separate read stores kept up to date by projections, which introduces replication lag and therefore eventual consistency in the user interface. The heaviest form combines CQRS with event sourcing, so that the write side appends events and the read side is entirely derived. Only the last two require the consistency conversation, and it is a product conversation: after a user clicks save, may the list they return to show stale data for two seconds? Sometimes yes; sometimes the answer determines the architecture. The standard mitigations — reading your own writes from the write model, optimistic UI updates, version tokens that let a client wait for a projection to catch up — are all ways of buying back a guarantee you deliberately sold.

Event sourcing

Event sourcing stores every change to application state as an immutable event in an append-only log, and derives current state by folding that log.107 The current balance is not stored; it is computed. Snapshots exist purely as an optimisation and carry no authority.

The idea is older than software. Double-entry bookkeeping is event sourcing with quills: you never erase a ledger entry, you post a compensating one, and the balance is a derived quantity. Pat Helland's argument that immutability changes everything develops the general case — that append-only data with derived views is the natural shape for distributed systems, because immutable facts can be copied, cached, and replicated without coordination.108 Version control systems are the same design: Git stores commits and derives the working tree.

What it buys is unusual and hard to get any other way. A complete audit trail is a structural property rather than a feature, which matters enormously in regulated domains. Temporal queries — what did this look like on the 14th? — are a fold with a stop condition instead of a data-warehouse project. Debugging can replay production events against a fixed version of the code. New read models can be built retroactively over history that predates the question, which is the capability teams miss most once they have had it. And the log is a natural integration point: other systems subscribe to the same facts.96

The costs are equally structural. Event schema evolution is permanent: an event written in 2019 must still be readable in 2029, which means upcasters, versioned event types, and a strong prohibition on ever changing the meaning of a past fact. Querying current state requires either replay or maintained projections, so CQRS is effectively mandatory rather than optional. Deletion is philosophically awkward and legally consequential — "the right to erasure" collides directly with an immutable log, and the usual answer, crypto-shredding by discarding a per-subject encryption key, is a real technique with real operational weight. Finally, the domain modelling bar rises: events must be named in business language and at business granularity, because OrderRowUpdated is a database change dressed as a fact and will teach you nothing on replay.

The style pays for itself in domains where history is the product: ledgers and payments, trading and settlement, insurance policy lifecycles, clinical records, supply-chain custody. It is expensive theatre in a content management system.

Domain-driven design

Domain-driven design is not a topology. It is the discipline for deciding where the boundaries go, which makes it the missing input to every style above. Eric Evans's 2003 book supplies two separable halves, and the industry has consistently over-weighted the wrong one.109

The tactical half — entities, value objects, aggregates, repositories, factories, domain services — is a set of object-modelling patterns. Useful, teachable, and the part that gets adopted first, usually as a naming convention applied to an unchanged design. The strategic half is where the architectural content lives.

Its central concept is the bounded context: an explicit boundary within which a model and its language are internally consistent.109,110 The observation behind it is that no useful enterprise-wide model of "customer" or "product" exists, because different parts of a business mean genuinely different things by the same word. In sales, an account is a lead with a probability attached. In fulfilment, it is an address and a set of delivery constraints. In billing, it is a ledger with a payment instrument. In support, it is a case history. These are not views of one entity; they are different entities that share a noun. Every attempt to build the canonical enterprise model produces a schema with 140 nullable columns that satisfies nobody, and this failure is the strongest available argument that the correct number of canonical models is zero.

The corresponding artefact is the context map, which records not just the contexts but the relationships between them, each with a named pattern and a power dynamic: shared kernel (two teams jointly own a subset, and must coordinate every change), customer–supplier (downstream's needs enter upstream's backlog), conformist (downstream accepts upstream's model wholesale because it has no leverage), anti-corruption layer (downstream translates at its boundary to protect its own model), published language, open host service, separate ways.109,111 The anti-corruption layer is the one that earns its keep most often — it is the pattern that lets a modern system integrate a legacy one without inheriting its ontology, and it is what makes strangler-fig migrations survivable.112

Also strategic, and frequently decisive: distinguishing the core domain — the thing the business is actually differentiated by — from supporting and generic subdomains. It is the analysis that tells you to write the pricing engine yourself and buy the identity provider, and it is where most of the return on architectural investment is concentrated. Ubiquitous language, the practice of using one vocabulary in conversation, code, and tests within a context, is the mechanism that keeps the model and the business from drifting apart; it sounds like a soft practice and functions as a hard constraint.

The relationship to service topology is direct and load-bearing: a bounded context is the smallest defensible unit for a service boundary. The most reliable published advice on microservice decomposition reduces to "align services to bounded contexts, and if you cannot identify them, you are not ready to split."21,81 Uber's DOMA is the same idea applied retroactively at scale: group 2,200 services into roughly seventy domains and let consumers depend on domain gateways.92 Collaborative discovery techniques such as EventStorming exist precisely because context boundaries are found by talking to the business rather than by reading the schema.113

The costs deserve stating plainly. DDD is expensive in the currency organisations have least of: sustained access to domain experts. It has a substantial vocabulary that invites cargo-culting, and a tactical pattern set that is genuine overkill for a system whose logic is create-read-update-delete. Evans himself is clear that it is meant for complex domains, and the honest test is whether the business rules are hard enough that getting them wrong costs money.

Backend for frontend

The BFF pattern gives each client experience its own dedicated server-side component, owned by the team that owns that client, instead of routing every client through one general-purpose API. The term was coined by Phil Calçado at SoundCloud around 2013 and documented publicly in 2015;114 Sam Newman's write-up, drawing on the same experience plus work at REA, is the canonical statement.115,116

The problem it solves is a specific and painful organisational one. A single API serving iOS, Android, web, and partner integrations accumulates the union of everyone's requirements. Mobile wants few round trips and small payloads because of radio wake-up cost and metered data; web wants richer documents and does not care as much; partners want stability above all and change on a multi-year cadence. Serving all four from one artefact means one release train, one backlog, and — worst — a separate backend team that every client team must negotiate with. Newman's observation is that the general-purpose backend does not just create technical friction; it creates a queue with a political dimension.115

A BFF is deliberately thin: it aggregates calls to downstream services, reshapes and trims responses for one client, and handles client-specific concerns such as session translation and protocol adaptation. It contains no business logic that another client would need, because that is the boundary condition separating a BFF from a distributed monolith. SoundCloud eventually ran dozens of them serving hundreds of millions of requests per hour, and also reported the predictable difficulty: authorisation logic and creeping business rules accumulated inside BFFs and made them harder to maintain than intended.114

The costs are duplication and proliferation. Aggregation code is written several times; a downstream contract change may touch four BFFs; and each is a service to deploy, monitor, and secure. The standard mitigation is a shared library for genuinely common concerns, applied carefully, since a shared library across BFFs is a shared release in disguise. Newman's practical guidance is to define a BFF per user experience rather than per device — one mobile BFF if the iOS and Android experiences are the same product, two if they are not.115

GraphQL is the pattern's most common alternative, letting each client specify the shape it wants against one schema, moving the tailoring from a per-client server into a per-query language.117 The trade shifts rather than disappears: a single schema is a single point of governance and a single release train for the type system, and query cost control becomes a first-class operational concern. Many organisations run both — a GraphQL gateway for flexible clients, dedicated BFFs where a client's needs are peculiar enough to warrant code.

No real system is one style

The catalogue is a set of vocabulary items, not a menu of exclusive choices. Every substantial production system in the world is a composition, and the compositions are more informative than the pure styles.

A representative modern arrangement: microservices at the top level, aligned to bounded contexts; each service internally organised as a hexagon with an inward dependency rule; the services communicating through a mix of synchronous peer calls for queries and an event log for state propagation; one or two services internally using CQRS because their read and write loads differ by two orders of magnitude; exactly one service — the ledger — event-sourced because auditability is a legal requirement; a BFF per client platform at the edge; and the whole thing running on an orchestrator that is itself a plugin architecture. Six of the sixteen styles, each applied at the scale where it pays.

The instructive cases are the reversals, because they are where the trade-offs became measurable.

Segment moved from a monolith to per-destination microservices to solve fault isolation — one failing destination should not block the others — and then moved back. The operational overhead of maintaining separate codebases, queues, and dependency versions across more than a hundred services consumed the productivity gain; defect rates rose and feature velocity fell. The rebuilt system consolidated destinations into a single service with a shared worker pool, explicitly trading away modularity and per-destination isolation in exchange for tractable operations.118,119,120 The moral is not "microservices are bad"; it is that the number of services a team can operate is a hard constraint with a specific value, and exceeding it converts a benefit into a cost.

Amazon Prime Video's video quality analysis team rebuilt a serverless, distributed monitoring pipeline as a single process and reported an infrastructure cost reduction of more than ninety per cent.121 The detail that matters is the reason: the workload moved large volumes of video frames between components, so the architecture was paying orchestration state transitions and object-store round trips for data movement that became a memory reference once the components shared a process. This is a data-locality result, not a verdict on distributed systems, and the same team retained the same conceptual components before and after. Widely mis-reported as Amazon abandoning microservices, it is better read as evidence that the correct boundary depends on the shape of the data crossing it.

Shopify chose neither pole, componentising a very large Rails monolith with enforced boundaries and building tooling to keep them enforced.30,33 Uber went the other way and then imposed structure after the fact.92 Netflix completed a seven-year migration to a cloud-native microservice estate and invested correspondingly in resilience tooling and deliberate failure injection to keep it operable.87,88 LMAX concluded that its latency requirements were better served by one thread and an event log than by distribution of any kind.99 Five organisations with excellent engineers reaching five different answers is not a sign of confusion in the field; it is a sign that the answer is determined by constraints the field cannot supply.

Choosing, and changing your mind later

Architecture selection is a trade-off analysis, and the useful methods make the trade-offs explicit rather than scoring designs. ATAM's core mechanic — elicit quality attribute scenarios, identify the architectural approaches that address them, and locate the sensitivity and trade-off points where one attribute can only improve at another's expense — remains the most rigorous available procedure.18,19 Most teams need a lighter version, but the lightweight version should keep the same skeleton: name the scenarios, name the approaches, name what each one costs.

Four practical inputs dominate in practice:

  • Organisational shape. Conway's law is a constraint, not an observation.6,7 An architecture that fights the communication structure will lose or will silently re-form to match it. If you want independently deployable services, you need independently accountable teams first; Team Topologies is the current standard treatment of designing the org to induce the architecture.8
  • Domain complexity. If the business rules are thin, an inward-pointing architecture is ceremony. If they are the product, it is the cheapest insurance available.109
  • The actual scaling axis. "We need to scale" is not a requirement. Scaling reads, scaling writes, scaling team headcount, and scaling deployment frequency call for entirely different structures, and only one of them is solved by services.
  • Operational capacity. The number of services an organisation can run well is bounded by its platform investment, and organisations consistently overestimate it. The DORA research programme's finding is that loosely coupled architecture and team autonomy predict delivery performance — but the operative variable is the ability to test and deploy independently, which is achievable in a modular monolith and absent in a distributed monolith.122

Two practices make revision affordable rather than traumatic. The first is architecture decision records: short, immutable, dated notes capturing context, decision, and consequences, kept beside the code, superseded rather than edited.24 Their value is that they preserve rationale, which is the component of architecture that disappears first and is impossible to reconstruct. The second is evolutionary architecture: treat architectural characteristics as testable properties and guard them with fitness functions in the pipeline, so that erosion is caught by a build rather than by an incident.23 For communicating the result, the C4 model's four levels — context, container, component, code — remain the most practical answer to Kruchten's original observation that different audiences need different views.123,16

When the decision does need to change, the migration pattern is nearly always the same: the strangler fig. Put a façade in front of the existing system, route one capability at a time to the new implementation, and retire the old code path when nothing calls it.112,91 The alternative — a parallel rewrite with a cut-over date — has a failure record so consistent that proposing one should require a written justification.

Recognisable failure modes

Four failure modes recur often enough to be worth naming, and each is a specific misuse of a boundary rather than a general lack of skill.

The distributed monolith. Services that cannot be deployed independently, because of a shared database, a shared library on a synchronised version, or a synchronous call chain that must be released in lockstep. It has every cost of distribution and no benefit. The diagnostic is a single question asked of a release manager: can any one service ship on a Tuesday without coordinating? 21

The big ball of mud. Structure erodes through accumulated expedients until no boundary is reliable; Foote and Yoder's paper describes the process without moralising and is more useful for it.29 The important insight is that mud is a rational local response to schedule pressure, so the counter-measure must also be structural — enforcement in the build, not exhortation in review.

Architecture as fashion. Adopting a style because a conference talk described its benefits at an organisation with a hundred times your traffic and a platform team you do not have. The published reversals are the best available inoculation, and the reason to read them closely is that each one names the specific constraint that made the original choice wrong for them.118,121

Premature boundaries. Splitting before the domain is understood produces boundaries that cut across the actual axis of change, and every subsequent feature crosses three services. This is the most expensive error on the list, because a wrong boundary in a distributed system is dramatically harder to move than a wrong boundary inside one process — which is the entire argument for the monolith-first default and for the modular monolith as a genuine destination.31,32

Underneath all four is one observation. Every style in this article is a way of spending complexity in order to buy something. The failures are not caused by choosing the wrong style; they are caused by paying for a benefit the organisation was never in a position to collect.

Where to read further

Foundations, in order of usefulness for a working engineer. Parnas's 1972 paper is four pages and still the highest-value reading in the field.9 Conway's 1968 note is shorter.6 Waldo and colleagues on distributed computing should be read before any decision to introduce a network boundary.41 Fielding's dissertation chapters 2 and 5 are the model for reasoning from constraints to properties.44

Textbooks and surveys. Bass, Clements, and Kazman for the discipline as taught and for quality-attribute-driven design.20 Richards and Ford for a modern, style-by-style treatment aimed at practitioners, and its companion volume on distributed trade-offs.47,124 Shaw and Garlan for the historical formalisation.15 Buschmann and colleagues for the original pattern catalogue.17

Data and distribution. Kleppmann is the single best book on what happens to information when it crosses a boundary.97 Helland's two short papers repay repeated reading.84,108 Hohpe and Woolf remain the reference for messaging vocabulary.94 Gray and Reuter for what a transaction actually guarantees, which is the thing microservices give up.125

Style-specific primary sources. Cockburn on hexagonal;63,64 Palermo on onion;66 Martin on clean;68 Evans and Vernon on DDD;109,111 Lewis and Fowler, Newman, and Richardson on microservices;79,21,81 Young and Fowler on CQRS and event sourcing.104,105,107

Practice. Nygard on production failure modes and on decision records;85,24 Ford, Parsons, and Kua on fitness functions;23 Forsgren, Humble, and Kim for the empirical link between architecture and delivery;122 the Google SRE book for what operating any of this actually involves.28

Tooling worth knowing by name. Fitness functions: ArchUnit (JVM), NetArchTest (.NET), import-linter (Python), dependency-cruiser (JavaScript), or thirty lines of your own. Documentation: C4 with Structurizr or PlantUML;123 ADRs as plain Markdown in the repository. Observability, without which none of the distributed styles are operable: OpenTelemetry for instrumentation, and any tracing backend that implements the Dapper model.27

A closing note on how to use all of this. The styles are not a ladder, and there is no version of maturity in which a team graduates from one to the next. They are a set of answers to the question at the top of this article — where is the boundary, and which way may the arrows cross it — and the right answer is determined by the domain, the organisation, and the operational budget, in roughly that order. The discipline is not in knowing the sixteen names. It is in being able to say, for your system, which boundary you drew, what you bought with it, what you paid, and what you will watch for to find out whether the trade was good.
