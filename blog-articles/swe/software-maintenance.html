MR-01The premise

You stop choosing when it changes

Delivery is not the end of a piece of software. It is the moment the software acquires a constituency, and with it a set of obligations that the delivery team no longer controls. From that point on, two clocks run against each other. One is the environment's: operating systems reach end of life, protocols are deprecated, regulations arrive, hardware is retired, certificates expire, dependencies publish advisories. The other is the constituency's: every consumer of the system has its own capacity to absorb change, and none of them will move on your schedule.

Software maintenance is the discipline that lives between those clocks. It is not a phase after the interesting part. On the evidence, it is most of the work. Reported United States federal IT spending puts roughly 79% of a more-than-$100-billion annual budget into operating and maintaining what already exists.1 The international standard that governs the activity says the same thing in fewer words: because maintenance consumes a major share of a software life cycle's financial resources, it should be an important project consideration.2

This article is about what that work actually consists of — the four classical types plus the two the current standard adds, the economics that make it expensive, the patterns for changing systems that are already load-bearing, and the three contracts (deprecation, versioning, backward compatibility) through which a maintainer negotiates with everyone downstream. Along the way it uses a small companion program, written for this article, to test four claims that are usually asserted rather than demonstrated.3

The organising idea is deliberately narrow. Every act of maintenance begins as a modification request: a statement that something about a running system should be different. The standard's central move is that an MR must be classified before it can be scheduled, because the class determines who pays, how urgently, against which budget, and with what evidence.2 Get the classification wrong and every downstream decision inherits the error. So the piece is structured as a queue of requests, and the recurring device is a numbered plate describing one kind of request: what triggers it, what class it falls in, what a maintainer does about it, what happens if it is deferred, and the observable sign that an organisation is getting it wrong. They are collected at the end into a single ledger.

MR-02Definitions

What the word actually names

The word is unhelpful. Maintenance in engineering usually means restoring a thing to a condition it has drifted from — changing the oil, replacing the bearing. Software does not wear out. Bits do not corrode. And yet the term stuck, and with it a persistent misconception that maintenance is repair work.

ISO/IEC/IEEE 14764:2022 defines software maintenance as the totality of activities required to provide support to a software system, and immediately widens it in two directions that matter.2 First, maintenance activities are performed during the pre-delivery stage as well as the post-delivery stage: planning for supportability, determining logistics, and designing for maintainability are maintenance work done before anything ships. Second, the standard introduces software sustainment — the activities required to support, maintain and operate a system so that it remains operational — to capture the fact that in modern practice these are performed concurrently and iteratively rather than in sequence.2

The standard sits inside the life-cycle framework of ISO/IEC/IEEE 12207, whose statement of purpose is the most compact definition available: the purpose of the maintenance process is to sustain the capability of the system to provide a service; it monitors the system's capability to deliver, records incidents for analysis, takes corrective, adaptive, perfective and preventive actions, and confirms restored capability.4,2 Note what is being sustained. Not the code. The capability to provide a service, in an environment that will not hold still.

That framing explains why the standard is explicit that the need for maintenance arises from causes other than latent defects: changes to interfaced systems or infrastructure, evolving security threats, and the technical obsolescence of system elements over the life cycle.2 Nothing in that list is a bug. The system is correct, unchanged, and progressively less able to do its job, because everything around it moved.

Two further pieces of vocabulary are worth fixing now. A modification request is an information item identifying and describing proposed changes to a product or service; it may later be classified as a correction or an enhancement and identified with one of the maintenance types.2 A problem report documents a problem detected in a product.2 The distinction is procedural, not pedantic: a problem report asserts that the product fails to meet its requirements, and a modification request proposes a change whether or not it does. The rest of the vocabulary — maintainability, sustainment, transition, disposal — follows the same standard, which itself draws its quality terms from the SQuaRE series.5,6 Where the concern is the ongoing management of an application portfolio rather than the engineering of changes to one product, the companion standard on application management covers the same ground from the service side.7

MR-03History

How the field learned the word

The problem was named before it was measured. The 1968 NATO conference at Garmisch, which gave the discipline the phrase “software engineering,” also produced the first sustained institutional admission that large systems were becoming unaffordable to change after delivery.8 What it did not produce was data.

The data arrived in the mid-1970s from two directions at once. Belady and Lehman published a model of large program development built on the release history of IBM's OS/360, and found systematic growth and complexity behaviour that looked more like a natural process than a management outcome.9 In the same year, at the second International Conference on Software Engineering, E. Burton Swanson proposed the classification the field still uses. He divided maintenance into three types by the condition that prompted it: corrective, undertaken in response to failures; adaptive, in response to changes in the data or processing environment; and perfective, undertaken to eliminate processing inefficiencies, enhance performance, or improve maintainability. Swanson's own gloss on the third is the sharpest line in the paper: corrective and adaptive work merely keep a program up and running, while perfective work is directed toward keeping it up and running at less expense, or so as to better serve its users.10

Two years later, Lientz, Swanson and Tompkins published the survey that gave the field its first effort numbers, and a decade of received wisdom: 17.4% of maintenance effort corrective, 18.2% adaptive, 60.3% perfective, 4.1% other.11 The expanded study of 487 data processing organisations followed in 1980.12 Those figures have been quoted so widely, and so loosely, that the underlying studies are often conflated; they are also, as we will see, contradicted by direct measurement.13

Lehman's 1980 synthesis reframed the whole subject. By classifying programs according to their relationship with the environment in which they execute — S-type, solved against a fixed specification; P-type, approximating a problem; E-type, embedded in and altering the world it models — he identified why E-type programs generate never-ending maintenance activity as an intrinsic consequence of being used at all.14 The laws were revised and extended over the following two decades.15,16

The rest is consolidation. Parnas gave the decay of a system's structure the name software aging and argued it was preventable but not by accident.17 IEEE Std 1219 standardised a maintenance process,18 later harmonised with ISO/IEC 14764 and, in the 2022 third edition, aligned to ISO/IEC/IEEE 12207:2017 and extended with modern approaches.2 Rajlich and Bennett recast the life cycle as a sequence of stages with irreversible transitions.19 Cunningham supplied the metaphor that finally let engineers argue about maintenance in front of finance.20 And the SWEBOK Guide, now in its fourth version, carries software maintenance as a knowledge area in its own right.21

MR-04Taxonomy

The class is not in the diff

The 2022 edition of 14764 recognises five maintenance types, not the classical four: corrective, preventive, adaptive, perfective, and additive — modification performed after delivery to add functionality or features to enhance the usage of the product. The standard separates additive from perfective explicitly, so that relatively large additions can be negotiated as their own item of maintenance strategy, resourcing and service level between supplier and acquirer.2 It also names emergency maintenance: unscheduled modification performed to keep a system temporarily operational pending corrective maintenance.2

Underneath the five names are two orthogonal questions. Is this a correction (the product does not meet its defined requirements) or an enhancement (the requirement itself has moved)? And is it reactive (something has already happened) or proactive (nothing has failed yet)? The standard notes that reactive covers corrective and adaptive types, and proactive covers preventive, perfective and additive.2

The matrix has an instructive hole. There is no reactive enhancement, because you cannot react to a requirement nobody has yet stated. And adaptive maintenance straddles the correction/enhancement boundary in a way the standard is careful about: adaptive work can be requested from an MR classified as an enhancement, when the software is to be adapted to an environment that is evolving, and from an MR classified as a correction, when the software must be adapted to an environment that has already changed.2 That is not bureaucratic hair-splitting. The two cases have different urgency, different budgets and different blame.

Here is the part that matters operationally. The class of a change is a property of the reason for it, not of the code it touches. The same three-line diff can be corrective, preventive, adaptive or perfective depending only on what prompted it. This has an uncomfortable consequence: if the reason was not recorded at intake, the class is not recoverable afterwards.

The companion program tests exactly this. It takes forty modification requests from one quarter of work on a payment-reconciliation service, each carrying the four facts a maintainer would record at intake — did a live system fail, does a fault exist without having surfaced, did something outside the product move, did a stakeholder ask for something new — and applies the standard's decision procedure. All six classes separate cleanly. It then discards the intake facts and reclassifies from the shape of the change alone, as a researcher mining a repository must. Of the forty requests, 25 — 62% — land in the wrong class once the reason is gone. 3 Studies that classify maintenance by mining commit histories are doing archaeology on exactly this missing evidence, which is one reason their results diverge so widely from surveys.22,23

The practical instruction is small and unglamorous: put the classification field on the intake form, make it required, and do not let it be edited after the work is scheduled. Everything downstream in this article — effort measurement, budget defence, preventive allocation, deprecation planning — depends on having it.

MR-05Type one

Corrective: the work you did not schedule

Corrective maintenance is modification performed after delivery to correct discovered problems, repairing the product so that it satisfies its defined system requirements.2 It is the type everyone pictures, and the only one that is not optional. Its defining property is not difficulty. It is that the arrival time is chosen by the fault, not the plan.

Three well-documented failures show the range. In August 2012, Knight Capital deployed new order-routing code to seven of its eight production servers. The eighth still carried Power Peg, a function that had been dormant since 2003, and a flag that the new code had repurposed. When orders arrived carrying that flag, the eighth server activated the old code, which — because a safety counter had been moved elsewhere in 2005 and Power Peg had not been retested afterwards — sent child orders without ever registering that they had been filled. In roughly 45 minutes the firm executed over 4 million trades in 154 stocks and took a loss of more than $460 million. The SEC's order records that 97 automated e-mails naming the error were generated before the market opened and were not treated as alerts, and that an attempt to remediate by uninstalling the new code from the seven correct servers made the situation worse by activating Power Peg on those as well.24 Every element of that failure is a maintenance failure: dead code left in place, a flag reused rather than retired, a deployment with no verification step, and a rollback that had never been rehearsed.

Aviation supplies the cleanest illustration of a latent arithmetic fault becoming a corrective emergency. In 2015 the FAA issued an airworthiness directive requiring a repetitive maintenance task on all Boeing 787s, because a software counter internal to the generator control units overflows after 248 days of continuous power, putting all four units into failsafe mode simultaneously and causing loss of all AC electrical power regardless of flight phase.25 The interim remedy was to power the aircraft off before the counter could wrap; a software update followed. Five years later a second directive addressed a different 51-day counter in the common core system, whose expiry could cause stale data to be displayed as valid flight-critical data, including misleading airspeed on both displays with the stall and overspeed warnings inoperative.26,27 Rebooting the aeroplane is an emergency-maintenance workaround in the standard's precise sense: unscheduled modification to keep the system operational pending a real fix.2

And then there is the upper bound on difficulty. In November 2023 Voyager 1 began returning unusable telemetry; by March 2024 JPL had traced the fault to a single failed chip holding part of the flight data subsystem's memory, including some of the computer's own code. No single free memory block was large enough to hold the displaced code, so engineers broke it into sections, placed them in separate locations, and rewrote the cross-references so the pieces would still run as one program. The first relocated section was transmitted on 18 April 2024 and confirmed on 20 April, a round trip of some 45 hours. Science data resumed by June.28 The team's own account of the constraints is the part every maintainer should read: the assembly language was documented, but there were no development tools, hardware testbeds or emulators left, so the modifications could not be tested before uplink and verification was by inspection only.29 That is corrective maintenance at the limit — and a fair description of what happens to any system whose supporting apparatus is allowed to decay faster than the system itself.

MR-06Type two

Adaptive: the clock you do not own

Adaptive maintenance is modification performed after delivery to keep a product usable in a changed or changing environment; the standard's own example is that an upgrade to the operating system results in changes to the applications.2 It is the type that most reliably surprises organisations, because nothing about their own product has gone wrong.

The taxonomy's split between adaptive-as-correction and adaptive-as-enhancement is exactly the difference between being surprised and not being surprised.2 Almost all environmental change is announced in advance. Runtimes publish sunset dates; protocols are formally deprecated; distributions have release calendars. The work is the same either way. The difference is whether you do it inside the announced window or after an outage.

Python's major-version transition is the reference case for how long the window can be. Python 3.0 shipped in 2008; the 2.7 end-of-life date was pushed five years into the future to relieve users who could not yet migrate, finally landing on 1 January 2020.30 The ecosystem needed a coordination mechanism beyond the language's own schedule: the Python 3 Statement collected pledges from projects to require Python 3 by 2020, precisely so that each project could plan a transition knowing others would too.31 The tooling story is instructive as well — the shipped conversion tool was widely judged insufficient on its own, and the practical path ran through compatibility libraries and straddling code that ran under both versions during the crossover.32 A twelve-year migration for a language with an unusually cooperative community is a reasonable prior for how long a large adaptive change takes.

Operating systems make the same point with harder edges. Windows 10 reached end of support on 14 October 2025: no further technical assistance, feature updates or security updates through ordinary channels.33 Microsoft's Extended Security Updates programme is the archetype of the paid runway — critical and important security updates only, no feature improvements, no technical support — and it is explicitly finite.34 ESU is not a reprieve. It is the invoice for an adaptive change that was not made in time.

Date arithmetic deserves its own note, because it produces adaptive work on a schedule known decades ahead. The Year 2000 remediation was, in the standard's terms, a global preventive-and-adaptive programme with a fixed deadline; the US Department of Commerce put cumulative American Y2K readiness spending in the neighbourhood of $100 billion between 1995 and 2001.35 The successor is already in the calendar: signed 32-bit Unix time overflows on 19 January 2038, and the remediation has been running for years. Linux 5.6 was the first kernel release that could serve as the base for a 32-bit system designed to run past 2038, with the caveats that user space must be compiled against a 64-bit time_t and applications must be ported to the newer system calls.36 The kernel work being finished is not the same as the problem being solved; the long tail is embedded and vendor-supplied systems that nobody will recompile.

MR-07Type three

Preventive: paying before the invoice

Preventive maintenance is modification of a product after delivery to correct latent faults before they become operational faults in the live system.2 It is the only type whose success is invisible, which is why it is the first thing cut and the most expensive thing to have cut.

The largest category of preventive work in practice is patching components you did not write. The evidence on how well that goes is unambiguous and getting worse. Verizon's 2025 report found that only about 54% of edge-device vulnerabilities were fully remediated over the year, at a median of 32 days — against a median time from disclosure to mass exploitation for those same vulnerabilities of zero days — and that across the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue as a whole the full-remediation rate was 38%.37,38 The 2026 edition records a deterioration on every axis: vulnerability exploitation became the leading initial access vector at 31% of breaches, the median time to full remediation rose from 32 to 43 days, KEV remediation fell from 38% to 26%, and the median organisation had roughly 50% more KEV vulnerabilities to patch than the year before. Verizon's survival analysis is the number to remember: at day seven, between 60% and 70% of KEV vulnerabilities remain open regardless of year, volume or organisational maturity.39

Equifax remains the canonical demonstration of what the gap costs. Apache disclosed CVE-2017-5638 in the Struts framework and released a patch on 7 March 2017; the US Computer Emergency Readiness Team notified the credit agencies the following day; an internal instruction to apply the patch went out on 9 March; a scan on 15 March failed to identify the vulnerable systems; and the vulnerability remained unpatched until 29 July, when the security team noticed suspicious traffic on the online dispute portal.40 The congressional report on the breach is a study in how an organisation can have every individual control and still not be able to answer the question “where do we run this component?”41 That question is what a software bill of materials exists to answer, and why its minimum elements were specified at national level.42

The second category of preventive work is structural: correcting latent faults in the product's own code before they surface. This is where refactoring belongs — behaviour- preserving transformation of internal structure, formalised in Opdyke's dissertation and made a practitioner discipline by Fowler.43,44 Refactoring is not preventive by definition; it is preventive when it is done to remove a specific class of latent fault or to make a specific future change safe, and perfective when it is done to reduce cost. The distinction matters at budget time, which is the only place classification ever matters.

The third category is supply-chain hygiene, and 2024 supplied the argument for treating it as a first-class maintenance concern. The xz-utils backdoor, CVE-2024-3094, was the product of a multi-year social-engineering campaign in which a contributor built credibility over roughly two years while sock-puppet accounts pressured the original maintainer — who had described being in a difficult position with limited resources — to hand over co-maintainership. The malicious code was placed in release tarballs rather than the public repository, and reached the point of shipping in distribution pre-releases before being caught by an engineer investigating a performance anomaly.45,46 The vulnerability was in the maintenance arrangement before it was ever in the code. The Linux Foundation's Census II work exists to make that class of exposure visible in advance, by identifying which widely-deployed components rest on unreasonably thin support.47

MR-08Types four and five

Perfective and additive: the work that looks like development

Perfective maintenance is modification to provide enhancements for users, improvements of information for users, and recoding to improve performance, maintainability or other attributes.2 Swanson's original definition is more useful about the decision rule: perfective work is undertaken when justified, that is, when the improvements to be achieved outweigh the costs of making them.10 It is the only maintenance type with an explicit business case built into its definition.

The 2022 standard's addition of additive maintenance is a recognition of something practitioners had been doing awkwardly for years: shipping substantial new function through the maintenance process because that is where the team and the budget were. The standard distinguishes additive from perfective so that larger additions can be negotiated explicitly — strategy, methods, resources, agreements and service levels — and notes that additions can be handled through the maintenance process while larger changes may warrant a new development effort.2 The boundary is a commercial one, not a technical one.

This is where the historical effort figures do the most damage. If you take the 1978 survey at face value — 60.3% perfective — the obvious conclusion is that maintenance is mostly enhancement work and therefore mostly discretionary, which makes it easy to cut.11 The conclusion does not survive contact with measurement, as the next section shows. It also does not survive careful reading: Swanson's perfective category includes improving maintainability, which is preventive work in everything but name, and the survey's respondents were reporting their own perceptions of their own work.

The useful discipline here is to keep perfective work tied to an observable cost. “This module is ugly” is not a justification. “The last four changes in this module each took three times the estimate, and here are the estimates” is. The maintainability characteristics in the SQuaRE product quality model — modularity, reusability, analysability, modifiability, testability — give the vocabulary for saying which property you are buying,5 and ISO/IEC 5055 provides automated source-code measures for counting specific severe violations of good architectural and coding practice rather than arguing about taste.48 Older single-number proxies — cyclomatic complexity, the maintainability index — remain useful as triggers for a conversation and remain poor as targets.49,50

MR-09Economics

What it costs, and how badly we measure it

The single most-quoted claim in this field is that maintenance consumes most of the life-cycle cost of software. It is almost certainly true and almost never properly sourced. What can be said with confidence is narrower and more useful.

First, the direction of the reported spend. Agencies covered by the US Chief Financial Officers Act reported about $83 billion of a planned fiscal-2025 IT budget — roughly 79% — going to operations and maintenance of existing systems.1 That figure is a spending category rather than a measurement of engineering effort, but it is a large, audited, consistently reported number pointing the same way as every informal estimate.

Second, the composition. Here the literature disagrees with itself in a way that is itself the finding. The 1978 survey reported 17.4% corrective, 18.2% adaptive, 60.3% perfective and 4.1% other, based on what practitioners said they did.11 In 2003, Schach and colleagues measured the same question directly on three products — a commercial real-time system, the Linux kernel, and GCC — by examining the changes themselves, and found distributions that were statistically very highly significantly different: corrective maintenance was always more than twice the survey value, and more than three times it in the summed data.13 Two decades of planning advice rested on the survey.

The lesson generalises beyond this one discrepancy. Self-reported effort distributions in maintenance are unreliable in a specific direction: people under-report corrective work, because corrective work is interstitial, unplanned, and rarely gets its own ticket. Classifying changes at intake, as in this article's worked example, is the cheapest available correction — and, as experiment 1 showed, it is not something that can be retrofitted from the repository afterwards.3

The estimation literature took the same problem in a different direction. Boehm's cost-estimation framework modelled maintenance effort as a function of the proportion of a system's code touched per year — annual change traffic — multiplied by the effort the original development would have required, which encodes the useful intuition that maintenance cost scales with the product's size and its rate of change rather than with the size of the changes alone.51 The model's calibration is long obsolete; the shape of the relationship is not.

Third, the aggregate. CISQ's 2022 assessment put the cost of poor software quality in the United States at at least $2.41 trillion, with accumulated software technical debt at approximately $1.52 trillion — a figure the report notes is roughly equal to the total spent on the entire US IT labour base that year.52 Numbers at this scale should be handled as order-of-magnitude arguments rather than measurements, and the report is candid that there are no good estimates of the accruing interest. What they are good for is settling the direction of a management argument: the accumulated cost of deferred maintenance is not a rounding error against the cost of doing it.

That is the setting in which Cunningham's metaphor did its work. His 1992 experience report described shipping first-time code as going into debt — acceptable so long as it is paid back promptly with a rewrite, dangerous when the debt is not repaid, because every minute spent on not-quite-right code counts as interest.20 Cunningham's own emphasis was on code that encodes an immature understanding of the domain, not on sloppiness; Fowler's later treatment separates deliberate from inadvertent and prudent from reckless debt,53 and Kruchten, Nord and Ozkaya pushed the metaphor toward something with enough structure to manage against.54 The systematic mapping literature that followed is large, and its most honest summary is that the metaphor is excellent for communication and still weak as a measurement instrument.55

MR-10Why it never ends

Lehman's laws and software aging

Lehman's central classification is the part of his work that has aged best. An S-type program is fully specified by a formal statement and can, in principle, be finished. A P-type program implements an approximation to a problem whose exact solution is impractical. An E-type program is embedded in the world it models, and by executing becomes part of the environment it describes.14 The consequence is structural: an E-type program alters the world that generated its requirements, which generates new requirements, which is why using a program produces a process of never-ending maintenance activity.

From that follow the laws, formulated across 1974–1996 and usually cited as eight. Four carry most of the practical weight. Continuing change: an E-type system must be continually adapted or it becomes progressively less satisfactory in use. Increasing complexity: as a system evolves its complexity increases unless work is done to maintain or reduce it. Continuing growth: functional content must increase to maintain user satisfaction. Declining quality: quality will appear to decline unless the system is rigorously adapted to changes in its operational environment.14,15,16

Read as a control system, the laws describe a reinforcing loop with only one balancing arm. Use generates demands; demands generate change; change increases complexity; complexity raises the effort per subsequent change and degrades apparent quality; degraded quality generates more demands. The only place a maintainer can intervene is the complexity term, and the two intervention types are precisely the proactive ones: preventive and perfective work. Every law in the set says the same thing from a different angle — that the discretionary work is what keeps the non-discretionary work affordable.

Parnas gave the phenomenon its complementary framing. Software ages for two reasons: the world changes around it, and changes to the software itself degrade its structure. Crucially, the second cause is not inevitable but is the default. Ignorant surgery — changes made without understanding the design — damages the structure of a program in ways that make subsequent changes harder, and the damage accumulates. Parnas's remedies are unglamorous and remain correct: design for change, keep documentation that reflects the design rather than the code, and review changes against the design intent, not merely against the tests.17

Brooks arrived at the same place from a different direction. His distinction between the essential difficulty of software — the complexity of the conceptual construct itself — and the accidental difficulty of representing it explains why tooling improvements have never produced the order-of-magnitude reduction in maintenance cost that each generation expects: the complexity being managed is intrinsic to the problem, and changeability is one of its irreducible properties, because successful software gets used in ways its designers did not anticipate.56 The Mythical Man-Month put the mechanism more bluntly a decade earlier: program maintenance is an entropy-increasing process, and even the most skilful repairs tend to destroy structure and so hasten the decay of the system.57

It is worth noticing what these two accounts jointly rule out. They rule out the belief that a sufficiently good initial design removes the need for maintenance, because the forcing function is external. And they rule out the belief that maintenance effort is a function of code quality alone, because a well-built system embedded in a fast-moving environment will generate more adaptive work than a poorly-built one that nobody uses. The combination that produces unaffordable systems is high environmental change plus no standing allocation to the balancing arm — which is exactly what this article's fourth experiment measures.

MR-11Life cycle

Evolution, servicing, phaseout

The most useful single reframing of the maintenance life cycle is Rajlich and Bennett's staged model, which replaces the undifferentiated block called “maintenance” with five stages that have different activities, different tools and different business consequences: initial development, evolution, servicing, phaseout, and closedown.19

In evolution, the architecture still holds. Changes can be made that add substantial function, because the team understands the design well enough to extend it. In servicing, that is no longer true: the system receives patches and small corrections, but no structural change, because nobody can predict the consequences of one. Phaseout means even servicing has stopped and users are being moved off. Closedown is withdrawal.

Two properties of the model do the real work. First, the transitions are effectively one-way. A system that has fallen into servicing does not return to evolution by a management decision, because what was lost — architectural coherence and the people who held it — cannot be reinstated by scheduling. Second, and consequently, the transition from evolution to servicing is usually not noticed when it happens. It is diagnosed later, from symptoms: estimates that keep growing, changes that are made by addition rather than modification, a rising ratio of coordination to code.58

This gives the practitioner something to watch for, which the type taxonomy does not. The taxonomy tells you what kind of request you are handling. The staged model tells you what kind of system you are handling it in — and therefore whether a proposed piece of perfective work is an investment in continued evolution or a doomed attempt to re-enter it.

The commercial reading is sharper still. Servicing is cheaper per change and enormously more expensive per unit of capability, because the capability is not being added. An organisation running a portfolio of systems in servicing is paying maintenance costs without buying evolutionary capacity, which is a description of most large legacy estates. The decision that follows is the one the standard calls disposal and everyone else calls politics: which systems get the investment to be returned to evolution, which are held in servicing until their function can be moved, and which are switched off.2

MR-12Diagnosis

What “legacy” actually means

Legacy is used as an insult, a budget category and a technical description, and the three do not coincide. The most operationally useful definition is Michael Feathers': legacy code is code without tests. It is deliberately provocative and it points at the right property — not age, not language, not aesthetics, but the absence of a mechanism for knowing whether a change broke something. The associated technique, building characterisation tests that pin the system's current behaviour before altering it, is the standard opening move on any system whose behaviour is better known to its users than to its maintainers.59

The staged model supplies the complementary definition: a legacy system is one that has transitioned from evolution to servicing.19,58 And the auditors supply an operational one. When GAO scored 69 US federal systems for modernisation need in 2025, it used sixteen attributes including age, hardware age, vendor support status, use of legacy programming languages, cybersecurity risk, ability to implement zero-trust architecture, operating and labour costs, and whether a modernisation plan existed at all.1 That is what “legacy” looks like when it has to survive an audit.

The resulting portfolio is worth studying because it is unusually well documented. The eleven systems most in need of modernisation ranged from about 23 to 60 years old and cost approximately $754 million a year to operate and maintain. Eight used legacy programming languages such as COBOL and assembly language code; four had unsupported hardware, software or operating systems; seven were operating with known cybersecurity vulnerabilities that could not be remediated without modernisation; and eight of the eleven lacked complete modernisation plans.1

The detail that best captures the real risk is not technical. The Defense Department's contract-management system was initially developed in 1964 and its technology and architecture remain largely in use; officials told GAO that although they currently have the skills to maintain it, the average age of the developers and technical subject-matter experts on the team is above 60, and that finding COBOL and assembly-language developers is difficult with a significant learning curve once found.1 The system is not failing. Its maintenance arrangement is.

The IRS Individual Master File is the most-cited example of the same pattern: a batch-oriented system established for individual taxpayer account processing that still uses assembly language code and COBOL, on which the agency's filing season depends.60,61 GAO's longitudinal data is the sobering part. Of the ten most critical systems it identified in 2019, three modernisations had been completed by February 2025; of the wider set of 65, 28 were complete, 34 in progress and three not yet begun.62,1 Six years, three of ten. Any modernisation plan built on more optimistic arithmetic than that is a forecast, not a plan.

MR-13Options

The modernisation ladder

“Modernise it” is not a decision. The decision is which of a small number of dispositions to apply, and they differ by two orders of magnitude in cost and risk. The cloud-migration literature popularised a ladder of strategies — rehost, replatform, repurchase, refactor or re-architect, retire, retain — which generalises cleanly to modernisation of any kind.63 Read from the bottom up, the rungs are:

  • Retain. Do nothing, deliberately, and record why. Legitimate when the system is stable, the environment around it is not moving, and the exposure is understood. Becomes negligence when it is a default rather than a decision.
  • Retire. Establish that the function is no longer needed and switch it off. The cheapest available modernisation and the most consistently overlooked; the standard treats disposal as a first-class process for this reason.2
  • Encapsulate. Leave the system in place and put a modern interface in front of it, so that new consumers are insulated from it. Buys time and adds a component.
  • Rehost. Move the same software to different infrastructure without changing it. Addresses hardware obsolescence and nothing else.
  • Replatform. Change the substrate — database, runtime, operating system — while preserving the application's structure. This is adaptive maintenance at scale.
  • Repurchase. Replace with a commercial or hosted product and migrate the data. The maintenance burden moves rather than vanishing: it becomes vendor management, configuration, and integration.
  • Refactor or re-architect. Change the internal structure to restore the capacity to evolve, preserving external behaviour. The only rung that addresses the underlying cause.44
  • Rebuild or replace. Write it again against current requirements. The highest risk and the only option that discards the accumulated behavioural knowledge embedded in the old system — which is exactly the knowledge that nobody wrote down.

Two failure modes account for most bad choices. The first is picking a high rung for a low-rung problem: re-architecting a system whose actual defect is unsupported hardware. The second is picking a low rung for a high-rung problem: rehosting a system into the cloud and discovering that its maintenance cost was never about the hardware. GAO's findings suggest a third, more mundane one — picking a rung without a documented plan covering milestones, the work necessary, and the disposition of the legacy system, which was the specific gap in eight of the eleven most critical federal cases.1

Chikofsky and Cross's taxonomy remains the right vocabulary for the middle rungs. Reverse engineering is the analysis of a system to identify its components and their relationships and to create representations at a higher level of abstraction; restructuring transforms one representation into another at the same level while preserving external behaviour; re-engineering is examination and alteration to reconstitute the system in a new form.64 Modernisation programmes that skip the first and go straight to the third are the ones that discover, late, that the system did something nobody had documented.

MR-14Execution

Migration: choosing where to put the risk

Migration is where modernisation decisions become operational, and where the difference between competent and incompetent execution is most visible. The strategic choice is not how much work to do. It is where to concentrate the risk.

The big bang concentrates all of it at one instant. Everything runs on the old system until a cutover, and then everything runs on the new one. Its appeal is that it is easy to plan and easy to fund. Its defect is that the plan has exactly one moment where it can fail, and that moment is not rehearsable at full scale.

TSB's 2018 migration is the reference failure. Between 2015 and 2018 the bank designed, built and tested a new platform, then migrated corporate and customer services onto it over a single weekend in April 2018. The data migrated successfully and the platform immediately experienced technical failures, disrupting branch, telephone, online and mobile banking for all branches and a significant proportion of 5.2 million customers. The FCA and PRA fined TSB £48.65 million for operational risk management and governance failures including management of outsourcing risk,65,66 and the PRA separately fined the bank's former chief information officer for failing to take reasonable steps to ensure the outsourcing arrangement was adequately managed and supervised.67 The regulatory finding is not that the technology was wrong. It is that the organisation could not control a change of that shape.

The strangler fig distributes the risk instead. Fowler's 2004 formulation is that rather than attempting a single cutover, you gradually create a new system around the edges of the old, letting it grow until the old system is strangled.68 In practice this means a routing or facade layer through which traffic is redirected capability by capability, so that each transition is small and individually reversible.69,70 It costs more in total — you build and operate an interception layer, and you run two systems in parallel for a long time — and less at any one moment.

Within a codebase, the equivalent technique is branch by abstraction: introduce an abstraction over the part being replaced, build the new implementation behind it, switch consumers over incrementally, then remove the old implementation and, optionally, the abstraction.71 For data and interfaces, the corresponding pattern is expand and contract: add the new form alongside the old, migrate readers and writers, then withdraw the old. Every one of these is the same manoeuvre at a different scale — make old and new coexist so that the cutover stops being an event.

Two disciplines separate the successful cases from the rest. The first is the parallel run: operate both implementations on live traffic and compare outputs before the new one is authoritative. It is the only technique that tests the new system against the behaviour users actually depend on rather than against the specification. The second is a rehearsed rollback. A migration plan whose day-of-move section has no rollback step is not a plan with a risk; it is a plan that has decided to have no risk appetite.72,73

MR-15Contract one

Deprecation: a schedule, not an opinion

Deprecation is how a maintainer removes a capability without removing it suddenly. Done properly it is a published schedule with defined phases and an explicit escape hatch. Done improperly it is a note in a changelog followed, some releases later, by an outage in somebody else's system.

OpenJDK's withdrawal of the memory-access methods in sun.misc.Unsafe is the best-documented example of a phased removal currently in flight. JEP 471 deprecated the methods for removal in JDK 23, noting that although few developers use the class directly, many applications depend on it indirectly through libraries, and introduced a command-line option — --sun-misc-unsafe-memory-access={allow|warn|debug|deny} — so that teams could assess their exposure before anything broke.74 JEP 498 then moved the default for that option from allow to warn in JDK 24, issuing a runtime warning on first use, with later phases moving the default to deny and eventually removing the option altogether.75 The design is explicit that its purpose is to prepare the ecosystem and to help developers realise when their applications rely, directly or indirectly, on the methods being removed.74

Kubernetes takes the calendar approach: API versions that have reached general availability must be supported for at least twelve months or three releases after deprecation is announced, whichever is longer, and beta versions for at least nine months or three releases; a version may not be deprecated until a replacement at least as stable exists, and deprecated features must warn when used.76 Python's transition, by contrast, shows what happens when the schedule has to be renegotiated in public.30,31 The web platform's removals are governed by the same logic — TLS 1.0 and 1.1 were formally deprecated by the IETF, with the RFC serving as the coordination point that let every implementer move on a common schedule.77

How long does the window need to be? This article's third experiment models it directly. Four thousand simulated dependents are given log-normally distributed dependency-bump intervals with a 120-day median — many projects update often, a long tail almost never does — and the simulation reports how many are still calling the removed API on removal day, as a function of window length and of how loudly the removal is signalled.3

The result is a curve with two distinct levers. Lengthening a silent window from two releases to four cuts the share of dependents still broken on removal day from 29.5% to 15.5%; doubling again to eight releases only reaches 6.8%, and even a four-year silent window leaves 2.5% behind. Signalling is the cheaper lever. Holding the window at four releases and adding a runtime warning at the midpoint takes breakage from 15.5% to 9.6%; adding a deny-by-default phase with a documented opt-out takes it to 5.1% — better than a silent window four times as long. And nothing drives it to zero: the most aggressive policy over sixteen releases still leaves 0.42% of dependents calling the removed API on the day it disappears.

The shape of that result is the practical argument for the phased approach. Waiting has sharply diminishing returns because the population is heterogeneous: the projects that would have migrated do so early, and the residue consists of projects that are not listening at all. Signalling converts some of that residue, because a runtime warning reaches a maintainer who never read the release notes. And because no amount of either reduces the residue to zero, the last phase before removal should be reversible by the affected party — deny by default, with a documented opt-out that buys one more release. That is precisely the structure both OpenJDK and Go adopted.75,78

MR-16Contract two

Versioning: what the number claims

A version number is a compressed claim about compatibility. Semantic Versioning makes the claim explicit: given MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH, increment MAJOR for incompatible API changes, MINOR for backwards-compatible additions, and PATCH for backwards-compatible fixes.79 The scheme is not a guarantee, because nothing enforces it. It is a promise, and promises can be measured against behaviour.

They have been. Raemaekers, van Deursen and Visser analysed seven years of release history in Maven Central and found that around one third of all releases introduced at least one breaking change — and, critically, that the figure was the same for minor releases as for major ones, meaning version numbers carried no information about interface stability. They also found that deprecation tags, which could have signalled the coming breaks, were hardly used.80,81 A differentiated replication by Ochoa and colleagues over roughly 120,000 upgrades put the rate of breaking changes in non-major upgrades at 20.1%, while finding that only 7.9% of client programs were actually affected — the gap between a broken interface and a broken consumer being the surface area the consumer happens to use.82 A large study of the Go ecosystem found 86.3% of library upgrades complying with the scheme, and still 33.3% of client programs potentially affected by breaking changes.83

Two conclusions follow. First, compliance is a property you have to check, not declare. Second, even in a compliant ecosystem, consumers are exposed, because compliance is measured against the published interface and consumers depend on more than that.

The checking is mechanisable, which is what this article's second experiment demonstrates. It takes a library API, applies a candidate revision, and classifies every difference: a removed public symbol, a removed parameter, a parameter that has become required, and a changed or widened return type are breaking; a new symbol or a new optional parameter is additive; anything else is a patch. The required version bump is then the maximum severity present. Applied to a candidate revision that the team intended to ship as a minor release, it finds 6 breaking changes against 2 additive ones: a removed public method, two parameters that have become required, a parameter dropped, a return type changed, and a return widened to admit a null the caller never had to handle. The required bump is major; the declared bump was minor. A second revision of the same feature set produces 0 breaking changes and 5 additive ones, and the declared minor version is correct.3

The instructive part is the second revision. It delivers the same capability with zero breaking changes by making three different design choices: keep the removed method present as a deprecated no-op, make both new parameters optional with defaults preserving the old behaviour, and add the new-typed accessor beside the old one rather than changing the type in place. The version number was never the problem. The number was a symptom of a design that had not been asked to be compatible.3

Two variations are worth naming. Calendar versioning — encoding a release date rather than a compatibility claim — is honest when the product's contract is “we support the last N releases” rather than “this will not break you.” And Go's module system encodes the major version in the import path itself, so that v2 of a module is a different package that can coexist with v1 in the same build, which converts a breaking change from an upgrade problem into a coexistence problem.84

MR-17Contract three

Backward compatibility and the escape hatch

Deprecation and versioning both assume you know what your contract is. Hyrum's Law says you do not: with a sufficient number of users of an API, it does not matter what you promise in the contract, because all observable behaviours of your system will be depended on by somebody.85 Software Engineering at Google treats this as the governing constraint on change over time, and explicitly analogises it to entropy — something that can be mitigated but never eliminated.86

The examples are mundane and endless: iteration order of a collection documented as unordered, the exact wording of an error message, the order of fields in a serialised response, how long a call happens to take, which exception subclass is thrown, and — most awkwardly — bugs that consumers have written compensating code around. Google's answer is the Beyoncé Rule: if you liked it, you should have put a test on it. If a behaviour matters to you, it belongs in continuous integration, where the person changing the system will see it break and fix it; if it is not tested, the breakage is not the infrastructure team's problem.86 This is less a moral position than a mechanism for converting an implicit contract into an explicit one.

At the other extreme sits the Linux kernel's rule, which is that there is no contract question at all. Torvalds' formulation is that if a change results in user programs breaking, it is a bug in the kernel, and the commit is reverted unless it is fixed; the user space is never blamed, and the possibility that other unknown breakage exists is not an excuse for known breakage.87 The rule is absolute because the kernel's constituency is unbounded and unenumerable.

Most maintainers cannot afford either extreme, which is why the interesting engineering is in the middle: mechanisms that let a breaking change ship while giving affected parties a way to defer it.

Go's is the most systematic. The Go 1 compatibility promise commits that existing programs will continue to run correctly with new releases, and acknowledges that some changes — fixing buggy or insecure behaviour, enabling a newer protocol by default — are unavoidable and permitted. For those, the GODEBUG mechanism preserves the old behaviour behind a key-value setting; settings added for compatibility are maintained for a minimum of two years, some indefinitely, and the default for each is chosen by the Go version declared in the module's own go.mod, so upgrading the toolchain does not silently change behaviour.88,78,84 Microsoft's answer to the same problem is the application compatibility shim infrastructure: a shipped database of applications, drivers and BIOS versions against which Windows applies per-application workarounds, emulating its own former behaviour for programs that depended on it.89,90

The general principle is that a breaking change becomes survivable when the party who is broken can choose when to absorb it. That is what a GODEBUG setting, a shim, a compatibility flag and a supported previous API version all purchase: not the avoidance of the change, but control over its timing — which returns us to the two clocks.

MR-18Tooling

The apparatus that makes change cheap

Every argument in this article reduces at some point to a capability: can you make a change and find out quickly whether it broke something? Everything that raises the answer's confidence or lowers its latency is maintenance infrastructure, and it is the part of the budget that is easiest to defer and most expensive to lack. Voyager's engineers describe the endpoint of that deferral precisely: documented assembly language, no development tools, no testbed, no emulator, verification by inspection only.29

The apparatus has four layers. The first is a regression suite that encodes what must not change, plus characterisation tests capturing what the system currently does where nobody recorded what it should do.59 The second is continuous integration, which turns that suite from an artefact into a feedback loop and makes Hyrum's-Law dependencies visible at the moment they break.86,72 The third is deployment control: feature flags, canary releases, staged rollout and rehearsed rollback, which convert a change from an event into a process with an abort button. Knight Capital's loss is a case study in the absence of all three — a partial deployment nobody verified, a flag repurposed rather than retired, dormant code never removed, and a remediation attempt that had never been tested.24 The fourth is inventory: knowing which components you ship, at which versions, in which builds, which is what a software bill of materials provides and what Equifax could not produce.42,41

Above those sits automation for change itself. Large-scale mechanical changes across a codebase — migrating an API, replacing a deprecated call, updating a dependency's usage pattern — are better performed as programs than as pull requests. Rule-based transformation systems make a migration a reviewable, rerunnable artefact rather than a project.91

The current frontier is language-model-assisted migration, and there is now at least one substantial experience report with numbers attached. Google's engineers describe applying LLM-based tooling to internal migrations across product areas, reporting that across their case study 74.45% of code changes and 69.46% of individual edits were model-generated. For a JUnit3-to-JUnit4 migration they report 5,359 files and roughly 149,000 lines changed in three months, with about 87% of generated code committed without modification, and an estimated 50% reduction in the time the migration would otherwise have taken; a Joda-Time-to-java.time migration reached a higher figure still.92

Three qualifications belong with those numbers. The report is an experience report, not a controlled study, and says so. The authors observe that in most cases a human had to revert at least some model changes that were incorrect or unnecessary. And given the sensitivity of the modified code, effort still had to be spent carefully rolling out each change to users.92 Which is to say: the tooling compresses the mechanical transformation and leaves the verification, the staged rollout and the compatibility reasoning exactly where they were. Those were always the expensive parts.

MR-19Sustainment

The maintenance arrangement is part of the system

The standard's term sustainment covers the processes, procedures, people, material and information required to support, maintain and operate a system.2 The inclusion of people is not decorative. In most of the failures examined here, the component that had degraded past the point of safety was the arrangement, not the artefact.

The federal portfolio makes the point in personnel terms: a system from 1964 whose maintenance team's average age is above 60, in languages for which developers are hard to find and slow to train.1 Open source makes it in economic terms. The xz-utils backdoor succeeded because a widely-deployed compression library used by essentially every Linux distribution was maintained by one person with limited resources, and because pressure to add a co-maintainer was socially legitimate — it is what an overloaded project is supposed to do.45,46 The vulnerability was a governance property of a project with a bus factor of one.

What follows practically is that maintenance planning has to include the arrangement. The standard's maintenance plan asks for exactly this: designation of the maintenance organisation, resources, training, and an estimate of maintenance costs over the life of the product.2 Three of those four are about people. In practice the useful questions are unglamorous. Who is the second person who can make a non-trivial change to this system? When did they last do so? What would we do if the first person left this month? For components you depend on but do not own, the equivalent question is whether the project's support is proportionate to your reliance on it — which is what the Census II work was built to make answerable.47

There is a documentation corollary. Parnas's remedy for aging was to keep documentation that records the design and its rationale rather than paraphrasing the code, because the code cannot record why a structure was chosen and therefore cannot warn the next person away from destroying it.17 Most of what makes a system unchangeable is not missing information about what it does — that can be recovered by reading — but missing information about what it must not do, and why. That information exists only in people until somebody writes it down.

MR-20The end

Disposal: the maintenance nobody plans

14764 is unusual among engineering standards in devoting a full clause to software disposal: a disposal strategy, disposal tasks, disposal notification, post-disposal tasks and the archiving of records.2,4 It is the least-read clause in the document and the one whose absence is most visible in practice.

The reasons for the neglect are structural rather than technical. Nobody is promoted for switching something off. The cost of a retained system is diffuse — a licence here, a patch cycle there, a line in an inventory — while the cost of retiring it is concentrated, immediate and owned by one team. And retirement requires establishing that nothing depends on the system, which is the same problem as Hyrum's Law with worse tooling.

The consequences show up in every dataset in this article. GAO reports that of 231 legacy systems the IRS had identified, 191 had no specific or long-term plans, and that of 107 systems identified for retirement, 105 had no specific decommissioning plans.1 Systems identified for retirement, without a plan to retire them, remain in the estate consuming maintenance capacity indefinitely.

Two practices make disposal tractable. The first is to attach a removal date to everything at the moment it is deprecated, so that the deprecation carries a scheduled disposal rather than an intention. The second is to instrument before removing: log usage of the capability, and let the log rather than the argument establish who is still calling it. Both are the same discipline that makes deprecation work at API scale, applied at system scale.

It is also worth stating the positive case. Retirement is the only modernisation option that reduces total maintenance load rather than relocating it. Every other rung on the ladder leaves you with something to keep running.

MR-21Evidence

The case for a standing allocation

The recurring management question in maintenance is what proportion of capacity to reserve for work that no customer asked for. The argument is usually conducted with anecdote. This article's fourth experiment conducts it with a model, which is not the same as evidence but is at least explicit about its assumptions.3

The model runs one product for ten years at one hundred units of capacity per month. Each period, the environment takes a fixed adaptive tax. Latent faults surface in proportion to structural complexity — Lehman's second law made operational. Corrective work is served first out of what remains, because failures are not schedulable, and each fix costs more in a more complex product. Preventive work, if any capacity is allocated to it, removes latent faults before they surface and pays down complexity, with saturating returns inside a period. Whatever survives buys new function, which injects new latent faults and raises complexity again.14,15,3

With no standing preventive allocation, the capacity reaching new function falls from 25.0 units per period in year one to 2.8 in year ten — a 89% decline — while corrective work rises from 55.1 to 76.3 units per period and structural complexity reaches 4.31 times its as-designed value. A standing 25% allocation delivers 1404 units of function over the decade against 832: 69% more, while doing strictly less feature work in every single period. The optimum is broad — every allocation between 20% and 30% lands within 1.5% of the best result — and the expensive decision is not which number to choose but whether to choose zero.

Three features of that result are worth separating from the specific numbers, which are a product of the model's parameters and should not be quoted as measurements.

First, the decline is in the composition of capacity, not in the amount of it. The team in the zero-allocation run is not working less. It is working almost entirely on corrective and adaptive maintenance, which is what an organisation means when it says its engineers are busy and nothing is shipping.

Second, the optimum is broad and its exact location is not the interesting quantity. The penalty for choosing 20% when 25% was optimal is negligible. The penalty for choosing zero is most of the decade's output. This matches the practical experience that arguments about whether the allocation should be 15% or 20% are a waste of the meeting; the decision that matters is whether the allocation exists as a standing commitment rather than a residual.

Third, no allocation escapes the trend. Even at the optimum, new function per period falls sharply over ten years. That is the model reproducing Lehman's laws rather than contradicting them: preventive and perfective work are the balancing arm on a reinforcing loop, and a balancing arm slows a loop rather than reversing it. Preventive work buys years, not immunity. The systems that stay evolvable for decades are the ones that also periodically do the expensive thing — re-architecting — and the ones that retire the parts that no longer earn their keep.

MR-22Practice

What good looks like

Collecting the article's arguments into things a team can actually do, in rough order of cost:

  1. Classify every modification request at intake, and make the field mandatory. Correction or enhancement; reactive or proactive; which of the five types. It is the cheapest instrument in this article and the only one that cannot be added retrospectively.2,3
  2. Keep an inventory with dates. Every dependency, runtime, platform and protocol you rely on, with its announced end-of-life date. Adaptive work is almost always announced in advance; being surprised by it is a records failure, not a technical one.33
  3. Keep a component inventory that answers “where do we run this?” in an afternoon. That is the question a vulnerability advisory asks, and the one Equifax could not answer inside five months.42,41,40
  4. Make the standing preventive allocation explicit and defend it in the plan, not in the retrospective. Any reasonable number beats treating it as residual.3
  5. Attach a removal date to every deprecation, and phase it. Announce, warn, deny by default with a documented opt-out, remove. Signalling does more work than waiting, and the opt-out is what makes the last step survivable.74,75,76,78,3
  6. Let the diff choose the version number. Classify every interface difference mechanically and take the maximum severity. If the required bump is unwelcome, change the design, not the number.79,81,3
  7. Test the behaviours you care about, including the ones you did not document. An untested behaviour that consumers depend on is a liability with no owner.86,85
  8. Build the characterisation harness before touching a legacy system, not after.59
  9. Migrate incrementally unless you can articulate why a single cutover is safer. Coexistence — strangler fig, branch by abstraction, expand and contract, parallel run — costs more in total and less at any one moment.68,71,70
  10. Rehearse the rollback. A rollback that has never been executed is an assumption.24,72
  11. Name the second maintainer, and check the answer annually.1,47
  12. Plan disposal at the same time as adoption. Retirement is the only modernisation that reduces total load.2

None of this is novel, and most of it was known by 1980. The reason it remains worth restating is that the forces described in this article do not respond to being understood. The environment will keep moving whether or not you have an inventory; consumers will keep depending on undocumented behaviour whether or not you tested it; complexity will keep rising whether or not anyone has budgeted for the balancing arm. Maintenance is the practice of arranging matters so that when those things happen, they are modification requests rather than incidents.

Which is the whole argument, restated: you do not get to choose whether a running system changes. You get to choose whether the change arrives as work you planned or as work that happened to you.

  which is why the escape hatch, not the calendar, is what makes removal safe.

  With no standing preventive allocation, the capacity reaching new function

  A standing 25% allocation delivers 1404 units over ten years against 832:
  69% more function while doing strictly less feature work per period.

  The optimum is broad: every allocation from 20% to 30% lands within 1.5% of the best result.
  The expensive decision is not which number to pick. It is picking zero.
  No allocation escapes the decline: even at 25%, new function per
  period falls to 4.3 by year 10. Preventive work buys years, not immunity.

Reading, standards and data

Standards and reference works

  • ISO/IEC/IEEE 14764:2022 — the maintenance process itself: types, modification requests, the maintenance plan, and the disposal clause. The single most useful document in this article's bibliography for anyone writing a maintenance policy.2
  • ISO/IEC/IEEE 12207:2017 — the life-cycle process framework that 14764 elaborates.4
  • SWEBOK Guide v4.0 (IEEE Computer Society, 2024) — the software maintenance knowledge area, plus the newer operations and security areas that overlap it.21
  • ISO/IEC 25010:2023 and ISO/IEC 5055:2021 — the vocabulary for maintainability as a quality characteristic, and automated source-code measures for counting violations rather than debating them.5,48

Foundational papers worth reading in the original

  • Swanson, The Dimensions of Maintenance (1976) — five pages; the classification and the reasoning behind it.10
  • Lehman, Programs, Life Cycles, and Laws of Software Evolution (1980) — the S/P/E classification is the part that has aged best.14
  • Parnas, Software Aging (1994) — the clearest short statement of why structure degrades and what prevents it.17
  • Rajlich & Bennett, A Staged Model for the Software Life Cycle (2000) — six pages that change how you read a maintenance budget.19
  • Schach et al., Determining the Distribution of Maintenance Categories (2003) — the survey-versus-measurement result.13

Practitioner books

  • Feathers, Working Effectively with Legacy Code — characterisation tests and seams; still the best book on changing systems you do not understand.59
  • Fowler, Refactoring, 2nd ed. — the transformation catalogue.44
  • Winters, Manshreck & Wright, Software Engineering at Google — chapter 1 on programming over time is the best available treatment of Hyrum's Law and its consequences.86
  • Newman, Monolith to Microservices — the incremental-migration patterns, including strangler fig applied concretely.70
  • Humble & Farley, Continuous Delivery; Nygard, Release It! — the deployment-control and operability apparatus.72,73

Policies worth copying

  • Kubernetes deprecation policy — the clearest published set of removal windows.76
  • Go's compatibility documents and the GODEBUG mechanism — the best worked example of an escape hatch with a defined lifetime.84,78,88
  • OpenJDK JEPs 471 and 498 — a phased removal of a widely-used internal API, documented as it happens.74,75
  • Semantic Versioning 2.0.0 — short, and worth reading alongside the empirical work on how often it is followed.79,80,82

Data sources

  • GAO's legacy-systems reports (GAO-19-471, GAO-25-107795) — the best public longitudinal data on large legacy portfolios anywhere.62,1
  • Verizon DBIR, annual — the patching-latency and exploitation figures.37,39
  • CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue — the prioritisation input most patch programmes should be driven by.38
  • Linux Foundation Census II — which widely-used components rest on thin support.47
