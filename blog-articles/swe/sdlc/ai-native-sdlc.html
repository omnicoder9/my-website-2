1Intent, cheapened

For most of the history of software engineering, the expensive step was turning a decision into code. Requirements, designs and reviews existed largely to make sure that expensive step was spent on the right thing. When a coding agent can produce a plausible pull request in minutes, that economy inverts. Code becomes cheap, and the scarce resource becomes the ability to establish that what was produced is what was meant.

That is the shift this article calls the AI-native software development lifecycle. The phrase is used loosely, often as a synonym for “using coding assistants a lot”. A more useful definition comes from the research vision that popularised the term: Hassan and colleagues distinguish AI-assisted software engineering, in which foundation-model tools help with tasks inside an unchanged process, from an AI-native discipline organised around intent-centric, conversation-oriented collaboration with AI teammates1. The distinguishing test is not how much code a model writes. It is whether the operating model of the engineering organisation has been redesigned around the fact that it does.

The redesign has an obvious half and a less obvious half. The obvious half is that agents now read issues, plan, edit many files, run tests and open pull requests. The less obvious half is where that leaves everything else in the lifecycle. Every stage of a development process is a translation: from what a stakeholder wants into words, from words into a specification, from a specification into a design, from a design into code, from code into a running service. Each translation can lose some of the original intent. In a traditional process the losses were caught, imperfectly, by the people doing the translating, because they were slow and they carried context from one step to the next. An agent is fast and, unless it is given that context, carries none.

So this article organises everything around one question: at each stage of the lifecycle, where is intent held, and what checks that the artefact still matches it? The answer varies enormously. Some intent lives in a schema that a pipeline validates on every commit. Some lives in a constitution file that an agent reads and may ignore. Some lives only in a reviewer’s head at the moment they approve a diff too large to read. The practices that make up an AI-native lifecycle—spec-driven development, context and harness engineering, automated verification, AI-aware security—can each be understood as a way of moving intent up that ladder, from places where only a person can check it to places where a machine can.

The argument proceeds in four steps. Sections 2 and 3 set out how the lifecycle got here and what the evidence on AI productivity does and does not show. Sections 4 to 10 walk the lifecycle stage by stage, with a small deterministic lab whose numbers make several of the claims concrete. Section 11 places the methodologies and standards that are beginning to codify the field. Section 12 collects twenty-four practices as plates, each coded by where its intent is held and who closes the gap, and Section 13 says what the census shows.

2A short history of where intent was kept

Every lifecycle model is, among other things, a theory of where intent should be written down. The document most often cited as the origin of the sequential “waterfall”, Winston Royce’s 1970 paper, actually argued that a single pass from requirements to operation was risky and recommended iteration, including doing the job twice2. The word “waterfall” itself appears later, in a 1976 paper by Bell and Thayer3. What the sequential reading took from Royce was the idea that intent is captured early, in documents, and then carried forward by people.

The problem with that idea was named repeatedly. Parnas and Clements argued in 1986 that no real project proceeds rationally from requirements to code, and that the documents should be written as if it had, because the documents are what later readers need4. Lehman’s laws of software evolution made the complementary point: a program used in the real world must keep changing or become less useful, so any specification that is not maintained alongside it decays5. Brooks, in “No Silver Bullet”, separated the essential difficulty of software—deciding precisely what to build—from the accidental difficulty of expressing it, and predicted that no tool attacking only the accidents would yield an order-of-magnitude gain6. Boehm’s spiral model turned risk, rather than documents, into the organising principle of the lifecycle7.

The 2001 Agile Manifesto moved intent out of documents and into conversation and working software8. Test-driven development made the test the first written statement of intent for each unit of behaviour9, and behaviour-driven development pushed that statement upward into examples readable by the business10. Continuous delivery made the deployment pipeline the place where every change is checked against everything already known11, and the research programme that became DORA showed that throughput and stability, measured at the delivery pipeline, predict organisational performance12. Underneath all of these sat an older tradition that tried to hold intent in a form a machine could check: axiomatic program reasoning13, design by contract14, mutation analysis as a way of testing the tests15, and property-based testing16.

Large language models entered this history as an attack on the accidental difficulty. Codex, published in 2021, solved 28.8% of the HumanEval problems on a single sample and 70.2% when 100 samples were drawn per problem, against 0% for GPT-317. GitHub Copilot opened as a technical preview the same summer, described as an AI pair programmer18. In 2023 SWE-bench moved evaluation from self-contained functions to 2,294 real GitHub issues across 12 Python repositories, where the best model at publication resolved 1.96%19. The interface the model worked through turned out to matter as much as the model: SWE-agent’s purpose-built agent-computer interface reached 12.5% on the same benchmark20.

2025 was the year agents became products. Claude Code launched as a research preview in February21, OpenAI’s cloud Codex agent in May22, and GitHub’s Copilot coding agent days later, working from assigned issues inside a GitHub Actions environment23. The infrastructure for connecting agents to tools and to each other was standardised in parallel: Anthropic open-sourced the Model Context Protocol in November 202424, Google’s Agent2Agent protocol moved to the Linux Foundation in June 202525, and in December 2025 the Linux Foundation formed the Agentic AI Foundation around MCP, Block’s goose and OpenAI’s AGENTS.md, by then adopted by more than 60,000 open-source projects26. Measurement work at METR found that the length of software tasks frontier agents can complete with 50% reliability had been doubling roughly every seven months27.

Alongside the agents came a counter-movement. “Vibe coding”—Andrej Karpathy’s term for prompting a model in natural language and accepting what comes back—was named Collins Word of the Year in November 202528. Within months, GitHub, Amazon and others were shipping tools built on the opposite premise: that the specification, not the prompt, should be the durable artefact29,30. Standards bodies followed. IEEE approved a recommended practice for a GPT-empowered software engineering life cycle in September 202631, and a reference model mapping generative-AI tool capabilities onto the ISO/IEC/IEEE 12207 and 15288 processes was authorised in May 202632.

3Three operating models and what the evidence shows

It helps to separate three operating models that are often run together. They differ less in how much code a model writes than in which activities are designed around the model and who holds responsibility for what.

ModelWho implementsWhere AI sitsWhat checks the workAccountability
TraditionalPeople, with conventional automationBuild and test toolsHuman review of human codeEngineers own every step
AI-assistedPeople, with completion and chatInside individual tasks: suggest, explain, draft testsHuman review; tests mostly human-writtenEngineers remain the implementers
AI-nativeAgents, from specifications and plansAcross the lifecycle: clarify, plan, implement, test, open pull requests, respond to CIPipeline checks independent of the generator, plus human approval of consequential changesPeople set intent and boundaries, design checks, and stay accountable

An organisation using completion to write Java methods is AI-assisted. One where agents interpret requirements, plan, modify Spring services, run integration tests, open pull requests and respond to CI failures, inside checks it does not control, is moving toward AI-native.

The evidence on whether any of this makes teams faster is more contested than either enthusiasts or sceptics tend to admit, and the disagreement is instructive. In a 2023 controlled experiment, developers given GitHub Copilot completed a task—writing an HTTP server in JavaScript—55.8% faster than a control group33. In a 2025 randomised trial, 16 experienced open-source developers working on 246 real tasks in repositories they knew well took 19% longer when AI tools were allowed; they had forecast a 24% speed-up beforehand and still believed afterwards that they had been sped up by 20%34. When METR attempted a larger follow-up with 57 developers across 143 repositories, its point estimates moved to speed-ups of 18% for returning developers and 4% for new ones, with confidence intervals spanning zero; but the study could not be completed as designed, because developers increasingly declined tasks they did not want to do without AI, and METR concluded that its estimate was a poor proxy for the real effect35.

Organisation-level data shows a different gap. Faros AI’s analysis of more than 10,000 developers in 1,255 teams found that teams with high AI adoption completed 21% more tasks and merged 98% more pull requests, while pull-request review time rose 91% and average pull-request size rose 154%; company-level delivery metrics showed no significant correlation with adoption36. The 2025 DORA report, drawing on nearly 5,000 respondents, found AI adoption now positively associated with delivery throughput but still negatively associated with delivery stability, and summarised its findings as the claim that AI does not fix a team; it “amplifies what’s already there”37.

Developer sentiment tracks the same tension. In Stack Overflow’s 2025 survey, 84% of respondents used or planned to use AI tools, but distrust of AI accuracy rose to 46%, and the most common frustration was solutions that are almost right but not quite38. The 2026 survey, published in October, reported that 66% of respondents using AI now use coding agents, with Claude Code and GitHub Copilot the most used, but only 20% use AI for deploying, operating or troubleshooting production systems39. Generation has moved into the lifecycle much faster than verification and operation have.

Read together, these results are less contradictory than they look. Where the task is self-contained and the check is cheap (does the server respond?), generation dominates and the gain is large. Where the task sits inside a large codebase with implicit constraints, the time saved writing is spent establishing that the result fits. At organisation scale, volume rises and the cost moves to review and stability. The variable that explains the spread is not the model. It is how much of the intent the work must satisfy is available, in checkable form, at the moment the work is done.

4The lifecycle as a chain of translations

An AI-native lifecycle keeps the familiar stages but changes what each one produces and who consumes it. The figure below lays out eight stages as a chain of translations, each turning one artefact into the next. Under each stage is the artefact that carries intent forward, and the colour of the marker gives the mean custody rung of the practices at that stage (defined in Section 12): how much of the stage’s intent survives in a form a machine can check.

Discovery and requirements. Agents can draft user stories, propose domain models and, most usefully, ask clarifying questions. AWS’s AI-Driven Development Lifecycle (AI-DLC) makes this a ritual it calls mob elaboration, in which the whole team validates the model’s questions and proposals together before any construction40. The risk at this stage is fluency: Breaux and Singhal warn that well-formed AI-written requirements can mislead human evaluators, and argue that requirements should be judged by their usefulness on long-horizon tasks rather than by the quality of their prose41.

Specification and planning. This is where spec-driven development (Section 5) lives: specifications, acceptance criteria, an explicit plan and a task list that agents will execute. Breaux and Singhal describe the broader movement as a pendulum swinging back toward plan-driven development, because cheaper implementation makes up-front precision worth more41.

Architecture and design. Interface contracts, schemas and threat models become agent inputs as well as human documents. The strongest practices here encode architecture as something a build can fail on (Section 7).

Implementation. Agents edit code in sandboxes, run the project’s tests and iterate. OpenAI’s Codex, for example, runs each task in an isolated cloud sandbox preloaded with the repository and cites terminal logs and test outputs as evidence of what it did22. The evidence an agent produces about its own work is itself an artefact to be checked.

Verification and testing. The stage that changes most (Section 6). When the same kind of system writes the code and the tests, the tests stop being independent evidence.

Review and integration. Human review becomes the bottleneck as change volume rises (Section 10). GitHub’s coding agent pushes commits to a draft pull request, and its pull requests require human approval before CI/CD workflows run23—a design choice that places a person at the integration boundary by default.

Deployment and release. Continuous delivery practices carry over almost unchanged, and matter more: progressive rollout and automatic rollback are the runtime checks that catch what earlier stages miss11.

Operations and feedback. Production behaviour, incidents and user feedback should flow back into the specification, not just into the code. Breaux and Singhal propose exactly this ordering for brownfield systems: change requests update the specification first and then propagate to scenarios, plans, tests and code41. The feedback arrow in the figure is the one most organisations do not yet have.

5Specifications as operational inputs

Spec-driven development (SDD) makes a structured, version-controlled specification the central artefact that guides implementation. In a conventional process, a specification is documentation: people read it, interpret it and write code, and it drifts. In SDD the specification is an operational input to agents and to the pipeline. The central idea is simple: instead of asking an agent to build something from a short prompt, give it a structured, testable description of the outcome.

Tools and workflows

GitHub released Spec Kit, an open-source toolkit for this workflow, on 2 September 202529. It installs a set of agent commands that move through gated phases—specify, plan, tasks and implement—with each phase validated before the next begins, and it works with several agents including GitHub Copilot, Claude Code and Gemini CLI42. A project constitution records the principles every later phase must respect, and a clarify step resolves ambiguities before planning. By May 2026 the project had a dedicated lead maintainer, an extension system approaching 100 extensions and support for more than 30 agents, and its stated principle was that specifications don’t serve code—“code serves specifications”43.

Amazon’s Kiro, an agentic IDE previewed in July 2025 and generally available in November, builds the same idea into the editor30. Its specs move through requirements, design and an implementation plan, and requirements are written as user stories with acceptance criteria in EARS notation, of the form WHEN a condition THE SYSTEM SHALL respond44. EARS itself dates from a 2009 Rolls-Royce paper that reduced natural-language requirements to five patterns—ubiquitous, event-driven, state-driven, unwanted behaviour and optional feature—each introduced by a keyword45. Kiro’s general-availability release added property-based testing developed with AWS’s Automated Reasoning Group, which extracts properties from the specification and tests the code against them rather than against chosen examples30.

How authoritative is the specification?

Birgitta Böckeler’s review of Kiro, Spec Kit and the Tessl framework introduced a distinction that has since become standard46. A spec-first process writes a specification before coding and uses it for the task at hand. A spec-anchored process keeps the specification after the task and uses it for the feature’s ongoing evolution. A spec-as-source process treats the specification as the primary artefact: humans edit only the specification, and the code is generated from it. Breaux and Singhal adopt the same three levels and add the mechanism that distinguishes the middle one: in a spec-anchored process, alignment between specification and code is enforced through testing41.

Böckeler’s finding was that nearly every tool she examined was spec-first in practice. Spec Kit creates a branch per specification, which suggests the lifespan of a change request rather than of a feature; only Tessl explicitly aimed at spec-anchored and spec-as-source, marking generated files as not to be edited, and even at its one-spec-per-file granularity she observed different code generated from the same specification46. She also drew the obvious historical parallel: spec-as-source resembles model-driven development, which never took off for business applications, and risks inheriting both its inflexibility and the non-determinism of language models. Her closing worry was that SDD might turn out to be a Verschlimmbesserung—an improvement that makes things worse—by feeding agents existing workflows too literally.

The lab makes the stakes of the three levels concrete with a deliberately simple drift model (experiment E5). Suppose each change request touches 3% of the statements in a specification, and that after the change the affected statements are updated with probability u. With u = 0, which is spec-first, half the specification is wrong after 22.8 change requests. With manual updating at u = 0.6, the half-life is 57.4. When tests enforce alignment, as in the spec-anchored definition, and u rises to 0.95, it is 462. Spec-as-source moves the problem rather than removing it: if each regeneration has a 5% chance of changing some behaviour the specification does not mention, half of that unspecified behaviour has changed after 13.5 regenerations. The parameters are illustrative; the shape is the point. Drift is governed by the enforcement mechanism, not by the quality of the first draft.

A worked example

Consider a funds-transfer capability in a Spring Boot microservice. A prompt-first request—“build an API that lets users transfer money between accounts”—leaves every important question open: currencies, inactive accounts, duplicate submissions, partial failure, authorisation, concurrent transfers against the same balance. The EARS lines can be parsed and the Gherkin scenarios can be executed. Neither guarantees the specification is complete, and the lab found an example in its own version of this specification. While writing invariant-based tests for the transfer function (Section 6), it emerged that the acceptance criterion “duplicate requests return the original result” does not say whether a rejected request consumes its idempotency key. If it does, a retry after the customer tops up their balance must still be rejected; if it does not, the retry should succeed. Both readings are defensible, the specification above is silent, and an agent would pick one without saying so. That is the residue SDD cannot remove by structure alone: the specification can only be as complete as the questions someone thought to ask, which is why the clarify step and mob elaboration exist.

Dijkstra made the general version of this argument decades before language models: natural language makes it easy to state nonsense that a formal notation would rule out, so programming in it would not make the work simpler47. SDD is in effect a negotiated position between his view and vibe coding. It keeps natural language for what people must agree on, constrains it with templates like EARS where structure helps, and moves to executable or formal notations—scenarios, schemas, properties—wherever a machine must decide whether the result conforms.

6The oracle problem returns

A test is only as good as its oracle: the means of deciding whether an output is correct. The oracle problem was a central topic in testing research long before AI48. AI-native development makes it acute in a specific way. When a model writes both the implementation and its tests from the same context, the tests are no longer independent evidence. They can encode the same misreading of the specification that the code does.

Correlated oracles

Experiment E2 quantifies this with a minimal model. Each ambiguity in a specification is resolved wrongly by the implementer with probability 0.20. A test catches a wrong resolution only if the test’s author read the specification correctly. If the test author is independent, a wrong implementation is caught 80% of the time. If with probability ρ the test simply inherits the implementer’s reading—because the same agent, in the same context, wrote both—the catch rate falls linearly to 20% at ρ = 0.75 and 8% at ρ = 0.9. Per hundred ambiguities, escaped misreadings rise from 4.0 to 18.4. A Monte Carlo run over 200,000 ambiguities agrees with the closed form to within 0.003. This is why so many AI-native workflows insist that acceptance criteria be written, or at least approved, by someone other than the implementing agent.

Coverage is not checking

Experiment E3 runs real mutation analysis on a 16-line implementation of the transfer function from Section 5. The lab parses the function, generates 20 mutants (flipped comparisons, swapped arithmetic, off-by-one constants, swapped rejection reasons and deleted statements) and runs three test suites against each. The first suite is typical of quickly generated tests: it exercises every branch and asserts only the status of each result. It achieves 100% line coverage and kills 9 (45%) of the mutants. The second suite is written from the acceptance criteria and asserts balances, reasons and idempotence; it kills 20 (100%). The third checks invariants derived from the specification—money is conserved, no balance goes negative, replays change nothing, a valid transfer is never rejected—over 300 random operation sequences, and kills 16 (80%). Every suite has the same line coverage. Coverage measures what ran, not what was checked; mutation analysis, introduced in 1978 as a way of testing the tests15, measures the second.

The suites also fail in complementary ways. The invariant suite misses all four reason-swap mutants, because conservation and idempotence hold whichever rejection reason is returned. Experiment E4 shows the converse. It introduces a realistic bug that no mutation operator in E3 generates: the duplicate-request check is moved below the balance check, so a replayed request whose original succeeded is re-validated against the current balance. The acceptance suite, which killed every mutant, passes this implementation. The random invariant suite finds it in a median of 2 generated sequences, at most 8, across 200 independent seeds. The targeted counterexample is two lines long: a first transfer of 40 from a balance of 50 completes, and its replay is rejected for insufficient funds.

Industrial experience points the same way. Meta’s TestGen-LLM, which only proposes tests that build, pass reliably and raise coverage, found that 75% of generated tests built correctly, 57% passed reliably and 25% increased coverage; engineers accepted 73% of its recommendations49. Its successor, ACH, inverts the goal: it generates mutants representing specific fault classes and then generates tests that kill them, with engineers accepting 73% of its tests across Messenger and WhatsApp test-a-thons50. The shift from “generate tests that pass” to “generate tests that fail on the faults we care about” is the oracle problem being taken seriously.

When the benchmark is the oracle

The same issue appears in the evaluation of coding agents themselves. EvalPlus showed that extending HumanEval with roughly 80 times more tests substantially lowered measured pass rates, because the original tests accepted many incorrect solutions51. In February 2026 OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified: an audit of 138 problems that its o3 model often failed found material defects in 59.4%, including overly strict tests that reject correct solutions, and every frontier model tested could reproduce gold patches or verbatim problem details for some tasks52. And agents optimise against whatever check they are given. METR documented frontier models overwriting a grader’s timer, monkey-patching an evaluator so every submission passed, and overriding an equality operator; on its RE-Bench tasks, 39 of 128 runs (30.4%) involved reward hacking53. An agent that is rewarded for green tests will, sometimes, make the tests green.

Retrying until green

A common pattern in agent harnesses is to retry until a check passes. Experiment E6 shows what that buys. If an attempt is correct with probability p = 0.8, the chance that at least one of eight attempts is correct (pass@8) is 0.999997, but the chance that all eight are correct (pass^8) is 0.168. A pipeline that relies on an agent behaving correctly every time it runs needs the second number, not the first. And retrying until a verifier accepts converges not to p but to the verifier’s error: if the check wrongly accepts a bad attempt with probability a, the fraction of accepted results that are wrong is (1−p)a / (p + (1−p)a), which is 4.76% at a = 0.2 and 11.1% at a = 0.5 (a 200,000-run simulation gives 4.69% for the first). Retrying makes a weak check the limiting factor. Improving the check is worth more than improving the generator.

The top of the ladder

At the far end of the ladder are formal methods: specifications precise enough that a tool can check every reachable state. Hoare’s axiomatic basis is the root of this line13; the best-known industrial account is Amazon’s use of TLA+ to find subtle bugs in distributed protocols that testing had missed54. The attraction for AI-native development is obvious—if a model writes the code, a proof checker is an oracle that does not share its blind spots—but so is the cost. Formal models must be kept in correspondence with the code, which is the drift problem of Section 5 in its hardest form. Property-based testing is the practical midpoint: properties stated once, checked over thousands of generated inputs, with none of the obligation to prove16.

7Context and harness engineering

An agent can only honour intent it can see. Two disciplines have formed around that fact.

Context engineering is the practice of deciding what information an agent has in its context window at each step. Anthropic’s engineering team framed it in September 2025 as curating the smallest set of high-signal tokens for the task, noting that recall degrades as context grows—“context rot”—so the attention budget must be managed55. The techniques they describe for long tasks are compaction (summarising a nearly full context and continuing in a fresh window), structured note-taking to files outside the window, sub-agents that work in clean contexts and return condensed summaries, and just-in-time retrieval, where the agent holds lightweight references such as file paths and loads content only when needed55. Prompt engineering is about how an instruction is phrased; context engineering is about the whole information environment: rules, code, documentation, retrieved history, tool descriptions and state.

Harness engineering is the broader discipline of building the environment around the agent: what it can touch, how its work is checked and when it must stop for a person. OpenAI’s February 2026 account of an internal product built with Codex gives the most concrete data point so far56. Over about five months, a team that grew from three engineers to seven shipped roughly a million lines of code with no manually written source, opening and merging about 1,500 pull requests at an average of 3.5 per engineer per day. The practices they credit are almost all about intent custody. The AGENTS.md file is kept to about 100 lines and treated as a table of contents into a structured documentation directory, because a single large instruction file crowded out context and went stale. Architectural layering is enforced mechanically by structural tests, so an agent cannot add a forbidden dependency. Agents read logs, metrics and traces to reproduce bugs. The summary principle was three words: “Humans steer. Agents execute.”56

The interface between the agent and the environment is itself a design problem. SWE-agent showed that a purpose-built agent-computer interface, with commands for viewing, searching and editing files sized for a model rather than a person, materially improved results20. MCP standardises how tools and context sources are described to agents, and by December 2025 had more than 10,000 published servers26. A typed tool description is a contract over the shape of a call; it does not say when the tool is the right one to call, which is why tool selection remains a common failure.

Not every problem needs an autonomous agent. Anthropic’s guidance on building agents draws a line between workflows, where model calls are orchestrated through predefined code paths, and agents, which direct their own process, and recommends starting with the simplest composition that works57. The Agentless study made the point empirically: a fixed three-phase pipeline of localisation, repair and patch validation resolved 32% of SWE-bench Lite at about $0.70 per issue, competitive with far more elaborate agents at the time58. Industrial deployments often look like this. Google’s account of AI-assisted code migrations describes LLM-generated changes inside a structured pipeline with human review; in its reported migrations about 80% of the code modifications in landed change lists were AI-authored and total migration time fell by an estimated 50%59.

One architectural distinction is easy to blur. Orchestrating an agent’s reasoning steps (what to read, what to try next) is a different problem from orchestrating software delivery (build, test, deploy). Agent frameworks do the first; CI/CD systems do the second. An AI-native lifecycle needs both, and the boundary between them is where permissions, audit and approval gates belong.

8Many agents, one contract

A natural extension is to assign specialised agents to product, architecture, backend, frontend, testing, security and operations, mirroring a human team. This buys parallelism and costs coordination. The most systematic study of failures in multi-agent LLM systems, MAST, annotated more than 1,600 traces across seven frameworks and found fourteen failure modes in three groups: specification and system design problems, inter-agent misalignment, and weak task verification60. The categories are a lifecycle in miniature: agents that do not share a precise statement of the task, do not agree on interfaces and do not check each other’s results.

Experiment E7 shows why shared contracts matter more as teams of agents grow. If each pair of agents that coordinate through implicit assumptions has a 5% chance of holding inconsistent ones, the probability of at least one inconsistency among n agents grows with the number of pairs, n(n−1)/2. With seven agents there are 21 pairs and the probability is 65.9%. If instead each agent conforms to a single versioned contract—an OpenAPI or AsyncAPI document, a shared schema—and contract tests catch 90% of non-conformance, the risk grows only with n and is 3.4% for seven agents. Contract-first development, long useful between human teams, becomes close to mandatory between agents.

The broader lesson matches the Agentless result: a multi-agent architecture is not better by default than one capable agent with good tools and a well-specified task. It is better when the work genuinely decomposes along interfaces that can be written down and checked.

9Security as a property of the lifecycle

Security research on AI-generated code has been consistent for five years. In 2021–22, Pearce and colleagues generated 1,689 programs from 89 scenarios with GitHub Copilot and found approximately 40% vulnerable61. Perry and colleagues found in a user study that participants with an AI assistant wrote significantly less secure code than those without one, and were more likely to believe it was secure62. Veracode’s 2025 evaluation of more than 100 models on 80 coding tasks found security flaws introduced in 45% of cases, over 70% for Java, and no meaningful improvement with newer or larger models, even as syntactic accuracy improved63.

Agents add new routes to harm. Code-generating models recommend packages that do not exist: one study of 576,000 code samples from 16 models found 19.7% of recommended packages were hallucinated, a supply-chain opening now known as “slopsquatting” when an attacker registers the invented name64. Quality debt accumulates too. GitClear’s analysis of 211 million changed lines found that 2024 was the first year copy-pasted lines outnumbered moved (refactored) lines, with duplicated blocks of five or more lines rising about eightfold65.

The governance response is to treat AI-generated changes as ordinary changes subject to stronger gates, not as a separate category. NIST’s Secure Software Development Framework (SP 800-218) remains the baseline practice set66, and its community profile SP 800-218A adds practices specific to developing generative-AI models and the systems that use them across the lifecycle67. OWASP’s Top 10 for agentic applications catalogues risks specific to agents that act, such as goal hijacking and tool misuse68. In terms of this article’s ladder, the security practices that work are the ones that move a property from K1 (a reviewer’s alertness) to K4 (a scanner, policy or provenance check that runs on every change): secret detection, dependency existence and signature checks, static analysis, least-privilege tool permissions and sandboxed execution.

10Review is the new bottleneck

If generation gets cheaper and verification does not, the cost of the lifecycle moves to verification. Human review is the most visible place this happens. Experiment E1 makes the arithmetic explicit. A team has a fixed 10 hours of review attention per day. Each change carries a defect with probability 0.10, and the chance a reviewer spots it grows with the time spent, as 1 − e−t/τ with τ = 0.5 hours. At ten changes a day each gets an hour and review catches 86.5% of defects. Doubling volume to twenty halves the time per change, recall falls to 63.2%, and defects escaping per day rise by a factor of 5.44, not two. Holding escaped defects constant at the doubled volume needs 26.9 review hours a day, 2.69 times the original. That is consistent with what Faros measured—98% more pull requests and 91% more review time36—and with DORA’s finding that adoption still harms stability37.

The remedies follow from the model. Smaller changes make each review cheaper. Moving checks out of the reviewer’s head and into the pipeline—contract tests, property tests, structural tests, policy gates—means the reviewer reads for intent rather than for everything. And measuring the right things exposes the shift: DORA’s delivery metrics (lead time, deployment frequency, change failure rate and recovery time) remain the core measures12, supplemented by agent-specific ones such as intervention rate, rework rate and the share of review time spent on AI output.

DORA’s follow-on research names the organisational conditions. Its AI Capabilities Model identifies seven capabilities that amplify AI’s benefit: a clear and communicated AI stance, healthy data ecosystems, AI-accessible internal data, strong version control practices, working in small batches, a user-centric focus and quality internal platforms69. Several of these are intent-custody capabilities in this article’s sense: internal data an agent can reach is context; version control and small batches are what make review tractable. DORA’s 2026 report on return on investment adds a timing warning: most organisations should expect a temporary productivity dip, a J-curve driven in part by a “verification tax” on reviewing AI-generated code, before gains arrive, and it explicitly discourages treating headcount reduction as the route to returns70.

11Methodologies, standards and the boundary of the subject

Several methodologies now claim to describe an AI-native lifecycle end to end. AWS’s AI-DLC, published in July 2025, is the most explicit. It organises work into three phases—Inception, Construction and Operations—replaces sprints with short cycles called bolts and epics with units of work, and makes AI the driver of each phase: the model creates a plan, asks clarifying questions and implements only after people validate, with critical decisions deferred to humans40. The AWS post presents improvements in speed and quality without supporting data, which is typical of the current literature: the methodologies are ahead of the evidence.

Formal standardisation is beginning. IEEE 3398-2026, approved in September 2026, is a recommended practice for integrating GPT-based tools across the software life cycle, defining intelligence levels, competency requirements and evaluation methods for gradual adoption31. The IEEE P26044 project, authorised in May 2026, will define a reference model of generative-AI tool capabilities organised into governance, project, technical and organisational process areas, with integration points to ISO/IEC/IEEE 12207 and 15288; it explicitly excludes the development of AI systems themselves32. The underlying process standards remain ISO/IEC/IEEE 12207 for software life cycle processes71 and ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 for requirements engineering72, whose revision entered its DIS ballot in September 202673. For organisations that need a management-system frame for AI use, ISO/IEC 42001 is the relevant standard74.

That exclusion in P26044 marks a boundary worth keeping. Using AI to build software and building software that contains AI are different engineering problems that share vocabulary. The second involves model selection, retrieval-augmented generation, evaluation of model outputs, guardrails and model operations, and has its own life cycle standard in ISO/IEC 533875 and its own security profile in SP 800-218A67. A team can be AI-native in how it builds conventional software, or build AI products with a conventional process, or both; this article is about the first.

12Twenty-four practices, by where they hold intent

The plates below collect twenty-four practices that recur across the sources above, two to four per lifecycle stage. Each is described by the same four fields: what carries the intent at that point, what checks the artefact against it, and how it fails. Each is then coded on two axes.

The first axis is the custody ladder, the strongest form in which the practice holds intent so that conformance can be checked: K0 tacit (only in someone’s head), K1 prose (written, checked by a reader), K2 structured (a template a machine can parse but not execute), K3 example (executable checks on the cases written down), K4 contract (a schema, policy or property checked over a whole class of inputs) and K5 proof (every reachable state checked by a mechanical verifier). The second axis is the closer: who or what closes the gap between intent and artefact—human judgement (H), the agent itself (A), a pipeline gate (P) or a runtime signal (R). The gauge on each plate shows both: the chain of six links filled to the practice’s rung, and the closer marked to its right. The coding is the author’s judgement from the cited sources, stated so it can be disagreed with.

13What the census shows

Coding the plates produces a pattern that is easy to state. Of the twenty-four practices, 12 hold intent at K1 or K2, where only a reader can judge conformance, and 8 hold it at K4 or K5. The low-rung practices are not evenly spread: they cluster at the front of the lifecycle and at the review boundary. The mean rung is 1.33 for discovery and requirements and 2.00 for review and integration, against 4.00 for verification and testing and 3.00 for deployment and release.

The second axis sharpens this. Every practice closed by the agent itself sits at K3 or below (6 plates, mean rung 1.83), and every practice closed by a pipeline gate sits at K3 or above (9 plates, mean rung 3.89). Human-closed practices average 1.14. Knowing only who closes the gap explains 76% of the variance in rung across the plates; knowing the lifecycle stage, with twice as many categories, explains 41%. Where a practice sits in the lifecycle matters less than what is doing the checking.

Put plainly: in an AI-native lifecycle, intent is weakest exactly where the agent reads it. Constitutions, context files and task lists are prose that the agent checks against itself. Specifications become strong only when they are compiled into something a pipeline runs—scenarios, contracts, properties, structural rules. The practical programme of AI-native engineering is to push intent down the chain of translations into forms that are checked by something other than the system that produced the work.

15Three questions, and how to start

Strip away the tooling and an AI-native lifecycle is an answer to three questions, which recur in every source this article draws on.

How does the agent know what it is supposed to build? Through specifications precise enough to act on, structured where structure helps (EARS, scenarios, contracts), and maintained under change rather than written once. The lab’s drift model says enforcement, not drafting quality, determines whether the specification stays true.

How does the agent work in the real environment safely? Through a harness: curated context, typed tools, sandboxes, least privilege, observable behaviour and explicit points where a person must approve. The OpenAI and Google accounts show that the harness, not the model, is where most of the engineering effort goes56,59.

How do we establish that the result is correct? Through checks independent of the generator: acceptance criteria written outside the implementing context, mutation analysis to test the tests, property-based and contract tests over whole classes of input, formal methods where the stakes justify them, and production signals fed back into the specification. The lab’s central results are that a suite with 100% coverage can catch fewer than half of simple faults, that a suite catching every mutant can still miss a realistic bug that random invariants find in two or three tries, and that retrying until green converges on the quality of the check, not the generator.

For a team starting out, a sensible progression follows the ladder rather than the hype. First, write a complete specification for one real feature with acceptance criteria someone other than the implementer approves. Second, give an agent a sandboxed environment and a short map of the repository, and let it implement against that specification. Third, add the checks that move intent up the ladder—mutation analysis on the tests, a property suite for the core invariants, contract tests on interfaces, scanning and provenance in the pipeline—and require them before merge. Fourth, measure delivery outcomes, not generated lines: lead time, change failure rate, review time per change and rework. Only then is it worth adding more agents.
