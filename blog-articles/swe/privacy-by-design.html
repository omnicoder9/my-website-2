1. The question

Every system that handles personal data makes a set of promises. Most of them are sentences: we only collect what we need, we delete your data within thirty days, we do not log personal information, your activity in one product is not linked to another. Each sentence describes a state of the world the system is supposed to maintain. None of them, on its own, maintains anything.

The useful question for an engineer is not whether a system has privacy properties. It is: what would have to be impossible for this sentence to be true, and what in this system makes it so? The answer is usually a specific artefact with a name and an owner: a column that does not exist, a key held by a party with no reason to use it, a service that has no network route to another, a check that runs before every read and can refuse. When there is no such artefact, the sentence is still there, and it is still doing work, but the work it is doing is reputational rather than technical.

This is not a new observation, and it is not a cynical one. Spiekermann and Cranor gave it its clearest formulation in 2009 when they split privacy engineering into privacy by policy and privacy by architecture: the first relies on notice, consent and enforcement after the fact, the second on system design that makes the undesirable outcome unavailable in the first place.1 The distinction survives every rebranding because it tracks something real. A policy can be violated by a person having a bad afternoon. An architecture has to be rebuilt.

What follows treats privacy by design as an engineering discipline with a measurable output. The measurement is a two-part rating, applied to eighteen concrete decisions that appear in almost every consumer or business system: the rung a claim implies, and the rung a mechanism actually holds. The distance between them is the promise gap. It is not a moral failing when the gap is wide; some gaps are economically correct, and closing every one of them would build a system nobody could afford to run. But an unmeasured gap is a liability with no owner, and gaps have a property that makes them worth measuring: they widen on their own. Nobody has to edit the sentence for the system underneath it to drift out from under it.

Everything numerical in this article comes from a lab of eight deterministic experiments, listed in full at the end, which you can run yourself; every claim about standards, case law or deployed systems is cited. Where the lab measures something a published study also measures, both appear, and where they disagree the disagreement is stated rather than smoothed over.

2. Where the idea came from, and what it lost on the way

The modern privacy conversation in engineering is much older than the software. Warren and Brandeis argued in 1890 that a new technology, instantaneous photography combined with cheap newspapers, had created an injury the existing law of contract and defamation could not name.2 Their solution was legal. It set a pattern that held for a century: technology creates the exposure, law is asked to repair it, and the repair arrives a decade late.

The first framework recognisably aimed at system builders came from a 1973 report by an advisory committee of the United States Department of Health, Education, and Welfare, which set out five practices for record-keeping systems: no secret systems, a way for a person to find out what is held and how it is used, a way to prevent information collected for one purpose being used for another without consent, a way to correct records, and an obligation on the holder to ensure reliability and prevent misuse.3 Westin's work on privacy as control over information about oneself had supplied the concept a few years earlier.4 Those five practices became the OECD guidelines of 19805 and the Council of Europe's Convention 108 in 1981,6 modernised as Convention 108+ in 2018,7 and then the 1995 European Directive8 and eventually the GDPR.9 Read the OECD text today and the striking thing is how much of it is unchanged: collection limitation, data quality, purpose specification, use limitation, security safeguards, openness, individual participation, accountability.10 The vocabulary of the field was fixed in 1980 and it was fixed as a list of obligations on an organisation, not as a list of properties of a system.

The mechanism track ran in parallel and largely apart. In 1981 Chaum described untraceable electronic mail using layered public-key encryption and mixing, which is the direct ancestor of every anonymity network since.11 In 1985 he described a transaction system based on blind signatures and per-organisation pseudonyms designed specifically so that the parties could not link a person's dealings across contexts.12 Read now, the 1985 paper is startling: it is a complete privacy architecture, invented before there was a web to apply it to, and its central move is exactly the one the industry rediscovers every decade, which is to make the linkage cryptographically unavailable rather than administratively forbidden.

The two tracks met in 1995. A joint report by the Dutch data protection authority and the Information and Privacy Commissioner of Ontario described a class of privacy-enhancing technologies and, more importantly, the idea of an identity protector sitting inside a system to separate identity from transaction.13 That report is where the phrase "privacy by design" is usually dated from, and it is worth noting what it actually was: an engineering document about where in an architecture to put a boundary.

What happened next is the part that engineers tend to find frustrating. The idea was distilled into seven foundational principles: proactive not reactive, privacy as the default setting, privacy embedded into design, full functionality in a positive-sum rather than zero-sum framing, end-to-end security through the full lifecycle, visibility and transparency, and respect for user privacy.14 In 2010 the international conference of data protection commissioners adopted these principles by resolution in Jerusalem, which made them the global vocabulary,15 and in 2012 the United States Federal Trade Commission recommended privacy by design as one of three pillars of its consumer privacy framework.16 By any measure of adoption this was an extraordinary success.

It was also a translation loss. The seven principles are aspirations about posture, not statements about mechanism, and they do not distinguish between the two things Spiekermann and Cranor had just separated. "Privacy embedded into design" is satisfied, on its face, by a well-attended design review. Gürses, Troncoso and Diaz made the criticism directly in 2011: the principles are too vague to guide construction, and if you want a single technical instruction that actually generates architectures, it is data minimisation — decide what you do not need to collect, and then build the system that works without it.17 Their later restatement worked through concrete cases where starting from minimisation produced fundamentally different architectures from starting from compliance.18 Rubinstein and Good tested the claim historically by walking back through ten Google and Facebook privacy incidents and asking, for each, which design principle would have prevented it; their answer was that the technical principles had real preventive power and the vague ones did not.19

Three efforts since then have tried to close the gap between the vocabulary and the work. Hoepman's privacy design strategies give eight named moves — minimise, separate, abstract, hide, inform, control, enforce, demonstrate — each of which decomposes into tactics an architect can apply to a specific data flow.20 The ENISA report of 2015 surveyed what was actually available to build with, and concluded bluntly that the gap between policy language and engineering practice was the central problem.21 LINDDUN turned privacy analysis into a threat-modelling method with the same shape as STRIDE, cataloguing linkability, identifiability, non-repudiation, detectability, disclosure of information, unawareness and non-compliance against the elements of a data-flow diagram;22 its empirical evaluation found the method productive but expensive,23 which is why the lightweight card-based LINDDUN GO followed.24 In parallel the German supervisory authorities built the Standard Data Protection Model around six protection goals, adding data minimisation, unlinkability, transparency and intervenability to the familiar security triad,25 which descends from Rost and Pfitzmann's argument that privacy needs its own goals rather than a footnote in confidentiality,26 and from Pfitzmann and Hansen's terminology work that gave the field precise definitions of anonymity, unlinkability and unobservability.27

The legal track, meanwhile, did something the engineering track had not managed: it made one rung mandatory. Article 25 of the GDPR requires controllers to implement appropriate technical and organisational measures designed to implement the data protection principles, and separately requires that by default only personal data necessary for each specific purpose are processed.9 The second half is the sharper one. A default is a property of code, it is observable from outside, and it is the setting the overwhelming majority of people will ever have — a point established well outside privacy by the organ-donation default studies.28 The European Data Protection Board's guidelines on Article 25 spell out that the obligation applies before processing begins and continuously thereafter, and that defaults include both what the controller can set and what the person can.29 Enforcement has followed the default rather than the policy: the Irish supervisory authority's decisions against Instagram in 2022 and TikTok in 2023 both turned on the settings that applied to children's accounts when nobody touched them.3031

Standardisation arrived late. ISO 31700-1, published on 31 January 2023, is the first international standard for privacy by design in consumer products, setting out thirty high-level requirements across the product lifecycle;32 a companion technical report supplies worked use cases.33 It deliberately does not specify methods or technologies, which makes it a management standard rather than an engineering one. On the technical side, NIST's privacy engineering work translated the familiar security objectives into three privacy ones — predictability, manageability and disassociability34 — and the NIST Privacy Framework put them into a risk-management structure,35 with a version 1.1 draft in 2025 realigning it with the Cybersecurity Framework and adding a section on the privacy risks of AI systems.36 In March 2025 NIST published SP 800-226, which is the document to hand a team that has been told to "use differential privacy": it is largely a guide to telling a real guarantee from what it calls privacy theatre.37

Two developments since 2025 matter for how an engineer should read all of this. The first is legal. On 4 September 2025 the Court of Justice of the European Union held, in EDPS v Single Resolution Board, that whether pseudonymised data are personal data is relative to who holds them: the same records can be personal for the sender, who holds the key, and not personal for a recipient who has no means reasonably likely to be used to identify anyone.3839 That is a legal conclusion, but it is a statement about system structure — identifiability is a property of a party's position in an architecture, not an intrinsic property of a table — and it puts a legal premium on being able to demonstrate exactly what a given party can and cannot join. The European Commission's Digital Omnibus proposal of 19 November 2025 would have written a version of that relativity into the definition of personal data itself; the Board and the Supervisor opposed it in a joint opinion, the Council's compromise text removed it, and as this is written the data half of the package is still in negotiation.4041 The EDPB's own guidelines on pseudonymisation, issued in January 2025, take the conservative line that pseudonymised data remain personal data for a controller who retains the ability to reverse the process.42

The second is industrial, and it is a caution against believing that the mechanism track always wins in the end. Google's Privacy Sandbox was the largest attempt ever made to replace an entrenched tracking mechanism with an architectural one — on-device topic inference, aggregate attribution reporting, auctions that never expose an identifier. On 17 October 2025 Google announced the retirement of ten of those APIs, including Topics, Protected Audience and Attribution Reporting, across Chrome and Android, citing low adoption and ecosystem feedback; third-party cookies stay.43 A handful of narrower pieces survive. The lesson is not that privacy architecture failed on the merits. It is that an architectural control which depends on voluntary adoption by the parties it constrains is, in the terms of this article, sitting several rungs lower than its design would suggest.

One more strand belongs in this history because it shaped what engineers were allowed to promise. Through the 2000s the working assumption in most organisations was that removing direct identifiers made data safe to release. Ohm's survey of the re-identification results argued that this assumption had already failed and that the law built on it was resting on a broken premise;44 a subsequent exchange between practitioners and statisticians produced a durable disagreement about how much the demonstrated attacks generalise to well-executed de-identification.45 That disagreement is not settled, and an engineer should hold both facts at once: the demonstrations are real and were often against careless releases, and the formal techniques that replaced ad-hoc redaction have costs that make them unsuitable for many of the uses redaction was serving. The vocabulary is at least now standardised, in terminology for de-identification techniques46 and a framework for applying them,47 on top of the older general privacy framework;48 regulators have published usable technical guidance on the same ground.49

It is also worth naming what changed underneath all of this while the frameworks were being written. Gürses and van Hoboken point out that the software industry moved from shipping releases to operating services, and that this agile turn dissolved exactly the design moment the frameworks assume exists: there is no longer a point before deployment at which the system is decided.50 A privacy control that lives in a design review is a control applied once to a system that changes weekly. Every mechanism recommended in this article is therefore chosen to survive that: a schema, a failing test, a default, a generated artefact — things that run every time, not things somebody does at the start.

3. The assurance ladder

To compare decisions across a system you need a scale, and the scale has to measure the thing that actually varies. It is not how sensitive the data is, and it is not how serious the organisation is. What varies is what has to be true for the property to fail. Six rungs are enough to cover the range, and the boundaries between them are the boundaries that matter operationally: whether the datum exists at all, whether a secret is needed, whether a boundary must be crossed, whether a check runs on the request path, whether anything runs at all, and whether the only artefact is a sentence.

Two things about this scale need stating plainly, because they are where the disagreements are.

Higher is not always better. R5 for a payments ledger would mean not knowing who paid, which is not a privacy improvement but a broken product. The right rung for a decision is the highest one compatible with the system doing its job, and a great deal of real privacy engineering consists of discovering that the job needs less than the current design assumes. That discovery is what data minimisation means in practice, and it is why minimisation is the strategy the critics of the seven principles kept returning to.1720 The R5 rung also carries its own trade-off: a system that cannot identify anyone cannot help a person who has been locked out of their account, and it cannot answer their access request either. There is no free lunch in this area and the formal literature says so explicitly.51

The rung is where the ordinary build lands, not where the best build could. Rating the mechanism you could have built is how ladders become flattering. The rule used throughout this article is: rate the mechanism a competent team produces with the default scaffolding — the framework's ORM, the cloud provider's managed keys, the standard log pipeline, the observability vendor's SDK — and note separately what it would take to move up. Every plate below therefore carries a note saying what the next rung would cost.

The rungs also map onto older ideas that engineers already trust. R2, a check that runs on every access and can refuse, is Saltzer and Schroeder's complete mediation, and R3 is their principle of least privilege applied to data rather than to code.52 The idea that a boundary in a system should carry a label that propagates with the data is Denning's lattice model,53 which became language-based information flow54 and decentralised label models,55 and reappeared as whole operating systems built around explicit flows56 and as languages that enforce a policy at the point of output.57 None of that is exotic; it is the same instinct that produced type systems. The reason it stays at the edge of practice is that privacy labels, unlike types, are not usually attached to the data at the moment it is created.

4. Eighteen decisions

What follows is the ledger proper: eighteen decisions that recur in nearly every system that holds personal data, each rated twice. The claim rung is the rung implied by the sentence a product of this kind normally publishes; the enforced rung is what a mechanism holds in a system built the ordinary way. Each plate carries the same five fields — what the system says, what actually enforces it, whom that stops, how it leaks, and the check that would detect the gap — plus a note on what the next rung costs.

The plates are grouped by where in the life of a datum the decision sits. The grouping is not decorative: the gap widens as data moves outward, and it widens again when a person tries to act on it.

4 What enters

Decisions taken before anything is stored. Every later control is downstream of these, and only these can reach R5.

Group A contains the only decisions that can reach R5 cheaply, and the reason is arithmetic rather than moral: a field that was never collected has no retention schedule, no access-control policy, no export path, no breach exposure and no line in the record of processing activities. Every other group is downstream of this one, which is why minimisation keeps being the answer that the engineering critiques of the seven principles arrive at.1720 The corollary is uncomfortable: most of the privacy work an organisation funds is spent managing consequences of collection decisions that were made in an afternoon and never revisited.

P01 Collecting the field at all R5 claimed · R1 enforced · gap 4

The system says
We only collect what we need.
Enforced by
A schema review and a design document. The column is nullable, the form field is optional, and nothing in the running system knows the difference between a field that is needed and one that was easy to add.
Which stops
Nobody. It stops a future reviewer, if one reads the document.
Leaks as
Silently, and by accretion. The field arrives for a good reason, outlives it, and is still being written years later because removing it is a migration and keeping it is free.
The check
Count fields written but never read. A column with writes and no reads for a quarter is a collection decision that has already expired.

Next rung This is the only plate that can reach R5 cheaply, and the only one where R5 costs nothing to operate. Deleting the field ends every downstream obligation at once.

P02 Choosing the identifier R3 claimed · R2 enforced · gap 1

The system says
Your activity in one product is not linked to your activity in another.
Enforced by
Per-context pseudonyms derived with a keyed hash, so that no service holds an identifier another service can recognise.
Which stops
A recipient who has only the identifier column. It does not stop anyone who also has a clock.
Leaks as
Through correlated fields rather than through the identifier. Two records that share a timestamp to the second are the same event under two names.
The check
Try the join you claim is impossible, using every other column you keep. Experiment E2 is that test written down.

Next rung Keyed derivation is an R4 mechanism protecting an R2 property: the identifier is unrecoverable and the person is identifiable anyway.

P03 Precision and granularity R5 claimed · R2 enforced · gap 3

The system says
We store only an approximate location, and only the day.
Enforced by
Truncation in the ingest path: the exact value arrives, is rounded, and the rounded value is written.
Which stops
Everyone downstream of ingest. Nobody upstream of it.
Leaks as
At the edge and in the buffers. The precise value existed in a request log, a load-balancer trace, and an SDK crash report before the rounding code ever saw it.
The check
Search for the precise value, not the rounded one, across every store the request touched before ingest.

Next rung Truncating on the device moves this to R5, because the precise value is never transmitted. Truncating on the server moves it to R2, because the precise value existed and something chose to forget it.

P04 Computing on the device R5 claimed · R4 enforced · gap 1

The system says
The processing happens on your phone; we never see the content.
Enforced by
Local inference or local aggregation, with only a result leaving the device — often under an aggregation protocol that no single party can invert.
Which stops
The service itself, its infrastructure providers, and anyone who later compels the service.
Leaks as
Through what the result implies, and through the metadata of sending it: that you sent one, when, from which address, how large it was.
The check
Ask what the smallest possible message is, and whether the one you send is larger. The difference is the leak.

Next rung The narrowest gap on the ledger, and the most expensive to build. R5 is reserved for the case where the result itself carries no individual signal.

P05 Telemetry defaults R5 claimed · R2 enforced · gap 3

The system says
Analytics are optional.
Enforced by
A consent flag consulted by the reporting client before a batch is sent, with the events themselves buffered locally either way.
Which stops
The analytics backend, for as long as the flag is consulted on the path that sends.
Leaks as
Through the second SDK. The flag is honoured by the code that was written when the flag was introduced, and unknown to everything added since.
The check
Run the app with telemetry off and capture the network. Compare the destinations against the ones your privacy notice lists.

Next rung Article 25(2) is about exactly this rung: the default is the setting most people will ever have, so the default is the policy.

Plate 2 is worth dwelling on because it is where good intentions most often produce a mechanism at the wrong rung. Deriving a separate pseudonym per context, with a key held in a service that analytics cannot call, is a genuinely strong construction — it is Chaum's per-organisation pseudonym idea in modern packaging,1227 and it makes the identifier column useless for joining. Listing 1 is the shape it takes in practice.

Note the third property in that docstring, which is the one that pays for itself later. If the derivation key is per person, then destroying it is an erasure that reaches places no delete statement can go: append-only stores, immutable object versions, warehouses with no update path, and backups on tape. Crypto-shredding is an old idea — revocable backup systems date to 1996,58 assured delete via key destruction to 2005,59 and the policy-based generalisation to 201360 — and the systematisation of secure deletion research makes the underlying point that deletion guarantees at one layer say nothing about the layer beneath.61 It is the only mechanism on this ledger that moves plate 8 above R1.

And yet experiment E2 shows what the construction does not buy. Two services holding events for the same people under unrelated pseudonyms can be re-linked by nothing more than the timestamps: when the second service stores time to the second, 98.21% of 50,000 users are uniquely and correctly matched, and at one-hour granularity the figure is still 66.12%. Unlinkability is a property of the whole record, not of the identifier column. That is why plate 3 exists, and why the place the rounding happens matters more than how coarse it is.

5 What can be joined

Decisions about shape and custody. They do not change what exists; they change who can put two things together.

Group B decisions do not change what exists. They change who can put two things together, which is the same question the CJEU was answering in EDPS v SRB: identifiability is relative to a party's position.38 An engineer can read that judgment as a design instruction. If you can state precisely which party holds which key, which service can reach which store, and which columns can be joined by whom, you have both a privacy property and the evidence for it. If you cannot, the honest description of your architecture is that everyone with production access can join everything, which is usually true and rarely written down.

P06 Separating identity from behaviour R3 claimed · R1 enforced · gap 2

The system says
Identity is kept separate from behavioural data.
Enforced by
Two tables, sometimes two databases, joined by a user identifier that both of them carry and any analyst can type.
Which stops
Nobody who can write SQL against both.
Leaks as
In the first dashboard that needs a name next to a number.
The check
Look for the join key. If a single query can produce the joined row, the separation is a diagram, not a boundary.

Next rung Separation becomes architectural only when the two halves sit in different trust domains: different accounts, different credentials, no route.

P07 Encryption and key custody R4 claimed · R1 enforced · gap 3

The system says
Your data is encrypted.
Enforced by
Volume-level or column-level encryption with keys the application service can fetch on demand.
Which stops
Someone who takes the disk, and nobody else. The application decrypts for every request, so every path that reaches the application reaches the plaintext.
Leaks as
In the backup that stores the key alongside the ciphertext, and in the read replica whose credentials were copied into a notebook.
The check
Name the party the encryption is meant to exclude and check whether they can obtain the key. If the answer is yes, this is compliance, not secrecy.

Next rung Per-person keys change the rung twice over: they make erasure a key deletion rather than a search, which is the only erasure that reaches backups.

P08 Retention and deletion R5 claimed · R1 enforced · gap 4

The system says
We delete your data within thirty days.
Enforced by
A scheduled job that visits the stores on a register a person maintains by hand.
Which stops
The stores on the register, on the schedule of the job.
Leaks as
Everywhere the register does not reach. Experiment E4 puts that at 19 of 35 reachable stores, 10 of which have no clearing mechanism of any kind.
The check
Compute the reachable set from the collection point and subtract the register. Fail the build when the difference is not empty.

Next rung Nothing on this ledger degrades as fast: every new derived store widens the gap without anyone editing the sentence.

P09 Who can read it R3 claimed · R2 enforced · gap 1

The system says
Only the people who need it can see it.
Enforced by
Role-based grants, issued at table granularity because that is the granularity the database makes convenient.
Which stops
Everyone outside the role. Nobody inside it.
Leaks as
Sideways, into the columns nobody asked for. In experiment E8 the median job is granted six columns for every column it reads, and 31 columns no query ever touches are reachable anyway.
The check
Diff the columns your workload reads against the columns your grants convey. The difference is unearned reach.

Next rung This is a genuine runtime check, which is why it sits at R2 — it is simply asking the wrong question, at the wrong granularity.

P10 Logs, traces and crash reports R5 claimed · R1 enforced · gap 4

The system says
We do not log personal data.
Enforced by
A redaction filter with patterns for the shapes of personal data someone thought of.
Which stops
The shapes on the list.
Leaks as
Through everything unshaped. In experiment E5 the filter recovers 56.2% of items and nothing at all from free text, opaque keys, encoded blobs or stack-trace locals — while wrongly redacting 2,522 harmless tokens.
The check
Generate a line for every carrier your codebase actually produces, not for the carriers your regular expressions expect.

Next rung An allowlist logger reaches R3 by construction: if the API takes fields and never a formatted string, the unshaped carrier cannot be expressed.

Plate 10 deserves its rung. Logging is the most reliably underestimated data flow in any system, because it is written by every engineer, reviewed by none, and retained by default. The standard control is a redaction filter, and experiment E5 measures what that filter is worth against a corpus of 5,000 log lines carrying 8,354 personal-data items: 56.2% overall recall, 100% on the carriers that have a recognisable shape, and zero on free text, opaque customer keys, base64-encoded blobs and stack-trace locals — while wrongly redacting 2,522 harmless tokens that happened to look like identifiers. The alternative is not a better regular expression.

Plate 9 has the same structure one layer down. Grants are issued at table granularity because that is what the database makes convenient, and the result in experiment E8 is that the median job is conveyed six columns for every column it reads, the worst job 11.5, and 31 of 61 columns that no query in the workload ever touches remain reachable. The correction is to make the unit of grant a view whose column list is the minimisation decision, and to name roles after purposes rather than after teams, because purposes are what the lawful basis and the privacy notice are written against.925

6 What leaves

Decisions about release — to an analyst, a model, a partner, a network path. Release is the last point at which a property is still yours to hold.

Release is the last point at which a property is still yours to hold. After a figure is published, a model is shipped or a partner has the file, every control you have is procedural. This is the group where the formal literature is strongest and the practice weakest.

P11 Releasing an aggregate R5 claimed · R2 enforced · gap 3

The system says
The published figures are anonymous.
Enforced by
A minimum cell size, or a k-anonymity check, applied at release time.
Which stops
An attacker who wants to name a row. Not one who wants to learn an attribute.
Leaks as
Through homogeneity and through repetition. In E7, 31.4% of records in the smallest classes at k=2 sit in a class whose members all share the sensitive value; and any suppression rule leaks through the pattern of what it suppressed.
The check
Ask what an adversary learns who already knows which class the person is in. k-anonymity says nothing about that question.

Next rung Differential privacy with a budget ledger is the same plate at R5, because the guarantee holds against the attacker's whole query history rather than one release.

The system says
The model does not contain your data.
Enforced by
A deduplication pass and an intention.
Which stops
Nothing in particular. Deduplication reduces repetition, which is the variable that matters, but it is not a bound.
Leaks as
Through the rare record, which is exactly the record that identifies someone. In E6 a canary repeated four times is the model's single best completion of its own prefix.
The check

Next rung An oblivious relay reaches R4 by splitting knowledge: the relay sees the address and not the request, the gateway sees the request and not the address, and neither can be asked for both.

The history of plate 11 is a history of the same lesson learned repeatedly. Sweeney showed in 2000 that the combination of five-digit postal code, date of birth and sex uniquely identified 87% of the United States population;62 Golle's recomputation on later census data put the figure lower but still substantial.63 AOL published twenty million search queries in 2006 with user identifiers replaced by numbers, and a reporter identified a named individual within days.64 Narayanan and Shmatikov de-anonymised the Netflix Prize dataset with auxiliary knowledge from a public film-rating site.65 New York City's taxi data was released with medallion numbers hashed by a function with a 22-million-element input space, which is a lookup table rather than a hash.66 Four spatio-temporal points identify 95% of people in a mobility dataset;67 four points identify 90% in credit-card metadata;68 fifteen demographic attributes are enough to re-identify 99.98% of Americans in any incomplete dataset.69 Strava published an aggregate heat map of exercise routes and revealed the layout of military installations.70

Experiment E1 reproduces the mechanism rather than the specific numbers, on a synthetic population of 200,000 where the ground truth is known. Postal area, birth year-month and sex leave 42.62% of people alone in their class. Adding platform, language and time zone — three fields nobody thinks of as identifying, all of which arrive unbidden in an HTTP request — takes it to 93.58%, and the seventh attribute reaches 99.12%. Browser fingerprinting research has been making the same point since 2010.7172 The lesson for a release decision is that the quasi-identifier set is not the set of fields that look sensitive; it is every field, because uniqueness is a property of combinations.

k-anonymity7374 was the first formal answer, and its successors, l-diversity75 and t-closeness,76 exist because k-anonymity says nothing about what an attacker learns who already knows which class a person is in. Experiment E7 measures both halves of that. Reaching k=2 on a 50,000-record table costs 19.4% of the information in the quasi-identifiers; reaching k=25 costs 33.3%. And at k=2, among the classes that sit closest to the threshold, 31.4% of records are in a class where every member shares the same sensitive value: the record is anonymous and the attribute is disclosed anyway. Regulators know this. The Article 29 Working Party's 2014 opinion analysed the anonymisation techniques against exactly these three risks — singling out, linkability and inference — and concluded that each technique fails at least one.77 Cohen and Nissim later showed that the law's notion of singling out can be formalised, and that k-anonymity does not provide it in general.7879

Differential privacy is the answer that changed the shape of the problem.8081 Its contribution is not noise; it is that the guarantee is stated over an attacker's entire interaction rather than over one release, and that guarantees compose in a way you can account for. Dinur and Nissim had already shown that answering too many queries too accurately reconstructs the database,82 and the Census Bureau's demonstration of reconstruction against its own published tables is what moved that result from theory to policy.8384 Deployments followed: randomised response at scale in Chrome,85 shuffling to strengthen the guarantee,86 secret-shared aggregation with Prio,87 and Apple's telemetry.88 The field also learned to be suspicious of the parameter: an early analysis of Apple's deployment found per-datum epsilons that summed to a daily budget large enough to make the guarantee weak,89 which is why the standing recommendation is to publish your epsilons and your accounting method,90 and why choosing epsilon at all remains an economic question rather than a mathematical one.9192 There are serious dissents worth reading on both flanks: that differential privacy is often misapplied to problems it does not fit,93 and that its costs to data utility have been understated.94 Synthetic data, frequently offered as the alternative, does not escape the trade-off either.95

Plate 12 is the newest entry on this ledger and the one where the gap is widening fastest. A model trained on personal data is a release of that data under a different name. Membership inference recovers whether a given record was in the training set;96 the secret sharer methodology showed that a canary inserted into training data can be extracted, and that its extractability scales with repetition;97 extraction from production language models followed, at scale and cheaply.9899 Deduplication helps, measurably,100101 but it is a mitigation rather than a bound; the bound is per-example clipping with noise during training.102 Experiment E6 shows the shape of the dependency on a corpus of 43,000 documents: a four-digit canary seen once has a median exposure of 2.76 bits, at four repetitions it is the model's single best completion of its own prefix in eight of twelve trials, and by eight repetitions in all twelve. Per-contributor clipping holds every repetition count at the exposure of one.

Plate 14 is the one engineers most often decide is somebody else's problem. Every request carries an address, a timing and a size, and each of those is an identifier of some strength; the IETF's own guidance on privacy in protocol design has said so since 2013.103 The mechanisms that address it are all variations on splitting knowledge between parties who have no reason to collude, which is Chaum's original construction in modern clothing: onion routing separates the party who knows who you are from the party who knows what you asked;104 Oblivious HTTP does the same for a single request-response pair, so that the relay sees the address and not the content and the gateway sees the content and not the address;105 Privacy Pass lets a service check that a client is entitled to something without learning which client it is;106 and sealed sender removes the sender identifier from a message the server must still route.107 None of these is free, and all of them require a second party willing to run infrastructure it cannot monetise, which is the real reason they remain rare rather than any technical obstacle.

Plate 13 has a measurement literature that is worth reading before writing the contract. Third-party tracking on the web was characterised a decade ago108 and measured at million-site scale shortly afterwards;109 on mobile, an empirical study of Android applications documented SDKs using covert and side channels to obtain data the permission system had denied them, which is a failure of the platform boundary rather than of the agreement.110 Apple's response has been to move part of the problem to build time: since 1 May 2024 an application submitted with a listed third-party SDK must carry privacy manifests, declared reasons for covered APIs, and valid signatures.111 The measured effect of the earlier tracking-consent change was real but partial — fewer third-party identifiers, and continued tracking by other means112 — which is roughly what the ladder predicts for a control that depends on the constrained party's own declaration.

Next rung Article 25(2) makes this the one plate where the law names the rung directly: by default, only what is necessary for each purpose.

Plates 8 and 16 share a denominator, and experiment E4 is about that denominator. Build the data-flow graph of a mid-size consumer service — 35 stores and services, from the signup API to the cold archive — and every one of them is reachable from the signup form. A register maintained by hand lists 16. Deletion driven by that register clears 28.6% of the reachable stores by day 30, against 54.3% for deletion driven by lineage. Neither number reaches 100%, and the reason is not scheduling: 10 of the 35 stores have no clearing mechanism of any kind, no time-to-live, no rotation, no contractual deletion clock. An access request runs against the same register, which is why the honest formulation of both promises is the same sentence: whatever you cannot enumerate, you can neither show nor delete.

Plate 15, consent, fails in a specific and avoidable way. Withdrawal is implemented as a state change on a consent record, and the services written after that record was introduced consult it, while the copies made while the answer was yes sit untouched in the warehouse, the feature store and the audience export. The fix is visible in Listing 4: make the consent join a condition on the read path rather than an input to a nightly job. The empirical literature on consent interfaces is uniformly discouraging about the other end of the same plate — what people are actually agreeing to113114115 — and the deletion and opt-out flows those consents point at are frequently unusable.116 There is also a trap on plate 16 worth naming: identity verification for access requests has itself been abused to extract other people's data, so the mechanism that implements the right has to be designed against the attacker who is using it.117

Plate 17 is the only entry rated R0, and it is the keystone. If the published description of a system is a document maintained by hand, every other claim on this ledger is unfalsifiable, because there is nothing to compare the system against. Large-scale static analysis of mobile applications against their own privacy policies finds contradictions at scale.118119 The mechanism that fixes it is unglamorous: carry the classification on the field, generate the map, and fail the build when a persisted field has no purpose, no basis and no retention period.

8. The lab

Eight experiments, standard library only, one seed, no network. They are small and synthetic on purpose: a synthetic population has a known ground truth, so a claim about re-identification can be checked rather than asserted, and a reader can change a parameter and watch the conclusion move. Their job is not to establish new facts about the world. It is to make the failure modes on the ledger reproducible on a laptop, and to put a number on how far a mechanism carries. The complete output is embedded verbatim at the end of this article, and every figure below is drawn from that output.

E1 · Uniqueness is a property of combinations

Three attributes leave 42.62% of people unique. Six leave 93.58%. The three that do the damage after the obvious ones — platform, language, time zone — are not fields anyone decided to collect; they arrive in the request and get written because they were there. Coarsening reverses it sharply: holding the same three attributes but rounding the postal area by ten and bucketing birth dates into five-year bands takes uniqueness from 42.62% to essentially zero, with a median anonymity set of 648. The cost of that coarsening is the entire content of plate 3, and it is a product decision, not a privacy one.

E2 · A pseudonym is only as unlinkable as the rest of the row

Fifty thousand users, two services, unrelated per-context pseudonyms, and an average of 5.52 actions per person that both services happen to record. Matching on time alone links 98.21% of people uniquely and correctly when the second service stores timestamps to the second, and 66.12% at one-hour granularity. The breakdown by activity is the operationally useful part: at one-hour granularity, users with four or more shared actions are linked essentially always, users with two or three about 42% of the time, and users with one action never. Unlinkability is not a property you buy once. It degrades with every additional co-observed event, which means it degrades with engagement — the people best protected by a pseudonym scheme are the ones who barely use the product.27

E3 · The noise is not the control

A differencing attack asks a count over a set containing the target and the same count over the set without them; the difference is one bit about one person. Laplace noise at epsilon 1.0 makes a single such pair 61.4% accurate, barely better than a coin flip, and at epsilon 0.1 it is 52.3%. That number is what gets quoted in design documents. It is also irrelevant on its own, because nothing about adding noise prevents asking again: 9,559 repetitions at epsilon 0.1 recover the bit at 99% confidence, which the lab confirms empirically at 99.00%. Under a global budget of epsilon 1.0 the same attacker gets ten queries and 53.9% — a coin flip. The mechanism that produced the privacy was the refusal to answer the eleventh query, not the noise on the first ten. This is why the accounting and the parameter publication matter more than the sampler,90 and why NIST's guidance is largely about telling a real guarantee from a decorative one.37

E4 · You can only delete what you can enumerate

The graph is hand-built and deliberately unremarkable: an identity database, a profile service, a billing path, a support tool, an event pipeline into a lake and a warehouse, a feature store, a recommender index, four external recipients, a nightly backup and a cold archive. Nothing exotic, nothing that a five-year-old system would not have. Every node is reachable from the signup form; the register lists 16 of 35. Register-driven deletion clears 28.6% of reachable stores by day 30 against the lineage-driven 54.3%, and by day 365 the two are 34.3% and 71.4%. The ceiling exists because 10 stores — the partner CRM, the analytics vendor, the training snapshots, the marts, the invoice PDFs, the cold archive and the rest — have no deletion mechanism at all. For those, no scheduling change helps; only a design change does, and the cheapest one available is to make the data unreadable by destroying a key rather than by finding rows.585960

E5 · Denylists redact what they recognise

The filter in the experiment is not a straw man: it matches email addresses, prefixed user identifiers, and the values of known-sensitive key names, which is what production redaction filters do. It achieves 100% recall on every carrier with a recognisable shape and 0% on free text, opaque customer keys, base64-encoded payloads and stack-trace locals, for 56.2% overall — while redacting 2,522 tokens that were ports, durations, totals and version fragments. Both halves of that result are the point. A denylist is simultaneously too weak, because the carriers that matter have no shape, and too strong, because the shapes it does match are shared by harmless data. The allowlist alternative in Listing 3 gets to 100% by a different route: it removes the API that lets a caller interpolate a value into a message at all, so the unshaped carrier becomes unrepresentable rather than unmatched.

E6 · Repetition is what turns a record into a memory

The model here is a character n-gram count model, not a neural network, which is exactly why the result is worth showing: the dependence on repetition is a property of fitting a distribution to data, not an artefact of any particular architecture. A canary appearing once has a median exposure of 2.76 bits out of a ceiling of 13.29 — it is findable in principle and lost in the noise in practice. At four repetitions it is the model's top completion of its own prefix in eight of twelve trials; at eight, in all twelve. This is the same shape reported by the secret sharer work on real networks97 and it is the reason deduplication has measurable privacy value.100101 It is also the reason deduplication is not a guarantee: the records at risk are the rare ones, and a rare record repeated twice is still rare.

E7 · k-anonymity buys indistinguishability, not secrecy

Two costs are usually discussed and one usually is not. The discussed ones are information loss, which runs from 19.4% at k=2 to 33.3% at k=25 under a minimal full-domain generalisation with at most 1% suppression, and the collapse in the number of equivalence classes, from 16,893 raw to 2,484 at k=2 and 135 at k=25. The undiscussed one is that k-anonymity constrains only how many people share a class, not what they have in common: at k=2, 31.4% of the records in classes at the threshold sit in a class whose members all share the same sensitive value. Knowing which class a person is in is then sufficient. That residual is what l-diversity75 and t-closeness76 were introduced to address, and it is why release decisions should be made against a stated adversary rather than against a threshold.

E8 · A grant is a claim about need

Six tables, 61 columns, twelve job templates, 400 queries. The workload reads 30 distinct columns; table-level grants convey all 61. Per job the ratio is starker than the aggregate: the median job is conveyed six columns for every one it reads and the worst 11.5. Thirty-one columns are never read by any query in the workload and are reachable anyway. The password hash, the multi-factor secret, the card token and the date of birth are each within reach of two or three of the six purposes under table grants, and of none under column-and-purpose grants. Least privilege is an old idea;52120 what is new is only that the unit at which it must be applied to data is the column and the purpose, not the table and the team.

9. The ledger

Read down the column of hollow markers first. Twelve of the eighteen claims imply R5 — that the thing simply cannot happen. One mechanism on the ledger reaches R4 or above. The mean gap is 2.89 rungs and the median is 3, and the six widest gaps — collection, retention, logging, training, request metadata, and answering an access request — all share a structure: the claim asserts something about the state of the world and the mechanism is a job that runs later. Every mechanism rated R2 or higher on this ledger sits on the request path. Every mechanism rated R1 runs after the fact, on a schedule, over a list somebody maintains.

The second pattern is that the gap tracks the strength of the sentence. Claims implying R5 have a mean gap of 3.33 rungs; claims implying R3 have a mean gap of 1.80. This is not a coincidence and it is not hypocrisy. Sentences are free and mechanisms are not, so in the absence of a forcing function the language drifts upward while the implementation stays where the budget put it. The forcing function that does exist — Article 25(2), which names the default as the object of the obligation — is attached to the one plate on this ledger where the law describes a rung instead of a goal.929

The third pattern is positional. Group means run 2.40 for what enters, 2.80 for what can be joined, 3.25 for what leaves and 3.25 for what the person can do. The gap widens as the datum moves away from the point of collection, and it is widest at the point where a person tries to exercise a right — which is the point at which the organisation's own map of its systems becomes load-bearing, and, per experiment E4, that map is 16 stores out of 35.

10. What this changes on Monday

Nothing above requires adopting a framework. It requires four habits, each of which produces an artefact somebody else can check.

Write the claim down before the design review, and rate it. The sentence your product will publish is a requirement. Put it at the top of the design document, state the rung it implies, state the rung the design holds, and if there is a gap, say who accepted it. This costs a paragraph and it converts an invisible liability into a decision with a name on it. It is also what a threat-modelling method like LINDDUN does at greater depth, and if you have the appetite for the fuller method it is worth the time;2224 the card-based variant exists precisely because the full method was too expensive for routine use.

Move one control per quarter from after the fact onto the request path. The R1-to-R2 transition is the cheapest real improvement available in most systems, and it is nearly always the same move: something that ran nightly over a list becomes a condition evaluated at read time. Consent checked in the join rather than in a batch job. Retention expressed as a partition the query cannot see rather than a delete statement. Purpose carried in a session variable that a row-level policy reads. None of this is novel; it is complete mediation.52

Generate the map from the code. A field carries its purpose, its basis and its retention as an annotation; the record of processing activities, the privacy notice's data table, the deletion plan and the export are all generated from those annotations; and the build fails when a persisted field has none. This is the single highest-leverage change on the list because it converts plate 17 from R0 to R2 and, in doing so, makes every other claim on the ledger checkable. It also turns the hardest recurring question in privacy engineering — what do we actually hold? — from an archaeology project into a query.

Test the property, not the control. A test that asserts the redaction filter was called is a test of the control. A test that generates a line for every carrier in your codebase and asserts that no personal value survives is a test of the property. Likewise: assert that the closure of your data-flow manifest has an erasure handler for every node, not that the deletion job ran; try the join you claim is impossible, using every column you keep; insert canaries before training and measure exposure afterwards. Each of these is a few dozen lines, and each one fails the day somebody adds a sink, which is the only day the addition is cheap to reverse.

One organisational note, because the empirical work on developers is consistent and slightly bleak. Studies of practitioners find that engineers hold a mostly security-shaped mental model of privacy, reach for access control and encryption, and have little vocabulary for minimisation or unlinkability;121 and that even motivated developers struggle to translate requirements into design decisions without concrete methods.122 The remedy in that literature is not more training on principles. It is putting the decision in front of the engineer at the moment they are making it, in the form of a default, a schema, a failing test or a required field — which is the same conclusion this ledger arrives at from the other direction.

If you want a fuller apparatus than four habits, the useful sources are specific rather than general. For threat elicitation, LINDDUN and its interaction-based refinement give a systematic way to enumerate privacy threats against a data-flow diagram.123 For process, PRIPARE assembled the engineering activities into a methodology aligned to normal development phases,124 and there is a worked procedure for conducting a data protection impact assessment that treats it as an engineering artefact rather than a form.125 For control catalogues, NIST SP 800-53 revision 5 integrated privacy controls with security controls in a single frame,126 which is the practical answer to the question of what to put in a compliance matrix. For the deeper argument about why minimisation beats governance, Hoepman's book is the readable long form.127 And the original operational guidance from the Ontario commissioner's office remains a fair statement of the ambition, provided it is read alongside the engineering critiques rather than instead of them.128 Where an analytic platform is what you need, the privacy-integrated-query approach — a query interface that carries the privacy accounting with it rather than leaving it to the analyst — is the design worth copying.129

A last caution about the user-facing half. It is tempting to treat a choice offered to a person as an R2 control, since the code does check the setting. The behavioural literature is a warning against that reading: people's stated preferences and their choices diverge sharply under ordinary conditions of uncertainty and effort,130 and the survey of two decades of work concludes that privacy behaviour is highly sensitive to how a choice is framed and presented.131 A control that is technically real and practically unused is enforcing something, but not the thing the notice describes. That is the argument for spending scarce effort on the default rather than on the settings screen.

11. What this article does not establish

The rung assignments are judgements. They are stated by a rule — rate the mechanism the ordinary build produces, not the best one reachable — and the rule is applied consistently, but a team with a strong platform will legitimately rate several plates a rung higher, and a team with a legacy estate will rate several lower. The ledger's value is in the comparison across decisions within one system, not in the absolute numbers. If your gaps are narrower than these, the exercise has still told you where they are widest.

The lab is synthetic throughout. E1 and E7 use a generated population whose attribute distributions are plausible but invented, so the uniqueness percentages should be read as demonstrations of a mechanism, not as estimates for any real population; the published re-identification studies are the source for the real figures.626369 E2's linkage assumes both services record the shared actions reliably, which flatters the attacker; real event streams drop data, and drops break intersections. E4's graph is hand-built, and the register is simulated by an annotation probability that decays with distance from the collection point — the shape is right, the exact 14-of-35 is a draw from a seeded generator. E6 uses a count model over a tiny alphabet; the dependence on repetition transfers, the absolute exposure numbers do not. E8's workload is generated from twelve templates and real workloads are far more heterogeneous, which would generally make the over-permission ratio worse rather than better.

Three things this article leaves out that a complete treatment would cover: secure multi-party computation and private set intersection, which change what two parties can compute without either learning the other's input; federated learning and the substantial literature on what it does and does not protect; and the entire question of inference from data that is not personal at all, where the privacy loss happens in a model that never saw a name. It also treats the law as a source of requirements rather than as a subject, which understates how much of the current uncertainty — the status of pseudonymised data after EDPS v SRB, the fate of the Digital Omnibus, the phasing of the Californian regulations through 2027 to 2030 — will land on engineering teams as changed requirements rather than as legal news.3840132

Finally, a limit on the framing itself. The ladder measures what a system makes impossible, which is the right question for a builder and the wrong one for a citizen. Contextual integrity asks a different and prior question: whether a flow is appropriate to the context it occurs in, regardless of whether it was consented to or technically constrained.133134 A system can score well on every plate here and still be doing something it has no business doing. R5 on a datum that should never have been in the product is a well-engineered answer to the wrong question.135136

12. Method, and the lab output in full

The eighteen plates were written before the experiments were run, and two rung assignments moved afterwards: plate 9 was raised from R1 to R2 once the grant model in experiment E8 turned out to be a genuine runtime check asking the wrong question, and plate 2 was lowered from R3 to R2 once experiment E2 showed that a strong identifier mechanism does not by itself buy unlinkability. No experiment was re-run after its result was known, except where a defect was found and disclosed: E5's instance counting initially scored encoded payloads as carrying nothing, E6's ranking was rewritten to use mid-ranks because a count model produces large ties, and E7's k-anonymity search was rewritten to allow one per cent suppression after the first version drove every k to full postcode suppression.
