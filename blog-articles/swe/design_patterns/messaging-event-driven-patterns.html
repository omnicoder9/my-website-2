Every messaging pattern is sold as decoupling, and the word hides more than it says. This article reads the catalogue another way. Each pattern gives the sender permission not to know something about its receivers: who they are, how many there are, whether they are running, how fast they work, whether they succeeded, or what shape of data they need. Each permission is paid for somewhere else, by a broker, a store, a consumer, an operator, or the sender itself. Twenty-four patterns are coded on those six dimensions and five payers, with a history running from queued transactions to Kafka share groups, examples in eight technologies, and a nine-experiment laboratory that prices the bills. The census finds that identity, number and liveness are granted by 11, 8 and 8 patterns and settled by none, while outcome is granted by 3 and settled by 8: the ignorance that decoupling buys cheaply is paid once in infrastructure, and the ignorance it buys dearly is paid per message in application code, most often by the producer it was meant to free.

01The question

Engineers reach for a message broker when they want two pieces of software to stop depending on each other. The word they use is decoupling, and it is almost always true and almost never precise. A queue between two services removes some dependencies, adds others, and moves several to places nobody is watching. The patterns in the standard catalogues describe the mechanisms well [1–3]; what they rarely do is state, for each one, exactly what dependency has gone and where it went.

The most useful earlier attempt is Eugster, Felber, Guerraoui and Kermarrec's survey of publish–subscribe, which split decoupling into three: space, meaning the parties need not know each other; time, meaning they need not be active together; and synchronisation, meaning the producer is not blocked while consumers work [4]. The split was a real gain in precision. It does not cover everything a modern event-driven design lets a sender forget, and it says nothing about the other side of the ledger.

This article asks a single question of each pattern: what is the sender allowed not to know, and who is made to know it instead? The answer is recorded on six dimensions of permitted ignorance, extending Eugster's three.

Two of the six split an Eugster category. Space decoupling becomes identity (who receives) and number (how many), because patterns grant them separately: a point-to-point queue hides the reader but promises there is exactly one. Synchronisation decoupling becomes pace. Time decoupling becomes liveness. The remaining two are new: outcome, whether handling succeeded, and form, what shape of data the receiver needs. Outcome turns out to be the dimension that organises the whole catalogue.

A pattern can do two things on a dimension. It can grant ignorance, letting the sender stop tracking something. Or it can settle it, paying back an ignorance granted elsewhere, usually by returning some knowledge to the sender or by adding machinery at the receiver. Every grant is paid by someone. The ledger names five payers: the producer, the broker or routing fabric, a store such as a log or database, the consumer, and the operator, meaning a person. Each bill is given a severity from one to three.

The device used throughout is a waybill, a small consignment note attached to each of 24 pattern plates. Its six tabs show what the pattern grants (solid) and settles (struck through); its lower lane shows where the bill lands and how heavy it is. The plates share a fixed set of fields, and the ledger in the penultimate section collects them.

The coding is a judgement, stated so it can be disputed. A different analyst could reasonably score competing consumers as granting number ignorance, or change-data capture as a store bill rather than a producer one. The laboratory therefore computes its census from the coding table rather than from prose, and reports which conclusions would move if a cell changed.

02From queued transactions to the log

Messaging is older than the vocabulary now used for it. The theoretical roots are in concurrency research of the 1970s. Hewitt's actor formalism made asynchronous message passing between independent agents the primitive of computation [5], and Hoare's communicating sequential processes made synchronous channels the primitive of a programming language [6]. Armstrong's Erlang later showed that isolated processes exchanging asynchronous messages could carry telecom-grade reliability, with failure handled by supervision rather than prevention [7]. Lamport's paper on logical clocks supplied the fact that every later ordering guarantee has to respect: distributed events have only a partial order unless something imposes more [8]. Gelernter's Linda proposed a shared tuple space where writers and readers never meet, the purest statement of identity and liveness ignorance in the literature [9].

Commercial messaging grew out of transaction processing. TP monitors in the 1980s used queues so that a request could be accepted when a server was busy or down and executed later inside its own transaction; Bernstein, Hsu and Mann formalised recoverable requests built this way in 1990 [12], and Bernstein and Newcomer's textbook traces the line into IBM's MQSeries and its competitors [33]. On the publish–subscribe side, Teknekron's Information Bus, described at SOSP in 1993, used subject-based addressing so that financial trading applications could be added without reconfiguring the producers of market data [13]. Birman's ISIS toolkit, a few years earlier, had shown that group communication could offer ordering and membership guarantees strong enough to build replicated services on [11].

The late 1990s and early 2000s standardised the interfaces. Java Message Service unified queue and topic programming under one API [16], later continued as Jakarta Messaging [34]. The object-oriented community had meanwhile named the in-process version: the Observer pattern of the Gang of Four, descended from Smalltalk's model–view–controller dependents [14,35]. Academic content-based routing networks such as Siena and Gryphon explored what happens when subscriptions filter on message contents across a wide-area network [15,36]. MQTT began at IBM as a lightweight telemetry protocol for constrained links and was later standardised by OASIS in 2014 and by ISO in 2016 [24,37,38]. Luckham's work on complex event processing moved attention from delivering events to reasoning over streams of them [39].

The 2000s produced the catalogue itself. Hohpe and Woolf's Enterprise Integration Patterns named sixty-five patterns and gave them a visual language that is still used [1]; Eugster's survey appeared the same year [4]. JPMorgan's frustration with proprietary middleware led to the Advanced Message Queuing Protocol, an open wire protocol whose 1.0 version became an OASIS standard in 2012 and ISO/IEC 19464 in 2014 [22,40,41]. RabbitMQ implemented the earlier AMQP 0-9-1 model of exchanges, bindings and queues and became one of the most widely deployed open-source brokers [42]. Amazon made SQS generally available in July 2006, and AWS describes it as one of its first three public services [18,43]. Hintjens's ZeroMQ argued the opposite position, that messaging patterns belong in a library with no broker at all [44].

Two ideas from the same period changed what an event was for. Fowler described event sourcing in 2005: store the changes, derive the state [17]. Young developed command–query responsibility segregation alongside it and published his notes in 2010 [20]. Helland's paper on life without distributed transactions argued that scalable systems must be built from entities that communicate by messages and cannot rely on atomic commitment across them [19].

Then the log arrived as infrastructure. LinkedIn built Kafka to move activity data at a scale that existing brokers could not handle, presenting it at the NetDB workshop in 2011 [21,45]; Kreps's 2013 essay argued that the replicated append-only log was the right abstraction for data integration generally [23]. Stream processors grew up around it: Storm at Twitter, Samza at LinkedIn, Flink from the Stratosphere project, and Google's Dataflow model for unbounded, out-of-order data [46–49]. Yahoo's Pulsar, a top-level Apache project from 2018, separated serving from storage [26]. Change data capture turned database logs into event streams at LinkedIn and Facebook [50,51].

The most recent decade has been about interoperability and convergence. CloudEvents standardised an event envelope and graduated in the CNCF in January 2024 [30,52]; AsyncAPI 3.0 appeared in December 2023 [28]; MQTT 5 added request–reply, flow control and reason codes in 2019 [27]. The brokers have borrowed from one another. RabbitMQ added replicated quorum queues and log-like streams, and in 4.0 removed its older mirrored classic queues entirely and made AMQP 1.0 a core protocol [29,53,54]. Kafka replaced ZooKeeper with its own Raft-based metadata quorum, and version 4.0 in March 2025 shipped without ZooKeeper and with an early access release of share groups, which give Kafka queue semantics [31,55,56]. Kafka 4.2, announced on 17 February 2026, declared share groups production-ready [32,57,58]. EventStoreDB, the best-known dedicated event-sourcing database, was renamed KurrentDB when its company rebranded in late 2024 [59,60]. The log learned to queue and the queue learned to log.

03What travels, and what it promises

Three kinds of message are worth separating before the patterns. A command asks a specific receiver to do something and expects it to be done once. A document or reply carries data someone asked for. An event states that something has already happened and is addressed to nobody in particular [1,61]. The kinds grant different amounts of ignorance by construction. A command sender necessarily knows there is someone whose job it is to act; an event sender need not know anyone is listening. Fowler's taxonomy of event-driven styles is built on this distinction [62], and textbook accounts of message-oriented middleware draw the same line between transient and persistent communication [63].

The channel topologies underneath the patterns are few. A point-to-point channel delivers each message to one receiver. A publish–subscribe channel delivers each message to every subscriber. A partitioned log retains every message and lets any number of readers progress through it independently, with order preserved only within a partition.

Delivery guarantees come in three grades. At most once: send and forget; a crash loses messages. At least once: retain until acknowledged; a crash between processing and acknowledgement duplicates messages. Exactly once is not a delivery property on an unreliable network. The two-generals problem, stated first by Akkoyunlu, Ekanadham and Huber and named by Gray, shows that no finite exchange of messages over a lossy link lets both sides be certain they agree [65,66], and the Fischer–Lynch–Paterson result rules out deterministic consensus in an asynchronous system with even one crash [67]. What systems advertise as exactly once is effectively once: at-least-once delivery combined with idempotent or transactional processing so that repeats have no additional effect [25,68].

That single fact explains most of the catalogue. Every durable channel is at-least-once underneath. At-least-once creates outcome uncertainty that nobody asked for, and a large share of the patterns exist to settle it. The ledger shows exactly how large.

Ordering is the other promise that is always narrower than it sounds. A queue drained by one reader is ordered; drained by several, it is not, because a slow consumer finishes after a fast one that started later. Every technology that offers ordering at scale offers it per key, per session or per partition, never globally [64,69–71]. And every replicated broker is, underneath, a consensus system subject to the usual trade-offs between consistency and availability under partition [72]. Independent testing has found acknowledged writes lost in brokers under faults more than once: Jepsen's 2013 and 2014 analyses of Kafka and RabbitMQ, and its December 2025 analysis of NATS JetStream 2.12.1, which attributed data loss in part to a default of flushing to disk every two minutes rather than before acknowledging [73–75]. A delivery guarantee is a claim about a protocol and its configuration, not about a product name.

04Channels

The first family is the channels themselves. They grant the cheapest ignorances in the ledger, identity, number and liveness, and pay for them in broker and storage capacity: resources bought once and shared by every message.

A1

Point-to-point queue

Sender may forget
Which process will read the message, and whether any reader is running right now.
Remembered instead by
The broker, which holds the message durably until exactly one consumer takes and acknowledges it.
The bill
Storage and replication on the broker; an unbounded backlog if readers stay away.
Breaks when
The queue is treated as a database: long retention, ad hoc inspection, or reliance on the order of a queue that several readers drain.
In practice
SQS standard queues [76]; RabbitMQ quorum queues [53]; JMS and Jakarta Messaging Queue [16,34]; Azure Service Bus queues [77].

The oldest pattern in the catalogue and still the plainest: a named place that stores messages until one reader removes each. Its ancestry is the recoverable request queue of transaction-processing monitors, where a request was written to a queue inside one transaction and removed inside another so that neither side needed the other to be up [12,33]. The sender addresses the queue, not a reader, so identity and liveness drop out of its knowledge. What it keeps is cardinality: a point-to-point channel promises that one consumer, not several, will act [1]. Replication of the stored messages is now a consensus problem; RabbitMQ's quorum queues and Kafka's KRaft metadata both run Raft underneath [53,55,78].

A minimal RabbitMQ setup shows how much of the queue's contract lives in configuration. The queue type, the dead-letter route and the delivery limit are declared once; publisher confirms turn the send into an acknowledged hand-over, and manual consumer acknowledgements make redelivery possible [53,79,80].

SQS exposes the same contract through a lease. A received message becomes invisible for the visibility timeout; if it is not deleted before the timeout expires, it reappears for another consumer [76]. The deletion is the acknowledgement.

A2

Publish–subscribe topic

Sender may forget
Who is listening and how many listeners there are.
Remembered instead by
The subscription table, held by the broker or a routing fabric, which expands one publish into many deliveries.
The bill
Fan-out egress on the broker; a subscriber that was not connected when the message passed may simply never see it.
Breaks when
A subscriber is assumed to be durable when the subscription was not, or a publisher quietly depends on a particular subscriber reacting.
In practice
RabbitMQ fanout and topic exchanges [81]; SNS topics fanning out to SQS queues [82]; MQTT topics [27]; Pulsar subscriptions [83]; the Azure Publisher-Subscriber pattern [84].

Publish–subscribe removes two things from the sender at once: who receives and how many. Eugster and colleagues called the first space decoupling and noted that it arrives bundled with time and synchronisation decoupling in most systems, though not all [4]. The bundling matters. A JMS non-durable subscriber, an MQTT client without a persistent session, or a plain NATS subject all forget messages published while the subscriber was away, so the grant of liveness ignorance is a property of the subscription, not the topic [16,27,85]. The in-process ancestor is the Observer pattern and, before that, Smalltalk's model–view–controller dependency mechanism [14,35].

A3

Partitioned log

Sender may forget
Who reads, how many groups read, whether they are running, and how far behind they are.
Remembered instead by
An append-only, retained log; each consumer group remembers only its own offset into it.
The bill
Retained storage, sized by time rather than by consumption; consumers now own their position and must manage it.
Breaks when
Retention is shorter than the slowest reader's worst outage, or a consumer commits an offset before the work it stands for is durable.
In practice
Kafka topics and consumer groups [64,86]; RabbitMQ streams [54]; Pulsar topics with persistent cursors [83]; Kinesis-style shards.

The log inverts the queue's contract. A queue forgets a message once it is taken; a log keeps it for a retention period and lets every reader keep a cursor. Kreps, Narkhede and Rao designed Kafka around this at LinkedIn precisely so that new consumers could be added without asking the producers [21], and Kreps later argued that the log is the unifying abstraction behind databases, replication and stream processing alike [23]. The idea is older than Kafka: write-ahead logging in ARIES and the replicated state machine of Schneider's tutorial both treat an ordered log as the thing that is true, with state as a cache [87,88]. The log grants the most ignorance of any channel on the plates, and pays for it in bytes.

The difference between a queue and a log shows up most clearly when a new reader appears. A queue has already given its messages away; a log still has them. Kafka's documentation describes the consumer's position as a single integer per partition, which is what makes replay and late joiners cheap [64].

A4

Request–reply

Sender may forget
Which instance will answer, and whether it is up at the moment the request is sent.
Remembered instead by
The requester, which must hold a correlation identifier, a reply address and a timer for every request in flight.
The bill
Per-request state and a timeout policy on the sender; a late reply after the timer fired is a second decision nobody planned for.
Breaks when
Request–reply is used to hide a synchronous call behind a queue, so a slow responder becomes an unbounded wait at the caller.
In practice
JMS JMSReplyTo and JMSCorrelationID [16]; AMQP 1.0 reply-to and correlation-id properties [22]; MQTT 5 response topic and correlation data [27].

Request–reply over messaging buys back something the channel took away: the requester wants to know the outcome. The Return Address and Correlation Identifier patterns exist to make that possible [1], and they were standardised directly into the headers of JMS, AMQP 1.0 and, in 2019, MQTT 5 [16,22,27]. The asymmetry with remote procedure call is instructive. Birrell and Nelson made the call look local [89]; Waldo and colleagues argued that pretending partial failure away was the error [90]. Request–reply over a queue does not pretend: the timeout is visible in the code, and so is the question of what a late answer means.

A5

Dead-letter channel

Sender may forget
What happens to a message that no consumer can process.
Remembered instead by
A separate channel that collects failed messages together with the reason and delivery count.
The bill
A human, eventually: someone must inspect, fix and replay, and the replay itself is a new source of duplicates.
Breaks when
Nobody watches the dead-letter channel, or messages are replayed after the code or schema they depend on has moved on.
In practice
RabbitMQ dead-letter exchanges and the quorum-queue delivery limit [29,80]; SQS redrive policy and maxReceiveCount [91]; Kafka Streams dead-letter handling in 4.2 [32]; share-group delivery counts [58].

A poison message fails every time it is delivered, and an at-least-once channel will deliver it forever. The dead-letter channel breaks that loop by moving the message aside after a bounded number of attempts [1]. RabbitMQ 4.0 made the bound a default: quorum queues now dead-letter or drop a message after 20 redeliveries, with the release notes explaining that the limit protects nodes from consumers stuck in a fail–requeue loop [29]. Kafka, which had no per-record delivery count in consumer groups, gained one with share groups [58]. The plate grants nothing to the sender. It settles outcome, and it settles it by handing the question to an operator.

05Consumers

The second family concerns how receivers share work and how they absorb the consequences of the channel's promises. Here the bills start to land on application code.

B1

Competing consumers

Sender may forget
How many workers exist and which one will take a given message.
Remembered instead by
The channel, which leases each message to one worker and takes it back if the lease expires unacknowledged.
The bill
Consumers must tolerate redelivery and must not depend on the relative order of messages handled by different workers.
Breaks when
Throughput is scaled by adding workers to a channel whose unit of parallelism is fixed elsewhere, such as a partition count.
In practice
SQS visibility timeout [76]; RabbitMQ prefetch and manual acks [79]; NATS queue groups [85]; Pulsar shared subscriptions [83]; Kafka share groups, production-ready in 4.2 [58,92].

Competing consumers is the workhorse of the catalogue: several receivers on one point-to-point channel, each taking the next message [1,77]. The producer's ignorance of the worker count is what makes elastic scaling possible. E1 in the lab shows the queueing arithmetic: at 800 messages per second of offered load on ten-millisecond work, a ninth consumer takes mean wait from unbounded to 6.53 ms, and going from nine to twelve cuts it 18.7-fold. The same experiment shows why Kafka needed a new group type. A classic consumer group assigns each partition to one member, so on 8 partitions a ninth member sits idle whatever the load [93,94]. Share groups remove that cap by acknowledging records individually, which Kafka 4.0 shipped as early access and 4.2 declared production-ready [32,56,58].

Kafka's share consumer makes the change visible in the API. Instead of committing an offset, the consumer acknowledges each record as accepted, released for redelivery, or rejected [92,94]. That is the competing-consumers contract of a traditional queue, expressed over a log.

B2

Keyed ordering

Sender may forget
Nothing new. The producer must now know which key names the unit whose order matters.
Remembered instead by
The channel, which serialises delivery per key, per session or per message group.
The bill
Parallelism is capped by the hottest key; a slow message blocks everything behind it in the same group.
Breaks when
Key popularity is skewed, or the key chosen is not the unit the consumer actually needs ordered.
In practice
Kafka partition keys [64]; SQS FIFO MessageGroupId [69]; Azure Service Bus sessions [70]; Pub/Sub ordering keys [71]; Pulsar key-shared subscriptions [83].

Every technology that offers ordering offers it per key, never globally, because a total order is a single serial bottleneck [8]. Keyed ordering hands pace knowledge back to the producer: it must pick the key, and the key determines how much of the consumer fleet can work at once. E2 measures the ceiling. With ten thousand keys under a Zipf popularity of exponent 1.2 the hottest key carries 20.84% of traffic, so no number of partitions can deliver more than 4.80-way parallelism; at 128 partitions the measured figure is 4.76. At exponent 1.0 the ceiling is 9.79. The plate's signature is identical to back-pressure's, and the lab reports the collision rather than hiding it: both return knowledge of the consumer's pace to the sender.

B3

Idempotent receiver

Sender may forget
Whether a message has already been handled.
Remembered instead by
The consumer, in a store of processed message identifiers or in the natural idempotence of the operation itself.
The bill
A deduplication store, a retention window for it, and a message identity that survives every retry path.
Breaks when
The window is shorter than the slowest redelivery path, or the identifier is regenerated on retry.
In practice
NATS JetStream Nats-Msg-Id with a stream Duplicates window [97]; SQS FIFO deduplication IDs [69]; Azure Service Bus duplicate detection [98]; Kafka idempotent producer sequence numbers [25].

At-least-once delivery is the honest default of every durable channel, because the sender cannot distinguish a lost message from a lost acknowledgement; this is the two-generals problem in its original setting [65,66]. The consumer therefore has to make a repeated message harmless. Helland's advice is to design operations so that repetition is a no-op, and to remember what has been done where it cannot be [68]. The window is the whole question. E3 generated 7,573 duplicates from four realistic paths and found that every window from two minutes to one hour catches exactly the same 93.33%: all of the misses are dead-letter replays, which arrive hours later. The platform defaults the experiment borrowed are two minutes for JetStream, five for SQS FIFO and ten for Service Bus [69,97,98].

NATS JetStream implements deduplication at the server: a publisher sets a message identifier and the stream drops repeats that arrive inside its configured duplicate window [97]. The window is a stream property, which is the right place for it, and it is finite, which is the point of E3.

B4

Queue-based load levelling

Sender may forget
How fast the consumer drains relative to the rate the producer fills.
Remembered instead by
The queue, which absorbs the difference as depth.
The bill
Latency during bursts, and broker storage sized for the largest burst the design admits.
Breaks when
The average arrival rate exceeds capacity even slightly; the queue then grows without bound and levels nothing.
In practice
Azure Queue-Based Load Leveling [99]; SQS in front of a fixed worker pool [76]; SEDA stage queues [100].

A queue between a bursty producer and a steady consumer converts a rate mismatch into a depth, which is the whole point of the pattern [99]. Welsh, Culler and Brewer built an entire server architecture from stages joined by such queues [100]. The condition is arithmetic, and Little's law makes it exact [101]. In E8 a producer averaging 740 messages per second against a capacity of 1,000 peaks at a depth of 30,869 and drains fully; a producer with the same bursts but a mean just 1% over capacity ends an hour with 35,458 messages it will never catch up on. Kleinrock's first volume is the reference for why the transition at utilisation one is a cliff and not a slope [96].

B5

Back-pressure

Sender may forget
Nothing. The sender learns the consumer's pace again, as a signal to slow down.
Remembered instead by
Credit, demand or flow-control state passed upstream from the consumer.
The bill
The producer blocks, drops or buffers; the pressure has to end somewhere, and it ends at the origin.
Breaks when
A buffer between producer and consumer is unbounded, so the pressure signal never arrives and memory runs out instead.
In practice
Reactive Streams request(n) and JDK Flow [102]; AMQP 1.0 link credit [22]; RabbitMQ publisher flow control and prefetch [79]; MQTT 5 Receive Maximum [27].

Back-pressure is the settlement for load levelling's grant. If the queue must not grow forever, something has to tell the producer to stop, and the only thing that can is the consumer's own pace, sent upstream. Reactive Streams makes this a protocol: a subscriber signals how many items it can take and the publisher may not send more [102]. AMQP 1.0 builds the same idea into the wire format as link credit [22], and MQTT 5 added a Receive Maximum so a client can cap in-flight QoS 1 and 2 messages [27]. Nygard's catalogue of stability patterns treats unbounded buffers as a failure waiting for a trigger [103]. The bill lands where decoupling was supposed to lift it: on the sender.

06Routing and form

The third family moves knowledge about destinations and data shapes out of senders and receivers into shared intermediaries. It is where the operator starts to appear as a payer, because routing rules and schemas are maintained by people.

C1

Content-based router

Sender may forget
Which destination handles which kind of message.
Remembered instead by
A router, whose rules are configuration somebody maintains.
The bill
Routing knowledge is centralised; changing a destination is a change to shared infrastructure, not to the sender or receiver.
Breaks when
The router accumulates business rules until it is an unowned application in its own right.
In practice
RabbitMQ topic and headers exchanges [81]; EventBridge rules on an event bus [104]; content-based networks such as Siena and Gryphon [15,36].

A content-based router reads each message and chooses its destination, so the sender addresses a function rather than a place [1]. Research systems in the late 1990s pushed the idea across whole networks: Siena and IBM's Gryphon matched subscriptions against message content at every hop [15,36]. In commercial systems the rule language usually lives in the broker, as in RabbitMQ's exchange types or EventBridge's event patterns [81,104]. The knowledge has not vanished; it has moved into a table maintained by people. That is why this plate's bill lands on the operator rather than on any running process.

C2

Scatter–gather

Sender may forget
Which responders exist and how many there are.
Remembered instead by
The requester's aggregator, which must decide when the set of replies is complete.
The bill
A completion rule, a timeout, a policy for partial results and state held for every open request.
Breaks when
The completion rule assumes a responder count that the grant of ignorance was meant to hide.
In practice
Scatter–gather and Aggregator [1]; request fan-out with hedged or partial answers in tail-tolerant services [105].

Scatter–gather broadcasts a request and aggregates whatever comes back [1]. It grants identity and number ignorance on the way out and then must settle outcome on the way in, and the settlement is the hard half. An aggregator has to decide when it has enough, which is a statement about responders the sender supposedly did not need to know. Dean and Barroso's account of tail latency describes the same tension in synchronous fan-out: waiting for the slowest of many replies makes the tail the median [105]. The practical answers are timeouts and partial results, both of which the requester owns. This is one of three severity-three bills that land on the producer.

C3

Claim check

Sender may forget
Whether the payload fits the channel's size limit or retention.
Remembered instead by
An external store holding the payload; the message carries only a reference.
The bill
Two systems with different lifetimes: a reference can outlive its payload, and a payload can outlive every reference.
Breaks when
Payload deletion is not tied to message consumption, so consumers meet dangling references or storage fills with orphans.
In practice
Azure Claim-Check [106]; the SQS extended client pattern with object storage; any message carrying a URI to a blob.

Brokers are poor at large payloads. The claim check stores the payload elsewhere and sends a token, which keeps the channel small and fast [1,106]. The sender may now ignore the channel's size constraint, which is the only form-related ignorance on the plates that concerns the channel rather than the receiver. The cost is that the message and the payload are no longer one object. Their lifetimes have to be reconciled by hand, and every consumer now depends on a second system being available at read time. The pattern trades a hard limit for a consistency problem.

C4

Envelope and schema contract

Sender may forget
Nothing. The producer takes on the duty to describe its message in a form others can route and check.
Remembered instead by
A shared envelope and a schema contract, often enforced by a registry or by consumer-driven tests.
The bill
Discipline at the source: metadata on every message, compatibility rules on every change.
Breaks when
The envelope is adopted but the payload schema is not versioned, so routers understand messages that consumers cannot.
In practice
CloudEvents 1.0 [52]; AsyncAPI 3.0 channel and message descriptions [28]; Avro and Protocol Buffers schema evolution [107]; consumer-driven contracts [108].

Every grant of form ignorance needs a counterpart that keeps messages intelligible. The envelope pattern puts routing metadata outside the payload [1]; CloudEvents standardises that envelope with a handful of required attributes and became a graduated CNCF project in January 2024 [30,52]. AsyncAPI does for message-driven interfaces what OpenAPI did for HTTP, describing channels, operations and payloads in a machine-readable document; version 3.0 separated operations from channels [28]. Schema evolution rules decide which changes old readers survive [107], and consumer-driven contracts let each reader state what it relies on [108]. The producer pays, but only a little, which is why the severity is one.

A CloudEvents envelope carries the four required attributes (specversion, id, source, type) and optional ones such as subject, time and dataschema [52]. Routers can act on the envelope without parsing the payload; consumers still need the schema.

MQTT shows the same concerns at the constrained end of the spectrum. Topics are hierarchical strings that brokers match against subscriptions with wildcards, quality of service is chosen per message, and a retained message plus a will message together give late subscribers the last known state and an automatic notice when a client disappears [27,109].

07Events as the record

The fourth family changes what a message is for. Instead of carrying work between components, events become the record of what happened, and other components derive what they need from it. The grants are the widest on the plates; so are the bills.

D1

Event notification

Sender may forget
Who cares that something happened, how many care, whether they are up, how fast they act and whether they succeed.
Remembered instead by
Each subscriber, which fetches any detail it needs from the source when it reacts.
The bill
The flow of the system is no longer written down anywhere; it has to be reconstructed from subscriptions.
Breaks when
Subscribers call back to the source for data, so the source is coupled by query to every listener it does not know about.
In practice
Domain events [61,110]; SNS and EventBridge notifications [82,104]; Thialfi-style invalidation notices [111].

Fowler separated four meanings of “event-driven” and put event notification first: the source announces that something changed and does not care what anyone does about it [62]. This is the widest grant on the plates, five of six dimensions, and it is shared with only two others. Google's Thialfi is a clean example at internet scale: it delivered notices that an object's version had changed, leaving clients to fetch the new state [111]. The bill is architectural rather than operational. Fowler's warning is that a chain of notifications can implement a business process that no single piece of code describes [62], so the plate's payer is the operator trying to understand it.

D2

Event-carried state transfer

Sender may forget
The same five things as event notification, and also what data the receiver needs.
Remembered instead by
Each consumer, which keeps its own replica of the state it cares about, built from the events.
The bill
Storage and staleness on every consumer; each replica is eventually consistent and some will be wrong for a while.
Breaks when
Events carry deltas that only make sense with prior state a new consumer never saw.
In practice
Compacted Kafka topics as replicated tables [23,64]; Wormhole and Databus change streams [50,51].

Event-carried state transfer puts enough data in each event that consumers never call back [62]. The receiver builds a local copy and can answer from it even when the source is down, which is liveness ignorance in both directions. Bailis and Ghodsi's survey is a useful reminder of what the consumer has then signed up for: eventual consistency with no bound on staleness unless something else supplies one [112]. Kafka's log compaction exists for this pattern, keeping the latest value for each key so a new consumer can rebuild a table without reading all history [64]. The bill is paid by consumers, and it scales with how many of them there are.

D3

Event sourcing

Sender may forget
Which read models will exist in future and what shapes they will need.
Remembered instead by
The event store, which keeps every state-changing event as the system of record.
The bill
The events are permanent. Their schemas must be readable forever, and every future reader pays replay time.
Breaks when
An event schema has to change in a way old events cannot be migrated to, or replay becomes too slow to use.
In practice
KurrentDB, formerly EventStoreDB [59,60]; Kafka as an event log [3]; aggregates in domain-driven design [61].

Event sourcing stores the sequence of changes rather than the latest state and derives state by replay [17]. The writer need not know how the data will be read, because any future read model can be built from the log. Helland's essay on immutability gives the general argument: append-only records are simpler to replicate and reason about than updatable ones [113]. The cost is the other half of the same sentence. Overeem and colleagues interviewed practitioners and found schema evolution to be the recurring pain, with teams choosing between versioned events, upcasting on read and rewriting the store [114]. E5 prices replay: without snapshots, a five-year aggregate at fifty events a day costs 1.825 s per load; with snapshots every 32 events the work per event falls to 311 microseconds.

The core of an event-sourced aggregate is small: a function that decides whether a command is allowed and records an event, a function that applies events to state, and an append that fails if someone else has appended since the aggregate was loaded [17,61]. The optimistic concurrency check on the expected version is what stands in for a lock.

D4

CQRS

Sender may forget
What queries the read side must answer, and how quickly those views are refreshed.
Remembered instead by
One or more read models, updated asynchronously from the write model's events.
The bill
Consumers of the read side see stale data and must be designed, and sometimes user interfaces must be designed, to tolerate it.
Breaks when
A user writes and immediately reads, and the read model has not caught up.
In practice
Young's CQRS documents [20]; Fowler's cautionary note [115]; projections in KurrentDB [60]; Kafka Streams and Samza state stores [47].

Command–query responsibility segregation splits the model that validates changes from the models that answer questions [20]. Young developed it alongside event sourcing, and the two are usually taught together, but neither requires the other. The grant is form ignorance on the write side, plus pace: projections can lag. Fowler's own summary is cautionary, noting that the pattern adds risky complexity and suits only some parts of some systems [115]. The stream-processing literature supplies the machinery for keeping derived views correct under failure, from Chandy and Lamport's snapshots to Flink's checkpoints and Samza's changelogged state [47,48,116].

D5

Change data capture

Sender may forget
That it is publishing anything at all.
Remembered instead by
The database's own transaction log, tailed by a connector that turns row changes into events.
The bill
The producer's table layout becomes a public contract it never agreed to offer.
Breaks when
The source team renames a column, and consumers they have never heard of fail.
In practice
Debezium [117]; LinkedIn Databus [50]; Facebook Wormhole [51].

Change data capture reads the write-ahead log of a database and publishes each committed change [118]. LinkedIn's Databus and Facebook's Wormhole both built pub–sub on this principle, Wormhole to keep geo-replicated caches and indexes in step with MySQL [50,51]. The grant is total: the application does not know it is a producer. That is exactly why the bill is severe and lands on the producer. Internal schema becomes an interface by accident. The outbox (E1) is the usual cure, publishing a deliberately designed event from a dedicated table rather than the raw rows of the business tables [117,119].

08Coordination without a coordinator

The last family deals with the question distributed transactions used to answer: how several services agree on an outcome when none can lock the others. Helland's answer was that at scale they do not agree atomically; they exchange messages and reconcile [19]. Every plate here settles outcome, and most of them bill the producer for it.

E1

Transactional outbox

Sender may forget
Whether the broker is reachable at the moment the business transaction commits.
Remembered instead by
An outbox table in the same database, written in the same transaction, drained later by a relay.
The bill
A table, a relay process and duplicate publishes whenever the relay crashes between sending and marking.
Breaks when
The relay is not monitored, or consumers are not idempotent and the duplicates it guarantees cause double effects.
In practice
Transactional outbox [2,119]; Debezium outbox event router [117]; recoverable request queues [12].

A service that updates its database and publishes an event is performing two writes to two systems, and without a distributed transaction one can succeed while the other fails. E4 counts the damage over 1,000,000 operations: commit-then-publish lost 1,574 events; publish-then-commit emitted 2,514 events for changes that never happened. The outbox writes the event into the same database transaction as the state change, so the two cannot disagree, and a relay publishes it afterwards [119]. In the same run it lost nothing and invented nothing; it produced 477 duplicates instead. That is the trade in one line: loss becomes duplication, and duplication is what B3 already knows how to absorb.

The outbox is a few lines of SQL. The relay can be a polling loop or a log-tailing connector; Debezium's outbox event router reads the outbox table's changes and routes each row to a topic named after its aggregate type [117].

E2

Orchestrated saga

Sender may forget
How long each participant takes; no locks are held across the steps.
Remembered instead by
An orchestrator that records which steps have completed and drives compensations when one fails.
The bill
Compensating logic for every step, written by hand, plus visible intermediate states nobody can roll back.
Breaks when
A compensation is itself impossible (an email sent, a card charged and the refund rejected) or the orchestrator's own state is lost.
In practice
Sagas [10]; orchestration in the Azure and microservices.io pattern guides [120,121].

Garcia-Molina and Salem defined a saga in 1987 as a long-lived transaction broken into a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating transaction that semantically undoes it [10]. It is not a rollback: the intermediate state was visible, and compensation is a new forward action. The orchestrated form puts the sequence in one component, which knows every participant and every outcome. E6 compares it with two-phase commit. For five participants 2PC needs all six parties up at once, 99.40% of the time at 99.9% each, and blocks if the coordinator fails mid-protocol [122]; the saga never blocks, but with a 2% business rejection per step it aborts 9.61% of the time and then runs 1.96 compensations on average.

E3

Choreographed saga

Sender may forget
Who else takes part and in what order, beyond the event it reacts to.
Remembered instead by
Nobody in particular: each participant reacts to the previous one's event and emits its own.
The bill
The process exists only as an emergent chain; monitoring, reasoning about and changing it fall to operators.
Breaks when
A new participant subscribes mid-chain, or a compensation event is missed, and no component knows the saga is stuck.
In practice
Choreography in the saga pattern guides [120,121]; Hohpe's coffee shop [123].

The choreographed saga removes the orchestrator. Each service listens for the event that concerns it, does its local work and publishes the next event [121]. It grants more ignorance than orchestration, since no component knows the whole sequence, and it pays for it in the one place that cannot be automated away. Hohpe's short essay on why a coffee shop does not use two-phase commit remains the clearest statement of the design stance: accept that things go wrong, correlate by order number, and compensate or write off [123]. Laigner and colleagues' survey of practitioners found that consistency across microservices is still handled mostly by such application-level logic rather than by platform support [124].

E4

Transactional read–process–write

Sender may forget
Whether the previous attempt at a consume–transform–produce step committed.
Remembered instead by
A transaction coordinator in the broker, which commits output records and input offsets atomically.
The bill
Broker-side transaction state, fencing of zombie producers, and a guarantee that stops at the broker's edge.
Breaks when
The step has a side effect outside the broker, such as a database write or an HTTP call, which the transaction cannot include.
In practice
Kafka transactions and read_committed consumers [25,125]; Pub/Sub exactly-once delivery within a region [126].

Kafka's exactly-once semantics, introduced by KIP-98 in 2017, combine an idempotent producer with transactions that include the consumer's offsets, so a read–process–write cycle either happens entirely or not at all as seen by read_committed readers [25,125]. It settles outcome for a closed loop: from Kafka, through a processor, back to Kafka. Two caveats belong on the plate. The guarantee does not cover effects outside the broker. And in 2024 Jepsen's analysis of Bufstream reported write loss, aborted reads and torn transactions arising from ordering gaps in the Kafka transaction protocol itself, affecting Kafka and compatible systems, with a fix planned under KIP-890 [127]. Exactly-once is a property of a protocol, and protocols have bugs.

In Kafka, the read–process–write loop looks like this. The producer is transactional, the consumer reads only committed data, and the consumer's offsets are committed inside the producer's transaction [25,125].

E5

Retry with backoff and jitter

Sender may forget
Nothing. Retrying means the sender keeps track of outcome.
Remembered instead by
The sender's retry loop and its randomised delay.
The bill
Latency on the retrying client and, if the delays are synchronised, a second outage caused by the recovery.
Breaks when
Clients retry in lockstep after a shared failure, or retries are not bounded and not idempotent.
In practice
Full, equal and decorrelated jitter [128]; client retry policies in every cloud SDK; circuit breakers [103].

Retrying is how a sender settles outcome ignorance it cannot otherwise resolve, and it only works against an idempotent receiver. The subtle part is timing. Brooker's simulations showed that exponential backoff alone leaves contending clients synchronised, and that adding randomness is what spreads them out [128]. E7 reproduces the effect. One hundred clients released together with deterministic exponential backoff stay in lockstep and none of them ever succeeds; the same clients with full jitter finish in 8.55 calls each on average. Nygard's circuit breaker is the complement: stop retrying altogether when failure is persistent [103].

Four findings follow from the census, and the laboratory computes each from the coding table.

The cheap ignorances are never settled. Identity is granted by 11 plates, number by 8 and liveness by 8, and not one plate in the catalogue exists to pay any of them back. Their bills are paid once, in infrastructure: broker capacity, retained storage, subscription tables. This is the decoupling that works as advertised.

Outcome is the centre of gravity. It is granted by only 3 plates, all of them event-style (D1, D2, D5), yet settled by 8: request–reply, the dead-letter channel, the idempotent receiver, scatter–gather, both sagas, broker transactions and retry. The imbalance has a source outside the plates. At-least-once delivery creates outcome uncertainty on every durable channel whether or not any pattern grants it, and a third of the catalogue exists to clean it up. The grants of the cheap dimensions are paid in bulk; the settlements of outcome are paid per message, in code.

The producer pays most often. Of the 24 bills, 8 land on the producer, twice as many as on any other payer, and 3 of those are severity three. Among the 8 plates that settle outcome, 4 bill the producer. Decoupling is marketed as relief for the sender, and in the cheap dimensions it is. In the expensive one, the bill comes back addressed to the party that was supposed to be freed.

The catalogue splits into grants and settlements. 13 plates only grant, 7 only settle, and 4 do both. The settlements are the patterns engineers adopt second, after an incident; the grants are the ones adopted first, at design time. A team that has adopted a grant has also, whether it knows it or not, adopted the obligation to choose a settlement.

As a classification, the waybill signature distinguishes 275 of 276 plate pairs (99.6%), against 83.3% for the family grouping. The one collision is keyed ordering and back-pressure, which both settle pace at the producer's expense. The finding most sensitive to coding is the producer's share: moving the choreographed saga's bill from the operator to the producer would raise it to nine, and moving change-data capture's to the store would lower it to seven. Neither change removes the producer's lead.

11Choosing by what you are willing not to know

The ledger suggests a way to choose among patterns and technologies that starts from the dependency to be removed rather than from the product. Four questions do most of the work.

Must the sender not know who acts, or also how many? If exactly one component should act on each message, a point-to-point channel with competing consumers is the plain answer; SQS, RabbitMQ quorum queues, NATS queue groups, Service Bus queues and now Kafka share groups all provide it [53,58,76,77,85]. If several independent components should each see every message, the choice is between a topic that forgets (SNS, core NATS, a RabbitMQ fanout exchange) and a log that remembers (Kafka, Pulsar, RabbitMQ streams) [54,64,81–83,85]. The log is the right choice when a future consumer might need the past.

Does anything need to be ordered, and by what key? If so, the key is a design decision that sets the parallelism ceiling, and E2 shows how quickly skew erodes it. Kafka partitions, SQS FIFO message groups, Service Bus sessions and Pub/Sub ordering keys are all versions of the same answer [64,69–71].

Where will outcome be settled? This question is the one most often skipped. Every durable channel delivers at least once, so every consumer needs either natural idempotence or a deduplication store whose window covers the slowest redelivery path, including manual replays [68]. Broker transactions settle outcome only for loops that stay inside the broker [25]. Anything that spans services needs a saga, and the choice between orchestration and choreography is a choice between a producer bill and an operator bill [10,121].

Who will own the form? If events are a contract, something must version them. An envelope standard such as CloudEvents, a description such as AsyncAPI and a schema registry or consumer-driven contracts cost the producer a little; change-data capture of raw tables costs it a great deal later [28,52,108,117].

The technology map is a summary of documented capabilities, not a benchmark. Every entry depends on configuration: replication factors, acknowledgement settings, fsync policies and retention decide whether a product actually provides the property its documentation lists, as the Jepsen reports repeatedly show [75,127].

12Limits of this account

The six dimensions are a proposal. They extend a published taxonomy [4] and they separate the plates well, but a different cut, for example treating ordering as its own dimension rather than as a consequence of pace, would change some counts. The five payers compress real organisations; in practice the operator and the consumer's owner are often the same team.

The coding of each plate is one analyst's judgement, applied consistently and published in full so it can be checked. The census conclusions were tested against single-cell changes, as reported with the ledger, and the headline results survived them. They would not survive a wholesale recoding.

The laboratory models are small by design. Poisson arrivals, exponential service, independent failures and fixed delays are simplifications that real systems violate, sometimes badly. Their purpose is to show the shape of each bill (a cliff, a ceiling, a flat valley, a lockstep) rather than to predict any particular system's numbers. The platform defaults used in E3 were taken from current documentation and should be checked again before being relied on.

Finally, the catalogue here is not complete. Hohpe and Woolf's book has sixty-five patterns [1]; the microservices literature adds more [2,129]. The twenty-four plates were chosen to cover each family and each technology discussed, and the method is meant to be applied to the rest.
