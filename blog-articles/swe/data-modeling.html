I Premise

A Model Is a List of Things That Cannot Happen

Ask an engineer what their data model is and they will show you a diagram of boxes: customer, order, order_line. The boxes are the least interesting part. Any bag of key–value pairs can hold a customer. What distinguishes a model from a bag is the set of states it has been engineered to refuse: an order with no customer, a line item with a negative quantity, two people holding the same email address, a price in a currency that does not exist, a shipment that predates the order it fulfils.

This is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the operational definition. Every serious modelling construct in this article — keys, cardinalities, aggregates, normal forms, foreign keys, isolation levels, schema compatibility modes, retention rules — is a mechanism for making some category of world state unrepresentable, or at least unwritable. Codd's relational model was introduced precisely as a way to protect applications from representational change while pinning down what the data means1; the normal forms he went on to define are stated as restrictions, not as capabilities2. Domain-driven design's aggregates are described as consistency boundaries — a rule about what a single transaction is allowed to touch3. Schema evolution rules in Avro and Protocol Buffers are lists of edits you must not make4,5.

Held this way, the discipline stops being a taxonomy exercise and becomes an engineering trade. You are not asking “what fields does a customer have?” You are asking: which impossible states am I willing to pay to exclude, where will I enforce the exclusion, who finds out when it fails, and what does that enforcement cost me in write throughput, join depth, migration risk, and the ability to change my mind in eighteen months?

Those questions have accumulated seventy years of answers. What follows walks the whole span: from Bachman's navigational records to Codd's relations, from entity–relationship diagrams to aggregate-oriented NoSQL and back to SQL, from CREATE TABLE to Protobuf field numbers to Iceberg column IDs, and out to the domains — healthcare, finance, geospatial, version control — where modelling decisions made decades ago are still constraining what software can be built today.

II The frame

Three Levels, Two Mappings

The most useful piece of architecture in this field is fifty years old and was never implemented as specified. In 1975 the ANSI/X3/SPARC study group proposed that a database be described at three levels: an external level of per-user views, a conceptual level holding a single implementation-independent description of the enterprise's data, and an internal level describing physical storage6. The follow-up framework report made the point sharper: the value is in the mappings between levels, because it is the mappings that let one level change without disturbing another7.

No mainstream DBMS implements the model faithfully. Almost every practising modeller uses its vocabulary anyway, usually in the collapsed form of conceptual → logical → physical. The collapse is worth resisting slightly, because the three levels answer different questions and fail in different ways.

The conceptual level records what the business asserts is true — not what the current application happens to store. “A shipment fulfils one or more order lines” is a claim about the world that outlives your persistence choice. Chen's entity–relationship model was designed for exactly this register: a notation for the things and the relationships between them, deliberately above the choice of relational, network, or hierarchical implementation8. Fact-based modelling traditions such as object-role modelling push the idea further, expressing everything as elementary facts with the constraints attached9.

The logical level turns those assertions into structures a system can check: relations and attributes, keys, foreign keys, cardinality, nullability, domains. This is where most modelling errors are actually made, because it is where you must decide whether an assertion becomes a constraint the database enforces, a rule the application enforces, or a hope nobody enforces.

The physical level chooses indexes, partitioning, file layout, compression, replication. It is the level engineers most enjoy and the one with the least semantic authority. Adding an index cannot make a bad model correct. Removing one cannot make a correct model wrong.

-- CONCEPTUAL: an order belongs to exactly one customer, always.
-- LOGICAL:
CREATE TABLE orders (

-- Drop the index and every query still returns the same rows, slower.
-- Drop the REFERENCES and the database will happily store an order
-- belonging to a customer who does not exist.

Modern tooling conflates the levels aggressively. An ORM model class is simultaneously a conceptual statement (“an order has a customer”), a logical one (“this becomes a nullable bigint column”), and often a physical one (“index this”), and a migration generator will emit all three from one edit. That convenience is worth having, and it is also why so many production schemas contain columns nobody can explain: the conceptual assertion was never written down separately from the code that happened to implement it. The corrective is cheap — keep a conceptual model in some durable form, even a plain list of sentences that must remain true — and it is the thing teams skip first.

The reason to keep the levels distinct is that they have different rates of change. Physical layouts change quarterly; logical schemas change with releases; conceptual models change when the business does. When the levels are conflated — when a table exists because of an index strategy, or a business rule exists only inside an ORM's save() path — you lose the ability to change one without auditing all three10.

III History, 1959–1979

From Navigating to Declaring

The first data models were programs. In Charles Bachman's Integrated Data Store, built at General Electric in the early 1960s, related records were threaded together by pointer chains and the programmer walked them, one hop at a time. Bachman's 1973 Turing Award lecture, The Programmer as Navigator, is a defence of exactly that mental model: the application developer as someone steering through an n-dimensional space of records, taking one route rather than another11. The CODASYL Data Base Task Group report of 1971 standardised the approach, giving the network model its SET constructs, currency indicators, and navigation verbs12. IBM's IMS, shipped in the late 1960s for the Apollo programme, took the stricter hierarchical route: parent–child segments in a tree, which made some queries trivial and others impossible.

Both models tied the meaning of data to its access path. A query was a traversal; changing the traversal meant changing the storage structure, and changing the storage structure meant rewriting programs. E. F. Codd's 1970 paper attacked precisely this coupling, opening with the observation that users of large data banks must be protected from having to know how the data is organised internally1. Relations, keys, and a closed algebra replaced pointer chasing with predicates. The applications no longer navigated; they described what they wanted.

The relational turn produced its theory quickly. Codd's follow-up work defined second and third normal form as remedies for insertion, update, and deletion anomalies2. Armstrong axiomatised functional dependency13. Heath had already identified the stronger condition that Codd and Boyce would formalise as Boyce–Codd normal form14,15. Fagin extended the programme to multivalued dependencies and fourth normal form16, Rissanen to join dependencies and the fifth17, and Fagin again to domain–key normal form, which folds all of it into a single statement about domains and keys18. Beeri and Bernstein settled the computational cost of the associated design problems19; Zaniolo gave 3NF a cleaner definition20; and Kent produced the plain-English guide that most working engineers actually learned it from21.

Meanwhile the modelling notation caught up with the algebra. Chen's entity–relationship model gave designers a way to talk about entities, relationships, and attributes before committing to tables8, and Codd himself extended the relational model with richer semantic apparatus — identity, generalisation, and integrity rules — in RM/T22. The implementations arrived in parallel: SEQUEL at IBM Research23, System R24, and INGRES at Berkeley25. By 1986 ANSI had a SQL standard, and the declarative model had won the commercial argument outright.

IV History, 1980–2010

The Long Argument With Objects

The relational victory created a new seam. Application code was increasingly written in object-oriented languages whose model of the world — identity, inheritance, encapsulation, graphs of references, behaviour attached to state — did not line up with relations, sets, and joins. The resulting friction acquired a name, object–relational impedance mismatch, and eventually a proper taxonomy: mismatches of structure, instance identity, encapsulation, and data type, each with different consequences and different workarounds26.

Two responses competed. One was to change the database: object database systems, standardised by the ODMG as a persistent object model with its own query language27. They found real niches — CAD, telecoms, some scientific work — and never displaced SQL in general business computing. The other was to change the mapping: object–relational mappers, whose patterns Fowler catalogued in detail — identity map, unit of work, lazy load, table-per-hierarchy versus table-per-class28. ORMs won on adoption and imported a lasting problem: they made it easy to define a model in application code and let the database schema fall out of it as an artefact, which quietly relocates every integrity decision into a layer that only runs when your process does.

Alongside this, XML made a bid to be the universal data model. XML Schema gave documents a formal type system with structural constraints, namespaces, and derivation29, and for a decade many inter-organisational data models were XSDs. The semantic web pushed in a third direction with RDF's subject–predicate–object triples30 and an explicit theory of shared conceptualisations31 — ideas that stayed influential in knowledge graphs and vocabularies long after the broader agenda stalled.

The decisive shift came from operations rather than theory. Google's Bigtable described a sparse, distributed, sorted map with no joins and no cross-row transactions, and explained why that was the right trade at web scale32. Amazon's Dynamo went further, choosing availability under partition over consistency for shopping-cart writes and accepting that reconciling divergent replicas was the application's job33. Stonebraker's “one size fits all” critique34 and his call for a complete rewrite of the OLTP stack35 provided the intellectual cover. Vogels's account of eventual consistency turned the trade-off into something a product engineer could reason about36.

What emerged was not a rejection of modelling but a different modelling default. Sadalage and Fowler named it precisely: these systems are aggregate-oriented, storing pre-joined clusters of data that are retrieved and written as a unit, with the aggregate boundary doing the work that transactions used to do37. That is a data modelling decision, not a storage detail — and it maps almost exactly onto a concept Eric Evans had published five years before Dynamo3.

V History, 2010–now

Convergence, and the Return of the Schema

The 2010s undid most of the era's sharper dichotomies. Google's Spanner demonstrated that a globally distributed database could offer external consistency — strict serializability — by making clock uncertainty explicit rather than pretending it away38. Amazon Aurora showed that a conventional relational engine could be rebuilt on a distributed storage layer without changing the model presented to applications39. Relational systems absorbed the document model: SQL:2023 added a native JSON type alongside an entirely new part for property graph queries40,41,42, and ISO published GQL as a standalone graph query language in 202443, drawing directly on a decade of property-graph research and on Cypher44,45,46. Document databases went the other way, adding declarative schema validation to systems that had been sold partly on not having schemas47.

Analytics converged too. The data lake began as a proposal to keep raw data in its native form and impose structure at read time48. In practice schema-on-read decayed into schema-on-nobody: a directory of files whose meaning lived in the heads of the three people who wrote the ingestion jobs. Columnar formats reintroduced structure at the file level — Parquet's nested encoding, descended from Dremel's repetition and definition levels49,50, and Arrow's in-memory layout51 — and table formats reintroduced it at the dataset level. Delta Lake brought ACID snapshots to object storage52; Iceberg made schema evolution safe by tracking columns with immutable numeric IDs rather than names or positions, so a rename cannot silently resurrect a dropped column53,54. The lakehouse argument — that warehousing and data-lake workloads should sit on one open, transactional substrate — is at bottom an argument about where the schema lives55.

The current frontier is organisational. Iceberg v3 adds default column values, so adding a column to a petabyte-scale table is a metadata write rather than a backfill, along with row lineage tracking and deletion vectors56. And the vocabulary of data contracts — explicit, versioned, testable agreements between producer and consumer, enforced in CI57,58 — is the field rediscovering, for pipelines, what ANSI/SPARC said in 1975 about mappings between levels.

The through-line across all three eras is the same. Every generation discovers that constraints removed for velocity reappear later as incidents, and re-adds them somewhere else: in the application, in a validation library, in a registry, in CI. The interesting question was never whether to have a schema. It is only ever where the schema is enforced and who is holding it when it breaks.

VI Construct

Entities, and the Problem of Sameness

An entity is a thing the system must be able to recognise as the same thing on two different occasions. That is the whole definition, and everything hard about entities follows from it. A customer who changes their name, email, address, and payment method is still that customer; the model must have something that persists through all of it. Codd's RM/T made the point structurally by giving entities surrogate identifiers distinct from any of their descriptive attributes22; Evans made the same point in application terms two decades later3.

The perennial argument is natural versus surrogate keys. A natural key is drawn from the domain: an ISBN, a VAT number, an email address. It is meaningful, verifiable by a human, and stable exactly as long as the issuing authority says it is — which is to say, not reliably. Countries merge, ISBNs get reissued, national identifier schemes get reformed, and the person who swore that a supplier's tax number never changes leaves the company. A surrogate key is generated by you and carries no meaning, which is its entire value: nothing in the world can invalidate it.

CREATE TABLE customer (
    customer_id  uuid PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),  -- surrogate
    email        citext NOT NULL UNIQUE,                      -- natural
    created_at   timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

-- The surrogate is stable: a customer who changes their email is
-- still the same customer, and every foreign key survives the change.
-- The natural key is meaningful: it is the one a human can verify.
-- Declaring both is not redundancy. It is two different promises.

The practical answer is usually both. Use a surrogate as the primary key so that foreign keys never need rewriting, and declare the natural key as a UNIQUE constraint so the database still forbids duplicates. Declaring only the surrogate gets you a table that cheerfully stores the same customer four times.

What kind of surrogate

The choice of identifier generator is a physical decision with logical consequences. Sequential integers are compact and index beautifully but leak volume and ordering information, and they require coordination — a problem the moment you shard or accept writes offline. Random UUIDv4 identifiers are coordination-free but scatter B-tree inserts across the whole keyspace, which costs write throughput and cache locality. RFC 9562, which obsoleted RFC 4122 in 2024, resolves the tension by standardising UUIDv6 and UUIDv7: time-ordered layouts explicitly designed for database locality, with UUIDv7 the recommended choice for new systems59.

Identity over time

Entities change, and a model must decide whether it remembers. Overwriting an address in place asserts that only the current value matters — fine for a session token, wrong for anything an auditor, a dispute, or a reproducible report will ever touch. Dimensional modelling names the choices explicitly: a type 1 slowly changing dimension overwrites; type 2 closes the current row and inserts a new one with its own validity period, so historical facts continue to join to the attributes that were true when they occurred; type 3 keeps a previous-value column for the one attribute anybody asks about60. The general version of type 2 is bitemporal modelling (§XVIII), and the decision to adopt it is much cheaper made at design time than retrofitted after somebody asks why last quarter's report changed.

Identifiers leak

An identifier that appears in a URL, a filename, or a support ticket is part of your public interface whether you intended it or not. Sequential integers disclose volume and creation order, which is a competitive-intelligence leak for some businesses and an enumeration vector for all of them, since /orders/1042 invites a request for /orders/1043. The mitigation is not secrecy — identifiers are not authorisation, and treating them as such is the underlying error — but the choice still belongs in the model: an internal sequential key for storage locality plus an external opaque key for exposure is a two-identifier design, and it should be a deliberate one rather than an accident of which column the router happened to bind.

Identity across systems

The subtler failure is not choosing a key badly but assuming your key is universal. In a system of services, the same real-world customer typically has an identifier in billing, another in the CRM, another in the support tool, and a hashed one in the analytics warehouse. Nothing enforces that these agree. Entity resolution — deciding that two records denote the same thing — becomes a first-class modelling problem, and one that Data Vault and anchor modelling address directly by separating immutable business keys (hubs, anchors) from their descriptive attributes and their relationships61,62. Whatever the technique, the modelling decision is to name explicitly which identifier is authoritative for which purpose, rather than discovering during an incident that two teams disagreed.

VII Construct

Value Objects, or Making Illegal States Unrepresentable

A value object is the complement of an entity: something defined entirely by its attributes, with no identity of its own and no meaningful notion of change. Two instances holding the same values are the same value. £24.00 is £24.00; there is no “which one”3.

Treating values as first-class is the cheapest correctness win available to a modeller, because a well-chosen value type makes a whole class of errors syntactically impossible. If every monetary amount in the system is a Money carrying its currency, then adding dollars to euros is a type error at compile time rather than a reconciliation ticket in Q328. If dates that must be ordered are a DateRange whose constructor rejects an end before its start, no downstream code needs to re-check. This is the design discipline Wlaschin summarises as making illegal states unrepresentable63, and the one King formulates for boundaries as parse, don't validate: convert untyped input into a type that carries the guarantee, once, at the edge — rather than re-validating the same raw string at every layer and hoping nobody forgets64.

Listing 3A value object is defined by what it refuses to bePython
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class Money:
    minor_units: int          # never float: IEEE 754 cannot hold 0.10
    currency: str             # ISO 4217 alphabetic code

    def __post_init__(self):
        if self.currency not in ISO_4217:
            raise ValueError(f"unknown currency {self.currency!r}")

    def __add__(self, other: "Money") -> "Money":
        if self.currency != other.currency:
            raise ValueError("cannot add across currencies")
        return Money(self.minor_units + other.minor_units, self.currency)

# Money(1000,"GBP") == Money(1000,"GBP")  ->  True. There is no
# "which thousand pence" question to ask; there is no identity here.

The representations that go wrong

  • Money as a float. IEEE 754 binary floating point cannot represent 0.10 exactly65. Store minor units as an integer, or a decimal type, and put the currency alongside it.
  • Timestamps without zones. A naive local timestamp is not a point in time; it is a point in time plus an unstated assumption. Store instants in UTC per RFC 333966, keep the originating zone as a separate attribute when the local wall-clock reading matters, and remember that ISO 8601 permits representations most parsers do not accept67.
  • Quantities without units. A column called weight is a unit conversion incident with a delay fuse. Encode the unit in the type or in the column name, never in a comment.
  • Enumerations as free text. A status column of type text will eventually contain "shipped", "SHIPPED", and "shipped ". Constrain it, in the database.

Where value objects land in storage is a separate decision from whether they exist in the model, and the two are often wrongly coupled. A Money in the domain may be two columns in a relational table, an embedded sub-document in MongoDB, a struct in Parquet, or a nested message on the wire — and it can be all four in the same system without any inconsistency, because the value has no identity that must be preserved across representations. This is precisely the property entities lack: an entity represented four ways creates four opportunities to disagree about which one is current.

Value objects also simplify the levels question from §II. Because they carry no identity, they can be embedded, copied, and cached without creating a consistency obligation. In a relational schema they usually collapse into columns of the owning table; in a document or wide-column store they nest naturally; on the wire they serialise as sub-records. In every case the trade is the same one the whole article turns on: you accept redundancy in exchange for the ability to enforce a rule at the point of construction rather than at the point of use.

VIII Construct

Relationships Carry the Constraints

Entities are nouns; relationships are where nearly all the enforceable rules live. Chen's original notation treated relationships as first-class objects with their own attributes precisely because they are not reducible to the entities they connect8. Three independent dimensions describe one:

  • Degree — how many entity types participate. Binary relationships dominate; ternary ones are usually a disguised entity.
  • Cardinality — the maximum: one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many.
  • Optionality — the minimum: is participation zero or one?

Optionality is the dimension teams under-specify and then pay for. Every optional side of a relationship becomes a nullable column, and every nullable column is a branch in every piece of code that reads it. Date has argued for decades that nulls introduce three-valued logic with genuinely surprising semantics into what should be a two-valued system68, and while few production teams eliminate nulls entirely, the discipline of asking “is this genuinely optional, or have I just not decided?” catches a startling number of modelling errors before they ship.

Many-to-many is always an entity in disguise

A many-to-many relationship cannot be represented by a foreign key in either participant. The resolution — an associative or junction entity — is mechanical, but the interesting part is what it reveals: the relationship almost always has attributes of its own that had nowhere to live.

When a relationship is really an entity

Chen's notation permits relationships of any degree, but ternary and higher relationships are almost always a signal rather than a solution. “A supplier supplies a part to a project” looks like a genuine three-way relationship until you ask what constraints hold: can a supplier supply a part to no project? Can the same supplier–part pair appear against two projects at different prices? The moment the answer involves an attribute — a price, a date, a contract reference — the relationship has become a thing in its own right, with a lifecycle, a key, and rules of its own. Making that promotion explicit (reification) is what lets the database enforce those rules; leaving it implicit pushes them into application code that only some callers execute.

A useful diagnostic: if you can imagine querying the relationship independently of both participants — “show me all supply agreements signed in March” — it is an entity. If you can imagine it having a status, it is definitely an entity.

Hierarchies, graphs, and time

Self-referencing relationships model hierarchies — org charts, category trees, bills of materials — and each encoding trades write cost against read cost: adjacency lists are cheap to write and expensive to traverse; nested sets and closure tables invert that; recursive CTEs make adjacency lists tolerable in standard SQL. When traversal is the dominant access pattern rather than an occasional one, the property-graph model stops being an exotic choice: edges become first-class, and the query language is built for variable-length paths44,45. That capability is now available inside SQL itself through SQL/PGQ41,46 and as a standalone ISO language43.

Finally, relationships have lifespans. “This employee reports to that manager” was true last year and may not be now. Modelling a relationship without a validity period silently asserts that it has always held and always will — an assertion that is false often enough to matter, and one that SQL:2011's application-time period tables were added to express69,70. We return to this in §XVIII.

IX Construct

Aggregates Draw the Transaction Boundary

An aggregate is a cluster of associated objects treated as a single unit for data changes, with one member designated the root; external references point only at the root, and the root is responsible for enforcing the cluster's invariants3. Stated that way it sounds like an object-modelling nicety. It is not: it is the single most consequential physical decision in a distributed system, because the aggregate boundary is the transaction boundary.

The working rules distilled by Vernon are short and unusually load-bearing71,72:

  • Protect true invariants inside a single aggregate — and only true invariants, not conveniences.
  • Design small aggregates. A large one is a lock held over unrelated data and a contention hotspot.
  • Reference other aggregates by identity only, never by object reference.
  • Update one aggregate per transaction; use eventual consistency between them.
Listing 4The constraint that makes an aggregate an aggregateSQL
-- OrderLine has no global identity: line_no is local to its order,
-- and the line cannot outlive the order it belongs to.
CREATE TABLE order_line (
    order_id          text NOT NULL REFERENCES orders (order_id)
                           ON DELETE CASCADE,
    line_no           int  NOT NULL CHECK (line_no > 0),
    sku               text NOT NULL REFERENCES product (sku),
    qty               int  NOT NULL CHECK (qty > 0),
    unit_price_cents  bigint NOT NULL CHECK (unit_price_cents >= 0),
    PRIMARY KEY (order_id, line_no)
);
-- Note what is absent: no FK from order_line to customer. Lines reach
-- the customer only by going through their root. That absence is the
-- aggregate boundary, written down.

That last rule is the one people argue with, and it is the one that pays. Helland made the general case a decade ahead of the microservice era: once your data is spread across independent scopes of serializability, you cannot have distributed transactions in any economically sane form, so applications must be written to reference other entities by identifier and reconcile asynchronously73. Every subsequent pattern in this space — sagas as long-lived transactions with compensating actions74, the transactional outbox that makes “update state and publish an event” atomic75,76 — exists to make cross-aggregate consistency achievable without a two-phase commit.

The recurring practical question is how big an aggregate should be, and the usual mistake is to draw it around a noun rather than around an invariant. A Customer aggregate that contains every order the customer has ever placed satisfies no invariant that a smaller boundary would not — there is no rule of the form “a customer's orders must collectively satisfy X” — and it guarantees that two unrelated order updates contend on the same root. Three questions settle most cases. Is there a rule that must hold across these objects at every instant, not merely eventually? Would a user accept seeing a stale version of the outer object while the inner one changes? Does the collection have a bound, or does it grow with usage? An unbounded collection inside an aggregate is nearly always the wrong boundary, because loading the root becomes proportional to the customer's lifetime.

Aggregates as a storage layout

In aggregate-oriented databases the boundary becomes the physical unit of storage and retrieval: the document, the item collection, the partition key37. DynamoDB single-table design is the purest expression — enumerate the access patterns first, then pre-join the entities that are read together into one item collection under a shared partition key, so the common read is a single query rather than a waterfall of network round trips77,78. Cassandra modelling methodologies formalise the same query-first inversion79. The consequence is that a change to your access patterns is a change to your physical model, which is exactly the coupling Codd set out to break in 1970 — accepted deliberately here, in exchange for predictable single-digit-millisecond reads.

Event sourcing

Event sourcing inverts what is stored: instead of persisting current state, persist the ordered sequence of state-changing events and derive state by replaying them80. The aggregate becomes a fold over its own history, the event stream is append-only, and audit is free rather than bolted on. Paired with CQRS — separating the write model from one or more read models built for querying81,82 — it gives you unlimited denormalised projections without corrupting the source of truth. The costs are real and structural: events are permanent, so a badly modelled event is permanent too; replay time grows without snapshots; and GDPR erasure against an immutable log is a genuine problem rather than a delete statement (§XVIII).

X Technique

Normalization Is a Theory of Redundancy

Normalization is widely taught as a checklist and widely dismissed as academic. Both readings miss what it is: a formal account of when redundancy in a schema permits states that contradict themselves. The mechanism is the functional dependency. X → Y asserts that any two rows agreeing on X must agree on Y. Armstrong's axioms — reflexivity, augmentation, transitivity — let you compute everything implied by a set of declared dependencies13, and the closure of an attribute set under those axioms tells you whether it is a key.

Anomalies are what happen when a relation stores facts about two different things. Codd's original examples are still the clearest: if supplier city lives in the same relation as supplier–part shipments, you cannot record a supplier with no shipments (insertion anomaly), deleting the last shipment loses the city (deletion anomaly), and a supplier relocation requires updating an unbounded number of rows consistently (update anomaly)2.

Worked concretely: put order_id, line_no, customer_id, and customer_email in one relation. The key is (order_id, line_no), but email depends only on customer_id, which is not a superkey. Three consequences follow immediately. A customer with no orders cannot be recorded at all, because there is no key to hang them on. Deleting an order's last line erases the fact that the customer has that email. And an email change must update every line of every order that customer ever placed, atomically, forever — a requirement no amount of care satisfies once two processes write concurrently. These are not hypothetical; they are the three anomaly classes, and they are exactly what the dependency violation predicts.

The rungs are worth stating precisely, because the informal versions mislead:

  • 1NF: attribute values are atomic with respect to the model — no repeating groups. What counts as atomic is a modelling judgement, not a fact about the data type, which is why a JSON column is not automatically a 1NF violation.
  • 2NF: no non-prime attribute depends on part of a composite candidate key.
  • 3NF: no non-prime attribute depends transitively on a candidate key — the popular gloss being that every non-key attribute depends on the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key21.
  • BCNF: every determinant is a candidate key, which also closes the loop for prime attributes14,15.
  • 4NF: no non-trivial multivalued dependency that is not implied by a superkey — the fix for independent repeating lists multiplying into a cross product16.
  • 5NF: no non-trivial join dependency not implied by candidate keys, so no decomposition-and-rejoin invents rows17.
  • DK/NF: every constraint is a logical consequence of domain and key constraints alone18.

Two practical facts get lost in the ritual. First, decomposition to BCNF is always lossless but is not always dependency-preserving; 3NF is always achievable with both properties, which is why real designs sometimes stop at 3NF19,83. Second, normalization operates on declared dependencies. It cannot discover a rule you failed to state. The hard work is not running the algorithm; it is knowing that customer_email → customer_id holds — and therefore that email is a second candidate key, not just a column.

§XXI runs this analysis mechanically on a deliberately under-normalised order relation and prints the candidate keys, the BCNF violations, and a lossless decomposition. The output contains a small surprise about which attribute the algorithm chooses to keep, which is itself instructive about equivalent keys.

XI Technique

Denormalization Is a Budget, Not a Betrayal

Denormalization is the deliberate reintroduction of redundancy to make a read path cheaper. The word “deliberate” carries all the weight. An un-normalised schema is one where nobody worked out the dependencies; a denormalised schema is one where somebody did, priced the join, and chose to pay for the copy instead.

The canonical form is dimensional modelling. Kimball's star schema puts additive measurements in a central fact table surrounded by wide, denormalised dimension tables, deliberately violating 3NF because analytical queries filter and group on dimension attributes and cannot afford to snowflake out to a dozen lookup tables60. Inmon's corporate information factory takes the opposite ordering — a normalised enterprise warehouse feeding dimensional marts — and the decades-long argument between the two camps is really an argument about where in the pipeline the denormalisation should happen84. Data Vault splits the difference again, with normalised hubs and links for auditability and derived marts for consumption61.

Outside analytics, the same trade recurs under different names:

  • Materialised views — the database maintains the copy and tells you how stale it is. Strongly preferred, when available, precisely because the redundancy remains declared.
  • Read models / projections — CQRS makes the copies explicit and asynchronous, with the event stream as the reconciliation mechanism81,82.
  • Item collections and adjacency lists — pre-joining entities under a shared partition key so the read is one query77,78.
  • Derived columns and counter caches — the smallest and most commonly wrong version, because comment_count is easy to add and easy to forget to update on the delete path.

Pricing the copy

A useful discipline is to write down, for every denormalisation, four answers before merging it: what is the source of truth; what is the maximum acceptable staleness; what process repairs divergence; and how would we detect that divergence had occurred. Teams reliably answer the first two and reliably skip the last two, which is why the failure mode of denormalisation is not slowness but silent, gradually accumulating disagreement between two representations of the same fact.

Note also the asymmetry with normalisation. Normalising is a schema change you can reason about statically: the dependencies either hold or they do not. Denormalising is a change to your operational obligations, because it converts a constraint the database was enforcing into a job someone must keep running. Helland's observation that immutable data sidesteps the whole problem is the cleanest escape available — copies of an immutable fact can never disagree, which is why event logs, content-addressed objects, and versioned snapshots denormalise so much more safely than mutable rows85,86.

XII Technique

Choosing: The Same Domain, Four Ways

The constructs so far are technology-neutral. The choice of store is not, and it is made badly when it is made first. The productive order is: characterise the workload, enumerate the invariants, then pick the store that enforces the most of them for the least money.

Six questions do most of the work.

  • What are the access patterns, and are they closed? A fixed, well-understood set of queries makes aggregate-oriented modelling viable. An open-ended analytical workload does not, because you cannot pre-join for questions nobody has asked yet77.
  • What is the unit of consistency? If several objects must agree at every instant, they belong in one aggregate and one store.
  • What is the read/write ratio, and where is the latency budget? Denormalisation moves work from read time to write time; that only helps if reads dominate.
  • How will the shape change? A domain with a stable core and a long, sparse tail of attributes wants a typed core plus a semi-structured column, not a hundred nullable columns and not EAV.
  • Who else reads this? Every additional consumer raises the value of enforced structure and lowers the value of flexibility.
  • What must be provable later? Audit, reproducibility, and regulatory reporting all imply immutability and time dimensions, which are expensive to retrofit.

XIII Guarantee

Four Kinds of Integrity, and Where to Put Them

Integrity is the property that the stored data satisfies the rules the model claims for it. The standard taxonomy splits it four ways, and the split is useful because each kind has a different enforcement mechanism and a different failure signature89,90.

  • Domain integrity — every value is drawn from its declared domain. Enforced by types, NOT NULL, CHECK, enumerations, and user-defined domains.
  • Entity integrity — every row has a complete, unique primary key. This is Codd's original rule that no component of a primary key may be null1,91, and it is the reason a nullable “unique” column is not a key.
  • Referential integrity — every foreign key value matches an existing primary key, or is entirely null (§XIII).
  • User-defined integrity — everything else the business requires: an account balance that may not go negative, a room that may not be double-booked, an order total that must equal the sum of its lines.

The exclusion constraint in that listing is worth dwelling on. It expresses “no two reservations for the same room may overlap in time” as a declarative rule the database enforces under concurrency, using a generalised index87. The equivalent application-level check — select overlapping rows, see none, insert — is a race condition that appears only under load and only in production.

The enforcement question

Every constraint can live in at least four places, and the choice is a real engineering trade rather than a matter of taste:

  • In the type system, at compile time. Cheapest to satisfy, strongest feedback, but it protects only code paths that go through your types.
  • In the application, at the aggregate root. Expressive enough for any rule, but enforced only while your process is the sole writer — which stops being true the first time someone runs a backfill script.
  • In the database, as a declared constraint. Enforced against every writer including the ones you did not write, under concurrency, forever. Costs write throughput, complicates migrations, and is genuinely awkward to express for rules that span many rows.
  • In a downstream check, as a data quality test or contract assertion in CI or a pipeline57,58. Detects rather than prevents; the only practical option for rules that span systems.

Constraints are not free, and pretending otherwise loses the argument with the engineer who has actually measured. A foreign key costs an index lookup on every insert and update of the referencing column, and it takes a lock on the parent row in some engines. A UNIQUE constraint is an index that must be maintained and that serialises concurrent inserts of the same value. Adding NOT NULL or a CHECK to a large existing table may require a full validation scan — which is why modern engines offer a two-step NOT VALID then VALIDATE CONSTRAINT path that takes a weaker lock87. None of these costs is a reason to skip the constraint. All of them are reasons to know the cost before the migration window rather than during it.

A reasonable default: put every rule you can express declaratively into the database, put the rules that need domain vocabulary into the aggregate root, and put the cross-system rules into continuous checks with alerting. The anti-pattern is putting a rule in exactly one place and assuming that place is always in the path — particularly when that place is an ORM.

XIV Guarantee

Referential Integrity, Including Where You Cannot Have It

Referential integrity is the promise that a reference resolves. If orders.customer_id is a foreign key to customer.customer_id, then every non-null value in that column names a customer that exists, and the database will reject any statement — from any client, in any language, at any hour — that would make it otherwise92,87.

The interesting design surface is not the constraint but what happens when the referenced row is deleted or its key changes. The referential actions differ sharply in blast radius.

A few field notes. CASCADE on delete is right for composition — order lines genuinely should not outlive their order — and wrong for association, where deleting a customer should not silently erase their invoices. SET NULL converts a referential violation into a nullability problem, which is only progress if the column was genuinely optional. RESTRICT and NO ACTION differ in when the check fires, which matters once you use DEFERRABLE INITIALLY DEFERRED to insert mutually referencing rows inside one transaction.

Soft deletes break it quietly

A deleted_at column is a widely used pattern and a widely underestimated one. The database's foreign keys do not know about it: after a soft delete the child rows still reference a parent that, semantically, no longer exists. Referential integrity is now enforced only by every query remembering to add AND deleted_at IS NULL — and the one report that forgets is the one shown to the regulator. If you soft delete, make the filter structural: a view, a row-level security policy, or a status column with the constraint expressed against it.

Across services, you cannot have it at all

When customer lives in one service's database and orders in another, no engine can enforce the reference. This is not a gap to be plugged; it is the deliberate consequence of the autonomy that made the split worthwhile, and it follows directly from Helland's argument about independent scopes of serializability73. What replaces the constraint is a set of explicit choices:

  • Tolerate dangling references and define the read behaviour — render a placeholder, degrade the feature, never crash.
  • Replicate the referenced data into the consuming service as an owned, read-only copy fed by events, accepting bounded staleness.
  • Reconcile continuously with a job that reports orphan rates as a monitored metric rather than discovering them during an incident.
  • Compensate rather than roll back: a saga with explicit compensating actions for each step that may need undoing74,75.

The failure mode worth naming is the fourth option unchosen: teams that drop the foreign key when they split the service and then never build any of the replacements, so the invariant simply stops being anybody's job.

Consistency Is Several Different Words

“Consistency” names at least three unrelated properties, and conflating them causes more confused architecture reviews than any other term in the field.

The C in ACID is the weakest and the least discussed: a transaction moves the database from one state satisfying its declared integrity constraints to another such state. It is really a statement about your constraints, not about the engine. The acronym itself comes from Härder and Reuter's 1983 survey, formalising a transaction concept Gray had laid out two years earlier93,94,95.

The C in CAP is linearizability: every read sees the most recent completed write, as though there were a single copy of the data. Herlihy and Wing gave the formal definition96; Gilbert and Lynch proved Brewer's conjecture that you cannot have it together with availability under network partition97,98. Brewer himself later pushed back on the binary reading — partitions are rare, the choice is per-operation, and the interesting engineering is in partition recovery99. Abadi's PACELC extension makes the more useful point: even when there is no partition, systems still trade latency against consistency, and that trade dominates the day-to-day100. Kleppmann's critique of the theorem's popular use is worth reading alongside all of them101.

Serializability is a different guarantee again: the result of concurrent transactions equals some serial order102. It says nothing about which order, or about real time. Strict serializability — what Spanner calls external consistency — is serializability plus linearizability's real-time ordering, and it is expensive precisely because it requires knowing something reliable about clocks38.

Ordering is a modelling assumption

Underneath every consistency guarantee is a claim about order, and in a distributed system there is no global clock to supply one. Lamport's 1978 result is the foundation: a partial order of events can be established from causality alone — the happens-before relation — without any physical clock, and logical clocks make that order observable103. The practical consequence for modellers is that any invariant phrased as “whichever happened last wins” is under-specified unless you say whose clock decided, and last-write-wins over wall-clock timestamps silently discards concurrent updates whenever two nodes disagree by more than the inter-write interval. The mechanics of establishing order safely — two-phase locking, timestamp ordering, multiversion concurrency control, and the recovery protocols that make them durable — are laid out in full by Bernstein, Hadzilacos, and Goodman104.

Isolation levels are named after implementations

The SQL standard's isolation levels are defined by which anomalies they permit, and Berenson and colleagues demonstrated in 1995 that the definitions are ambiguous and that real engines implement different guarantees under the same names105. Adya's generalised definitions fixed the formal problem106; the practical problem persists, which is why empirical verification — running real workloads against real clusters and checking histories for violations, as the Jepsen analyses do — remains the only way to know what a given version of a given database actually provides107.

Designing to avoid coordination

The most productive line of recent work asks which invariants need coordination at all. Bailis and colleagues showed that many common integrity constraints are invariant-confluent — safely mergeable without coordination — and that identifying them yields large throughput gains108,109. The CALM theorem gives the underlying result: a program has a coordination-free distributed implementation if and only if it is monotonic110. CRDTs are the constructive answer, data types whose merge function is commutative, associative, and idempotent, so replicas converge without consensus111 — now the foundation of collaborative editing and local-first architectures112. Note the direction of causation: it is the data model that decides whether coordination is required. Counters and sets merge; “the balance must never go negative” does not.

Schemas: The Model as a Checkable Artefact

A schema is the machine-readable form of the model — the part a validator, a compiler, or a query planner can act on. The recurring debate is when it applies.

Schema-on-write validates at ingest: nothing enters the store without conforming, so every reader can rely on the shape. Schema-on-read defers interpretation to query time, letting you land data whose structure you have not yet decided how to handle48. The framing is misleading, because there is no third option in which no schema exists. Schema-on-read means the schema lives in each consumer, unversioned and undocumented, and diverges. In practice most mature systems settle on a graded arrangement: permissive at the landing zone, strict at the curated layer, contractual at the published boundary.

JSON Schema constrains instances of a self-describing document format, with keyword vocabularies for validation, applicators, and annotation113,114. Schemas at the API and dataset boundary

The same discipline extends outward. OpenAPI describes HTTP interfaces including the shape of every request and response118; GraphQL goes further by making the schema the queryable type system itself, with the client selecting exactly the fields it needs119. Both are data models by another name, and both suffer identically when the API schema and the persistence schema are allowed to become the same object — at which point a storage refactor becomes a breaking API change.

At dataset scale, table formats carry the schema as metadata. Iceberg's use of immutable per-column IDs is the key design decision: because files reference columns by ID rather than name or position, renames, reorderings, and drops are metadata-only operations that cannot resurrect a deleted column's data by name collision53,54. Delta Lake achieves comparable guarantees through its transaction log52. A schema registry adds the missing organisational piece: a versioned, centrally checkable home for schemas with configurable compatibility rules enforced at publish time120.

Who owns the schema

The technical mechanisms above all presuppose an answer to an organisational question: whose release can break whom. A schema with no owner is a schema that changes when someone is in a hurry. The pattern that has proved durable is to make the producer accountable for a published shape and to make that accountability mechanical — the schema lives in version control next to the producer's code, changes go through review, compatibility is checked in CI against the registered previous version, and an incompatible change fails the build rather than the consumer120,58. That is what “data contract” means in practice: not a document, but a test that can block a deploy57.

The corollary is that a schema you cannot enforce at a boundary you control is documentation, not a contract. Internal-only schemas can be strict and can change weekly. Externally published ones inherit the FHIR problem from §XIX: once enough independent parties implement against a shape, the shape is effectively permanent, and every subsequent design decision is made in its shadow.

XVII Interface

Serialization: Where the Model Meets the Wire

Serialization converts an in-memory structure into bytes and back. It is usually treated as a library choice. It is a modelling decision, because the format determines what the receiver can know without asking, and therefore which constraints survive the trip.

The spectrum runs from self-describing to schema-dependent. JSON carries its field names in every record, so a reader with no prior knowledge can still parse it — at the cost of size and of a type system with six types and no integers distinct from floats114. Protobuf strips names, encoding a varint tag and wire type per field, so the reader needs the .proto but can skip fields it does not recognise5. Avro removes tags entirely: the fields appear in schema order with no framing at all, which makes it the most compact of the three and completely unreadable without the writer's schema4. Columnar formats reorganise further, storing values by column with repetition and definition levels to encode nesting — the technique Dremel introduced and Parquet standardised49,50 — and Arrow specifies the equivalent layout for memory and inter-process transfer51.

The details that cause incidents

  • Floating point. A JSON number is not a specified type. Send money as a float and you have shipped a rounding bug to every consumer that parses it as a double65. Send minor units as an integer, or a string.
  • Time. Serialise instants as RFC 3339 with an explicit offset, or as integer epoch values with the unit stated in the schema66. “2026-03-29 02:30” does not exist in Europe/London.
  • Canonical form. If bytes are hashed, signed, or compared for equality, the encoding must be deterministic. Map iteration order, optional whitespace, and default-value omission all break naive byte equality; Avro specifies a parsing canonical form for exactly this reason4.
  • Field order and unknown fields. A reader that rejects unknown fields cannot be forward compatible. A reader that preserves them can round-trip data it does not understand — usually what you want in a proxy.

Framing: how the reader knows which schema

A schema-dependent encoding is only usable if the reader can determine which schema produced the bytes, and that determination has to travel somehow. Avro's object container files embed the writer's schema in the file header, which is ideal for batch and useless per-message4. Streaming systems instead prepend a small envelope — typically a magic byte and a schema identifier resolved against a registry — so each record costs a few bytes rather than a kilobyte of JSON schema text120. Protobuf sidesteps the question by making the message type part of the API contract rather than the payload, which works until you need to put heterogeneous messages on one topic and must reintroduce a type discriminator by hand.

The choice matters because it decides what happens during an incident. If the schema identifier is in the payload, a malformed message can be quarantined and inspected later. If it is implicit in the topic or the endpoint, a producer that starts writing the wrong type produces bytes that decode successfully into the wrong values — the most expensive failure mode available, because nothing raises an error and the corruption is only discovered downstream.

Deserialization is an attack surface

Formats that reconstruct arbitrary objects from untrusted bytes — Java native serialization, Python pickle, PHP unserialize, unrestricted YAML tags — hand the attacker a way to instantiate types and invoke methods during parsing. The research that made this mainstream showed how ordinary library gadget chains turn a deserialization call into remote code execution121; it is catalogued as CWE-502122 and sits inside the OWASP Top 10 as part of software and data integrity failures123. The modelling implication is direct: a wire format should be a description of data, not a program. Prefer formats that can only produce values of a declared schema, and never deserialize untrusted input into types chosen by that input.

XVIII Change

Versioning: Making the Model Change Without Stopping

Every model is wrong eventually, and the interesting property of a model is not its correctness today but the cost of changing it tomorrow. Versioning is the set of techniques that keep that cost bounded.

The core vocabulary is compatibility, and it is defined relative to who is older, reader or writer120,124:

  • Backward compatible — a reader on the new schema can read data written with the old one. Achieved by deleting fields and by adding fields that have defaults.
  • Forward compatible — a reader on the old schema can read data written with the new one. Achieved by adding fields, and requires that old readers ignore what they do not recognise.
  • Full compatibility — both. In practice this means: only ever add optional fields with defaults, never narrow a type, never reuse an identifier.

The mechanisms differ by format but the logic is identical. Avro resolves a reader's schema against a writer's field by field: a field present in the writer but absent in the reader is skipped; a field present in the reader but absent in the writer takes the reader's declared default, and is an error if there is none4. Protobuf achieves the same effect with permanent field numbers and the reserved keyword to prevent a retired number being reused for an incompatible type5. Iceberg does it for tables by assigning immutable column IDs, and since v3 by supporting default values so that adding a column to an existing table is a metadata operation instead of a backfill54,56.

Expand and contract

For databases, the technique that makes migrations safe is parallel change: introduce the new shape alongside the old, migrate the readers and writers, and only then remove the old shape125,88,126. The reason it works is that at no point is any deployed version of the code inconsistent with the schema, which means every step is independently rollback-safe.

UPDATE orders SET total_minor = total_cents
 WHERE total_minor IS NULL AND order_id > $1 ORDER BY order_id LIMIT 5000;

-- 4 VERIFY: run continuously until it returns zero for long enough
SELECT count(*) FROM orders
 WHERE total_minor IS DISTINCT FROM total_cents;

-- 5 SWITCH READS: new column becomes the source of truth
-- 6 CONTRACT: only now, and only after the old readers are gone
ALTER TABLE orders ALTER COLUMN total_minor SET NOT NULL;
ALTER TABLE orders DROP COLUMN total_cents;

At scale, even the additive steps need care: on a large table, a naive ALTER can hold a lock long enough to cause an outage. Online schema change tools solve this by building a shadow table, copying rows in chunks, capturing concurrent changes, and cutting over with an atomic rename — via triggers in Percona's pt-online-schema-change127 or by tailing the binary log in GitHub's gh-ost, which avoids triggers and can be throttled or paused mid-migration128.

Versioning the interface, not just the store

API versioning faces the same problem with different constraints, because you do not control the clients. The options — URI versioning, media-type negotiation, or strictly additive evolution with no version at all — trade explicitness against proliferation, and the hypermedia-oriented view is that strong typing of the interface should reduce the need to version it in the first place129. GraphQL's answer is deprecation rather than versioning: add fields, mark old ones deprecated, and use per-field usage telemetry to know when removal is safe119. Whatever the mechanism, the operational requirement is identical: you cannot retire a field you cannot prove is unused.

XIX Change

The Data Lifecycle, Including the End of It

Most modelling discussion stops at the point data is stored. Regulation, storage cost, and incident response all live downstream of that point, and the model determines whether the downstream stages are cheap or impossible.

The stages are conventional; the modelling obligations are not:

  • Create and capture. Validate at the boundary, once, into types that carry the guarantee64. Record provenance now — source system, ingest time, schema version — because it cannot be reconstructed later.
  • Store. Choose the grain deliberately. Aggregating at ingest is irreversible; storing raw and aggregating on read is not.
  • Use and share. Every export is a new copy with its own lifecycle. Minimisation and masking belong at the point of sharing, expressed in the model rather than in a spreadsheet of manual steps.
  • Archive. Cold storage is cheap; cold storage you cannot query or interpret is a liability. Archive the schema with the data.
  • Destroy. The stage the model is least often designed for.

Deletion is a modelling requirement

Under the GDPR, storage limitation (Article 5(1)(e)) is a standing obligation to keep personal data in identifiable form no longer than necessary, independent of any request; the right to erasure (Article 17) is an additional on-demand right130. Together they mean that “how do we delete this?” is a design-time question with a schema-shaped answer. Research on the interaction between the regulation and database internals has documented the conflict directly: modern systems are built around append-only structures, replication, caching, and derived copies, all of which make targeted deletion expensive131,132.

The specific tension is with immutability. An append-only log is the best audit trail available and the worst substrate for erasure; event sourcing makes this acute, because the events are the source of truth. The common resolutions are all modelling decisions: keep personal data out of events and reference it by identifier; or encrypt per-subject and delete the key, so the ciphertext remains while the plaintext becomes unrecoverable. Crypto-shredding of this kind aligns with the cryptographic-erase technique described in NIST's media sanitization guidance133 — though it is a design pattern supervisory authorities assess case by case, not a blanket exemption, and it still requires that key destruction be documented and irreversible.

Expiry as a first-class attribute

The mechanism most likely to actually delete data is the one that requires no human decision. Key–value and document stores expose per-item TTLs; table formats and warehouses support partition expiry; object stores implement lifecycle rules that transition and then delete by prefix and age. All of them work at the granularity of the physical layout, which is the point that connects this section back to §II: if personal data is spread evenly across every partition, expiry is a scan; if the model partitions by a time dimension or by subject, expiry is a metadata operation. Retention is therefore a partitioning decision made years before anyone reads the retention policy.

The same logic applies to tiering. Moving cold data to cheaper storage is trivial when the access pattern is time-bounded and the layout matches it, and close to impossible when a single hot table interleaves ten years of history with this morning's writes. Neither of these is a storage problem in origin. Both are modelling decisions with a storage invoice attached.

Two clocks

Retention interacts with a subtler issue: most systems record only one time dimension and then discover they needed two. Valid time is when a fact was true in the world; transaction time is when the system came to believe it. Keeping both is bitemporal modelling, and it is what makes “what did we believe on 3 March?” answerable after a correction70,134,135. SQL:2011 standardised application-time and system-versioned period tables for exactly this69.

Five Domains, Five Different Answers

The abstractions above are load-bearing in domains that never talk to each other, and the contrasts are more instructive than any single example.

Healthcare: modelling for a hundred implementers

HL7 FHIR structures clinical and administrative data as roughly 150 discrete resource types — Patient, Observation, Condition — exchanged over REST136. Two of its choices repay study. First, extensibility is designed in: every resource admits typed extensions, because a standard for a hundred jurisdictions cannot enumerate every field anyone will need. Second, versioning is treated as a first-class governance problem. R4 brought core resources to normative status in 2019 with a backward-compatibility guarantee; R5 followed in 2023 with breaking changes and limited adoption; R6 has been in ballot through 2026 with the explicit aim of moving the bulk of the core to normative and freezing it137. That US federal interoperability rules are still written against R4 — leading many implementers to plan a direct R4-to-R6 migration and skip R5 entirely — is a clean illustration of what §XVII costs when the consumers are an entire industry.

Finance: the model is the message

ISO 20022 is unusual in specifying a metamodel and a business-concept dictionary from which the actual message schemas are generated, rather than standardising messages directly138. The conceptual level is the deliverable; the XML or JSON syntax is an output. FIX, by contrast, evolved from the trading floor as a tag–value protocol optimised for latency, where the model is expressed as a numbered field dictionary — the same design instinct as Protobuf's field numbers, arrived at independently and earlier139.

Geospatial: a type system for shapes

The OGC Simple Features model defines a geometry type hierarchy — point, linestring, polygon, and their collections — with well-known text and binary representations and a set of topological predicates such as contains, intersects, and touches140. It is a textbook case of value-object modelling at standards scale: geometries have no identity, they are compared by value, and the operations on them are total functions. It is also a case study in the physical level mattering, since none of those predicates is usable at scale without an R-tree or similar spatial index.

Version control: content addressing as identity

Git's object model has four types — blob, tree, commit, tag — where the identifier of every object is the hash of its content141. This collapses identity and value into one thing, with striking consequences: identical content is automatically deduplicated, integrity verification is inherent rather than added, and history is immutable because changing any object changes its name and every name that references it. It is the purest production example of Helland's argument that immutability changes everything85.

Filesystems: separating the name from the thing

The UNIX inode, described in 1974, separates a file's metadata and data blocks from the directory entries that name it142. Directory entries are references to inodes, multiple names can reference one inode, and the file is removed when the reference count reaches zero. That is reference counting as referential integrity, entity/value separation, and identity independent of naming — all shipped a year before ANSI/SPARC published its report, in an artefact most engineers use daily without recognising it as a data model.

XXI Evidence

Anti-Patterns, and What They Are Trying to Tell You

Most data modelling anti-patterns are correct solutions to the wrong problem. Naming the problem they are solving is more useful than banning the shape.

Entity–attribute–value

EAV stores each fact as a row of (entity, attribute, value), which lets you add attributes without altering the schema. It also discards typing, referential integrity, and any hope of a query planner producing a sane plan, since reconstructing one logical row requires one self-join per attribute. The biomedical informatics literature, where EAV is genuinely necessary because the attribute space is open and sparse, is also where its costs are best documented and its use most carefully bounded143,144. The rule that emerges there is sound generally: EAV for the genuinely open-ended sparse tail, conventional columns for the stable core, never EAV for the whole model. Where the platform offers a typed semi-structured column — a JSON or variant type with indexing40 — that is almost always the better answer.

The polymorphic foreign key

A commentable_type/commentable_id pair cannot be a foreign key, because the target table is data. The database is therefore prevented from enforcing the reference at all — and this is the tell that the model is missing a supertype. Either give the participants a shared parent table with a real key, or accept a nullable FK per target type with a CHECK asserting exactly one is non-null. Both preserve referential integrity; the polymorphic pair does not.

Everything nullable

A table where every column permits null encodes no requirements at all. It usually arises from a migration that added columns without backfilling and never tightened them afterwards — step 6 of the expand–contract cycle left undone. Each unnecessary null propagates into three-valued logic and a branch in every consumer68.

The stringly typed schema

Storing dates, numbers, booleans, and enumerations as text moves every validation into every reader and guarantees they will disagree. The variant that looks harmless and is not: storing an enumeration as free text because “we might add values later.” Adding a value to a constrained domain is a one-line migration; removing a bad value that got written for two years is a data cleanup project.

The JSON escape hatch

A metadata jsonb column is an excellent pressure release and a poor permanent home. The failure is predictable: within a year the column has ten well-known keys that the application depends on, none of which is typed, indexed, documented, or validated. The healthy pattern is to treat it as a staging area with an explicit rule — when a key is read by more than one consumer, or appears in a WHERE clause, it is promoted to a real column. Schema validation on the document itself is the middle ground47,113.

The god table

One table with ninety columns serving four unrelated concepts, distinguished by a type column and a convention about which columns are meaningful for which type. Every constraint becomes conditional and therefore undeclarable; every index serves one type and bloats for the others; every reader must know the convention. It is usually the fossil record of four features shipped by four people who each found an existing table more convenient than a new one. The tell is a CHECK constraint containing the word CASE, or the absence of the constraint that should contain it.

The surrogate key on a pure junction table

Adding an auto-increment id to a two-column junction table and making it the primary key, while leaving the natural composite unconstrained, is a small change that removes the entire point of the table: the composite key was the constraint forbidding duplicate pairs. If a surrogate is wanted for ORM convenience, keep it — and keep the UNIQUE (a_id, b_id) alongside it. The general form of this error is worth internalising, because it recurs everywhere: a key added for convenience does not replace a key that was carrying a rule.

Premature denormalisation

Adding a counter cache or a copied display name before there is a measured read problem buys nothing and costs a permanent consistency obligation. The inverse anti-pattern is real too: refusing to denormalise when the join is demonstrably the bottleneck, out of loyalty to a normal form. Both are the same error — deciding without measuring — and the honest version of both answers is the four-question checklist from §XI.

XXII Practice

Running the Model Against Itself

The three ideas this article keeps returning to — that a model is a set of refusals, that the refusals must be enforced somewhere, and that they must survive change — are all mechanically checkable. The program below does all three in about 300 lines of standard-library Python, and its output is reproduced verbatim underneath.

Pass 1 takes an order relation with ten attributes and five declared functional dependencies, computes the attribute closure under Armstrong's axioms13, finds the candidate keys, reports every BCNF violation, and produces a lossless decomposition14,15. Pass 2 loads rows containing deliberate defects and checks domain, entity, and referential integrity plus the one-aggregate-per-transaction rule3,71. Pass 3 implements Avro-style reader/writer resolution over three schema versions and derives the backward, forward, and full compatibility verdicts4,120.

                -> BCNF violations -> lossless-join decomposition
  2. ENFORCE    domain / entity / referential integrity over concrete rows,
                plus the aggregate rule "one transaction, one aggregate root"
  3. EVOLVE     reader/writer schema resolution -> BACKWARD / FORWARD / FULL

from itertools import combinations
from typing import Dict, FrozenSet, List, Optional, Sequence, Set, Tuple

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# PASS 1 -- NORMALIZE
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

FD = Tuple[FrozenSet[str], FrozenSet[str]]


def fd(lhs: str, rhs: str) -> FD:
    """'a,b -> c,d' written as fd('a b', 'c d')."""
    return (frozenset(lhs.split()), frozenset(rhs.split()))


def closure(attrs: Set[str], fds: Sequence[FD]) -> FrozenSet[str]:
    """Armstrong closure: everything functionally determined by `attrs`."""
    out = set(attrs)
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        for lhs, rhs in fds:
            if lhs <= out and not rhs <= out:
                out |= rhs
                changed = True
    return frozenset(out)


def candidate_keys(rel: Set[str], fds: Sequence[FD]) -> List[FrozenSet[str]]:
    """All minimal attribute sets whose closure is the whole relation."""
    keys: List[FrozenSet[str]] = []
    for size in range(1, len(rel) + 1):
        if keys:  # minimality: never look past the first size that succeeds
            break
        for combo in combinations(sorted(rel), size):
            cand = frozenset(combo)
            if closure(set(cand), fds) == frozenset(rel):
                keys.append(cand)
    return keys


def superkey(attrs: FrozenSet[str], rel: Set[str], fds: Sequence[FD]) -> bool:
    return closure(set(attrs), fds) == frozenset(rel)


def bcnf_violations(rel: Set[str], fds: Sequence[FD]) -> List[FD]:
    """X -> Y is a violation when X is not a superkey and Y is not inside X."""
    bad = []
    for lhs, rhs in fds:
        if rhs <= lhs:
            continue
        if not superkey(lhs, rel, fds):
            bad.append((lhs, rhs))
    return bad


def decompose_bcnf(rel: Set[str], fds: Sequence[FD]) -> List[FrozenSet[str]]:
    """Classic BCNF decomposition; lossless by construction (X is a key of R1)."""
    bad = bcnf_violations(rel, fds)
    if not bad:
        return [frozenset(rel)]
    lhs, _ = bad[0]
    determined = closure(set(lhs), fds) & frozenset(rel)
    r1 = frozenset(determined)
    r2 = frozenset(lhs) | (frozenset(rel) - determined)
    return decompose_bcnf(set(r1), project(r1, fds)) + decompose_bcnf(
        set(r2), project(r2, fds)
    )


def project(sub: FrozenSet[str], fds: Sequence[FD]) -> List[FD]:
    """Project the FD set onto a sub-relation (closure of every subset)."""
    out: List[FD] = []
    for size in range(1, len(sub)):
        for combo in combinations(sorted(sub), size):
            x = frozenset(combo)
            rhs = (closure(set(x), fds) & sub) - x
            if rhs:
                out.append((x, rhs))
    return out

    return "{" + ", ".join(sorted(s)) + "}"

def in_domain(domain: str, value) -> bool:
    if value is None:
        return False                       # NOT NULL is the default here
    if domain == "id":
        return isinstance(value, str) and value != ""
    if domain == "text":
        return isinstance(value, str)
    if domain == "email":
        return isinstance(value, str) and "@" in value and value == value.lower()
    if domain == "posint":
        return isinstance(value, int) and value > 0
    if domain == "nonneg_int":
        return isinstance(value, int) and value >= 0
    if domain == "iso4217":
        return value in ISO4217
    if domain == "iso3166":
        return value in ISO3166
    return False

def can_read(reader: List[Field], writer: List[Field]) -> Tuple[bool, List[str]]:
    """Can a consumer holding `reader` decode a record written with `writer`?"""
    wmap = {n: (t, d) for n, t, d in writer}
    problems: List[str] = []
    for name, rtype, rdefault in reader:
        if name not in wmap:
            if rdefault is None:
                problems.append(f"field '{name}' missing from writer and has no default")
            continue
        wtype = wmap[name][0]
        if rtype not in PROMOTES.get(wtype, set()):
            problems.append(f"field '{name}': {wtype} does not promote to {rtype}")
    return (not problems, problems)

Reading, roughly in the order it repays

  • Start with the argument. Codd's 1970 paper is eleven pages and still the clearest statement of why any of this matters1. Bachman's Turing lecture, read against it, shows what was actually being given up11.
  • For the systems view. Kleppmann's Designing Data-Intensive Applications is the single best synthesis of encoding, replication, and consistency for working engineers124; Gray and Reuter remains the reference for transactions95.
  • For the modelling craft. Simsion and Witt for the discipline itself145; Halpin and Morgan for fact-based modelling9; Hay, Silverston, and Fowler's Analysis Patterns for reusable structures you should not be reinventing146,147,148.
  • For the theory. Date's Database Design and Relational Theory is the most readable treatment of normalisation done properly10; Garcia-Molina, Ullman, and Widom for the algorithms behind it83.
  • For distributed models. Helland's two CIDR papers are short and reframe the problem permanently73,85; Hellerstein and Alvaro on CALM tells you which invariants need coordination at all110; Kingsbury's Jepsen reports tell you what your database actually does107.
  • For change. Ambler and Sadalage on database refactoring88, and Fowler's parallel-change note, which is two pages and will save you a weekend125.
  • For domain models. Evans for the vocabulary3, Vernon for the implementation detail72, Wlaschin for the type-driven version of the same instinct63.

One habit is worth more than any of them. When you next add a column, write down the sentence it makes false. If you cannot — if the column forbids nothing — you have added a field, not a model.
