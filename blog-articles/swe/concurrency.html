Concurrency · a field guide to the schedules your program permits

Between Any Two Instructions

A concurrent program is not one program. It is every execution its text permits — and almost all of the engineering is the work of permitting fewer.

Every mechanism in this article, from a mutex to an actor mailbox to mfence, answers the same question: what is allowed to happen between these two steps? The answers differ only in what they forbid, what they still permit, and what they charge.

01The gap between two instructions

Read the middle panel of Figure 0 again. Nothing in either thread's source code is wrong. Each thread does exactly what a single-threaded programmer would write: read the counter, add one, write it back. The bug is not in the text. The bug is in the space between the text — in the fact that after thread A reads and before thread A writes, something else was allowed to happen.

That gap is the subject of this article. It is the only subject. A concurrent program is not a program in the usual sense; it is a set of programs, one for every order in which its steps might actually occur, and the machine picks a member of that set for you each time you run it, without telling you which. Correctness means every member of the set is acceptable. Debugging means finding the member that was not.

Once you hold that framing, the field organises itself. A mutex is not “protection”; it is a device that deletes from the set every execution in which two threads are inside the same region at once. An atomic increment deletes every execution that lands between the read and the write. A memory fence deletes executions in which a reader sees a flag without the data it announces. An actor mailbox deletes every execution involving concurrent access to one actor's state, by making that state unreachable from anywhere else. Immutability deletes every write-write conflict by deleting the write. Each of these mechanisms buys a smaller set of permitted executions, and each charges for it — in latency, in throughput, in expressiveness, or in the sheer human cost of reasoning about what is left.

This is why concurrency is hard in a specific and unusual way. Most software defects are failures of construction: the code does not say what you meant. A concurrency defect is a failure of permission: the code says what you meant, and also permits something you never considered. Testing samples the permitted set; it does not enumerate it. A test that passes ten thousand times has observed ten thousand draws from a space that may contain quadrillions of members, drawn from a distribution your hardware, your scheduler, and your current cache state conspire to make wildly non-uniform.1 The one draw that matters is the one production makes at 3 a.m. under a load profile no test reproduced.

How to read this article Seventeen numbered permission plates appear through the text. Each names one mechanism and records four things: what it forbids, what it still permits, what it costs, and the tell — the observable symptom that tells you either that you need it or that it is failing. The plates are collected into a single ledger at the end. Every number quoted about schedules, states and outcomes comes from interleaving_lab.py, a dependency-free program printed in full near the end, which enumerates the complete execution space of small concurrent programs under two memory models and was executed to produce this page.

02Concurrency is not parallelism, and the difference is not pedantry

The two words are used interchangeably in casual speech and mean different things in engineering. Concurrency is a property of a program: its work is decomposed into tasks whose execution may overlap in time. Parallelism is a property of an execution: two or more operations physically happen at the same instant, on different hardware. Rob Pike's formulation, from a talk that did more than any paper to fix the distinction in working programmers' heads, is that concurrency is about dealing with many things at once and parallelism is about doing many things at once.2

The distinction matters because the two properties create different problems and are solved by different tools. Concurrency without parallelism still has races — a single-threaded event loop that suspends at every await can observe state changed by another task between the suspension and the resumption — but it has no data races on the loop's own heap, because two callbacks never execute simultaneously. Parallelism without concurrency, as in a vectorised inner loop or a GPU warp, has enormous throughput questions and almost no coordination questions, because there is one instruction stream. Only the intersection — concurrent tasks running in parallel on shared memory — produces the full catalogue of difficulty this article is about.

Historically the two arrived from opposite directions. Concurrency came from operating systems: the Atlas supervisor at Manchester was already multiplexing a single processor across programs and interrupt-driven devices by 1961, because a processor that waits for a drum transfer is a processor being wasted.3 Parallelism came from scientific computing, where the motivation was never latency-hiding but raw arithmetic throughput, and where Amdahl was already arguing in 1967 about the limits of the whole approach.4 The two traditions met in about 2005, when clock speeds stopped rising and vendors began selling cores instead of megahertz. Herb Sutter's essay that year gave the moment its name: the free lunch was over.5,6 Every programmer who had been getting faster software by waiting eighteen months now had to learn a discipline that operating system authors had been practising, carefully, for forty years.

03Sixty-five years of trying to close the gap

The primitives were mostly invented before 1980. What has changed since is not the inventory of mechanisms but the layer at which programmers meet them.

Edsger Dijkstra's 1965 work is the origin point for most of what follows. In Cooperating Sequential Processes he introduced the vocabulary of the critical section, described the mutual exclusion problem precisely, and proposed the semaphore with its P and V operations as a general solution.7 The same year he published a compact solution to mutual exclusion for n processes using only reads and writes.8 Three years later the THE multiprogramming system demonstrated that a whole operating system could be structured as a hierarchy of cooperating sequential processes with semaphores between the layers — a design argument as much as an implementation.9 In 1971 he introduced the dining philosophers as a teaching problem for deadlock and resource ordering.10

Semaphores are powerful and unstructured; a missing V hangs the system and a misplaced P corrupts it, and neither is visible in the type of anything. Per Brinch Hansen and C. A. R. Hoare independently proposed the monitor: a module whose data is private and whose procedures are implicitly mutually exclusive, with condition variables for waiting inside.11,12 Concurrent Pascal put monitors into a real language,13 and Brinch Hansen's later history of the period is a candid account of how much of it was arrived at by argument rather than proof.14 Every synchronized method in Java and every Mutex<T> in Rust is a descendant.

Then the field split. Hoare's Communicating Sequential Processes (1978) proposed that processes should not share memory at all but communicate by synchronous message passing over named channels.15 Carl Hewitt's actor model (1973) had already proposed something adjacent but importantly different: asynchronous messages to addressed entities with private state and unbounded mailboxes.16 Gul Agha's 1986 monograph gave actors their rigorous treatment.17 Go descends from CSP; Erlang, Akka, Orleans and Swift descend from actors. The distinction between a synchronous rendezvous and an asynchronous mailbox is not cosmetic, and it is one of the few genuine architectural forks in the subject.

The third strand is the theory of what an execution even is. Leslie Lamport's 1978 paper defined the happens-before relation — a partial order over events, not a total one — and with it the modern habit of reasoning about concurrent systems as partial orders.18 His 1979 note defined sequential consistency: a multiprocessor is sequentially consistent if the result of any execution is the same as if the operations of all processors were executed in some sequential order, and the operations of each processor appear in that order.19 That definition is the yardstick against which every real machine is measured, and every real machine fails it. Maurice Herlihy and Jeannette Wing then supplied linearizability, the correctness condition that makes concurrent objects composable by requiring each operation to appear to take effect at a single instant between its invocation and its response.20 Herlihy's 1991 paper on wait-free synchronisation established the consensus hierarchy, proving that some primitives are strictly more powerful than others — the reason every modern instruction set ships compare-and-swap or load-linked/store-conditional rather than merely test-and-set.21

04The size of the permitted set

How many executions does a small concurrent program permit? For k threads of n steps each, with no synchronisation and no reordering, the number of distinct interleavings is the multinomial coefficient (kn)! / (n!)k. Two threads of three steps: 20. Three threads of five steps: 756,756. Four threads of ten steps — still a trivial program, forty machine instructions in total — permits 4,705,360,871,073,570,227,520 distinct executions.

This is the central practical fact of the discipline, and it has a corollary that is easy to state and hard to accept: you cannot test your way to concurrency correctness. You can test your way to a great deal of confidence about the schedules your machine happens to like, which is not the same thing. Real schedulers are not adversarial but they are not uniform either; they revisit the same few interleavings billions of times and never visit others.22 This is why concurrency bugs have a distinctive epidemiology: they survive years of production, then appear in a cluster when a machine gets more cores, when a dependency gets faster, or when a garbage collector changes its pause pattern.

The empirical literature bears this out. Lu and colleagues examined 105 real concurrency bugs from MySQL, Apache, Mozilla and OpenOffice and found that around 97% involved fewer than four threads and the great majority involved only two variables or fewer — that is, the bugs are simple, and simplicity did not prevent them.23 The distributed-systems equivalent, TaxDC, catalogued 104 concurrency bugs from Cassandra, HBase, Hadoop and ZooKeeper and found the same pattern: most were triggered by a specific ordering of a handful of messages, and most had visible symptoms only far from the cause.24

Two responses to the size of the permitted set are respectable. The first is to shrink it deliberately, using the mechanisms this article catalogues. The second is to enumerate it — feasible only for models, not for production code, but extraordinarily clarifying when it is feasible. Both appear throughout.

The natural intuition, that a race is a rare event you might get away with, is exactly backwards: under exhaustive enumeration the correct outcome is the rare one. What makes races look rare in practice is not the schedule space but the scheduler's habits.

05Processes: isolation as the bluntest control

The strongest thing you can do about a shared variable is not to share it. A process is the operating system's unit of resource ownership: its own virtual address space, its own file descriptor table, its own accounting. Two processes cannot race on a variable because neither can name the other's memory.25,26

Isolation is expensive and worth it more often than fashion suggests. Chrome's site-per-process model, PostgreSQL's process-per-connection backend, nginx's worker processes, systemd's service supervision and every container runtime all buy the same thing: a fault in one unit cannot corrupt another's memory, and the kernel can reclaim everything a unit held by killing it. The cost is that any genuine sharing must be arranged explicitly — a pipe, a socket, a memory-mapped segment, a database row — and every such arrangement reintroduces exactly the concurrency problems the isolation removed, but now at a boundary you can see.

06Threads: sharing by default

A thread is a schedulable execution context — a stack, a register set, a program counter — inside a process's address space. Everything the process owns except the stack is shared: the heap, globals, statics, open files, and the memory reachable from any of them. That default is the source of both the model's convenience and its danger. Passing a pointer between threads is free, which is why threads won; passing a pointer between threads is also the entire bug class.

The canonical practitioner introduction remains Andrew Birrell's 1989 report, which is still the clearest short statement of what threads are and how to use them without ruining your life.29 The canonical critique is Edward Lee's The Problem with Threads, whose argument is precisely the one this article is built on: threads discard the most useful property of sequential computation, determinism, and hand back a model in which the programmer must prune nondeterminism explicitly, mechanism by mechanism.30 John Ousterhout's earlier talk made the pragmatic version of the case, and von Behren, Condit and Brewer replied with the equally pointed counter-argument that the event-driven alternative has its own pathologies and that most of the objection to threads is really an objection to bad thread implementations.31,32 That debate has never been settled and probably cannot be; both sides are describing real costs.

Cost and the shape of a thread

An OS thread reserves a stack — typically 1 MB of virtual address space on Linux and 1 MB on Windows by default, of which only the touched pages are resident — and its creation and context switch go through the kernel. That is affordable at hundreds of threads and painful at hundreds of thousands, which is why the C10K problem was a problem at all.33 The industry has answered it three times, in three different decades, with the same three shapes: thread per request, event loop, and user-mode threads multiplexed onto a smaller pool of kernel threads.

The third shape has now become the default answer. Erlang's processes (1986), Go's goroutines (2009) and Java's virtual threads, finalised in JDK 21 by JEP 444, all provide a thread that is cheap enough to allocate per request: a heap-allocated, growable stack scheduled by a runtime, mounted onto a carrier thread only while running.34,35 The engineering subtlety is in the edge cases, and they are instructive. A virtual thread that blocked inside a synchronized block used to pin its carrier — the carrier could not be reused, so a pool of carriers could be exhausted by threads that were not running. JEP 491 removed that limitation in JDK 24, and JDK 26 removed a further pinning case involving class initialisation.36,37 Those are not footnotes; they are the difference between a runtime that scales and one that deadlocks under load.

Go's equivalent story concerns not stacks but processors. From Go 1.5 until Go 1.24, GOMAXPROCS defaulted to the number of logical CPUs on the machine, which inside a Kubernetes pod with a CPU limit meant a runtime that believed it had 128 cores while the kernel throttled it to four — a throttling that shows up as tail latency, not as errors. Go 1.25 made the default container-aware, reading the cgroup bandwidth limit and re-reading it as it changes.38 Concurrency correctness and concurrency performance are different problems, and this is a clean example of the second.

07A race condition and a data race are different things

These terms are used interchangeably and should not be. The distinction is worth internalising because tools detect one of them and not the other.

A data race is a precise, mechanical property: two threads access the same memory location, at least one access is a write, and the two accesses are not ordered by happens-before.40,41 In C, C++, Rust and Go this is undefined or unspecified behaviour — not “you get one value or the other” but “the compiler was entitled to assume this could not occur and may have optimised accordingly.” Hans Boehm's papers are the standard demonstration that treating races as benign is not safe: compilers routinely introduce speculative writes, reload values they were allowed to assume unchanged, and turn a race whose worst outcome you predicted into one whose outcome is arbitrary.42,43

A race condition is a semantic property: the correctness of the result depends on the relative timing of events. Every data race is a race condition, but the converse fails, and the failure is where the expensive bugs live. A bank transfer implemented as two separate, individually well-synchronised transactions has no data race and a serious race condition. A check-then-act sequence — test whether a file exists, then open it — has no data race at all and is the classic TOCTOU security vulnerability.44,45 MITRE catalogues the general class as CWE-362.46

Happens-before is a partial order, and this is the conceptual move most worth making. Lamport defined it for distributed message passing;18 the Java and C++ memory models adopted it for shared memory almost verbatim.47,41 Program order within a thread gives you edges for free. Between threads, you get edges only from synchronisation: a lock release before a lock acquire on the same lock, a release store before an acquire load of the same atomic, a thread start, a thread join, a channel send before its receive, a semaphore V before the corresponding P. Two accesses with no chain of such edges between them are, by definition, racing — regardless of how many milliseconds apart they happen to occur on your machine.

Once you think in edges, several confusing rules become one rule. “Publish the object after you finish constructing it” is about placing the edge after the writes. “Do not let this escape from a constructor” is about the same thing. Double-checked locking was broken in old Java for exactly this reason, and the famous 2001 declaration signed by most of the language's designers said so plainly: without the memory model's ordering guarantees, a reader can obtain a reference to a partially constructed object.48 The fix, when it came, was a new memory model, not a new idiom.49,40

08Atomic operations: closing the gap in hardware

The lost update in Figure 0 exists because x = x + 1 is three operations with two gaps. An atomic read-modify-write is a single instruction that closes both gaps in hardware: the load, the arithmetic and the store occur with no observable point in between. On x86 this is a lock-prefixed instruction such as lock xadd; on ARM and RISC-V it is a load-linked/store-conditional pair or a dedicated atomic instruction; at the language level it is fetch_add, AtomicLong.getAndIncrement, atomic.AddInt64 or fetch_update.

The primitive that matters most is compare-and-swap: atomically, if this location still holds the value I expect, replace it with this new value and tell me whether you did. CAS is not merely convenient. Herlihy proved that the primitives available to a shared-memory system form a strict hierarchy by consensus number — the maximum number of threads for which the primitive can solve wait-free consensus. Atomic registers have consensus number one; test-and-set and fetch-and-add have two; compare-and-swap has infinity.21 That theorem is why every relevant instruction set built after 1990 ships CAS or LL/SC, and why every lock-free data structure you will ever read is written in terms of it.

Two hazards deserve naming. The first is ABA: a CAS succeeds because the location holds the expected bit pattern, but the value was changed to something else and back in the interval, and the operation you are completing is therefore based on a stale premise. The usual fixes are a version-tagged pointer, a double-width CAS, or a memory reclamation scheme that prevents the address from being recycled at all — hazard pointers, epoch-based reclamation, or RCU.50 The second is composition: two atomic operations are not one atomic operation. Reading an atomic counter and then writing an atomic flag based on it is a race condition built out of race-free parts. Atomicity does not compose, which is the deepest practical reason locks and transactions continue to exist.

09Locks: exclusion, and what it costs

A mutex is the mechanism most programmers reach for and the one whose costs are least often modelled. Its guarantee is narrow and absolute: at most one thread is inside the critical section. Everything else — which thread gets in, in what order, for how long, and what happens between two locked regions — remains permitted.

Implementations vary more than the API suggests. A pure spinlock burns a core while waiting, which is right when the expected wait is shorter than a context switch and catastrophic otherwise. A pure blocking lock always enters the kernel, which is right for long waits and wasteful for short ones. Linux's futex resolved the tension by making the uncontended path a single atomic operation in user space and entering the kernel only to sleep and wake — the design that made fast general-purpose mutexes possible on commodity hardware.51 Most modern mutexes are adaptive: spin briefly, then park.

Fairness is a design axis, not a detail. A strictly FIFO lock prevents starvation and destroys throughput under contention, because it forces a cache line to migrate between cores in exactly the worst order. An unfair “barging” lock lets the currently running thread reacquire immediately, which is much faster and can starve a waiter indefinitely. Java's ReentrantLock exposes the choice as a constructor argument, and the documentation is unusually honest that the fair variant is much slower.52,53 Go's sync.Mutex takes a hybrid position, switching a waiter into a starvation mode after one millisecond.

Reader-writer locks look free and often are not. They permit concurrent readers, but the read path still writes — it increments a shared reader count, which means an exclusive cache line, which means the read path does not actually scale with cores on a read-heavy workload. Courtois, Heymans and Parnas posed the readers-writers problem in 1971 and identified the starvation trade-off in the same paper: prefer readers and writers may never run; prefer writers and readers may never run.54 Seqlocks and RCU exist because reader-writer locks did not solve the read-mostly case well enough.

Granularity, convoys, and priority inversion

Lock granularity is a throughput decision with a correctness tail. One coarse lock is easy to reason about and serialises everything. Many fine locks scale and multiply the opportunities for deadlock and for subtly non-atomic composite operations. The historical arc of the Linux kernel is the standard illustration: the Big Kernel Lock introduced for SMP in 1996 was progressively decomposed over fifteen years and finally removed in 2.6.39.

Convoying occurs when a lock holder is descheduled and the waiters queue behind it; when the holder resumes, it hands the lock to a waiter which is immediately descheduled, and the system spends its time in transfer rather than work. Priority inversion is the real-time version: a low-priority thread holds a lock a high-priority thread needs, and a medium-priority thread that needs no lock preempts the low-priority holder, so the high-priority thread waits on a thread that is not running. The standard remedies are priority inheritance and the priority ceiling protocol, formalised by Sha, Rajkumar and Lehoczky in 1990;55 most RTOS mutexes implement inheritance and say so.56 The most famous instance of the problem is discussed later in this article.

10Deadlock, livelock, starvation

Deadlock is the failure mode that arrives with locks. Coffman, Elphick and Shoshani's 1971 survey established the four conditions that must all hold for it to occur, which is useful because it means breaking any one of them is a prevention strategy.57 Havender had already described the ordering approach in 1968.58

That lab number is worth dwelling on. A textbook deadlock in a four-instruction program is not a rare corner: half of its schedules hang. It survives in real code only because real schedulers rarely produce the interleaving, which is the same reason it appears without warning after an unrelated change. Impose a global order on lock acquisition and the deadlocked states do not become less likely; they cease to be reachable.

The alternatives to prevention are avoidance and detection. Avoidance — the banker's algorithm and its relatives — requires advance knowledge of maximum resource claims and is essentially unused in general-purpose software. Detection is common where transactions can be aborted: every serious relational database builds a wait-for graph, finds cycles, and kills a victim transaction with a deadlock error, which is why application code that talks to a database must be prepared to retry.59,60 Timeouts (try_lock_for, SELECT ... NOWAIT) are detection's cheap cousin and convert a hang into an error, which is usually an improvement.

Livelock is deadlock's active twin: threads keep running, keep responding to each other, and make no progress — two processes each backing off politely and colliding again. Starvation is indefinite postponement of one participant while others proceed, and is the failure mode of unfair locks, priority scheduling, and every optimistic scheme whose retry loop can be outrun. Deadlock stops the program and gets fixed; livelock and starvation degrade it and get misdiagnosed as performance problems.

11Coordination: semaphores, monitors, conditions, barriers

Exclusion answers “not at the same time.” Coordination answers “not until.” The two are separate mechanisms and the confusion between them causes real bugs.

A semaphore is a counter with atomic decrement-and-wait and increment-and-signal.7 Used with a count of one it is a lock; used with a count of n it is a permit pool, the right structure for a connection limiter or a bounded worker pool. Downey's Little Book of Semaphores works through the classical problems in this vocabulary and is the best available drill book for the underlying reasoning.61

A condition variable lets a thread release a lock and sleep until another thread signals that a predicate may now hold. Its single most important property is the one everyone gets wrong the first time: the wait must be inside a loop that re-tests the predicate. Mesa-style monitors — the semantics used by essentially every modern system, following Lampson and Redell's 1980 analysis — do not transfer the lock atomically to the signalled thread, so between the signal and the waiter's resumption, another thread can acquire the lock and invalidate the predicate.62 Spurious wakeups are permitted on top of that. if (!ready) wait(); is a bug in every mainstream language.

Barriers and latches coordinate phases rather than resources: a barrier makes n threads wait until all n arrive, which is the backbone of bulk-synchronous parallel algorithms; a latch is a one-shot barrier. Monitors bundle a lock, its conditions, and the data they protect into one module, which is the structural insight that made concurrent programming teachable.12,11 Java's synchronized, C#'s lock and Python's with lock: are all monitor syntax; Rust's Mutex<T> goes one step further by making the data inaccessible except through the lock, which turns “remember to take the lock” from a convention into a type error.39

For engineering purposes, the modern advice is to reach for the highest-level construct that fits: a concurrent collection rather than a lock around a plain one, an executor rather than raw threads, a channel rather than a condition variable. Doug Lea's java.util.concurrent, shipped in Java 5, is the most influential demonstration of that principle — it moved an entire language community from writing wait/notify by hand to composing tested components.52,63,53

12Memory consistency: the machine does not run your program

Everything so far assumed that a program's steps happen in the order written and that a write becomes visible to everyone at once. Neither is true. Compilers reorder, hoist and eliminate memory operations; processors buffer stores, speculate loads and complete instructions out of order; caches propagate values by protocol rather than by broadcast. A memory consistency model is the contract that says which of these liberties the machine is allowed to take, and therefore which executions your program permits.64

Sequential consistency, Lamport's 1979 definition, is the model everyone reasons with by default and no commercial multiprocessor implements.19 The reason is the store buffer. When a core executes a store, waiting for the line to become exclusive would stall it for tens or hundreds of cycles, so the store goes into a per-core FIFO buffer and the core proceeds. Loads check the local buffer first. The result is that a core sees its own stores immediately and other cores see them later — and that single optimisation is the entire difference between sequential consistency and x86's total store order.65

Sewell and colleagues' x86-TSO model is the rigorous statement of what Intel and AMD hardware actually guarantees, and it was written because the vendor prose of the time was ambiguous enough that experts disagreed about it.65,66 ARM and POWER are weaker still: stores and loads may be reordered in ways TSO forbids, and POWER historically was not even multi-copy atomic, meaning two observers could disagree about the order of two stores. Rigorous models for both exist, ARMv8's having been simplified to a multi-copy atomic model in 2018.67,68 Alglave, Maranget and Tautschnig's Herding Cats supplies the common framework and the litmus-test methodology that makes such models testable against silicon.69

Peterson's algorithm was correct, and your laptop broke it

Peterson's mutual exclusion algorithm uses nothing but ordinary loads and stores of two flags and a turn variable, and it is provably correct under sequential consistency.70 It is also broken on every machine you own, because the store to your own flag can sit in the store buffer while you load the other thread's flag — so both threads read zero and both enter. The lab checks this exhaustively.

This is the single most useful demonstration in the article, because it shows that the memory model is not an exotic concern for lock-free specialists. It is the reason your algorithm textbook is not directly executable. The fix is a fence — an instruction that forbids reordering across itself — or, in modern practice, atomic operations tagged with an ordering.

The language-level formulation is release/acquire. A release store publishes everything the thread wrote before it; an acquire load that reads that value obtains everything the releasing thread had done. That pair creates exactly one happens-before edge and is enough to make the publish-a-pointer idiom safe. Two practical rules survive all of this. First, if you find yourself using relaxed ordering, either you are writing a statistics counter or you are wrong. Second, threads cannot be added to a language as a library: the compiler must know about them, because otherwise it will optimise across synchronisation it cannot see. Boehm's 2005 paper made that argument and won it, which is why C11 and C++11 have memory models at all.43,75

13The cost of sharing: coherence, contention, and the ceiling

Synchronisation has a price, and the price is not mainly the instruction. It is the cache line. Multiprocessors keep caches coherent with a protocol — MESI and its descendants — in which a core wanting to write a line must first obtain it exclusively, invalidating every other copy.76 A contended atomic operation is therefore a request for a line to migrate across the interconnect, which costs one to two orders of magnitude more than an uncontended one. Ulrich Drepper's monograph remains the standard practitioner account of why memory behaves the way it does.77

The pathological case is false sharing: two variables that no thread shares, sitting in the same cache line, so that writes to one invalidate the other. The program is correct and slow, and profilers point at the wrong line.78 The fix is padding or alignment — Java's @Contended, C++'s hardware_destructive_interference_size, Go's manual padding structs, and the striped counters inside LongAdder.

Three laws about how far this goes

Amdahl's argument is the oldest and least escapable: if a fraction s of the work is inherently serial, speed-up is bounded by 1/s no matter how many processors you buy.4 Five per cent serial caps you at twenty-fold, forever. Gustafson's rebuttal is not that Amdahl was wrong but that he answered a question practitioners rarely ask: in practice, problem size grows with machine size, and if you hold time fixed rather than work, scaled speed-up is nearly linear.79 Both are correct about their own question, and knowing which question you are asking is most of the skill.

Neil Gunther's Universal Scalability Law adds the term that matters most in practice. Beyond the serial fraction (contention, σ) it models coherency (κ) — the cost of keeping n participants consistent with each other, which grows quadratically.80 The consequence is retrograde scaling: past some concurrency level, adding capacity reduces throughput. Anyone who has watched a service get slower after a thread pool was enlarged has seen the κ term.

Two further tools belong in the same toolkit. Little's law — the average number of items in a stable system equals arrival rate times average residence time — connects concurrency, throughput and latency with no assumptions about distributions, and is the fastest way to sanity-check a pool size.81 And Flynn's taxonomy remains the cleanest vocabulary for distinguishing the kinds of parallel machine under discussion.82 Cantrill and Bonwick's field report from building concurrent systems at Sun is the best short account of what these numbers feel like from inside an engineering team.83

14Lock-free, wait-free, and the progress hierarchy

“Lock-free” is a progress guarantee, not a description of the source code. The hierarchy is precise. A blocking algorithm can be stopped entirely by one suspended participant. An obstruction-free algorithm guarantees a thread completes if it eventually runs in isolation.84 A lock-free algorithm guarantees that some thread makes progress in a bounded number of steps, so the system as a whole never stalls, though an individual thread may starve. A wait-free algorithm guarantees that every thread completes in a bounded number of its own steps.21

The classical structures are worth knowing by name because they recur. Treiber's stack (1986) is the simplest useful lock-free structure: push and pop are CAS loops on a head pointer.85 The Michael-Scott queue (1996) is the lock-free FIFO that underlies java.util.concurrent.ConcurrentLinkedQueue and countless others.86 Both expose the memory reclamation problem — you cannot free a node while another thread might still be traversing it — for which hazard pointers and epoch-based schemes are the standard answers.50

Read-copy-update is the most consequential of these ideas in deployed systems. Its bargain is that readers pay nothing: no lock, no atomic, in the common case no memory barrier at all. Writers make a private copy, publish it with a single ordered store, and defer reclaiming the old version until every pre-existing reader has passed a quiescent point. McKenney and Slingwine's 1998 paper introduced it; the Linux kernel now has tens of thousands of RCU call sites, and the technique is essentially the only reason a read-mostly kernel data structure scales to hundreds of cores.87,88

The road not taken, so far, is transactional memory. Herlihy and Moss proposed hardware transactional memory in 1993 as architectural support for lock-free structures;89 Shavit and Touitou gave the software version its name two years later.90 The idea is seductive: mark a region atomic and let the system detect conflicts and retry. In practice STM's overheads, its inability to handle irrevocable operations such as I/O, and its poor interaction with existing code led to a famous 2008 assessment titled “why is it only a research toy?”91 Hardware support shipped in Intel's TSX and was repeatedly disabled by errata and security findings. The lesson is not that the idea was bad; it is that a concurrency mechanism must compose with everything else the program does, and the ones that survive are those that do.

15Parallelism as its own discipline

When the goal is throughput rather than responsiveness, a different toolkit applies. Its organising idea is work and span: the total operations, and the length of the longest dependency chain. Speed-up is bounded by their ratio, and a good scheduler achieves close to that bound automatically. Cilk demonstrated that a provably efficient work-stealing scheduler — idle workers steal from the tail of a busy worker's deque — could make fork-join parallelism both fast and simple to express.92,93 Doug Lea's Java fork/join framework brought the same design into a mainstream runtime, and it is the machinery underneath parallel streams and the default CompletableFuture executor.94 Go's scheduler, .NET's Task Parallel Library, Rust's Rayon and Intel's TBB are all in this lineage.

Data parallelism avoids most coordination by construction: apply the same operation across a partitioned collection and combine. OpenMP made it a set of pragmas over loops;95 MPI made it a message-passing standard for clusters;96 MapReduce made it a fault-tolerant batch model for datacentres,97 and Spark generalised that into a lineage-based in-memory model.98 All of these hide concurrency behind a restricted programming model, which is the recurring winning move.

GPUs take the restriction furthest. A CUDA warp of 32 threads historically executed in lockstep, so “concurrency” within a warp meant divergence handling rather than synchronisation.99 Volta's independent thread scheduling changed that in 2017 by giving each thread its own program counter, which made warp-level programming more flexible and simultaneously made a class of previously safe implicit-synchronisation idioms unsafe — the same lesson as Peterson's algorithm, arriving from a different direction.100 Modern GPU code needs explicit __syncwarp for exactly the reason modern CPU code needs fences.

C++ has spent a decade trying to unify all of this. std::execution — senders and receivers, proposal P2300 — was adopted for C++26 and provides a single vocabulary for describing asynchronous work, its scheduling and its composition, with a reference implementation that targets thread pools, io_uring and GPUs alike.101,102 Whether the abstraction is teachable at scale is still being argued; that it is the standard direction is not.

16Asynchronous execution: the event loop and its descendants

The other way to get concurrency is to stop having threads wait. An event loop keeps a set of ready callbacks and a set of outstanding I/O registrations, and runs one callback at a time to completion.103,104 There is no preemption, so there are no data races on the loop's heap and no locks; there is also no way to survive a callback that blocks.

The mechanism underneath is a readiness or completion notification interface: select, then poll, then epoll and kqueue, and most recently io_uring, which moves from readiness notification to completion notification with shared submission and completion rings and dramatically fewer system calls.105,106 Dan Kegel's C10K page catalogued the design space in 1999 and is still the best single map of it.33 nginx's architecture is the canonical production answer: a small number of single-threaded worker processes, each running an event loop, one per core.107

Two structural insights from the research literature are worth carrying. SEDA proposed decomposing a service into stages connected by explicit queues, so that overload becomes visible and controllable at each stage rather than appearing as an undifferentiated collapse.108 And Adya and colleagues identified precisely what the event-driven style costs: it is cooperative task management with manual stack management, and the pain everybody attributes to the former actually comes from the latter.109 That paper is, in retrospect, the design brief for async/await, for goroutines, and for virtual threads: keep cooperative scheduling, give the stack back.

The thread-per-core architecture is the current high-performance refinement. Pin one thread to each core, give it its own shard of the data, and communicate between cores by explicit message passing rather than shared memory — a shared-nothing design inside a single machine, which eliminates locks and cache line bouncing at the cost of requiring the workload to shard cleanly.110 It is the same trade the operating system made with processes, applied one level down.

17Futures and promises: a value that is not there yet

The abstraction that made asynchronous programming composable is older than almost everyone assumes. Friedman and Wise described it in 1976 as a consequence of applicative programming: a constructor that returns immediately and whose components are computed concurrently.111 Baker and Hewitt named the future in 1977 in a paper mostly about garbage collection.112 Halstead's MultiLisp gave it the form most people recognise, with future as an annotation that returns a placeholder and blocks only when the value is actually touched.113 Liskov and Shrira introduced promises in 1988 as typed, first-class objects for asynchronous remote procedure calls, explicitly to keep the call site from blocking.114

The modern distinction — a future is the read end, a promise is the write end — comes from that lineage and is respected by C++ (std::future/std::promise), Java (Future/CompletableFuture) and Rust (Future plus a channel), and blurred by JavaScript, where a single Promise object plays both roles.115

The real advance is not the object but composition. Once a pending result is a value, you can build combinators over it: map, flat-map, all, any, timeout, retry. That converts nested callbacks — whose error handling must be repeated at every level and whose control flow is inverted — into expressions. And once the combinators exist, a compiler can hide them: the async/await syntax is a source-to-source transformation into a state machine that resumes at each suspension point.

The syntax has spread with unusual speed and unusual uniformity. C# shipped async/await in C# 5 (2012) on top of the Task-based Asynchronous Pattern;116,117 Python added asyncio in 3.4 via PEP 3156 and dedicated syntax in 3.5 via PEP 492;118,119 JavaScript standardised it in ES2017; Rust stabilised it in 1.39 after several years of designing a zero-cost, poll-based Future trait that allocates nothing per await point, and completed the ergonomic story with async closures in Rust 1.85.120,121,122 Swift adopted it in 2021 alongside actors and a structured concurrency model.123

Two warnings follow. First, await is a suspension point and therefore an interleaving point: state you read before it may have changed after it, even in a single-threaded runtime. Async code is not race-free code; it is code with a smaller and more visible set of interleaving points. Second, coloured functions are contagious — an async function can only be awaited by another async function, so the property propagates up the call graph until it reaches a runtime. Virtual threads and goroutines are, in part, an attempt to get asynchronous efficiency without the colour.

18Structured concurrency: giving tasks a lifetime

Unstructured concurrency has the same defect as goto: a spawned task has no relationship to the code that spawned it. It may outlive its parent, it may fail with nobody listening, its errors do not propagate along the call stack, and a stack trace cannot tell you who started it. Nathaniel Smith's 2018 essay made the analogy explicit and proposed the fix: every task must be spawned inside a scope that does not exit until all its children have finished.124 Roman Elizarov developed the same idea in Kotlin, where coroutine scopes and cancellation propagation are the language's central concurrency abstraction.125,126

The consequences are larger than the syntax suggests. Cancellation acquires a direction: cancel a scope and every child is cancelled. Errors acquire a direction: a child's failure surfaces at the scope's join point, in the parent's stack. Resource lifetimes become block-scoped again, so try-with-resources and RAII start working across concurrency. And timeouts become composable, because a timeout is just a scope that cancels itself.

Adoption is uneven and instructive. Trio and Kotlin shipped it as the default. Swift built it into the language in 2021.123 Java has been iterating on StructuredTaskScope since JDK 19 and, as of JDK 26 in March 2026,127 it remains a preview API on its sixth round — the constructors became static factories in JDK 25, and joiners replaced subclassing.128,129,130 Six previews is not a failure; it is a language design team taking seriously that a concurrency API, once final, cannot be changed. Go, notably, has no structured concurrency in the language: go f() is precisely the unstructured spawn Smith objected to, and the ecosystem answer is errgroup plus a context convention.

19Actors and channels: concurrency without shared memory

If the problem is shared mutable state, one answer is to abolish it. Two traditions do so differently, and the difference is the shape of the synchronisation.

In the actor model, an actor has private state, an address, and an unbounded mailbox; it processes one message at a time and may send messages, create actors, and change how it will handle the next message.16,17 There is no lock because there is nothing to lock: no other actor holds a reference to the state. Erlang is the model's most complete realisation, with per-process heaps, preemptive scheduling of lightweight processes, and — crucially — supervision. Joe Armstrong's thesis argued that reliable systems come not from preventing failure but from isolating it and restarting from a known state, the “let it crash” philosophy.131,132 WhatsApp's reported two million concurrent connections on a single server remains the canonical demonstration that the model scales.133 Akka carried it to the JVM, Orleans added virtual actors that the runtime activates on demand, and Swift made actors a language-level type whose isolation the compiler checks — with Swift 6 turning data-race safety from a warning into a language-level guarantee.134,135,136,137,138

In CSP, the first-class object is the channel, not the process, and communication is a synchronous rendezvous: sender and receiver both block until the exchange occurs, so the transfer is a single event.15 Go's design is the most widely deployed descendant, with goroutines, typed channels and select. Neither model eliminates concurrency bugs; both relocate them. Deadlock moves from lock cycles to channel cycles or to two actors waiting on each other's replies. Atomicity across two actors is not available: if two entities must change together, the boundary is drawn wrongly and the fix is design, not synchronisation. Unbounded mailboxes turn overload into memory exhaustion. And message ordering guarantees are weaker than people assume — typically FIFO per sender-receiver pair, and nothing across pairs. Every one of these is easier to see than the equivalent shared-memory bug, which is the actual argument for the model.

20Producer-consumer and the classical patterns

Producer-consumer is the pattern from which most concurrent architecture is built, and the bounded buffer is the reason it works. Dijkstra posed it with semaphores; Hoare and Brinch Hansen posed it with monitors; every language now ships it as a library type: BlockingQueue, channel, asyncio.Queue, crossbeam::channel.7,12,61

The other classical problems are teaching devices that describe real failure modes. Readers-writers is the read-mostly access problem and the origin of the starvation trade-off.54 Dining philosophers is resource ordering and deadlock.10 The bakery algorithm shows that mutual exclusion is achievable with reads and writes alone, at a cost.140 Each recurs in production under a different name: readers-writers is your cache; dining philosophers is your distributed lock manager; the bakery algorithm is the thing you should not reimplement.

The architectural patterns built from these are worth naming as a checklist. The thread pool decouples task submission from thread creation and bounds concurrency; its sizing follows from Little's law and its most common failure is a pool whose tasks block on the same pool. The pipeline is producer-consumer chained, which trades latency for throughput and localises backpressure. Scatter-gather fans a request out and joins the responses, and is the pattern structured concurrency was designed for. The disruptor is a specialised ring buffer that avoids queue contention entirely by pre-allocating slots and using sequence counters, developed at LMAX for financial exchange latencies and widely copied since.141 And the job system of a modern game engine is fork-join work stealing under a frame deadline; Naughty Dog's fibre-based design is the best-documented example.142

21The same problem in eight domains

Concurrency looks different in each domain because each has a different dominant cost. The mechanisms are the same; the defaults are not.

Databases have the most mature theory, because they got there first. Serializability — an execution is correct if it is equivalent to some serial order — is the field's definition of correctness,143,144 and two-phase locking the classical enforcement mechanism.59 Optimistic concurrency control, proposed by Kung and Robinson, validates at commit instead of locking during execution.145 Multiversion concurrency control, from Reed's 1978 thesis, lets readers see a consistent snapshot without blocking writers and is now the default in PostgreSQL, Oracle, MySQL/InnoDB and essentially every modern engine.146,60 The critical practical fact is that the ANSI isolation levels are defined by which anomalies they forbid, that the definitions were shown to be ambiguous in 1995, and that the common default of read committed permits far more than most application invariants can tolerate.147,148,149

Operating system kernels run the most demanding concurrent programs in existence: preemptible, interrupt-driven, on hundreds of cores, with no ability to abort and retry. Browsers and JavaScript took the opposite route: a single-threaded main loop with a strictly specified task and microtask queue,103 with parallelism available only through Web Workers that communicate by structured-clone message passing — actors, in effect — and shared memory available only through the explicit, formally modelled SharedArrayBuffer and Atomics mechanism.152,74,73 The language with the most timid concurrency model has the most rigorously specified one.

Python is the interesting case in transition. The global interpreter lock made CPython's object model thread-safe by making bytecode execution serial, which gave Python threads for I/O concurrency and no parallelism. PEP 703 laid out how to remove it; PEP 779 set the criteria for calling the result supported; Python 3.14, released in October 2025, promoted the free-threaded build to officially supported status while keeping it a separate, opt-in binary.153,154,155 The interesting part for this article is what removing a global lock exposes: every piece of Python code that was accidentally atomic because of the GIL is now a candidate race, and the ecosystem is discovering which of its invariants were load-bearing.

Real-time and embedded systems care about worst-case bounds rather than averages, which inverts several defaults: unbounded queues are forbidden, dynamic allocation is often forbidden, priority inheritance is mandatory rather than optional, and blocking must be bounded by analysis.56,55 Games run a job system against a 16.7-millisecond frame deadline, where a lock held across a frame boundary is a visible stutter.142 Trading systems optimise for tail latency and eliminate queues, locks and allocation from the hot path entirely.141 And distributed systems are concurrency with the additional cruelty that a participant can fail silently and a caller cannot distinguish a slow peer from a dead one — the impossibility result that shapes the whole field.156,149 Ray's actor-based execution model for machine-learning workloads is a recent demonstration that the 1973 abstraction still absorbs new problem domains.157

22Four failures

Concurrency defects are over-represented among catastrophic software failures, for a structural reason: they are the defects most likely to survive testing and most likely to be triggered by load, which is exactly when the consequences are largest.

Therac-25, 1985–1987

The radiation therapy machine that delivered massive overdoses to six patients, killing at least three, had among its causes a race condition. An operator who edited the prescription quickly enough could complete the edit after the machine's setup task had already read the earlier values, so the turntable position and the beam energy disagreed. Nancy Leveson and Clark Turner's investigation is required reading not because the race is exotic but because everything around it — the removal of hardware interlocks, the reuse of software from a machine that had them, the treatment of an unreproducible fault as an operator error — is ordinary.158 The bug was found only after an engineer at a hospital reproduced it by practising the keystroke sequence until he could do it fast enough.

Mars Pathfinder, 1997

Days after landing, the spacecraft began resetting itself. The cause was textbook priority inversion: a low-priority meteorological task held a mutex on the VxWorks information bus; a high-priority bus management task blocked on that mutex; a medium-priority communications task, needing no mutex, preempted the low-priority holder; and the watchdog, seeing the high-priority task miss its deadline, reset the system. The fix was to enable priority inheritance on that mutex — a boolean parameter, uploaded to a spacecraft on Mars.159,55 The engineering lesson recorded by JPL was about process, not code: they had seen the resets in test, could not reproduce them, and shipped anyway with tracing left enabled, which is the only reason the cause was ever found.

The Northeast blackout, 14 August 2003

Fifty-five million people lost power across eight U.S. states and Ontario. The proximate causes were overgrown trees and transmission lines, but the reason a local problem became a cascade was that FirstEnergy's control room lost its alarm system and nobody knew. The U.S.–Canada task force's final report lists the silent failure of the energy management system's alarm function among the direct causes.160 The defect, found weeks later by GE engineers who had to slow the system down and inject deliberate delays to reproduce it, was a race condition on shared data in the alarm and event processing code, with a window measured in milliseconds.161 It had run for years without appearing.

Dirty COW, 2016

CVE-2016-5195 was a race condition in the Linux kernel's copy-on-write handling of private memory mappings that had been present for around nine years. By racing a write to /proc/self/mem against madvise(MADV_DONTNEED), an unprivileged local user could win the race and obtain write access to a read-only file mapping, escalating to root.162 It is the clearest modern demonstration that a race condition is a security vulnerability class, not only a correctness one: the attacker controls the schedule, so “the interleaving is unlikely” is not a mitigation. Anyone who can run code on the machine can run the race in a loop until it lands.

The common thread In all four, the defect had been present and harmless for a long time; the triggering interleaving was rare under normal load and reachable on demand under unusual load or adversarial control; and reproduction required deliberately distorting time. That last property is what the tools in the next section exist to provide.

23Finding the schedule that breaks you

Testing a concurrent program by running it repeatedly samples a distribution you do not control. Everything useful in this area is an attempt to change the distribution, enumerate it, or eliminate it.

Dynamic race detection is the highest-value tool most teams are not using. Eraser introduced the lockset algorithm in 1997 — track which locks are held on every access to a location, and report when the intersection empties.163 Modern detectors combine that with happens-before tracking via vector clocks. ThreadSanitizer is the standard implementation, shipping in Clang and GCC, and Go's -race flag is the same engine wired into the toolchain.164,165,166 The critical property, and its limit, is that these tools find races on code paths that actually execute: they do not need the bad interleaving to occur, only the access pair, which makes them enormously more sensitive than testing — but they find nothing on a path you never run.

Systematic exploration takes control of the scheduler. CHESS enumerated thread schedules with a bound on preemptions, on the observation that most concurrency bugs surface with very few preemptions.1 PCT randomised schedules with a provable probability of finding any bug of a given depth, which turns “run it again” into a measurable strategy.22 The idea is now available in ordinary toolchains: Rust's loom exhaustively permutes atomic and lock operations under the C11 memory model; AWS's shuttle does randomised exploration at larger scale; Microsoft's Coyote applies the approach to .NET async code and has found bugs in production Azure services; and Go 1.25 graduated testing/synctest, which runs concurrent tests inside a bubble with a virtual clock so that time-dependent tests are both fast and deterministic.167,168,169,170,171

Model checking moves up a level and checks the design. TLA+ specifies a system as a state machine with invariants and temporal properties, and the model checker explores the reachable state space.172 Amazon's report on using it for S3, DynamoDB and EBS is the most persuasive industrial account available: engineers found bugs in designs that had already passed review, in systems whose specifications were a few hundred lines long.173 SPIN occupies the same niche with a different formalism.174 For distributed systems, Jepsen's methodology — generate concurrent operations, inject partitions, then check the recorded history for linearizability violations — has repeatedly falsified vendor consistency claims.175,20

Finally, deterministic replay attacks reproduction directly: rr records a program's nondeterministic inputs, including the scheduling decisions, and replays them exactly, allowing reverse debugging.176 For a bug that takes a day to reproduce once, this is the difference between a fix and a rewrite.

24The lab: enumerating what the program permits

Everything numerical in this article comes from one dependency-free Python program. It models threads as lists of instructions, enumerates every reachable state under two memory models, counts complete executions by dynamic programming over the state graph, and reports outcomes, deadlocks and mutual-exclusion violations. It uses no threads: the point is to reason about schedules, not to sample them.

The core is the transition relation. Given a state, it returns every enabled next step — every thread that could run its next instruction, plus, under TSO, every store buffer that could drain.

interleaving_lab.py — the transition relation, abridged

# state = (pcs, regs, mem, lockowners, buffers, in_cs)

def successors(prog, state, model):
    pcs, regs, mem, owners, buffers, in_cs = state
    out = []
    for tid, thread in enumerate(prog.threads):
        pc = pcs[tid]

        # a buffered store may drain at any moment, on TSO
        if model == "TSO" and buffers[tid]:
            (var, val), rest = buffers[tid][0], buffers[tid][1:]
            out.append(("T%d:flush" % tid,
                        (pcs, regs, _set(mem, var, val), owners,
                         _set(buffers, tid, rest), in_cs)))

        if pc >= len(thread):
            continue
        op = thread[pc]

        if op[0] == "store":
            _, var, operand = op
            vi = prog.vindex[var]
            if model == "TSO":
                # visible to this core now, to others later
                nb = _set(buffers, tid, buffers[tid] + ((vi, val),))
                out.append((..., (_set(pcs, tid, pc + 1), regs, mem, owners,
                                  nb, in_cs)))
            else:
                out.append((..., (_set(pcs, tid, pc + 1), regs,
                                  _set(mem, vi, val), owners, buffers, in_cs)))

        elif op[0] == "lock":
            li = prog.lindex[op[1]]
            if owners[li] != -1:
                continue          # blocked: not an enabled transition
            out.append((..., (_set(pcs, tid, pc + 1), regs, mem,
                              _set(owners, li, tid), buffers, in_cs)))

        elif op[0] == "fence":
            if model == "TSO" and buffers[tid]:
                continue          # must drain before it may proceed
            out.append((..., (_set(pcs, tid, pc + 1), regs, mem, owners,
                              buffers, in_cs)))
        # load, add, mul, eqc, jnz, cas, unlock, cs_enter, cs_exit ...
    return out

A deadlock is then trivially definable: a state with no successors in which not every thread has finished. A mutual-exclusion violation is a state in which more than one thread's critical-section flag is set. Neither requires a heuristic or a timeout, because the search is exhaustive rather than empirical.

interleaving_lab.py — exploration and path counting, abridged

def explore(prog, model="SC"):
    init = initial_state(prog)
    states, order, edges = {init: 0}, [init], defaultdict(list)
    stack = [init]
    while stack:                        # reachable state graph
        s = stack.pop()
        for label, ns in successors(prog, s, model):
            edges[s].append((label, ns))
            if ns not in states:
                states[ns] = len(order); order.append(ns); stack.append(ns)

    terminals, deadlocks, violations = [], [], 0
    for s in order:
        if sum(s[5]) > 1: violations += 1       # two threads in the CS
        if not edges[s]:
            (terminals if is_final(prog, s) else deadlocks).append(s)

    # distinct schedules = number of maximal paths through the DAG
    paths = defaultdict(int); paths[init] = 1
    for s in topological_order(order, edges):
        for _, ns in edges[s]:
            paths[ns] += paths[s]

Four things recur across the seventeen rows, and they are the article's practical conclusions.

Nothing removes all interleavings. Every mechanism leaves a residue, and the residue is where the next bug lives. A mutex leaves the ordering between critical sections. An actor leaves message arrival order. A transaction leaves whatever its isolation level permits. Ask of every mechanism you adopt: what does this still allow?

The cheapest mechanisms are the ones that remove sharing, not the ones that manage it. Isolation, confinement and immutability appear at the top of the ledger because they cost nothing at runtime and eliminate whole categories rather than instances. Reach for a lock only after establishing that the data genuinely must be shared and mutable.

Composition is where guarantees die. Two atomic operations are not one atomic operation; two thread-safe methods do not make a thread-safe class; two correct services do not make a correct workflow. Almost every production concurrency incident is a composition failure rather than a primitive failure.

The tell column is the one to memorise. Concurrency defects rarely announce themselves as concurrency defects. They arrive as tail latency, as a memory leak, as an unreproducible support ticket, as a throughput curve that bends the wrong way at sixteen cores. Learning to read those symptoms backwards into the mechanism is most of what expertise in this area consists of.
