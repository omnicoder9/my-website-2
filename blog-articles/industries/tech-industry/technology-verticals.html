ONEThe question

Nineteen industries are commonly grouped under a single idea: a technology company organised around a domain rather than around a capability. Healthtech, fintech, legaltech, agritech, and the rest. The suffix does a lot of work. It implies that the domain is the salient thing, that these companies are alike in the way that companies in an industry are alike, and that knowing which one a company belongs to tells you something about how it will behave.

That implication is worth testing, because the more obvious reading of the list is that it is not a classification at all. It is a set of names that stuck. Some of the terms name a customer (govtech), some a subject matter (biotech software), some a business function (martech), some a physical asset class (proptech), and some an entire branch of engineering (aerospace technology). A production-oriented classification such as NAICS would refuse several of them outright: it groups establishments by the process they use rather than the market they serve, and it states plainly that the needs of regulatory and taxation users play no part in how it is built.1,2 The international systems do the same.3,4 The vertical names are not a taxonomy that anyone designed. They are a vocabulary that investors, recruiters and conference organisers found convenient.

So the question is what, if anything, the vocabulary is tracking. This article proposes one answer and then tests it against all nineteen. The proposal is that a technology vertical is the shadow cast by a permission structure - that what makes a domain support a durable population of specialist software companies is not the subject matter but the set of approvals standing between a working artefact and its lawful use. Where nothing has to be cleared, horizontal tools win and no vertical forms in any strong sense, whatever the domain. Where something has to be cleared, the shape of the thing that has to be cleared predicts the buyer, the sales cycle, the release cadence, the identity of the incumbent, and where the loss lands when the software is wrong.

The organising question, then, is a narrow one, and every section below is an attempt to answer it precisely for a named industry: who has to say yes before this runs?

The answer is rarely the answer the vertical's own marketing implies. The central finding of the coding exercise that follows is that in only six of the nineteen verticals does any authority examine the software itself. In twelve, the permission that matters is held by somebody else - the customer, the professional who signs, the exchange that admits you, the network whose rules you accepted - and the vertical software company's position is built on the strength of a licence it does not hold. In one, martech, nothing is cleared at all, and the vertical contains fifteen thousand products.

TWOWhat the list is a list of

Before proposing a replacement axis it is worth being precise about what is wrong with the list as given. Three things are wrong with it, and they are different problems.

First, the terms are not mutually exclusive. A company selling mortgage-origination software is fintech and proptech; a company selling claims software to a health insurer is healthtech and insurtech; a company selling telematics to a fleet is automotive technology and industrial technology and, if it prices the insurance, insurtech as well. Classification systems built for statistical work solve this by a primary-activity rule and by an explicit commitment to single parenthood. The vertical vocabulary has no such rule, which is why the same company routinely appears in several market maps.

Second, the terms are not at the same level of generality. "Adtech" names a layer of infrastructure perhaps four or five products deep. "Aerospace technology" names an industry with its own certification authorities, its own export-control regime, and a century of engineering practice. Treating them as siblings implies a comparability that does not exist.

Third, and most importantly for what follows, the terms describe the subject matter and are silent about the institutional environment. This is the substantive failure rather than a tidiness complaint. Two companies can act on the same subject matter under entirely different permission structures - a dashboard showing a hospital's bed occupancy and an algorithm recommending a dose are both healthtech, and only one of them is a medical device.5 Conversely, two companies in nominally distant verticals can face near-identical permission structures: an industrial safety-instrumented function and an aircraft flight-control component are governed by closely related functional-safety logic and by authorities that read one another's standards.6,7

The economics literature has long held that the returns to an innovation depend less on the innovation than on the complementary assets needed to commercialise it, and on who controls them.8 The literature on regulation makes a parallel point from the other direction: regulatory regimes are not exogenous constraints but resources that incumbents acquire and shape.9,10 The professions literature makes a third: a profession is defined by jurisdiction over a class of task, and defends that jurisdiction against technologies that would perform it.11 Put together, these three suggest that if you want to know why a domain supports specialist software firms, you should look at what the domain requires before it will accept work - which is a statement about institutions rather than about technology.12 The question of what a firm does inside itself and what it buys across a market boundary has always turned on the cost of transacting across that boundary;13 a permission only one party may hold makes the boundary very hard to move.

This is not a claim that technology is irrelevant. It is a claim about which differences between these nineteen industries are load-bearing. General-purpose technologies diffuse widely and are reshaped by each sector that adopts them; what varies is the complementary investment each sector must make before the technology is usable.14,15 In software, the largest such investment is very often not engineering at all. It is the evidence required by whoever must say yes.

THREEThe five doors

The framework has one dimension and five positions on it. Each position is a distinct kind of permission, and they are ordered by how narrowly the permission is held and how early it must be obtained. The order runs from a door that many parties can open, cheaply and repeatedly, to a door that one authority opens once, slowly, for one artefact.

The assignment rule matters more than the definitions, because without it the exercise becomes an exercise in adjectives. The rule used throughout is this: a door is closed for a vertical when a named instrument makes clearing it a precondition of lawful commercial deployment of that vertical's modal core product. Three parts of that sentence do real work. Named instrument means that if no statute, regulation, standard or scheme can be pointed at, the door is open, however difficult the sale may be in practice. Precondition of lawful deployment excludes things that are merely customary, prudent, or demanded by a nervous procurement department. Modal core product means the coding follows the typical product of the vertical rather than its most regulated corner; where a sub-segment closes a deeper door, that is recorded in the plate's note rather than in the matrix.

Each vertical then has two numbers. Its rung is the deepest door it must clear, from R0 to R5. Its count is how many of the five doors are closed. These are different quantities and they come apart in informative ways: a vertical can face one very deep door and nothing else, or four shallow ones.

Two clarifications forestall predictable objections. The first: horizontal law is not a door. The GDPR, state consumer-privacy statutes, employment law and general contract law bind every vertical equally and therefore explain none of the variation between them.16 A door counts only where the instrument attaches to the particular class of record, subject or act the vertical handles. The second: the AI Act is deliberately not treated as a door in the coding, because it is a horizontal instrument that borrows its depth from the sectoral regimes it sits on top of - its high-risk annex mostly points at activities that already have doors of their own, and the 2026 Digital Omnibus deferred those obligations further.17,18 The same reasoning excludes voluntary management-system certification, which any vertical may adopt and none must.19 An instrument that changes every vertical by the same amount cannot be what distinguishes them.

The rung is not a measure of how regulated an industry feels. It is a measure of how far you have to go before somebody with the power to refuse has to look at what you built.

FOURNineteen verticals, read as permission structures

Each plate below carries the same six fields and the same gauge. The fields are: what the software acts on; the system of record it must orbit; who must say yes before it may be deployed; who bears the loss when it is wrong; the unit the vertical's economics are denominated in; and a set of named participants. The gauge draws the five doors in plan. A closed door is a filled leaf with its swing; an open one is a bare pair of jambs. The threshold bar marks the rung.

The last field needs a word of explanation, because lists of companies are usually rankings and this one is not. The names are chosen to span the vertical's structural positions rather than its revenue: whoever holds the system of record, whoever is attacking it, and - where an artefact door is closed - at least one party that actually holds the authorisation, since that is the distinction the article is about. They are illustrative rather than exhaustive, in no order, and no endorsement is implied. Where a vertical contains both gated and ungated products, the field says which is which, because that split is usually invisible from the outside and is the single most useful thing to know about the vertical's competitive structure.

The plates are grouped by what the permission structure is made of rather than by subject matter, which produces some deliberately awkward neighbours. Insurtech sits beside legaltech and proptech rather than beside healthtech; industrial technology sits beside agriculture and aerospace. The awkwardness is the point of the exercise.

A hospital is inspected; a manufacturing site is inspected; the software inside them is examined only when it crosses the line into being a device in its own right. This produces a characteristic asymmetry in who pays for compliance. The customer bears the validation cost, the vendor bears the consequence of that cost in a lengthened sales cycle, and neither party has an incentive to shorten it unilaterally.

The seven verticals where software moves matter contain the deepest doors on the ladder and, in several cases, the shallowest ones sitting inches away. EXPERIMENT 1

The geometry of the matrix

The first question to ask of any proposed axis is whether it carries information the existing names do not. Five binary doors admit thirty-two profiles. SIXWhat the ledger says

Five results survive the coding, in descending order of how much they change the picture.

The permission almost never attaches to the software

Forty-three doors are closed across the nineteen verticals, and six of them - fourteen percent - require an authority to examine the artefact. The other eighty-six percent bind a customer, a professional, an operator or a scheme member. This is the finding with the largest practical consequence, because it means that in most verticals the compliance apparatus a software company builds is not a licence. It is evidence offered to a buyer who holds one. That is a much weaker asset than it is usually presented as: it is rivalrous only until a competitor assembles the same evidence, and it does not exclude anyone the way an approval does.

Depth and breadth are different things, and they have different effects

The ten verticals at R4 or R5 close 2.90 doors on average; the five at R2 or below close 1.00. But the rung is what predicts cadence and the count is what predicts integration surface, and they can diverge sharply. Energy technology closes only two doors and both are at the bottom of the ladder; traveltech closes two that are far apart. Reporting a vertical as simply 'regulated' collapses two variables that push in different directions.

The door that binds is frequently not the door in the statute book

Three of the nineteen currently have a headline instrument that is not operative. The personal financial data rights rule remains codified while a federal court has enjoined its enforcement and the agency rewrites it.44-46 The food traceability rule is in force with a congressional direction not to enforce it before July 2028.106 The governing constraint in adtech is now a behavioural decree rather than a statute, after a court declined in September 2026 to order divestiture of the ad exchange.88,90 Any framework that reads permission structures off legislation alone will misclassify all three. The coding rule used here - a named instrument that is a precondition of lawful deployment - is deliberately about what binds rather than what is enacted.

Private doors outnumber public ones

The most frequently closed door is the shallowest: a private scheme, closed in twelve of nineteen. Card network rules, MLS participation, exchange admission, agency accreditation, conformance testing, retailer-imposed certification, prime-contractor quality flow-downs. These are contracts, not laws, and they are enforced by exclusion rather than by penalty. They are also not light: the current card-data standard introduced 64 new requirements, 51 of them deferred to a single compliance date in March 2025.146 For a great many vertical software companies the party that can end the business tomorrow is not a regulator but a network that can decline to keep admitting them - which is the older observation that architecture and private rules regulate conduct at least as effectively as law does.147,148

Nothing cleared means everything entered

Martech is the control case, and it behaves exactly as the framework predicts a zero-door vertical should. Fifteen thousand five hundred and five products in 2026, growth of 0.79%, 1,488 added and 1,367 removed in a single year.80 Free entry, free exit, no concentration in the record, and no gate that anyone can stand behind. It is the largest vertical by vendor count and the one where the term 'vertical' does the least work.

SEVENConsequences

If the rung is what it claims to be, five downstream properties should follow from it, and each is checkable against the plates rather than asserted.

Who signs. At R3 the buyer is a professional and the sale is to a practice; at R4 it is an authorised firm and the sale runs through risk and compliance; at R5 the buyer is a manufacturer or an operator and the sale is a programme decision measured in years. The rung predicts the seniority and the function of the person who must sign more reliably than the deal size does.

How often you ship. Experiment 4 gives the mechanism. Latency dominates the delay, and submission budget determines cadence, so at R5 capital converts directly into product velocity. This is a structural reason - independent of engineering quality or distribution - for deep verticals to consolidate toward a small number of well-capitalised vendors.

What the moat is made of. At R5 the moat is the approval, and it is genuinely exclusionary. Below R5 the moat is the integration surface and the accumulated record of successful deployment, which is exclusionary only against firms unwilling to spend the same money. The distinction matters most when a horizontal platform arrives: it can absorb workflow, it can pay for integrations, and it can hire domain staff, but it cannot obtain an artefact authorisation without taking on the liability that comes with it. The R5 boundary is therefore the one place where the vertical's position is structurally rather than merely economically defended.

Who the incumbent is. Where the system of record is concentrated, the incumbent is the record-holder and the vertical company is a tenant. Epic holds 43.7% of US acute-care hospitals and 56.9% of beds; the multiple listing service holds the inventory; the global distribution systems hold the itinerary; the type certificate holds the aircraft. Where it is fragmented or absent, there is no incumbent to displace and no landlord to pay, which is a different business and usually a worse one.

Where the loss lands. This is the field most often left implicit in the vertical's own marketing, and it is the one that determines what the software is allowed to do. Where the loss lands on a licensed human, the product is constrained to produce things that a human can check before adopting - which is precisely the statutory test in the clinical decision support carve-out, and precisely the test that a model producing unverifiable output fails.22,23 A great deal of what looks like conservatism in deep verticals is this constraint, correctly understood.

A vertical software company's most valuable asset is usually a licence it does not hold and cannot obtain.

EIGHTWhen the basis cannot be reviewed

Three of the five doors rest on an assumption that is worth making explicit, because it is the assumption most obviously under pressure. The assumption is that the output of the software can be checked by a competent person before it takes effect. A licensed practitioner adopts a recommendation because they can evaluate it; an approval attaches to an artefact because the artefact holds still long enough to be examined. Systems whose reasoning is not inspectable and whose behaviour changes between deployments strain both.

The clearest place to see this is the clinical decision support carve-out, because it is a statutory explainability test rather than a policy preference. Software escapes the device definition only if it is intended for a clinician to review the basis of the recommendation and does not substitute for the clinician's own judgement.22,23 That test does not ask what the software is for. It asks whether its basis can be independently reviewed - which means the rung a healthtech product occupies is set by a property of the model rather than by the vendor's intent. Two products with identical clinical purposes sit on different rungs if one can show its working and the other cannot. Nothing else on the ladder has this character.

The second strain is on fixity. An artefact authorisation is issued to a version, and a system that is retrained or updated is, at some point, no longer the thing that was approved. Two regimes have converged on structurally similar answers from opposite directions. The FDA route is to pre-authorise a space of permitted future change, so that modifications inside a declared envelope do not require a fresh submission.26 The vehicle route is to make the software configuration part of the approval itself, identified by number, so that any change outside it is visible as a change to a type-approved characteristic.130,131 One buys flexibility by specifying the boundary in advance; the other buys accountability by making the current state auditable. Both are attempts to keep a door meaningful when what it guards will not hold still, and both push work onto the vendor rather than the reviewer.7,125

Insurance shows what it looks like when a regulator declines to treat the model as an exception. The Colorado framework requires life insurers using external data and predictive models to test and document them, and the NAIC bulletin adopted in more than twenty states reminds insurers that a decision made with an AI system must comply with the same law as a decision made without one, third-party tooling included.62,64,66 No new door was opened. An existing one - the filing that must be approved before a rating plan may be used - was simply pointed at a new kind of artefact.

Legaltech shows the opposite adjustment, and it is the more common one. When fabricated citations began reaching courts at scale, the response was not to regulate the tool but to restate and enforce the practitioner's existing duty: the signature carries the contents regardless of what produced them, and competence now includes understanding the tool's limits.50,54 Empirical evaluation of the specialist research products found that retrieval grounding reduced fabrication without eliminating it,56 which is exactly the regime a D3 door is designed to survive: it does not require the tool to be right, only that someone accountable checks. What the sanctions record demonstrates is that the check is expensive and is being skipped, and that the cost of skipping it falls on the person, not the vendor.51,52

The framework therefore makes a prediction it cannot yet test. Doors should deepen unevenly. Verticals whose D3 door rests on reviewability face a choice between migrating work to D5 and loading more verification duty onto the practitioner, and the two paths have very different consequences for who can build the product. Verticals at R0 and R1 face neither, because there is nothing there to deepen - which is why the loudest arguments about AI governance in martech and adtech are about horizontal instruments, and the quietest are in aerospace, where the answer was settled by a design assurance framework decades ago. The horizontal AI instruments excluded from the coding are the obvious way this could change: if their obligations came to attach to the artefact rather than to the deployer, they would stop being background and become a sixth door.17,18,113

The licensed-practitioner door does not require the tool to be right. It requires that someone accountable checks - and the sanctions record is a measurement of how often that check is skipped.

NINEWhat would falsify this

The framework makes claims that could be wrong, and it is worth being explicit about which.

The coding is contestable row by row, and three rows are genuinely arguable. Govtech is placed at D4 rather than D5 on the ground that a cloud authorisation admits a service to a class of buyer rather than to a market; someone could reasonably code it the other way, which would move govtech to R5 and change the headline count from six to seven.72 Insurtech's D5 rests on treating rate and form filing as an artefact authorisation, which is defensible when the filed rating plan is a model and strained when it is a table.62 Martech's R0 depends on treating messaging statutes as binding a sub-segment rather than the modal product;82 code it the other way and the control case disappears.

The framework would be substantially wrong if any of the following turned out to be true. If R5 verticals did not consolidate more than R1 verticals at comparable market size, the cadence mechanism would have no observable effect. If vertical software companies in R4 verticals were routinely acquired for their compliance apparatus rather than for their installed base, the claim that compliance is not a licence would be wrong. If horizontal platforms were seen to absorb R5 workloads at the same rate as R1 workloads, the artefact boundary would not be doing the work claimed for it. None of these is tested here; all are testable.

There is also a scope limit worth stating plainly. The coding is anchored on United States and European Union instruments, and permission structures are jurisdictional. A vertical that sits at R1 in one market can sit at R4 in another - health data localisation, insurance licensing, and vehicle type approval all vary enough to move a vertical two rungs. The claim is that the axis is the right one, not that any particular row is universal.

Finally, the framework says nothing about whether any of these doors should exist. Some of them plainly protect people from harms that software is well placed to cause; some of them plainly protect incumbents, which is the oldest finding in the regulation literature.9,149 Distinguishing the two requires an argument about each door that this article does not attempt. What it does claim is that if you want to understand why nineteen industries with almost nothing technical in common are described with the same suffix, the door is a better place to look than the domain.
