§0The question

A payment platform holds no money. What it holds are records: a row in its own ledger, a line in a processor's settlement report, a balance at a sponsor bank, an entry in a central bank's settlement account. Each record claims to describe the same value. They are written by different parties, at different times, under different rules, and for some interval after any movement they disagree. The architecture of a financial system is the set of decisions about which of those records is authoritative at each instant, how wide the interval of disagreement is allowed to become, and what happens to anyone who acts inside it.

That framing turns a list of sixteen familiar topics into one question asked sixteen times. Transaction processing, double-entry ledgers and balance management decide what counts as a record at all. Payment workflows, distributed transactions, idempotency and delivery guarantees decide how a record moves without being lost or duplicated on the way. Event sourcing, CQRS and auditability decide which records are primary and which are derived. Reconciliation and settlement decide when a record stops being one party's claim and becomes everybody's fact. Availability, recovery, scale and fault tolerance decide whether the record survives the machine that wrote it.

Each topic becomes a plate with the same four fields: the record the mechanism trusts, the divergence it admits, what closes that divergence, and how it fails. Each plate is placed on a six-rung finality ladder that asks one thing of a record: who, other than its writer, would have to agree before it could be wrong? Each also gets a posture (does the mechanism prevent divergence, detect it, or resolve it once it has happened?) and a time window over which it operates. The gauge on every plate draws that window on a logarithmic ruler from a hundred microseconds to a decade, so that where a mechanism lives in time is visible at a glance.

The census of those sixteen plates produces this article's headline finding. Every mechanism a team can build and run alone stops at the third rung. The twelve plates on rungs F1 to F3 operate over a median window of 0.77 seconds; the three on F4 and F5 operate over a median window of 6.9 hours, a gap of 4.51 decades. All eight mechanisms that prevent divergence sit on F1 to F3. Agreement and finality are not engineered inside a system; they are obtained from someone outside it, on calendar time. The internal architecture's real job is to make that wait safe: to keep every divergence bounded, attributable and detectable until an outside record closes it.

The evidence is a build-time laboratory of eleven deterministic experiments, written in standard-library Python with a fixed seed, whose output is embedded verbatim in the appendix. Every number quoted below comes from that output. The experiments are deliberately small. They are not benchmarks of any product; they are worked demonstrations of mechanisms whose behaviour is otherwise argued from intuition.

§1A ladder of finality

Accountants and engineers both use the word final, and they mean different things. To a database engineer a transaction is final once its commit record is on stable storage 1,2. To a distributed-systems engineer it is final once a majority of replicas has acknowledged it, because a minority can then fail without losing it 3,4. To a treasurer it is final when the counterparty's statement shows it. To a lawyer it is final when no insolvency administrator can unwind it 5. These are not competing definitions. They are rungs, and each one survives a strictly larger class of failure than the one below.

The ladder used throughout has six rungs. F0, in flight: the value exists only as a message that no store yet owns. F1, durable: one node has forced it to a log, so it survives a crash of that process but not the loss of that machine. F2, replicated: a quorum of copies has acknowledged it, so it survives the loss of any minority, which is the most that consensus can promise under the impossibility result of Fischer, Lynch and Paterson 6. F3, posted: it has been written as a balanced, immutable journal entry in the ledger of record, so it is not merely stored but accounted for, and any later change has to appear as another entry. F4, agreed: it has been matched against a record that someone else keeps independently, so an error in one party's systems shows up as a disagreement rather than as the truth. F5, final: it has been discharged in settlement money under a rule that the law will enforce against third parties, including a liquidator 5,7.

Two properties of the ladder matter more than its labels. First, the rungs are ordered by who must be wrong for the record to be wrong. At F1 one disk can betray it; at F2 a majority of machines; at F3 the ledger's own invariant; at F4 two independent parties at once; at F5 a legal system. Second, the first three rungs are inside the operator's control and the last two are not. A platform can buy more replicas or a stricter posting engine whenever it likes. It cannot reconcile against a statement that has not arrived, or settle in central bank money outside the hours the settlement system is open.

The posture axis is orthogonal. A mechanism that prevents divergence makes a bad state unrepresentable: atomic commit, a balanced-posting constraint, a conditional debit. One that detects divergence lets the bad state happen and guarantees it will be noticed: replay, reconciliation, a hash chain. One that resolves divergence decides, after the fact, which side wins: a payment state machine choosing a terminal state after a timeout, a settlement system declaring a transfer irrevocable, an operator declaring a disaster and failing over to a replica that is behind. The census in the figure below places all sixteen plates by rung and window. Every prevent plate sits on F1 to F3; detection reaches F4; only resolution touches both ends of the ladder.

The correlation between rung and the logarithm of a plate's window is r = 0.325, which is positive but loose, because payment workflows sit at F0 with a window of days. The cleaner statement is the split. Mechanisms the operator controls are fast because they only need agreement among the operator's own machines. Mechanisms that reach outside are slow because they wait for another institution's batch, cut-off or business day. Nothing in the engineering literature shortens that wait; only market structure does, which is why the move from T+2 to T+1 securities settlement in the United States in May 2024 required a federal rule rather than a software release 8, and why the European Union's equivalent applies from 11 October 2027 under Regulation (EU) 2025/2075 9.

One further discipline belongs before any plate: how amounts are represented. Binary floating point cannot represent most decimal fractions exactly, so sums of currency amounts drift 10. Production ledgers store signed integers in the currency's minor unit, whose exponent ISO 4217 fixes per currency (zero for the Japanese yen, two for the euro, three for the Kuwaiti dinar) 11, or they use the decimal formats that IEEE 754 has defined since its 2008 revision 12,13. The choice is not cosmetic. A ledger invariant that is only approximately zero cannot distinguish a rounding residue from a lost payment.

§2Transaction processing

The transaction is the oldest abstraction in this list and the one every other mechanism leans on. Gray's 1981 statement of the transaction concept named atomicity, consistency and durability as its properties 14; Haerder and Reuter added isolation and coined the acronym ACID two years later 1. The canonical workload that the field measured itself against was explicitly financial. The DebitCredit benchmark, written largely by Gray and published in Datamation on 1 April 1985 under the pseudonym Anon et al. on behalf of about two dozen practitioners, modelled a bank teller updating an account, a teller and a branch balance and appending a history record in one transaction 15,16. The vendor benchmark wars it set off led to the founding of the Transaction Processing Performance Council and its TPC-A benchmark, and Gray's retrospective twenty years later still called DebitCredit the standard measure of transaction processing 17.

The mechanism that makes atomicity and durability cheap is the write-ahead log. Changes are described in a log that is forced to stable storage before the transaction is reported committed; after a crash, the recovery manager redoes committed work and undoes uncommitted work from that log. ARIES gave the modern form, with log sequence numbers on pages, repeating history on restart and compensation log records for undo 2. The commit record is the plate's record of truth, and its failure mode is equally specific: an acknowledgement sent before the log write actually reached stable storage, whether through a misconfigured disk cache or a deliberately relaxed durability setting.

Isolation is where financial systems most often get hurt, because the anomalies are invisible in testing and lucrative in production. Berenson and colleagues showed in 1995 that the ANSI SQL isolation levels were defined ambiguously and that snapshot isolation, then widely deployed, permits write skew: two transactions each read an overlapping set, each make a decision that is valid on its own snapshot, and together violate an invariant neither wrote directly 18. Adya later gave implementation-independent definitions of the anomalies as cycles in a dependency graph 19. The gap between the names on the configuration screen and the guarantees delivered is still measurable: Jepsen's 2023 analysis of MySQL 8.0.34 found that its repeatable read level exhibits anomalies, lost update among them, that satisfy neither the ANSI reading of that level nor Adya's 20.

§3The double-entry ledger

Double-entry bookkeeping was codified by Luca Pacioli in 1494 as a section of a mathematics textbook 21; whether it mattered to the rise of commerce as much as Sombart later claimed is still argued 22. Its engineering virtue is easier to state than its history. Every movement is recorded as two or more entries that sum to zero, so the sum over every entry in the ledger is zero at all times. That one invariant converts a whole class of silent errors into loud ones.

The data model that follows from it has three nouns. An account is a named bucket with a currency and a normal balance side. A posting (journal entry) is the unit of change, carrying an effective time and an optional reference to a posting it reverses. Entries are the legs of a posting, one per account touched. A posting is accepted only if, for each currency it touches, its entries sum to zero, and only as a whole. Balances are never stored as the primary fact; they are sums of entries, cached for speed. Corrections never edit an entry; they post a reversal and a replacement, because the ledger's value as evidence depends on its history being append-only 23. A foreign-exchange transfer is two balanced postings, one per currency, linked through a position account that holds the firm's exposure.

-- A posting is accepted only as a whole, and only if it balances.
CREATE TABLE posting (
  posting_id   uuid PRIMARY KEY,
  effective_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
  reverses     uuid REFERENCES posting(posting_id)  -- corrections are new postings
);
CREATE TABLE entry (
  posting_id uuid   NOT NULL REFERENCES posting(posting_id),
  account_id bigint NOT NULL,
  currency   char(3) NOT NULL,            -- ISO 4217 alphabetic code
  amount     bigint NOT NULL,             -- signed, in minor units; never float
  PRIMARY KEY (posting_id, account_id, currency)
);
-- Enforced in the same transaction that inserts the entries:
--   for each (posting_id, currency): SUM(amount) = 0
-- and no UPDATE or DELETE privilege is granted on either table.

Experiment E1 measures what the invariant buys. It runs 100,000 random transfers among 1,000 accounts and crashes the writer between the two writes of a transfer with probability 0.001. In the first design the platform stores only balances and updates the payer and payee with two separate writes. In the second it stores journal legs but inserts them as separate, non-atomic rows. In the third it inserts each posting atomically.

All three designs see the same 109 crashes. The balances-only design ends the run 2,800,276 minor units short, and nothing in its data says which transfers tore: the balances are individually plausible and collectively wrong, and the shortfall is only visible to someone who already knows what the total should be. The non-atomic journal is just as broken, but its trial balance is -2,800,276, and grouping entries by posting identifies exactly 109 unbalanced postings, which are precisely the torn transfers. The atomic design's trial balance is 0. Double entry without atomicity does not prevent the failure; it localises it. Atomicity without double entry prevents the failure you anticipated and hides the ones you did not. The two together are what lift a record from F1 to F3.

§4Accounts and balances

A balance is a projection of postings, but customers and risk systems need more than one projection. The ledger balance is the sum of posted entries. The available balance subtracts open holds: card authorisations not yet cleared, payments initiated but not yet settled, funds frozen by an order. The distinction exists because payment schemes split the decision to pay from the movement of money. A card authorisation reserves funds in a request message, and the financial presentment that moves them may follow days later in a separate message or batch 24. A hold that never clears must expire, which is why this plate's window runs from milliseconds to about a week.

The invariant that matters to the customer is a floor: available balance must not go below zero, or below an agreed overdraft limit. Floors are where concurrency control earns its keep. Bailis and colleagues proved that an invariant of the form "greater than a constant" is not invariant-confluent under decrements: two replicas that each accept a withdrawal valid on their own state can merge into a state that violates the floor, so enforcing it requires coordination 25. Increments, by contrast, are confluent, which is why a conflict-free replicated counter converges but cannot enforce a lower bound 26. Warszawski and Bailis showed what happens when applications skip that coordination: in ACIDRain they found concurrency vulnerabilities in widely deployed e-commerce applications that let attackers overspend vouchers and stored credit by issuing requests in parallel, and they placed the 2014 losses at Bitcoin exchanges in the same class 27.

-- Read-check-write: two sessions can both read 100 and both pay 60.
SELECT available FROM account WHERE id = :a;          -- returns 100
-- application decides 100 >= 60, then:
UPDATE account SET available = :read_value - 60 WHERE id = :a;

-- Conditional debit: the check and the write are one statement.
UPDATE account
   SET available = available - 60
 WHERE id = :a AND available >= 60;                   -- 0 rows => decline

Experiment E3 isolates the race. An account holds 100. Each of n concurrent requests withdraws 60 using read-check-write, and the interleaving of their reads and writes is drawn at random, 200,000 times per n. With two requests the platform pays out more than the balance in 66.74% of trials, matching the exact value of two-thirds, since four of the six admissible orders put both reads before either write. With eight requests it overpays in 93.36% of trials, by an average of 148.35 per trial. In every overpaying trial the stored balance also disagrees with the total actually paid, because the last blind write overwrote the others. The conditional debit, which makes the check and the decrement one statement, permits exactly one success at every n.

Serialisable isolation, explicit row locks and conditional updates all close the race 28; they differ in what they cost under contention. A single heavily used balance such as a merchant's settlement account or a platform fee account becomes a hotspot that every posting must lock. Database designers met this problem in the 1980s. IMS Fast Path applied changes to frequently updated quantities as field calls verified at commit 29, and O'Neil's escrow method lets concurrent transactions reserve portions of a quantity against a bound without holding a lock across their whole duration 30. The modern equivalent, splitting one logical balance into several physical sub-balances, appears under scalability below.

§5Payment workflows

A payment is not a transaction; it is a conversation between institutions that may last from seconds to days, and its intermediate states are real states that customers see. A card payment passes through authorisation, capture, clearing and settlement, with side exits for decline, expiry, reversal, refund and dispute. A credit transfer passes through initiation, screening, submission to a clearing system, acceptance or rejection, and settlement. Instant schemes compress the sequence without removing it. The Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures defines fast payments by real-time or near-real-time transmission and availability of funds to the payee, on a near 24/7 basis 31; the Federal Reserve's FedNow service went live on 20 July 2023 32, and the European Union's Instant Payments Regulation requires euro payment providers to offer instant credit transfers with funds available within ten seconds 33.

The workflow's own state is the record of a payment in flight, which is why the plate sits on F0. The defining hazard is the ambiguous outcome. A request to a processor times out. The money may have moved, may not have moved, or may move later. Treating the timeout as a decline invites a duplicate when the customer retries; treating it as a success invites a loss when it was not. The correct state is a third one, unknown, with an explicit procedure to leave it: query the counterparty's status interface, wait for its asynchronous notification, or wait for the settlement report and let reconciliation decide. A workflow engine earns its place by making unknown a first-class state with a deadline rather than an exception.

Message standards determine how much a workflow can know. Card networks exchange ISO 8583 messages whose message type indicators distinguish authorisations, financial presentments and reversals 24. Interbank credit transfers increasingly use ISO 20022, whose structured remittance and party data exist largely to make downstream matching deterministic 34. On 22 November 2025 Swift ended the coexistence period for cross-border payment instructions, after which the old MT formats were no longer accepted for those flows; Swift reported that 97% of payment instructions were already being sent in ISO 20022 the day before its announcement, with a conversion service handling the remainder 35. Richer messages do not make a payment final any sooner. They make the later rungs cheaper to reach, because a reconciliation engine with a structured end-to-end reference matches on identity instead of guessing on amount and date.

§6Event sourcing and CQRS

Event sourcing stores the sequence of state changes as the primary record and derives current state by replaying it 36. Accounting reached this design five centuries before software did: the journal is the event log, balances are projections, and an auditor's first act is to recompute the projection from the log. What event sourcing adds is generality. Every aggregate, in the domain-driven design sense of a consistency boundary 37, keeps its own ordered stream; commands are validated against the aggregate's current state and, if accepted, emit events; events are never updated.

Command-query responsibility segregation separates the model that accepts writes from the models that answer reads 38,39. It generalises Meyer's command-query separation principle from methods to whole models 40. In a payment platform the write side is the posting engine and its aggregates; the read side is a set of projections, each built for one consumer: a customer's transaction history, a risk team's exposure view, a finance team's daily trial balance. A durable, partitioned log is the usual transport between them 41, and the distinction that matters is the one Kleppmann draws between a system of record and data derived from it, which can always be thrown away and recomputed 42.

The two patterns carry two different plates because they fail differently. Event sourcing detects divergence by construction: if a projection disagrees with a replay, the replay wins, and the disagreement is evidence. Its failure modes are about the log itself: an event rewritten in place destroys the evidence, and an event schema that later code cannot interpret makes the history unreplayable. CQRS admits a different divergence, the lag between a write and its appearance in a read model. That lag is harmless for display and dangerous for decisions. The rule that keeps it harmless is simple and frequently broken: any decision that guards an invariant, such as whether funds are available, must be taken against the write model, never against a projection.

Replay has a cost that grows with history. Experiment E4 models an account aggregate that receives 40 events a day and is cold-loaded 5 times a day, with a snapshot costing as much as applying 50 events. Without snapshots, a cold load after five years replays 73,000 events. With a snapshot every k events, the daily cost is the snapshot work plus the average replay since the last snapshot; the continuous optimum is k* = √(2·λw·S/λr) = 28.28, and an integer search confirms k = 28, at 138.9 event applications a day against 2000.0 for snapshotting every event and 2499.5 for snapshotting every thousand. The curve is flat near its minimum and steep at both ends: getting the interval roughly right is worth an order of magnitude, getting it exactly right is worth almost nothing.

§7Distributed transactions

When a payment touches two databases, two services or two institutions, atomicity no longer comes free. The classical answer is two-phase commit: a coordinator asks every participant to prepare, each forces a prepared record and votes, and the coordinator forces a decision and tells everyone 43,44. Its weakness was identified almost immediately. A participant that has voted to commit and then loses contact with the coordinator can neither commit nor abort on its own; it is blocked, holding locks, until the coordinator returns 45. Presumed-abort and presumed-commit variants reduced the logging and messaging cost 46, the X/Open XA interface standardised the participant contract 47, and Gray and Lamport later showed that running the decision through Paxos removes the single coordinator as a blocking point 48. Spanner runs two-phase commit across Paxos-replicated groups for exactly that reason 49; Calvin avoids it by agreeing on a global order of transactions before executing them deterministically 50.

Across organisational boundaries none of this applies, because no bank will hold locks on behalf of another company's coordinator. Helland's argument that scalable systems must give up distributed transactions in favour of entities that exchange messages, with each entity responsible for its own consistency and for tolerating duplicates, describes how payments actually work between institutions 51. The structured version is the saga: a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating transaction that semantically undoes it, run forward until it completes or backward until it is undone 52. In services, the transactional outbox pattern makes each saga step reliable by writing the outgoing message to a table in the same local transaction as the state change, for a relay to publish later 53.

Experiment E5 prices the trade. With each service available 99.9% of the time, an atomic commit that needs the coordinator and every participant up at once has availability 99.700% with two participants, 99.501% with four and 99.104% with eight, which is 4715 minutes a year of refusing payments against 526 for one service alone. A twenty-millisecond in-doubt window against a coordinator that fails once a month blocks 7.72 transactions per billion, which is negligible; the availability product is the real cost. A saga needs only the first service to accept a payment, but pays in another currency. If each step declines with probability 0.2%, a saga with eight steps compensates 1.392% of the time, and between the failed step and the completed compensation other transactions can see an intermediate state that no single-store design would ever expose.

§8Idempotency and delivery guarantees

Networks lose replies, so clients retry; brokers lose acknowledgements, so they redeliver. A financial system therefore receives every instruction at least once and sometimes more. Helland's formulation is the useful one: since messages may be repeated, every operation that changes state must be idempotent, or be made so by remembering which messages it has already processed 54. HTTP marks GET, PUT and DELETE as idempotent and POST as not 55, and creating a payment is a POST.

The standard remedy is a client-supplied idempotency key. Stripe's API stores the status code and body of the first request made with a key, including server errors, and returns them for any repeat; keys may be up to 255 characters, may be pruned once they are at least 24 hours old, and a key reused after pruning creates a new request 56. The IETF's HTTP APIs working group has been standardising the same mechanism as an Idempotency-Key request header, including a fingerprint of the request so that a reused key with a different body is rejected rather than silently answered; at its seventh revision in late 2025 it remained an Internet-Draft 57. The design detail that decides whether the mechanism works is transactional: the key and the effect must be written in the same transaction, or a crash between them recreates the duplicate the key was meant to prevent.

BEGIN;
INSERT INTO idempotency_key (key, fingerprint, created_at, state)
VALUES (:key, :sha256_of_request, now(), 'started')
ON CONFLICT (key) DO NOTHING;
-- 0 rows inserted: a prior attempt exists. Compare fingerprints;
-- return its stored response, or 409/422 if the body differs.
-- 1 row inserted: perform the effect *in this same transaction*,
-- then store the response against the key.
INSERT INTO posting ...;
UPDATE idempotency_key SET state = 'done', response = :resp WHERE key = :key;
COMMIT;

Retention is the other detail, and Experiment E2 quantifies it. Suppose 2% of a million payment requests end ambiguously and are retried with the same key, and that retries arrive in a mixture: 90% are automatic, 30 to 90 seconds after a timeout; 8% are a person pressing the button again, with a median delay of ten minutes; 2% come from offline clients or batch resubmission, with a median of eighteen hours. Without keys, all 20,000 retries duplicate. With a one-hour retention window 453 still do, at 24 hours 150.6, and at seven days 4.8. The tail is set by the slowest legitimate retrier, not the typical one: the 99.9th percentile retry in this mixture arrives after 93.3 hours. The price of a long window is storage. At a thousand requests a second, 24 hours of keys is 86,400,000 rows and seven days is 604,800,000. Retries themselves must be spaced with randomised backoff, or synchronised clients turn a brief outage into a sustained overload 58.

Exactly-once delivery is not available from any network, but exactly-once effect is, by combining at-least-once delivery with deduplication or with a sink that commits output and consumption together. Hohpe and Woolf named the receiving half the idempotent receiver 59. MillWheel achieved exactly-once processing at Google by checkpointing each record's identity with its output and discarding duplicates 60. Flink takes consistent distributed snapshots by flowing barriers through the dataflow, an adaptation of the Chandy-Lamport algorithm 61,62. Kafka added idempotent producers and transactions that commit output records and consumer offsets atomically 63. Every one of these guarantees ends at its own boundary. An email sent, a card network called or a bank file transmitted from inside a stream processor is outside the transaction and will be repeated on recovery unless the external call is itself idempotent.

§9Reconciliation

Reconciliation is the first rung that the operator cannot reach alone. It compares the firm's records with records that someone else keeps independently, such as a bank statement, a processor's settlement file or a custodian's position report, and it classifies every line as matched, matched with a tolerated difference, or a break that a person must investigate. Supervisors treat it as a control, not a convenience: the Basel Committee's principles for risk data aggregation expect banks to reconcile risk data with accounting and other sources 64.

Matching rules trade coverage against error, and Experiment E6 measures that trade on synthetic data with known ground truth. It generates 20,000 internal payments, about a third of them at common catalogue prices so that amounts collide, and a bank statement of 19,666 lines with realistic noise: settlement lags of one to three days, netted fees, missing references, payments that never reach the bank, 93 processor batch lines that each cover several payments, and 60 credits that nobody booked.

Exact matching on reference, amount and date matches 78.53% automatically and leaves 4,293 breaks. Allowing a three-day date window raises coverage to 92.75%, and tolerating a netted fee on lines whose reference already matches raises it to 94.58%, still with no wrong pairs, because the reference anchors identity. The next step is the tempting one. Falling back to amount and date for lines with no reference lifts coverage to 97.59%, and introduces 185 wrong pairs, 0.948% of all matches. A wrong pair is worse than a break. A break is a known unknown in a queue; a wrong pair is a closed item that hides two errors at once, the real counterpart of each side now orphaned or mismatched elsewhere.

The last row adds information instead of tolerance: batch lines are expanded using the processor's settlement report before the fallback runs. Coverage rises to 99.53%, breaks fall to 93, and wrong pairs fall to 80, because the fallback now has fewer unmatched candidates to confuse. The general lesson is that each loosening of a rule buys coverage with hidden error, while each additional authoritative field buys coverage with none. That is the practical case for structured references in ISO 20022 messages 34.

§10Settlement

Settlement discharges an obligation in an asset the payee accepts as final, usually central bank money. Until it happens, a payment is a claim on the payer's bank. Two designs dominate. Real-time gross settlement moves each payment individually and irrevocably as it is processed, which removes credit risk between banks but demands intraday liquidity 65. Deferred net settlement accumulates obligations and settles only each participant's net position at the end of a cycle, which economises on liquidity but leaves everyone exposed until the cycle completes 66.

The exposure is not theoretical. On 26 June 1974 German authorities closed Bankhaus Herstatt at the end of the German business day, after some counterparties had paid it Deutschmarks but before it had paid out the dollars it owed; the disruption led the G10 central bank governors to establish what became the Basel Committee 67. Foreign-exchange settlement risk, which the central banks' 1996 report analysed at length, is still called Herstatt risk 68. The legal answer in Europe is the Settlement Finality Directive, which makes transfer orders entered into a designated system enforceable and protects them from being unwound under insolvency law 5. The international standard is the Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures, whose eighth principle requires final settlement no later than the end of the value date, and intraday or real-time settlement where necessary 7.

Experiment E7 compares the liquidity each design needs. Twelve banks of unequal size exchange 20,000 payments with a total gross value of 3,563,674,125. Bilateral netting would settle 14.37% of gross. Real-time gross settlement, if every bank can recycle incoming payments to fund outgoing ones in arrival order, needs liquidity equal to the sum of each bank's peak cumulative net debit: 9.38% of gross. Multilateral netting needs only the sum of final net debit positions, 6.97% of gross. The difference between the last two is the liquidity price of finality during the day, and in practice it is larger, because payments do not arrive in a convenient order and banks delay outgoing payments while they wait for incoming ones, which Bech and Garratt model as a game 69.

Netting has a different price, which the same experiment also shows. If the largest net debtor fails before settlement and its payments are removed from the cycle, the net positions of 10 of the 11 surviving banks get worse, 4 switch between creditor and debtor, and the largest single swing equals 18.73% of the original net settlement total. Humphrey's simulations of unwinding on the US CHIPS system in the 1980s raised exactly this concern 70, later studies of other netting systems found such cascades rarer than feared but not absent 71, and the Lamfalussy minimum standards answered it by requiring that a netting system be able to complete settlement even if the participant with the largest single net debit cannot settle 66.

§11High availability

Availability for a ledger means continuing to accept and post payments while machines fail, without ever accepting two conflicting histories. Consensus protocols deliver that combination by replicating a log to a group and committing each entry once a majority has stored it; Paxos is the classical form 3, Raft the form most widely implemented today 4, and the gap between the published protocol and a production system is wide enough that Google's account of building one is still required reading 72. The CAP theorem, conjectured by Brewer and proved by Gilbert and Lynch, says that during a network partition a system must give up either consistency or availability 73,74; Abadi's PACELC refinement adds that even without a partition, replication trades consistency against latency 75.

Eventually consistent stores choose availability and reconcile later. Dynamo did so for shopping carts, merging divergent versions on read 76,77. That is the wrong choice for a balance with a floor, for the confluence reason given earlier, and the systems literature warned about it long before microservices: Gray, Helland, O'Neil and Shasha showed that update-anywhere replication becomes unstable as it scales, with a tenfold increase in nodes and traffic producing roughly a thousandfold increase in deadlocks or reconciliations 78. A ledger can accept eventual consistency in its read models and its analytics. Its posting path needs a single leader per partition, a quorum behind it, and fencing so that a deposed leader cannot keep writing.

Experiment E8 shows why the quorum is worth its cost. With each node available 99.5% of the time, one node is unavailable 2,629.8 minutes a year; a three-node majority quorum is unavailable 39.32 minutes; five nodes, 0.65. The improvement comes from requiring a majority rather than all members, the inverse of the two-phase commit product in E5.

§12Disaster recovery

Disaster recovery is high availability across a failure domain large enough to take out the whole quorum: a region, a data centre campus, a cloud provider's control plane. Its vocabulary is two numbers. The recovery time objective is how long the service may be down; the recovery point objective is how much committed work may be lost 79,80. Financial supervisors set the first explicitly. The 2003 interagency sound-practices paper asked core US clearing and settlement organisations to recover and resume within the business day, with a two-hour target 81; the PFMI expects critical systems to resume operations within two hours following disruptive events and settlement to complete by the end of the day 7. More recent regimes frame the same idea as tolerances for each important business service: the Basel Committee's 2021 principles for operational resilience 82, the Prudential Regulation Authority's impact tolerances, which firms had to be able to meet by 31 March 2025 83, and the EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act, applicable since 17 January 2025 84.

The recovery point is where disaster recovery stops being an infrastructure topic and becomes a ledger topic. Synchronous replication to another region loses nothing and adds a cross-region round trip to every commit. Asynchronous replication adds nothing to commit latency and loses whatever the replica had not received. Experiment E8 models a ledger committing 2000 transactions a second with a lognormal replication lag of median 40 milliseconds: a failover loses 120.8 committed transactions on average, 655.8 at the 99th percentile and 1245.8 at the 99.9th. Those are payments that customers were told had succeeded.

The architecture's answer cannot be to pretend they do not exist. The recovered region resumes from a state that is behind; the lost transactions are still recorded in the counterparties' systems, in card networks, in bank files and in the customers' own records; and reconciliation against those F4 records is how they are found and reposted. A disaster recovery plan that does not include a reconciliation step before resuming outbound payments resumes from a state that nobody has agreed is correct. Recent history supplies the cautionary cases. The Bank of England commissioned an independent review after its RTGS system suffered an outage on 20 October 2014 85. TSB's April 2018 migration to a new platform failed badly enough to prompt an independent review 86 and fines from the FCA and PRA totalling £48.65 million in December 2022 87.

§13Scalability

Ledgers scale by partitioning, usually by account, so that most postings touch one partition and run as local transactions. The difficulty is that money moves between accounts, so a share of postings always spans partitions and needs one of the cross-boundary techniques from the distributed transactions section, and some accounts are touched by a large share of all postings. A platform's own fee account, a large merchant's settlement account or an omnibus account at a sponsor bank can each sit on one side of a large fraction of every posting.

Queueing theory makes the consequence exact. Little's law relates the number of requests in a system to their arrival rate and time in system 88; for a single server with random arrivals and service, mean time in system is the service time divided by one minus utilisation 89. Experiment E9 gives one row a lock-hold time of 0.5 milliseconds per posting, offers the platform 30,000 postings a second, and sends 5% of them to the same account. That row runs at 75% utilisation, with a mean of 2.0 milliseconds and a 99th percentile of 9.21, and it caps the whole platform at 40,000 postings a second however many other partitions are added. A discrete-event simulation of the same queue gives a mean of 2.004 milliseconds, agreeing with the formula. Splitting the account into sixteen sub-balances that are summed on read cuts the 99th percentile to 2.416 milliseconds and raises the ceiling to 640,000. This is Amdahl's argument restated for ledgers: the serial fraction, not the parallel capacity, sets the limit 90,91.

Splitting a balance is not free. A floor that applied to one row now applies to a sum of rows, and enforcing it requires either reading all sub-balances under a lock or allocating each sub-balance its own share of headroom, which is O'Neil's escrow method in modern form 30. The design question is always which invariant is being given up or moved, never just how many partitions to add.

§14Fault tolerance

Fault tolerance is the umbrella under which partial failure is handled: a dependency that is slow rather than dead, a disk that returns errors on one block, a network that drops one direction. Distributed systems cannot reliably distinguish a slow component from a failed one 6, so every remote call needs a timeout, every timeout creates an ambiguous outcome, and every ambiguous outcome needs the idempotency machinery of the previous sections. At scale the slow case dominates. Dean and Barroso showed that when one request fans out to many servers, rare slow responses from individual servers become the common case for the request as a whole, and they described hedged and tied requests as remedies 92.

The remedies have their own failure mode. Retries multiply load exactly when a dependency is struggling, which is why they must be bounded, spaced by randomised backoff and stopped by circuit breakers 58,93. Fault injection in production, which Netflix formalised as chaos engineering, is the only reliable way to learn which of these assumptions actually hold 94. Faults also arrive through change rather than hardware. On 1 August 2012 Knight Capital deployed new trading code to seven of its eight servers; a repurposed flag activated obsolete code on the eighth, which sent millions of orders in about 45 minutes and cost the firm more than $460 million, and the SEC's order turned on the absence of controls to detect and stop the error 95. The fault was a single stale deployment; the loss was the absence of any check against an independent record of what the system was supposed to be doing.

Byzantine faults, in which components lie rather than stop, are usually treated as out of scope inside one operator's systems 96. Public blockchains are the case where they are in scope: Bitcoin's design makes every participant a potential witness, so a record reaches the agreed rung by being replicated to parties who do not trust each other 97. That is one way of buying F4. It is expensive, and it does not by itself buy F5, because legal finality depends on rules outside the ledger.

§15Auditability

An audit trail must let an outsider answer three questions about any balance: what happened, in what order, and whether anyone has changed the record since. The first two are answered by an append-only journal with stable identifiers, the posting model already described; order has to come from that journal's sequence rather than from wall clocks, because clocks on different machines disagree and only causal relationships between events can be ordered reliably 98. The third needs cryptography. Haber and Stornetta showed in 1991 that linking each timestamped record to the hash of its predecessor makes backdating detectable 99; Schneier and Kelsey designed audit logs whose earlier entries remain protected even if the logging machine is later compromised 100; Merkle trees allow a single entry's inclusion to be proved with a number of hashes logarithmic in the log's size 101, and Crosby and Wallach combined the two into tamper-evident history trees with efficient proofs of both inclusion and consistency 102. Certificate Transparency runs this design at internet scale for TLS certificates 103.

Experiment E10 shows where the guarantee actually comes from. It chains 100,000 entries with SHA-256 104. If one entry is edited and the stored hashes are left alone, verification fails at that entry, number 42,000. But an insider with write access can simply recompute the 58,000 hashes that follow, and the chain then verifies perfectly against itself. A hash chain proves nothing that only its owner can see. If the head is published outside the operator's control every 1,000 entries, the same rewrite breaks the first published head after it, at entry 42,999, so the undetectable window shrinks to at most 1,000 entries. A Merkle proof of one entry among 100,000 needs 17 hashes.

That is why the plate sits on F4. Tamper evidence is agreement with an external witness, in the same sense that reconciliation is agreement with an external statement. Regulators impose the retention and integrity requirements that define the plate's long window. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act's section 404 requires management to assess internal control over financial reporting 105. The SEC's 2022 amendments to its broker-dealer recordkeeping rule allow an audit-trail alternative to the older write-once requirement 106. PCI DSS requirement 10 expects audit logs to be retained for at least twelve months with the most recent three immediately available 107.

Three conclusions follow, each stated as narrowly as the evidence allows.

First, prevention is cheap only where the operator owns every record involved. Atomic posting, conditional debits and idempotency keys all work by making two writes one write inside a single transactional boundary. The experiments that tested prevent mechanisms found them complete inside their boundary: atomic posting left a trial balance of exactly zero (E1), and the conditional debit admitted exactly one success at every level of concurrency (E3). E2 shows what lies at the edge of such a boundary, here a boundary in time: the idempotency key stops every duplicate except the ones that arrive after it has been pruned. In the same way, the atomic posting cannot see a processor that disagrees, and the conditional debit cannot see a hold placed in another system.

Second, detection is what the architecture owes the outside rungs. Because F4 and F5 arrive hours or days later, the internal design must guarantee that every divergence that survives until then can still be found and attributed. Double entry localises torn postings (E1), replay exposes divergent projections at a cost that snapshots keep bounded (E4), structured references make reconciliation decisive rather than probabilistic (E6), and external anchoring turns a private hash chain into evidence (E10). Systems that skip detection do not avoid divergence; they discover it later and with less to go on.

Third, resolution should be explicit, scheduled and rare. The three resolve plates are the payment workflow's terminal states, settlement's finality rule and disaster recovery's declared failover. Each one decides which record wins when they disagree. Each is safer when the decision is written down in advance as a state, a rule or a runbook than when it is made under pressure by whoever is on call.

The ladder has limits that should be stated plainly. Its rungs are ordinal, so a mean rung is a summary rather than a measurement. Its placements are judgements about each mechanism's primary record, and several mechanisms span rungs, such as payment workflows, which begin at F0 and end at F5. Its time windows are typical ranges, not specifications. The experiments are synthetic, with parameters chosen to be plausible rather than fitted to any institution's data, and they demonstrate mechanisms rather than measure products. None of that weakens the main observation, which survives any reasonable recoding: a system can make itself internally consistent on its own, but it can only become correct by agreeing with someone else.

def e1():
    head("E1", "Torn transfers: balances-only vs non-atomic legs vs atomic posting")
    rng = random.Random(SEED + 1)
    NA, N, P_CRASH, START = 1000, 100_000, 0.001, 1_000_000
    draws = []
    for i in range(N):
        a = rng.randrange(NA)
        b = rng.randrange(NA - 1)
        b = b + 1 if b >= a else b
        amt = rng.randint(1, 50_000)
        crash = rng.random() < P_CRASH
        draws.append((i, a, b, amt, crash))
    # A: balances only, two independent writes
    bal = [START] * NA
    torn_a = 0
    for i, a, b, amt, crash in draws:
        bal[a] -= amt
        if crash:
            torn_a += 1
            continue
        bal[b] += amt
    missing_a = NA * START - sum(bal)
    # B: journal legs as separate rows, non-atomic
    legs = []
    for i, a, b, amt, crash in draws:
        legs.append((i, a, -amt))
        if crash:
            continue
        legs.append((i, b, +amt))
    trial = sum(l[2] for l in legs)
    per_tx = {}
    for i, acct, amt in legs:
        per_tx[i] = per_tx.get(i, 0) + amt
    unbalanced = sorted(i for i, s in per_tx.items() if s != 0)
    true_torn = sorted(i for i, a, b, amt, crash in draws if crash)
    # C: atomic posting: either both legs or neither
    legs_c = 0
    tot_c = 0
    for i, a, b, amt, crash in draws:
        if crash:
            continue  # posting rolled back as a unit
        legs_c += 2
        tot_c += (-amt + amt)
    say(f"  transfers {N:,}, accounts {NA:,}, crash probability between writes {P_CRASH}")
    say(f"  A balances-only : torn {torn_a}, money missing {missing_a:,} minor units, "
        f"locatable from balances alone: NO")
    say(f"  B non-atomic legs: trial balance {trial:,}; unbalanced postings found {len(unbalanced)}; "
        f"identical to true torn set: {unbalanced == true_torn}")
    say(f"  C atomic posting : trial balance {tot_c}; rolled back {len(true_torn)} postings, none torn")
    OUT["E1"] = dict(N=N, NA=NA, p=P_CRASH, torn=torn_a, missing=missing_a,
                     trial_b=trial, found_b=len(unbalanced),
                     exact_b=unbalanced == true_torn, trial_c=tot_c,
                     mean_missing=round(missing_a / max(torn_a, 1)))

def e2():
    head("E2", "Idempotency keys: duplicates that leak past the key's retention")
    rng = random.Random(SEED + 2)
    S = 400_000
    delays = []
    kinds = [0, 0, 0]
    for _ in range(S):
        u = rng.random()
        if u < 0.90:           # automatic retry after a 30 s timeout, jittered
            d = 30.0 + rng.uniform(0.0, 60.0); kinds[0] += 1
        elif u < 0.98:         # a person presses the button again
            d = rng.lognormvariate(math.log(600.0), 1.0); kinds[1] += 1
        else:                  # offline client or batch resubmits later
            d = rng.lognormvariate(math.log(18 * 3600.0), 1.0); kinds[2] += 1
        delays.append(d)
    delays.sort()
    REQ, AMBIG = 1_000_000, 0.02
    ttls = [("1 min", 60), ("1 h", 3600), ("6 h", 21600), ("24 h", 86400),
            ("72 h", 259200), ("7 d", 604800)]
    rows = []
    for lab, t in ttls:
        # count delays strictly greater than t
        lo, hi = 0, S
        while lo < hi:
            mid = (lo + hi) // 2
            if delays[mid] <= t:
                lo = mid + 1
            else:
                hi = mid
        frac = (S - lo) / S
        dup = REQ * AMBIG * frac
        rows.append(dict(ttl=lab, sec=t, frac=frac, dup_per_m=round(dup, 1)))
        say(f"  TTL {lab:>5}: {pct(frac, 3):7.3f}% of retries arrive after expiry "
            f"-> {dup:8.1f} duplicate effects per million requests")
    nokey = REQ * AMBIG
    say(f"  no key at all: {nokey:,.0f} duplicate effects per million requests")
    rate = 1000
    keys = {lab: rate * t for lab, t in ttls}
    say(f"  key-store size at {rate:,} req/s: 24 h -> {keys['24 h']:,} keys; 7 d -> {keys['7 d']:,}")
    p999 = delays[int(0.999 * S)]
    say(f"  99.9th percentile retry delay = {p999 / 3600:.1f} h")
    OUT["E2"] = dict(rows=rows, nokey=nokey, keys24=keys["24 h"], keys7=keys["7 d"],
                     p999_h=round(p999 / 3600, 1), ambig=AMBIG, mix=[0.90, 0.08, 0.02])

def e3():
    head("E3", "Concurrent withdrawals: read-check-write vs conditional debit")
    rng = random.Random(SEED + 3)
    BAL, W, T = 100, 60, 200_000
    rows = []
    for n in range(2, 9):
        over = 0
        excess = 0
        lost = 0
        for _ in range(T):
            toks = []
            for r in range(n):
                toks += [r, r]
            rng.shuffle(toks)
            seen = [0] * n
            readv = [0] * n
            bal = BAL
            paid = 0
            for r in toks:
                if seen[r] == 0:
                    readv[r] = bal; seen[r] = 1
                else:
                    if readv[r] >= W:
                        bal = readv[r] - W  # blind write of a stale value
                        paid += W
            if paid > BAL:
                over += 1
                excess += paid - BAL
            if bal != BAL - paid:
                lost += 1
        rows.append(dict(n=n, p_over=over / T, mean_excess=excess / T,
                         p_lost=lost / T))
        say(f"  n={n}: P(paid out more than the balance) = {pct(over / T):6.2f}%, "
            f"mean excess {excess / T:6.2f}, ledger disagrees with payouts {pct(lost / T):6.2f}%")
    say("  conditional debit (UPDATE ... WHERE balance >= 60): exactly 1 success, overpay 0.00% at every n")
    OUT["E3"] = dict(rows=rows, bal=BAL, w=W, T=T)

def e4():
    head("E4", "Event sourcing: how often to snapshot an aggregate")
    LW, LR, S = 40.0, 5.0, 50.0       # events/day, cold loads/day, snapshot cost
    def cost(k):
        return LW * S / k + LR * (k - 1) / 2.0
    best = min(range(1, 2001), key=lambda k: (cost(k), k))
    kstar = math.sqrt(2 * LW * S / LR)
    years = 5
    nosnap = LW * 365 * years
    say(f"  writes {LW:.0f}/day, cold loads {LR:.0f}/day, snapshot costs {S:.0f} event applications")
    say(f"  continuous optimum k* = sqrt(2*LW*S/LR) = {kstar:.2f}; integer search optimum k = {best}")
    say(f"  cost/day at k={best}: {cost(best):.1f}; at k=1: {cost(1):.1f}; at k=1000: {cost(1000):.1f}")
    say(f"  with no snapshots, one cold load after {years} years replays {nosnap:,.0f} events")

def e7():
    head("E7", "Settlement: gross, real-time gross with recycling, and multilateral net")
    rng = random.Random(SEED + 7)
    B, N = 12, 20_000
    w = [1.0 / (i + 1) ** 0.8 for i in range(B)]
    pays = []
    for _ in range(N):
        s = rng.choices(range(B), w)[0]
        r = rng.choices(range(B), w)[0]
        while r == s:
            r = rng.choices(range(B), w)[0]
        amt = int(rng.lognormvariate(math.log(50_000), 1.6)) + 1
        pays.append((s, r, amt))
    gross = sum(p[2] for p in pays)
    pair = {}
    for s, r, a in pays:
        k = (min(s, r), max(s, r))
        pair[k] = pair.get(k, 0) + (a if s < r else -a)
    bilat = sum(abs(v) for k, v in sorted(pair.items()))
    pos = [0] * B
    for s, r, a in pays:
        pos[s] -= a; pos[r] += a
    multi = sum(-p for p in pos if p < 0)
    # RTGS: each bank's liquidity need is its peak cumulative net debit
    cum = [0] * B
    peak = [0] * B
    for s, r, a in pays:
        cum[s] -= a; cum[r] += a
        if -cum[s] > peak[s]:
            peak[s] = -cum[s]
    rtgs = sum(peak)
    say(f"  {B} banks, {N:,} payments, gross value {gross:,}")
    say(f"  bilateral net     {bilat:,} ({pct(bilat / gross)}% of gross)")
    say(f"  RTGS peak need    {rtgs:,} ({pct(rtgs / gross)}% of gross) with perfect recycling, arrival order")
    say(f"  multilateral net  {multi:,} ({pct(multi / gross)}% of gross)")
    # unwind: largest net debtor fails, its payments are removed
    f = min(range(B), key=lambda i: (pos[i], i))
    pos2 = [0] * B
    for s, r, a in pays:
        if s == f or r == f:
            continue
        pos2[s] -= a; pos2[r] += a

def e10():
    head("E10", "Tamper evidence: a hash chain proves nothing its owner alone can see")
    rng = random.Random(SEED + 10)
    N, T, ANCH = 100_000, 42_000, 1_000
    entries = [f"{i}|A{rng.randrange(10_000):05d}|{rng.randint(-500_000, 500_000)}" for i in range(N)]
    def chain(es):
        h = b"\x00" * 32
        out = []
        for e in es:
            h = hashlib.sha256(h + e.encode()).digest()
            out.append(h)
        return out
    good = chain(entries)
    anchors = {i: good[i] for i in range(ANCH - 1, N, ANCH)}
    tam = list(entries)
    tam[T] = tam[T].rsplit("|", 1)[0] + "|1"
    # naive tamper: verifier recomputes against stored hashes
    bad = chain(tam)
    first = next(i for i in range(N) if bad[i] != good[i])
    # insider rewrite: hashes recomputed forward, chain internally consistent
    recomputed = N - T
    consistent = True
    # with anchors: first anchor at or after T no longer matches
    hit = next(i for i in sorted(anchors) if i >= T and bad[i] != anchors[i])
    window = hit - T + 1
    proof = math.ceil(math.log2(N))
    say(f"  {N:,} entries chained with SHA-256; head {good[-1].hex()[:16]}...")
    say(f"  edit entry {T:,} and leave stored hashes: first mismatch at {first:,} (detected)")
    say(f"  insider recomputes {recomputed:,} hashes forward: chain verifies cleanly against itself")
    say(f"  with the head published every {ANCH:,} entries: the rewrite breaks the anchor at {hit:,} "
        f"-- the undetectable window is at most {ANCH:,} entries")
    say(f"  Merkle inclusion proof for one entry among {N:,}: {proof} hashes, vs {N:,} for a linear re-check")
    OUT["E10"] = dict(N=N, T=T, ANCH=ANCH, first=first, recomputed=recomputed, hit=hit,
                      window=window, proof=proof, head=good[-1].hex()[:16])
