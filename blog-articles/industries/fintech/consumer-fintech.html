The question this article asks

Every product in the brief does one thing to its user before it does anything else: it changes how long, how many steps and how much attention a financial action takes. A budgeting app inserts a step before spending. A pay-in-four button deletes five steps before borrowing. A payroll default deletes the step of saving altogether. An instant-payment rail deletes the wait between deciding to send money and the money being gone. The industry calls this friction, and much of its commercial history is a history of removing it, which is also where most of its measurable gains come from: cheaper intermediation, wider access, faster settlement 1.

The question asked here of each product is narrower than whether it is innovative or inclusive. It is when the action it makes easier pays off, and when it costs. Banks call the date on which money actually moves a value date. Every financial action has two: one for its benefit and one for its cost. Saving has its cost today and its benefit later. Borrowing has its benefit today and its cost later. A payment, an aggregation screen or a remittance has both on the same day. The organising claim is that this one property, which side of today the cost falls on, predicts three things about a consumer-fintech product better than the heading it is filed under: whether making it faster helps or harms the person using it, how its provider is paid, and what its failure looks like.

The reason timing matters is the best-replicated regularity in household finance. People weigh the present against every later date differently from how they weigh any two later dates against each other. The quasi-hyperbolic or beta–delta model captures it with one extra parameter: everything that is not now is multiplied by a factor beta below one before ordinary exponential discounting applies 2,3. Present-biased individuals carry larger credit-card balances 4. There is a caveat that the rest of the article keeps: experiments that pay money at different dates find much weaker present bias than experiments over effort or consumption 5,6. Beta here is a property of consuming and of exerting effort, not of receiving cheques, which is the right object for products that sell purchases, trades, set-up chores and saving.

The consequence for interface design is direct. An instant, one-tap interface hands the decision to whoever is present when the button is pressed. A schedule, a default or a delay hands it to whoever set the schedule, which is the core of choice architecture 7, when both legs of the action were still in the future and beta cancelled out of the comparison. Fintech's characteristic move is to make things instant. For actions whose cost lands later that move gives the decision to the self who will not pay; for actions whose cost lands now it does the opposite, and the industry's tool for those is not speed but automation. For actions whose legs coincide, speed is close to free. Thirty-six products, coded on that property and two others, carry the argument. A runnable laboratory of nine experiments supplies every number quoted from it, tagged E1 to E9.

A ladder of value dates

Each product is coded on a six-rung VALUE-DATE LADDER by the action it exists to make happen: the action the provider is paid for where there is one, otherwise the action its interface puts first. The coding rule matters because several products display one thing and earn on another. A free credit-score screen shows information but is paid when a user applies for a loan beside it, so it is coded on the loan.

The three benefit-first rungs are B3 (cost a year or more away), B2 (weeks to months) and B1 (days). S0 has both legs on the same day. The cost-first rungs are C1 (benefit within weeks to months) and C2 (benefit years away). Two further axes are coded on every plate. FRICTION records what the product does to the coded action: E eases it (one tap, instant), A automates it (decided once, executed on a schedule) or I impedes it. REVENUE records how the provider is paid: U per use, B per balance held over time, or F by a flat fee or a third party. A verdict follows mechanically. Easing or automating a C-rung action, or impeding a B-rung one, is correcting; easing a B-rung action is amplifying; anything on S0 is neutral with respect to timing.

E1 makes the verdict quantitative. With beta 0.7 and annual delta 0.96, a benefit-first action whose later leg is a unit cost feels better than it is by a distortion d, and a cost-first action feels worse by the same construction. Let the long-run net value of each potential action be v. Instant execution takes the action when v + d > 0; planned execution takes it when v > 0. At rung B2 the distortion is +0.298: 18.98% of the actions an instant interface triggers are ones the planner would have declined. At C1 it is -0.298, and 23.43% of the actions the planner endorses are skipped when the choice is left to the moment. At S0 both shares are exactly zero. The C2 rung is the exception that shows the arithmetic: a benefit twenty years away is discounted so heavily by delta that the extra beta penalty is only -0.133, and 10.55% of endorsed actions are skipped.

Those shares are computed on a deliberately wide pool of candidate actions, v drawn from a standard normal. The pool matters more than beta. For impulse purchases most candidate actions are mildly not worth taking; with v centred at -0.1 and a spread of 0.25, the regretted share of instant B2 takers rises to 56.15%. Beta's effect is monotone and large: the B2 wrong-way share is 27.57% at beta 0.5 and 7.33% at 0.9. The distortion meter drawn on every plate is the E1 d for that plate's rung. It answers one question at a glance: does this action look better or worse at the moment of choice than it does from a distance?

Three limits apply throughout. The ladder ranks timing, not magnitude: a large, clearly worthwhile loan is still B3. It assumes the agent is naive enough to be exploited by instant execution, which is the empirically common case for the products concerned but not universal 3. And it treats the welfare criterion as the long-run self's, a choice behavioural welfare economics defends but does not settle 8.

Where a balance lives

Before any plate, a structural fact. The number a consumer-fintech app shows is almost never a balance on that firm's books in the sense a bank deposit is. It is a line in a ledger the app or a middleware firm keeps, which refers to a share of an omnibus account at a sponsor bank, which is itself a claim on that bank, which settles with other banks in central-bank money. Each layer has its own failure mode and its own protection.

Deposit insurance reaches the end user through this stack only if the records at the top identify each beneficial owner and the bank's account is titled as custodial 9. Stored-value balances in payment apps may not be deposits at all 11. Card wallets add a token layer: EMV tokenisation replaces the card number with a device-bound token and cryptogram, so a stolen phone number or merchant database yields nothing reusable 12. The data layer runs alongside. Aggregators historically read account data with users' passwords; open-banking regimes replace that with permissioned interfaces, in Europe since the second Payment Services Directive 13 and in the US under a Section 1033 rule finalised in October 2024 that would have made data access free to consumers and their agents 14. A federal court enjoined enforcement before the April 2026 first compliance date 15, and a reconsideration went to OIRA review on 6 August 2026 16,17. The right exists on paper and is not in force.

The rails underneath set what instant means. India's UPI cleared 24.51 billion transactions in August 2026 18 and Brazil's Pix 7.37 billion 19; Pix passed 63.8 billion transactions in 2024, more than all other Brazilian payment methods combined 20. The US FedNow service, live since July 2023, settled about 1.67 million payments a month in the second quarter of 2026 at an average of $54,957 21 across more than 1,800 participating institutions 22. The volume ratio is 14,712 to one for UPI and 4,422 to one for Pix (E9). The difference is not technology. Pix was designed by its central bank as public infrastructure with mandatory participation for large institutions and near-zero prices to users 23; FedNow competes for institutions' business and so far carries large business payments rather than consumers' coffee.

Instant and final is a value-date statement: the payer's cost and the payee's receipt land together, and nothing can be clawed back. That makes the S0 rung the home of authorised push-payment fraud, because the payer's error cannot be undone. In the US, Regulation E gives consumers error-resolution rights for unauthorised electronic transfers and for remittance errors 24, but a transfer the consumer was tricked into authorising is not unauthorised. The UK's answer was not to slow payments but to move the loss: from 7 October 2024 sending and receiving firms split reimbursement of APP scams up to £85,000 25. In its first year 88% of in-scope losses, £173 million, was reimbursed 26, and an independent evaluation estimated in-scope losses about 21% lower than in 2023 25. The EU took the complementary route of a payee-name check for euro instant transfers 27.

Personal finance and budgeting

Personal-finance tools sell information, and information sits on S0: looking at one's balances costs a moment and pays immediately. What separates them is what they are paid for. An aggregator funded by referral fees is paid when the user takes up a card or loan offer shown beside the balances; a budgeting tool funded by subscription is paid whether the user spends or not. That is the first appearance of the pattern the census confirms: the products that ease benefit-first actions are paid per action, and the products that ease cost-first actions are paid a flat fee. DellaVigna and Malmendier derived exactly this structure for firms selling to present-biased customers: investment goods are priced with low per-use charges and high flat fees, leisure goods the reverse 28,29.

Budgeting is the purest correcting product in the brief. Its whole function is to insert a C1 action, assigning income to purposes, before the B-rung action of spending, and to make the mental accounts people already keep explicit 30. Its evidence base is mostly about attention. Salience shocks reduce overdraft fees 31 and reminders raise saving 32, but attention is reallocated rather than created: card-payment reminders cut card late fees partly by pushing overdrafts up elsewhere 33. A budgeting product that sends ten alerts is spending a budget of its own.

Savings

Saving is the cost-first action by definition, and it is where the ladder's prescription is least controversial: do not make saving instant, make it automatic. The evidence is among the strongest in economics. Automatic enrolment raised participation for new hires from 37% to 86% 36. Pre-committing future raises to saving took average saving rates from 3.5% to 13.6% over 40 months 37. Commitment products that lock balances found demand concentrated among people who are present-biased 38. And automatic enrolment did not trigger offsetting unsecured borrowing in the one setting where it could be measured 39.

E2 separates two reasons defaults work. A population of which 85% is better off saving faces a set-up chore costing a median of twelve months' benefit, attends to the decision in 10% of months, and procrastinates naively. Without present bias, opt-in participation reaches 48.2% at twelve months; with beta 0.7 it reaches 41.4%, and 62.8% after two years. The default holds 92.5% at twelve months. Present bias explains a few points of the gap; inattention and the chore explain most of it, which is why a default beats a reminder. The default has a cost the opt-in path does not: 4.0% of the whole population, people who would be better off out, are still enrolled after two years because opting out is a chore too. That is the case for active-choice designs where needs are heterogeneous 40.

Automation fixes the direction; the price can still be wrong. Round-up products are a clean case. E7 computes the expected round-up as $0.495 a purchase, so forty card purchases a month save $237.60 a year. A $3 monthly fee is 15.2% of that year's contributions and 32.6% of the first year's average balance, falling to 3.6% by year five. A flat fee is a regressive price on exactly the users a micro-saving product attracts.

Emergency saving sits at C1, where the benefit is a shock absorbed within months, and it is where the US now legislates: SECURE 2.0 created pension-linked emergency savings accounts capped at $2,500, indexed 41. The size of the problem is measured annually; in the Federal Reserve's 2024 survey 63% of adults would cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent 42.

Investing

Investing appears on both sides of the ladder, and that is the most important thing to know about it. Holding a diversified portfolio and contributing to it on a schedule is a C2 action: cost now, benefit in decades. Trading is a B2 action: the decision, the position and its excitement now, the costs of turnover later. Account-level data show that the most active households earned 11.4% a year against the market's 17.9% 43. Robinhood users herded into stocks on top-movers lists and those episodes were followed by negative returns 44. Zero commission does not remove the per-trade price; it shrouds it in order-flow payments and spreads, which is the classic setting in which shrouded prices survive competition 45.

Robo-advice and scheduled contributions automate the C2 action, and the evidence on robo-advice is that it improves diversification and reduces behavioural biases among investors who were previously undiversified 46. Its cost is the asset-based fee. E7 compounds $10,000 plus $300 a month for thirty years at 6% gross: a 1.00% annual fee leaves 17.6% less than a 0.05% fund, and 0.25% leaves 4.1% less.

Engagement features deserve their own plate because they show the coding rule earning its keep. A push notification or celebratory animation is not a financial product; it is a zero-latency prompt for the action the platform is paid for. FINRA's 2021 action against Robinhood, at the time the largest financial penalty it had ordered, concerned misleading information, outages and options approvals 47. Crypto trading apps add a custody failure to the decision failure: the restructuring of FTX documented commingled customer assets and an absence of basic controls 48.

Credit management

Credit-management products split cleanly. Monitoring, building and reporting are cost-first or neutral; the offers that fund free monitoring are benefit-first. The census codes the free-score app on the loan offer because that is what pays for it. Ausubel's account of why card rates stayed high despite many issuers turned on consumers who do not expect to borrow and then do 49; a score app paid per approval is built around that consumer.

Underwriting is where fintech's inclusion case is strongest. In 2015 the CFPB counted 26 million US adults with no credit record and another 19 million whose files could not be scored 50. Cash-flow data from bank accounts predicts default and adds to bureau scores 51; even a digital footprint rivals a bureau score for small online loans 52. E6 shows where the gain lands. With a 10% base default rate and a 6% loss cap on the approved book, a thick-file population approved on a bureau score reaches 84.81%, and adding a noisier cash-flow score lifts that only to 86.45%; the AUC moves from 0.812 to 0.829. A thin-file population whose pooled default rate of 10.17% exceeds the cap cannot be approved at all on bureau data; with cash-flow data alone 76.77% is approved at the same cap. The value of a second signal is almost entirely at the margin where there was no first signal.

The same models can sort groups more finely. Machine-learned scores raise accuracy and can widen differences in outcomes between groups 53; algorithmic mortgage lenders discriminated less than face-to-face lenders but still charged minority borrowers more 54. And the data now flows in a new direction: FICO announced scores that incorporate BNPL repayment data in June 2025 55, which turns a credit file into the only friction a stacking BNPL borrower meets.

Digital banking

A digital bank account is an S0 product: holding and moving one's own money has no gap between benefit and cost. That is why the failures of digital banking are not failures of choice. They are failures of the ledger. When the value dates coincide the consumer's mistake disappears from the analysis and the institution's appears.

Synapse is the case. A middleware firm kept the ledger for about a hundred fintech apps whose customers' money sat in omnibus accounts at several partner banks 57. When it filed for bankruptcy on 22 April 2024, the banks found they held less than Synapse's records said end users were owed, a shortfall the CFPB put at $60–90 million 58. The CFPB sued in August 2025 for a $1 penalty whose purpose was to open its civil-penalty fund to victims 59, the bankruptcy case was dismissed 60, and $46.2 million was allocated from the fund in November 2025, about half the estimated shortfall 61. The FDIC proposed that banks keep beneficial-owner records for custodial accounts with transactional features 10.

E5 shows why aggregate reconciliation could not have caught it. Ten thousand users and three hundred thousand postings run through a middleware ledger. When a posting is credited to the wrong user, the omnibus account and the sum of the ledger stay equal to the cent: the aggregate gap is $0.00 at every misposting rate. At a misposting rate of 0.1%, 1.52% of users are nonetheless short, by $7,853.31 in total; at 0.5%, 6.73%. Leakage, where a credit is recorded but the cash never arrives, does show in the aggregate ($7,886.63 at 0.1%) but nobody's individual record looks wrong, and a pro-rata distribution at failure spreads the loss to 53.23% of users whose own money was intact. The two errors leave opposite fingerprints: misallocation is invisible in aggregate and concentrated in individuals; leakage is visible in aggregate and invisible in individuals. Only per-owner reconciliation sees both, which is what the FDIC proposal requires.

Speed on S0 has a systemic face as well. Silicon Valley Bank lost $42 billion of deposits in a single day in March 2023, with more queued for the next morning 62; the coordination failure Diamond and Dybvig modelled forty years earlier 63 now runs at the speed of a phone. The 2024 rule limiting overdraft fees at large banks was repealed under the Congressional Review Act on 9 May 2025, along with a rule bringing large payment apps under CFPB supervision 64,65.

Consumer lending

Consumer lending is the B side of the ladder, and the instant interface is its defining innovation: approval at checkout, wages before payday, a card that keeps working past zero. The ladder predicts that these are where instant execution does the most damage and where provider revenue is most tightly coupled to the action. Both hold in the census: every lending plate is amplifying and every one is paid per use or per balance.

The annual percentage rate is the regulatory measure of the price of credit 69, and E3 shows what it does to products that sell almost no time. A fixed $5 charge on $100 is 608% APR over three days and 5% over a year. The median overdraft, $34 on a $24 debit repaid in three days 66, is 17,236%. A two-week payday loan at $15 per $100 is 391% 70, and access to such loans increased households' difficulty paying mortgage, rent and utility bills 71. An earned-wage advance with a $4.99 expedite fee on $100 for ten days is 182%; a $3 tip on $50 for seven days is 313%. Pay-in-four on time is 0%; one $7 late fee on a $200 basket makes it 59.0%. A three-year instalment loan at 15% with a 5% origination fee is 18.7%. The ordering by APR is the ordering by rung: B1 products cluster in the hundreds and thousands of per cent because annualising a fixed fee over days multiplies it. Whether APR or dollar cost is the right number to show depends on who is choosing. Payday borrowers shown the dollar cost of rolling over borrowed about 11% less 72, and exponential-growth bias makes annualised rates hard to read in any case 73.

The policy record follows the same lines. The CFPB's December 2025 advisory opinion holds that covered, employer-integrated earned-wage access is not credit and that its expedite fees and tips are not finance charges 74,75, while California's regulator reported average APRs near 330% for the same products 76. The 2024 interpretive rule treating BNPL accounts as credit cards 77 was withdrawn in 2025 78; in the CFPB's own data 63% of BNPL borrowers had simultaneous loans at some point and about a third across several lenders 56, though a later CFPB working paper found no harm to repayment of other debt 79. BNPL access raises total spending rather than only shifting its timing 85, and in UK data a substantial share of BNPL repayments were made with credit cards 86, which turns a B2 product into a B2 product funded by another.

Remittances

Remittances are the clearest S0 product in the brief and the one where fintech's gains are least ambiguous. The sender's cost and the recipient's benefit land together, so there is no present-bias distortion to amplify, and every reduction in price or delay is a transfer to migrants and their families. The flows are large: officially recorded remittances to low- and middle-income countries were estimated at $685 billion in 2024, more than foreign direct investment and official aid combined 90. The price is still high. The World Bank's global average total cost of sending $200 was 6.49% in the first quarter of 2025, while the SmaRT average, the cost of the three cheapest transparent services in each corridor, was 3.29% 91; the SDG target is below 3% with no corridor above 5% 92, and digital services were already near 5% in 2024 93. Mobile-money corridors averaged 3.54% in the GSMA's survey 94.

The price that does the damage is the one the sender cannot see. E4 decomposes total cost into a fixed fee, a percentage fee and an exchange-rate margin. A zero-fee operator with a 3.5% margin is cheaper than a transparent operator charging $4.99 and a 1.2% margin only below $216.96; above it the zero-fee offer costs more, and all of its price is margin. A bank wire's fixed fee makes it 20.50% at $200. A stablecoin route is cheap in the middle and expensive at the ramps: 2.83% at $200 in the lab's parameters, nearly all of it on-ramp and off-ramp fees. If every dollar of the $685 billion paid the $200 global average rather than 3%, the difference would be $23.9 billion a year; at the SmaRT average it would be $2.0 billion. The RPW average is a simple average of $200 prices, not a volume-weighted cost, so these are bounds on the scale of the prize rather than estimates of it.

The long-run evidence is strongest for mobile money. Kenyan households with M-Pesa absorbed shocks by receiving transfers from wider networks 95, and access lifted an estimated 2% of households out of poverty, with larger effects for female-headed households 96. Globally, Findex 2025 found 79% of adults with an account and 15% with a mobile-money account 97, but also that measured financial health had not improved alongside access 98. The US GENIUS Act, signed 18 July 2025, gives payment stablecoins one-to-one reserve and monthly disclosure requirements 99,100 and takes effect by 18 January 2027 101.

Financial education and financial wellness

Education is a C2 action in the purest form: effort now, better decisions for years. Its evidence is better than its reputation and worse than its advocates hope. Across 76 randomised trials, financial education raised financial knowledge and downstream behaviours by amounts comparable to other education interventions 102. An earlier meta-analysis found that interventions explained about 0.1% of the variance in financial behaviour and that effects decayed over the following months 103. Financial literacy itself correlates robustly with planning, saving and wealth 104, and the OECD's 2023 survey across 39 economies measures it on a common scale 105. The ladder's reading reconciles the two literatures: knowledge acquired years before a decision is a C2 investment whose benefit leg decays before it lands, which is why the same meta-analysis recommends teaching just before the decision, where the action is C1.

Disclosure is education placed on the B rung, and it is the only lever in the census that impedes a benefit-first action rather than easing a cost-first one. It works when it puts the later leg in front of the present self in units that self reads 72; it fails when it informs a planner who is not the one deciding 89. Supervisory regimes increasingly judge outcomes rather than disclosures: the UK's Consumer Duty, in force since July 2023, requires firms to show that customers understand and get fair value 106, and it now applies to regulated BNPL 80.

Financial wellness is the brief's attempt to name the target. The CFPB defines it by four elements: control over day-to-day finances, capacity to absorb a shock, being on track for goals, and freedom to make choices 107, and measures it on a ten-item scale 108. Netemeyer and colleagues found two dimensions underneath, current money-management stress and expected future security, which relate differently to overall well-being 109. The two dimensions are the two sides of today. A wellness benefit that bundles an earned-wage advance with coaching sells one action from each side under one name, and should be judged as two products.

The ledger

The census in E8 counts 36 plates: B3 {e8_B3}, B2 {e8_B2}, B1 {e8_B1}, S0 {e8_S0}, C1 {e8_C1} and C2 {e8_C2}. {e8_E} ease their action, {e8_A} automate it and {e8_I} impede it. The verdicts are 11 amplifying, 14 correcting and 11 neutral.

The first finding is the one the opening claimed. The side of today the cost falls on predicts how the product is paid for. Of the 11 products that ease a benefit-first action, 10 are paid when the action happens; of the 14 that ease a cost-first action or impede a benefit-first one, 4 are, and the rest are funded by flat fees, employers or mandates. A one-sided Fisher exact test gives p = 0.0026. The sole amplifying exception, the safe-to-spend forecast, is paid by subscription; three of the four correcting tools paid per use are asset-based, paid for the balance the saving creates. Recoding the card wallet as credit-funded leaves the result intact. This is the pricing structure DellaVigna and Malmendier predicted for firms facing present-biased customers 28, observed across a product category rather than a gym.

The second finding is the dual of the first. When the value dates coincide, the consumer's mistake disappears and the institution's appears. None of the 11 S0 plates has a decision-timing failure. Their failure modes are custody (the middleware ledger, the stored-value balance, the stablecoin reserve), finality (authorised push-payment fraud), speed of withdrawal (runs) and, for the two information products, what the screen leaves out. The interventions that worked on S0 failures were recordkeeping and loss allocation, not friction.

The third finding concerns the brief's own headings. The coded profile, rung by friction by revenue, separates 93.2% of the 630 pairs of plates, and the eleven headings separate 93.0%: the profile is as discriminating as the taxonomy with three coded fields. It also cuts across it. Investing and credit management each contain both amplifying and correcting tools, and 8 of the 16 occupied profile cells are shared by more than one heading. A regulator organised by heading treats a robo-adviser and a trading app as one market; organised by value date it would see two.

What would falsify this account? A product that eases a benefit-first action, is paid per use, and still raises long-run welfare would be a counterexample to the verdict, and there are plausible candidates: cash-flow underwriting that replaces a payday loan with a cheaper instalment loan moves a borrower down in price while easing access, and E6 shows real inclusion gains. The ladder handles this only partly, by coding the substitution as a change in price within B3; a fuller version would code each plate against its realistic alternative, not against doing nothing. The coding is one reader's, against stated rules, and the lab's populations are stylised: E2's effort and attention parameters, E5's error rates and E6's signal strengths are chosen to show mechanisms, not to estimate magnitudes. Every policy status quoted here was checked against sources dated to early October 2026 and several, Section 1033 above all, are in motion.

def Phi(x):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))

def phi(x):
    return math.exp(-0.5 * x * x) / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)

def partial_mean(mu, sd, a, b):
    """E[v * 1(a < v < b)] for v ~ N(mu, sd)."""
    za, zb = (a - mu) / sd, (b - mu) / sd
    pa = Phi(za) if a > -1e300 else 0.0
    pb = Phi(zb) if b < 1e300 else 1.0
    fa = phi(za) if a > -1e300 else 0.0
    fb = phi(zb) if b < 1e300 else 0.0
    return mu * (pb - pa) + sd * (fa - fb)

def prob(mu, sd, a, b):
    pa = Phi((a - mu) / sd) if a > -1e300 else 0.0
    pb = Phi((b - mu) / sd) if b < 1e300 else 1.0
    return pb - pa

def irr_periodic(pv, flows):
    lo, hi = 0.0, 5.0
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        v = sum(c / (1 + mid) ** (k + 1) for k, c in enumerate(flows))
        if v > pv:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi)

def card_payoff(bal, apr, minfn, cap=1200):
    r = apr / 12.0
    months, interest = 0, 0.0
    path = [bal]
    while bal > 0.005 and months < cap:
        i = bal * r
        pay = min(bal + i, minfn(bal, i))
        bal = bal + i - pay
        interest += i
        months += 1
        path.append(bal)
    return months, interest, path

def auc(scores, labels):
    pairs = sorted(zip(scores, labels))
    n1 = sum(labels); n0 = len(labels) - n1
    rank_sum, i = 0.0, 0
    while i < len(pairs):
        j = i
        while j < len(pairs) and pairs[j][0] == pairs[i][0]:
            j += 1
        avg = (i + 1 + j) / 2.0
        for k in range(i, j):
            if pairs[k][1] == 1:
                rank_sum += avg
        i = j
    return (rank_sum - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * n0)

def census(plates, label):

    by = {}
    for p in plates:

    for k in ["amplifying", "correcting", "neutral"]:
        lst = by.get(k, [])
        earns = [p for p in lst if p[4] in ("U", "B")]
        out[k] = {"n": len(lst), "earns": len(earns), "ids": [p[0] for p in lst], "earn_ids": [p[0] for p in earns]}

    out["revenue"] = {f: sum(1 for p in plates if p[4] == f) for f in ["U", "B", "F"]}
    pairs = sep_prof = sep_fam = sep_rung = 0
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            pairs += 1
            a, b = plates[i], plates[j]
            if (a[2], a[3], a[4]) != (b[2], b[3], b[4]):
                sep_prof += 1
            if a[1] != b[1]:
                sep_fam += 1
            if a[2] != b[2]:
                sep_rung += 1
    out["pairs"] = pairs
    out["sep_profile"] = sep_prof / pairs
    out["sep_family"] = sep_fam / pairs
    out["sep_rung"] = sep_rung / pairs
    cells = sorted(set((p[2], p[3], p[4]) for p in plates))
    out["cells"] = len(cells)
    fams = sorted(set(p[1] for p in plates))
    mixed = [f for f in fams if len(set(verdict(p[2], p[3]) for p in plates if p[1] == f)) > 1]

    out["mean_d"] = sum(distortion(p[2]) for p in plates) / n
