A technical article on the fintech technology stack

What Has to Agree With the Ledger, and How Soon?

The fintech technology stack read as a system of copies

Fifteen layers make up a modern fintech stack, from the phone app to the deployment pipeline. Read each one as either the book of record or a copy of it, and the stack stops being a list of products and becomes a single design problem: for every copy, how long may it disagree with the ledger, and who finds out when it does?

1010 The question

A fintech product is, at bottom, a number in a database that people have agreed to treat as money. Everything else in its technology stack is either that number or a copy of it. The phone shows a copy. The API changes the number and reports a copy of the result. The event stream announces the change to other services, each of which keeps its own copy. The warehouse holds yesterday's copy for the regulator, the fraud model reasons over copies of copies, and the observability platform keeps copies of what the system did rather than what the money did. The layers in this article's brief, from web and mobile applications to DevSecOps, are best understood by asking one question of each: what has to agree with the ledger, and how soon?

The question is not a figure of speech; it is the design. The oldest integrity constraint in finance, that every entry's debits equal its credits, was printed by Luca Pacioli in 1494 1, and payment companies still build their systems of record around it: Stripe describes its Ledger as an immutable double-entry log processing some five billion events a day 2. Distributed-systems theory says a system cannot keep every copy identical and remain available when the network partitions 3,4. Helland's practical reading of that result is that large systems must learn to live without distributed transactions and to reason explicitly about uncertainty between their parts 5. A fintech stack is therefore a collection of deliberate, bounded disagreements with its own book of record. The engineering is in choosing each bound, enforcing it, and arranging to be told when it is exceeded.

Method

The article examines thirty-two plates, each a concrete component drawn from the fifteen layers of the brief: the balance on a phone, an idempotent payment API, a nostro account, a transactional outbox, a dead-letter queue, a fraud score, a stablecoin's reserves, the record of which code runs on which server. Every plate carries the same four fields: what it holds, what it is a copy of, how long it may disagree, and who or what finds out. Each plate is then placed on two scales.

The first is an agreement ladder of six rungs, measuring distance from the book of record. A0 is the book of record itself. A1 sits inside the commit: it changes in the same transaction as the ledger or gates whether a posting may commit at all. A2 follows the commit, updated from it within seconds. A3 is reconciled on a cycle, typically against another institution's record. A4 is inferred from copies, producing decisions rather than balances. A5 is about the system rather than the money: configuration, identity, telemetry, code.

The second is a detection scale with five values. Divergence may be impossible by construction (K), because an invariant rejects it at write; detected continuously (C) by a monitor; detected periodically (P) by a batch reconciliation; detected only externally (X) by an auditor, examiner, counterparty or trustee; or not at all (N). Each plate also carries a tolerated window, the longest disagreement its design admits, coded to an order of magnitude in seconds. The rung and window are the author's codings from the cited sources, not measurements, and they are stated on every plate so the reader can disagree with them.

Each plate's gauge is a T-account. The left, debit side lists the six rungs and fills the plate's own; the right, credit side lists the five detection modes and fills the plate's own; the rule beneath the T is a logarithmic time axis from a millisecond to a year, with the tolerated window marked in the rung's colour. The thirty-two plates are collected, with their gauges summarised, in the closing ledger of section 1120.

The fifteen layers of the brief map onto the plates as follows. Some layers need several plates because they contain copies at different distances; event-driven systems and message queues share plates because Kafka is both.

The finding, in advance

Construction-level protection never reaches further than two rungs from the journal. All seven plates whose divergence is impossible by construction sit at A0, A1 or A2. Of the eleven plates at A3 and A4, six are found out only by an outsider or not at all. Across the twenty-seven plates that hold or reason about money, the rank correlation between distance from the ledger and strength of detection is −0.718. The stack's hardest problems are therefore not in its newest layers but at its edges, where a copy leaves the system that can check it.

1020 Books: databases and ledgers

The ledger is the one component that is not a copy, so its correctness is the precondition for every other plate. Three properties carry most of the weight.

First, balanced entries. A posting is a set of legs whose signed amounts sum to zero; a transfer debits one account and credits another in the same entry, and a fee or a foreign-exchange spread is simply another leg. Balances are derived by summing legs, never stored as the primary fact. Fowler's accounting patterns describe this structure for software 6, and it maps directly onto event sourcing, in which the log of changes is the record and current state is a projection of it 7.

Second, immutability. A wrong entry is corrected by a new, reversing entry, never by an update, so that the history of what the institution believed and when is itself preserved 8. This sits in tension with data-protection law. The GDPR's right to erasure yields where processing is necessary to comply with a legal obligation 9, which is the usual basis for retaining transaction records, but personal data around the entries (names, free-text references, device data) must be designed so that it can be separated from the amounts.

Third, atomicity and isolation. The properties that Haerder and Reuter named ACID 10, implemented through write-ahead logging and recovery 11–13, are what make a two-legged entry indivisible. Experiment E1 shows what is at stake. A million transfers written as two separate rows, with a one-in-ten-thousand chance of a process crash between steps, leave 99 orphaned half-postings and a trial balance off by 5,010.62 currency units, against an expected 100 orphans. The same transfers written as one journal entry leave none. A saga that commits the legs separately but recovers by completing or compensating also leaves none, but only after 99 transient imbalances lasting a total of 2,649.4 seconds: a deliberate, visible, bounded disagreement.

Two modelling decisions sit underneath these properties. The first is how an amount is represented. Binary floating point cannot represent most decimal fractions exactly, so sums drift and comparisons fail in ways Goldberg catalogued long ago 14; ledgers store integers in a currency's minor unit, or fixed-point decimals, with the currency code and its number of minor units taken from ISO 4217 15. Rounding then becomes an explicit posting (a fractional remainder credited to a rounding account) rather than an accident of arithmetic. The second is the shape of the record. A minimal ledger has accounts, journal entries and entry lines; each line names one account, one currency and one signed amount, and each entry carries the idempotency key and the external reference that produced it. Entries move through explicit states (pending, posted, reversed) so that an authorisation hold and a settled payment are both first-class facts in the same book rather than in different systems.

Isolation is where ledgers most often fail in practice, because weak isolation levels admit anomalies that the SQL standard's own definitions understate 16,17. The canonical bug is check-then-act: read a balance, decide the withdrawal is affordable, write the new balance. Warszawski and Bailis found this class of race, which they called ACIDRain, throughout real e-commerce applications 18. Experiment E3 enumerates every interleaving of n concurrent withdrawals of 70 from a balance of 100. If each transaction writes the balance it computed, the fraction of schedules that lose an update is exactly 1 − 1/(2n − 1): 66.67% for two withdrawals and 88.89% for five, where on average 144.4 units of money are created per schedule. If instead the check is done on read and the write is an atomic decrement, the same fraction of schedules overdraws the account. A conditional decrement (UPDATE … SET bal = bal − 70 WHERE bal >= 70) or a row lock produces no violation in any order. In time rather than schedules, a customer who double-taps within 300 ms against a 20 ms read-to-write gap overlaps 12.89% of the time.

The non-negative balance is precisely the kind of invariant that cannot be maintained without coordination, as Bailis and colleagues showed formally; conservation of money, by contrast, can be preserved by construction if every change is a balanced entry 19. That distinction decides where coordination must be spent. Distributed books of record (Spanner's externally consistent transactions 20, Raft- or Paxos-replicated logs 21,22) pay for coordination with latency; mainframe cores pay for it by batching. Either way, the plates below that sit at A0 and A1 are the only ones in the stack where disagreement can be forbidden rather than detected.

The book's own edge is the first place where that stops being true. A bank's record of its balance at a correspondent, its nostro account, is a copy of someone else's ledger, reconciled when the statement arrives. A banking-as-a-service platform's sub-ledger of end-user balances is a copy of a pooled for-benefit-of account at a partner bank. When Synapse failed in 2024, its trustee reported an estimated shortfall of $65–96 million between the cash in those accounts and what the sub-ledger said was owed 23. Nothing in the system was positioned to find that out except the bankruptcy.

1030 Edges: web, mobile and APIs

The web and mobile front ends are where the ledger meets its owners, and they are almost entirely copies. A balance on a phone is rendered from an API response, which was read from a projection, which was derived from the journal. That is three hops, and the only control available at that distance is honesty about time: show when the number was true, and never let an optimistic interface display a payment as complete before the posting has committed 26.

Mobile clients add two further copies that are easy to forget. A push notification ("you paid £12.40 at…") is an event delivered outside the app, often before the posting is final, and it cannot be retracted once read. An offline queue holds instructions the customer believes were sent; when the device reconnects, perhaps hours later, it replays them. Both are reasons to treat the client as an untrusted, delayed participant: it may display and request, but every amount, fee and limit it shows must be computed on the server and every instruction it sends must carry a key that the server can recognise on replay.

APIs are different, because they write. Fielding's REST constraints 27 and HTTP's own semantics distinguish idempotent methods from POST, which is not 28. A client that times out after sending a payment does not know whether the server committed it, and the safe action, retrying, is the dangerous one. The standard remedy is an idempotency key: the client generates a unique key per intended operation, and the server stores the key with the outcome, in the same transaction as the posting, so that a retry returns the original result instead of executing again 29. The pattern is being standardised as an HTTP header field 30; Stripe's reference documentation notes that keys may be pruned once they are at least 24 hours old 31.

That pruning is the plate's tolerated window, and experiment E2 prices it. A million payments, a 0.4% chance that a response is lost after commit and a 2% chance that a retry is deferred (an app reopened, an offline queue flushed) to somewhere between an hour and a fortnight later, produce 4,022 duplicate executions with no key: every lost response becomes a second charge. A one-hour retention leaves 101 duplicates; 24 hours leaves 51; 72 hours leaves 29; seven days leaves 9; thirty days leaves none. The duplicates do not come from the network. They come from the mismatch between how long the server remembers and how long the client may wait.

Open-banking APIs add a third party's copy. Under PSD2 32, an account-information provider may read a customer's account without the customer present no more than four times in 24 hours unless otherwise agreed 33, so the provider's copy can be hours old by rule. The security of the channel is now well specified. The OpenID Foundation approved the FAPI 2.0 Security Profile as a final specification in February 2025 34,35; it builds on OAuth 2.0 36 with pushed authorisation requests 37, sender-constrained tokens via mutual TLS or DPoP 38,39, and the current OAuth security best practice 40. None of that makes the copy current. In the United States, the CFPB finalised its Section 1033 data-rights rule in October 2024, reopened it through an advance notice in August 2025 41, and has been enjoined from enforcing it while it reconsiders 42.

The most common API vulnerability is not cryptographic. Broken object-level authorisation, an API that returns or modifies an object because the caller supplied its identifier, heads the OWASP API Security Top 10 43. In a ledger API the object is an account, and the check belongs inside the commit, as a predicate on the posting, not in a gateway that sees only a URL.

Webhooks reverse the direction. A payment provider tells a merchant that something happened, at least once, retrying for up to three days in live mode 44. The merchant's order table is then an A2 copy whose consistency depends on deduplicating by event identifier and on periodic reconciliation against the provider's balance records, because a missing webhook is indistinguishable from a payment that never happened.

1040 Services

Microservices decompose an application into independently deployable services that own their data 45,46. In a fintech the decomposition usually follows the organisation, as Conway predicted 47: a payments team, a cards team, a ledger team, a risk team. Monzo built a bank on this model from its first year 48. The architectural consequence for this article is that every service boundary is a place where a copy can be made, because database-per-service forbids a shared transaction across the boundary.

Three responses recur. The first is to keep the ledger as a single service that owns every posting, so that all money-changing operations converge on one transactional store and other services hold only references. The second is the saga, a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions, first described by Garcia-Molina and Salem for long-lived database transactions 49 and now a standard microservice pattern 50. The third is to accept eventual consistency where the business can tolerate it, in the spirit of BASE 51,52. Bailis's coordination-avoidance criterion says which invariants can live with the third option: those whose validity is preserved when independently valid changes are merged 19. "No account below zero" is not one of them.

Decomposition also changes availability arithmetic. A request that passes synchronously through k services, each available 99.95% of the time, succeeds with probability 0.9995k. Experiment E8 shows that a 20-hop path at that per-service level is unavailable for 430.0 minutes in a 30-day month, against 21.6 minutes for one service. Latency compounds the same way. Dean and Barroso observed that tail latency at the leaves dominates a fanned-out request 53; with each backend slow 1% of the time, a request that fans out to 100 backends is slow 63.40% of the time.

The design lesson is not to avoid services but to keep the posting path short. The authorisation path for a card payment should touch the ledger, the risk decision and the identity check, and nothing that can be done afterwards from an event.

1050 Motion: events, Kafka and message queues

An event-driven architecture turns the ledger's changes into a stream that other components consume. Kreps's account of the log as the unifying abstraction of data systems 54 and the original Kafka design 55 explain why the pattern took hold: a durable, ordered, replayable log lets every downstream projection be rebuilt from scratch. Kafka 4.0, released in March 2025, is the first major version to run entirely without ZooKeeper, using its own Raft-based metadata quorum 21,56. Kafka transactions provide exactly-once processing from topic to topic 57, but not into a database or an external API, where idempotent consumers are still required.

The first problem is getting the event out. A service that commits a posting and then publishes an event performs a dual write, and a crash between the two loses the event; publishing first and committing second risks announcing a posting that never happened. Experiment E4 runs a million postings with a one-in-ten-thousand crash rate and a 0.05% commit-rejection rate. Commit-then-publish loses 110 events. Publish-then-commit emits 587 phantom events. A transactional outbox, which writes the event as a row in the same transaction as the posting and relays it afterwards 50, loses none and invents none; its only residue is 109 duplicates from relay crashes, which consumers deduplicating by event identifier absorb completely.

The second problem is how far behind the copies run. Treat a partition consumer as a single-server queue with exponential service at 2,000 events per second. Little's law 58 and the M/M/1 sojourn distribution 59 give the staleness of a projection directly, and experiment E5 checks them by simulation. At 90% utilisation the 99th-percentile staleness is 23.03 ms (simulated 21.02 ms); at 98% it is 115.13 ms. Read a balance projection 50 ms after a posting at 98% utilisation and it has not yet seen the posting 13.53% of the time. A two-second consumer pause, of the kind a group rebalance or garbage-collection stall produces, takes 18.0 seconds to drain at 90% utilisation and 98.0 seconds at 98%. A projection on an authorisation path is a balance that is sometimes wrong for a minute and a half.

Payment rails are message queues between institutions, and the messages are increasingly ISO 20022 60. Swift ended the coexistence of MT and ISO 20022 formats for cross-border payment instructions on 22 November 2025, reporting 97% of instructions sent natively in ISO 20022 shortly afterwards 25. Instant rails have tightened the window by law: euro instant credit transfers must make funds available within ten seconds 61. The scale is large. India's UPI processed 23.66 billion transactions in July 2026 62, a daily average of 763 million 63, or roughly 8,800 per second around the clock. At that volume, a rail status report is a copy of the counterparty's booking that must still be reconciled, because ordering between institutions has no global clock 64.

Finally, every queue that can fail to process a message has somewhere to put it. A dead-letter queue is a list of known divergences between the ledger and a copy, and it is detected by nobody unless its depth is alerted on and owned like a money metric.

1060 Substrate: cloud, containers and Kubernetes

Cloud computing, in NIST's definition, is on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable resources that can be provisioned rapidly with minimal management effort 65; its economic case was made early and remains sound 66. For a fintech the substrate is not a copy of the money, but it determines how many copies of the ledger exist and how far apart they are allowed to drift.

Containers package services with their dependencies, and NIST's container security guide sets out the image, registry, orchestrator and host risks they introduce 67. Kubernetes descends from Google's Borg 68 and is built around controllers that continuously compare desired state with observed state and act to close the gap 69. That is the same idea as a ledger reconciliation, applied to machines, with one difference: a Kubernetes controller has authority to change the world to match the record, whereas a financial reconciliation must decide which side is wrong.

Replication is where the substrate meets the book. A synchronous replica sits inside the commit at a latency cost; an asynchronous one follows it, and its lag is the recovery point objective. Experiment E8 shows why replica count is not the variable that matters. Two regions each available 99.95%, failing independently, are jointly unavailable for 0.011 minutes a month. If 1% of failures are common-mode (a shared control plane, a shared deployment, a shared vendor) that rises to 0.227 minutes; at 10% it is 2.170 minutes; at 50%, 10.805 minutes, no better than a single well-run region. The CrowdStrike content update of July 2024, which Microsoft estimated affected 8.5 million Windows devices 70,71, was a common-mode failure across infrastructure that its owners had every reason to believe was independent.

Infrastructure as code turns the substrate into one more record with copies. The declared configuration in a repository, the state file a provisioning tool keeps and the resources actually running in a cloud account can disagree, and the disagreement, usually called drift, is detected only when a plan is run against reality. Data residency adds a geographic constraint to the same picture: where a copy of personal or payment data may physically reside is set by data-protection and outsourcing rules, so a replica placed for availability can be a compliance failure if it lands in the wrong jurisdiction 9,72.

Regulators have reached the same conclusion. The EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act has applied since 17 January 2025 and requires financial entities to manage ICT risk, report major incidents, keep a register of ICT third-party arrangements and submit to oversight of critical ICT providers 73. In the United Kingdom, firms were required to remain within impact tolerances for their important business services by March 2025 74, alongside expectations on outsourcing and third-party risk 72; the Basel Committee's principles set the international baseline 75. Shared responsibility has a cost when it is misunderstood: a misconfigured firewall in Capital One's cloud environment led to the 2019 breach for which the OCC imposed an $80 million civil money penalty 76.

1070 Copies for insight: warehouses, lakes and real-time analytics

Analytical stores are copies by design. The enterprise data warehouse 77 and the dimensional model 78 organise yesterday's ledger for reporting; distributed batch processing made it cheap to recompute such copies at scale 79; and the lakehouse added ACID transactions and versioned "time travel" to tables held in object storage 80,81. Data mesh redistributes ownership of these copies to the domains that produce them 82. None of these changes the plate. A warehouse is an A3 copy reconciled on a cycle, and the question is what its reconciliation can see.

BCBS 239 requires banks' risk data to be accurate and reconciled with accounting sources 83. Experiment E7 tests what that means in practice. A copy of 10,000 ledger rows across 200 accounts is corrupted in one of five ways, 400 times each, and five controls are applied. A row count catches missing and duplicated rows and nothing else. A control total adds wrong amounts. Count and total together see nothing more than the total alone. Per-account totals add postings to the wrong account. Only a keyed, row-level match catches two errors in one account that cancel, which every summary control misses in all 400 trials.

The cost of a keyed match is not the obstacle it appears. A Merkle tree 84 over 220 rows localises one differing row with 41 hash comparisons, ten rows with 325 and a thousand with 20,429, against 1,048,576 row comparisons for a full scan. The Synapse sub-ledger of the previous section is the plate where this matters most: a sum would have shown that money was missing, but only per-user records could have said whose it was.

Real-time analytics shortens the window but not the problem. Streaming engines such as Flink 85 implement the Dataflow model's separation of event time from processing time, with watermarks that declare when a window is complete and triggers that handle data arriving late 86. A velocity rule ("more than five card-not-present attempts in a minute") that ignores lateness under-counts precisely when traffic is bursty, which is when the rule is needed.

1080 Inference: AI and machine learning

Models sit at A4: they do not hold balances, but they decide whether a balance may change, from features that are copies of copies. Two properties of fintech data dominate how they behave.

The first is base rate. Fraud is rare. In the widely used card-transaction dataset released by Dal Pozzolo and colleagues, 492 of 284,807 transactions are fraudulent, a prevalence of 0.17% 87. Statistical fraud detection has long been framed around this imbalance 88. Experiment E6 models scores as two unit-variance normal distributions separated by d′. A classifier with d′ = 3 has a ROC area of 0.9831, which looks excellent. At 80% recall its precision is 8.22%, and it raises 11.16 false alerts for every fraud it catches; at 90% recall that becomes 27.52. Its average precision is 0.4522. The same classifier at a 5% prevalence would have 73.16% precision. Precision-recall analysis is the honest summary for this regime 89,90, and alert volume per true positive is the number operations teams actually staff against.

The second is label delay. A fraud score's correctness is established by chargebacks, and scheme dispute rules allow those to arrive months after the authorisation 24. Under an illustrative delay distribution with a median of 35 days and a 95th percentile of 120, only 41.85% of the eventual fraud labels are known 30 days after the transactions and 89.63% after 90 days. A model can therefore drift for a quarter before its own performance metrics show it 91. Its window on the gauge is not the milliseconds of inference but the months of label maturation.

Anti-money-laundering monitoring shares the fraud problem's base rate without its labels. The FATF standards require a risk-based approach to customer due diligence and the monitoring of transactions 92, but a suspicious-activity report is rarely confirmed or refuted back to the institution that filed it, so most alerts never acquire a ground truth at all. Monitoring systems therefore combine rules, scenario thresholds and models, and their performance is judged by alert quality reviewed by investigators rather than by any equivalent of a chargeback. On this article's ladder, transaction monitoring is an A4 copy whose detection mode is external: an examiner, or a law-enforcement inquiry, is what finds out that it missed something.

Credit models add a regulatory frame. Credit scoring has a long statistical literature 93. In the United States, SR 11-7 requires model validation and effective challenge by people independent of the model's development 94, and the CFPB has stated that creditors using complex algorithms must still give specific and accurate reasons for adverse action 95,96. Post-hoc attribution methods such as SHAP are commonly used to produce such reasons 97, with the caveat that an attribution explains the model, not the world; fairness has its own literature and its own impossibility results 98. The EU AI Act classifies creditworthiness assessment of natural persons as high-risk 99; the Digital Omnibus deferred application of the Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 100.

Two further copies are specific to machine learning. Feature stores compute the same features twice, once for training and once for serving, and training-serving skew is the classic source of the hidden technical debt Sculley and colleagues described 101. Language-model assistants generate statements about balances, fees and disputes from retrieved documents and tool calls; a Canadian tribunal held an airline responsible for what its chatbot told a customer 102, and the CFPB has documented the risks of chatbots in consumer finance 103. An assistant that reads the balance through a tool is a copy; one that paraphrases a fee schedule from memory is not even that.

1090 Second books: blockchain

A blockchain is not a copy of the ledger; it is another ledger, with a different authority. Its ingredients predate Bitcoin: hash-linked timestamping 104 and Merkle trees 84. Nakamoto's contribution was to make agreement on the order of entries a product of proof-of-work among parties who need not trust each other 105; Ethereum generalised the state from coins to programs 106. Permissioned chains use Byzantine fault-tolerant protocols instead 107.

The property that matters for this article is finality. In a proof-of-work chain it is probabilistic. Experiment E9 reproduces the calculation in section 11 of Nakamoto's paper: the probability that an attacker controlling a share q of hash power ever catches up from z blocks behind. For q = 0.10, the probability falls below 0.1% at five confirmations (0.0009137); for q = 0.30 it takes 24; for q = 0.45, 340. These match the paper's own table and are the method validation for E9. Regulated payment systems use a different concept entirely: legal settlement finality, defined by statute and by the international principles for financial market infrastructures, which fixes the moment after which a transfer cannot be unwound regardless of what any computer does 108,109.

Ordering is itself an economic resource on public chains. Daian and colleagues showed that transaction ordering can be bought and front-run, a phenomenon now called maximal extractable value 110. For a fintech this means that "submitted" and "settled at the expected price" are separate states with an adversary between them.

The useful question is the one Wüst and Gervais posed: does the application need a blockchain at all? If a trusted third party is available and acceptable to all writers, a conventional database is simpler and stronger 111. Where a shared ledger is justified, the BIS has argued for a unified ledger combining tokenised central bank money and tokenised deposits, precisely so that settlement and the book of record are the same object 112. Stablecoins show the opposite arrangement. The token supply is on chain; the reserves are off it, in a conventional custodian's book. MiCA in the EU 113 and the GENIUS Act in the United States 114 both require reserve backing and disclosure, so the two books are compared on a schedule, by attestation. That is an A3 copy with external detection, however modern the technology on either side.

1100 Gatekeepers: identity and security

Identity and security infrastructure decides who may change the ledger. Most of it is about the system rather than the money (A5), but its strongest controls are the ones that move inside the commit.

Identity proofing establishes who a customer is, for know-your-customer obligations under the FATF standards 92. NIST SP 800-63-4, finalised in July 2025, restructured proofing, added continuous evaluation metrics and introduced controls for injection attacks and forged media 115. The verified identity is a copy of documents and checks at a past date, refreshed periodically; it is the slowest plate in the stack.

Authentication is faster, and in Europe it is bound to the transaction itself. Article 5 of the PSD2 technical standards requires that the authentication code for a remote payment be specific to the amount and the payee, so that changing either invalidates it 33. That is identity expressed as a constraint on the posting, which is why the plate sits at A1 with construction-level detection. Zero-trust architecture generalises the principle: no request is trusted because of where it comes from, and every access decision is made per request 116.

Card data is the clearest example of shrinking a copy rather than protecting it. PCI DSS version 4.0.1 governs any system that stores, processes or transmits cardholder data 117. Tokenisation replaces the card number with a surrogate everywhere except a vault, so that only the vault is in scope 118; network tokens extend the idea to the payment schemes 119. Cryptographic keys live in validated hardware modules 120. The next migration is already scheduled: NIST published its first post-quantum key-encapsulation standard in August 2024 121, and a NIST draft proposes deprecating today's public-key algorithms from 2030 and disallowing them from 2035 122. Every signed record that must remain verifiable beyond those dates is a copy whose validity has an expiry.

Threat modelling 123 and an information-security management system 124 organise the rest. The useful discipline from this article's frame is to ask, for each control, which plate it protects and whether the control sits inside the commit or watches from outside it.

1110 Watching and changing: observability and DevSecOps

Observability infrastructure keeps copies of what the system did. Distributed tracing descends from Google's Dapper 125 and is now standardised by OpenTelemetry 126; service-level objectives and error budgets turn telemetry into decisions about risk 127,128. All of this is A5: it can say that a service is slow, not that money is missing.

The plate that closes the gap is a money-movement monitor: continuous comparison of the ledger with rail status, bank statements and internal projections, reported as a service-level indicator whose unit is currency. Stripe describes its Data Quality platform, built on Ledger, as explaining more than 99.9999% of money movement 2. In this article's terms, that monitor is the detection mechanism for most of the A2 and A3 plates, and it is the single highest-leverage piece of observability a fintech can build.

DevSecOps governs the other kind of change: change to the code. Continuous delivery 129 and the research behind Accelerate 130 showed that frequent, small, automated releases improve both speed and stability. (The research programme behind that book is also called DORA, which is unrelated to the EU regulation of the same acronym.) The Secure Software Development Framework 131, SLSA's provenance levels 132 and the US executive order on software supply chains 133 define what a build should be able to prove about its own contents. Log4Shell showed the cost of not knowing where a component runs 134.

Two incidents mark the boundaries. In August 2012, Knight Capital deployed new trading code to eight servers and missed one; the old code on that server reacted to a repurposed flag and the firm lost more than $460 million in 45 minutes 135. The record of what was deployed was a copy of what was running, and nothing compared them. In April 2018, TSB migrated its customers to a new platform and was left with prolonged service failures; the FCA and PRA fined it a combined £48.65 million in December 2022 136, and the independent review attributed the outcome to the migration's planning and testing rather than to any single defect 137. Both are the A5 rung failing in a way that reached the money.

1115 Putting the stack together

Read across the sections, the fifteen layers sort into three rings around the journal, and the rings rather than the layers are the useful unit of architecture.

The inner ring is everything that must be true at the moment of posting: the journal and its balance projection, the idempotency record, the authentication bound to amount and payee, the token vault, the fraud decision when it gates authorisation, and the outbox row. These components share a transaction or a synchronous call path, and the design goal is to keep that path short. Experiment E8 prices every additional synchronous hop, and experiment E3 shows that the invariants which genuinely need coordination (no overdraft) must be enforced here with a conditional write or a lock, because nothing further out can enforce them. Technology choices in this ring are conservative for good reason: a relational or purpose-built ledger database with serialisable transactions or explicit locking, and synchronous replication where the recovery point objective is zero.

The middle ring is everything derived from the posting within seconds: the event log, balance and statement projections, streaming aggregates, caches, webhooks, rail messages and the money-movement monitor. Its components follow the commit, so its design goal is bounded staleness that is measured rather than assumed. The tools are the outbox, idempotent consumers keyed by event identifier, consumer-lag and dead-letter alerting, and continuous reconciliation whose unit is currency. Experiments E4 and E5 show where its failures come from: dual writes, and utilisation high enough that a short pause becomes a long one.

The outer ring is everything reconciled on a cycle or inferred from copies: warehouses and lakes, regulatory reports, correspondent and partner-bank balances, models, stablecoin reserves and the third-party copies made through open-banking interfaces. Its design goal is that every copy has an owner, a schedule and a control capable of seeing the defects that matter. Experiment E7 shows that the control must be keyed and row-level wherever errors can cancel, and experiment E6 that a model's performance is known only as fast as its labels mature.

The A5 rung (cloud accounts, Kubernetes, telemetry, identity records, deployment pipelines and software inventories) runs alongside all three rings. It does not hold money, but every failure in the case studies that reached money at scale, Knight Capital, TSB, Capital One and CrowdStrike among them 71,76,135,136, began there.

The ring model also clarifies build-versus-buy. Buying a payment processor, a core banking platform, a banking-as-a-service provider or a cloud does not remove a ring; it moves part of a ring into someone else's system and replaces a technical control with a contractual one. The questions to ask a supplier are the plate's four fields: what do you hold, what is it a copy of, how long may it disagree with ours, and how will we find out. The Synapse sub-ledger is the case in which nobody had answered the last of them 23.

1120 The ledger of plates

Thirty-two plates, coded on two scales, give the census in experiment E10. The grid below places each plate by rung and detection mode. Seventeen of the thirty cells are occupied.

Three regularities stand out. First, construction-level detection occurs only at A0, A1 and A2. The seven K plates are the journal, the balance projection maintained in the posting transaction, the idempotency key, authentication bound to amount and payee, the token vault, the transactional outbox and the consensus of a public chain. Each is a case of a copy being written in the same atomic step as the thing it copies. Nothing further away can be protected that way.

Second, detection weakens with distance. Mean detection strength (K = 4 down to N = 0) is 4.00 at A0, 3.50 at A1, 2.25 at A2, 1.14 at A3 and 1.25 at A4. The Spearman rank correlation between rung and detection strength is −0.572 across all thirty-two plates and −0.718 across the twenty-seven that hold or reason about money; A5, which describes the system rather than the money, is the exception, because infrastructure has its own continuous reconcilers.

Third, the weakest cells are at the stack's edges, not in its newest technology. Six of the eleven plates at A3 and A4 are found out only externally or not at all: the balance on the phone, a third party's open-banking copy, the for-benefit-of sub-ledger, the credit model, the language-model assistant and a stablecoin's reserves. Four of those six are places where a copy leaves the institution that could check it.

The tolerated window rises with distance, as expected, from a median of a millisecond inside the commit to about 50 days for inferred copies (log10 seconds −3.00 at A1 and 6.65 at A4). The pair (rung, detection) separates 95.6% of the 496 plate pairs, more than the ten layer families do (91.7%), which is the evidence that the ladder carries information the brief's own list does not.

The practical reading is a design rule with three clauses. Keep every money-changing invariant inside the commit, where it can be enforced. For every copy outside it, write down the tolerated window and build a monitor that measures it, preferably one whose unit is money. For every copy that leaves the institution, assume nobody will check it unless a contract or regulation says who will, and when.

# E3  Exhaustive interleavings of concurrent withdrawals
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
def interleavings(n, steps):
    """All distinct interleavings of n transactions each with `steps` ordered steps."""
    out = []
    def rec(prefix, counts):
        if all(c == steps for c in counts):
            out.append(tuple(prefix)); return
        for i in range(n):
            if counts[i] < steps:
                counts[i] += 1; prefix.append(i)
                rec(prefix, counts)
                prefix.pop(); counts[i] -= 1
    rec([], [0] * n)
    return out

def nakamoto(q, z):
    p = 1 - q; lam = z * q / p; s = 1.0
    for k in range(z + 1):
        poisson = math.exp(-lam)
        for i in range(1, k + 1):
            poisson *= lam / i

    return s
