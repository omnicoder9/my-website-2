1The question

A customer opens an app with a retailer's logo, taps a button, and receives a card, a balance that earns interest, a line of credit and an instalment plan at checkout. Five different businesses may have touched that tap. The retailer owns the interface. A fintech operator ran onboarding, set the risk rules and decided who got credit. A licensed bank or e-money institution holds the charter, the deposit account and the balance sheet. A processor or middleware company keeps the ledger that says which customer owns which dollar in a pooled account. And a vendor supplies the credit model, the identity check or the cloud on which it all runs. Financial regulation was written for an institution that did all five things under one roof. Fintech is, more than anything else, the practice of taking that roof off.

That makes the most useful question about fintech law a structural one rather than a topical one. The fourteen regimes this article covers (financial, banking, securities, insurance, consumer protection, privacy, anti-money-laundering, data protection, payments, licensing, capital, reporting, model governance and AI) each impose obligations. The question is where each obligation lands once the function it governs has been split across firms. Does it follow the function to whoever now performs it? Or does it stay with the licensed entity and reach the performer, if at all, only through that entity's contracts and oversight?

The answer is not uniform, and the pattern in it is the finding of this article. Across 28 obligations drawn two from each regime, the ones that travel with the function are those whose object is an act: a sale, a disclosure, a data transfer, a payment message, a decision. The ones that stay behind are those whose object is a stock: capital, client money, a beneficial-ownership ledger, a model inventory. Act obligations average rung 3.81 on a six-rung attachment ladder; stock obligations average 0.86, a gap of 2.95 rungs that survives random recoding of the evidence (95.9% of perturbed codings keep it above one rung). Stocks sit on balance sheets, and balance sheets do not unbundle. That single observation explains a surprising amount of fintech's recent regulatory history, from the collapse of Synapse to the capital arithmetic of banking-as-a-service, and it predicts where the next failures will come from.

The article proceeds regime by regime, in the order given in the brief, grouped where two regimes share machinery. Each regime contributes two plates, numbered within the regime, each with the same four fields and a gauge that shows how far the obligation reaches into the stack. Nine deterministic experiments, run at build time and reproduced verbatim in Appendix A, supply every quantitative claim the article makes about capital, monitoring, explanation, backtesting, re-identification and reconciliation. Legal facts are stated as of 4 October 2026 and cited to primary instruments wherever one exists.

2Method: an attachment ladder

Each plate is one obligation, stated as a duty someone must discharge. Four fields describe it the same way every time: Obligation (what must be done), Hooked to (the legal fact that triggers it: an activity, an instrument, a role, a balance sheet, a contract), In the stack (which of the five tiers it reaches and how), and Gives way (the condition under which the obligation stops doing its job). The five tiers are those in the figure above: customer interface (APP), program manager (PGM), licensed entity (LIC), processor or ledger (PRC), and model or cloud vendor (VND).

For each obligation every tier receives a code from 0 to 5, and those codes form the attachment ladder. A tier at 5 is bound by its own law: the rule attaches to what that tier does, whoever it is. At 4 a supervisor can examine or designate it directly. At 3 a rule or scheme prescribes contract terms the tier must accept. At 2 it is reached only through the licensee's required oversight. At 1 the licensee answers for the outcome but nothing addresses the tier itself. At 0 nobody answers. The plate's rung is the code of the tier that actually performs the function: the question is not whether the law reaches the bank (it nearly always does) but whether it reaches the place where the work now happens.

Three further labels are recorded but kept off the ladder so that they can be tested against it. The protected interest says whose loss the obligation prevents: solvency and the system (S), market integrity (M), the customer (C), the data subject (P) or the state's interest in suppressing financial crime (F). The object says whether the obligation is measured on an act or on a stock. The force says whether, on 4 October 2026, the instrument is binding and operative, guidance or a private standard, enacted but not yet operative (or enjoined), or absent.

The coding rule is the most specific operative instrument of general application in the United States (federal), the United Kingdom or the European Union, whichever the plate names. Two limitations follow and are stated here rather than buried. First, the codes are judgements by the author from the cited texts; Experiment E2 tests how much the headline depends on them by recoding at random. Second, the 28 obligations were chosen as functions that unbundling actually moves, so the performer is outside the licensee in 27 of 28 plates by construction. The ladder measures attachment, not permissiveness: an obligation that squarely binds a performer and forbids what it does still scores 5.

3Perimeter and licence

Financial regulation begins with a perimeter. In the United Kingdom the general prohibition in section 19 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 forbids anyone to carry on a regulated activity by way of business unless authorised or exempt, and section 23 makes breach a criminal offence [1]. The design choice that matters for fintech is that the prohibition is drawn around activities rather than around types of firm, so it follows the function by default (FR1). The same Act also supplies the main exception: under section 39 a principal firm may accept regulatory responsibility for an appointed representative, letting a fintech operate without its own permission (FR2). The FCA tightened the regime in 2022 because principals had been hosting more representatives than their oversight could carry [2]. The appointed-representative model is the clearest example in UK law of a deliberate design in which the performer is reached only through someone else, and it is no accident that it was where the supervisory pressure built.

Licensing turns the perimeter into a gate. Its fintech-relevant forms divide sharply by whether they attach to the activity or to the entity. The EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation requires authorisation to provide crypto-asset services and grants a passport across the Union (LC1); it applied in full from 30 December 2024, and its transitional period for firms operating under national regimes ended on 1 July 2026 [3]. The United Kingdom chose to bring cryptoassets inside FSMA instead of writing a standalone code. The Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Cryptoassets) Regulations 2026 were made on 4 February 2026, the FCA published its final policy statements on 30 June 2026, the authorisation window opened on 30 September 2026, and the regime commences on 25 October 2027 [4,5].

In the United States the comparable story is split between a statute that passed and one that did not. The GENIUS Act, signed on 18 July 2025, makes issuance of a payment stablecoin to US persons an activity reserved for permitted issuers holding one-for-one reserves and forbids paying holders interest or yield (LC2) [6]. Its effective date is the earlier of 18 January 2027 or 120 days after final regulations [7]. The OCC proposed a comprehensive 376-page implementing rule in February 2026, published in the Federal Register on 2 March [8]; the statutory rulemaking deadline of 18 July 2026 passed without final rules, and the Comptroller has said the OCC aims to finalise by November 2026 [9]. The market-structure bill that was meant to complete the picture did not survive. The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act passed the House in July 2025 [10], but on 15 September 2026 the Senate rejected cloture on the motion to proceed by 49 votes to 50, which practitioners read as ending its prospects in this Congress [11]. The agencies have since moved to use existing authority, with the CFTC sending a crypto market-structure rulemaking to OIRA two days after the vote [12].

4Banks, balance sheets and capital

Banking regulation attaches to the charter, and the charter attaches to the balance sheet. That is why banking-as-a-service exists: a fintech without a charter partners with a sponsor bank that holds the deposits, issues the cards and books the loans. Supervisors have two instruments for reaching the partner. The Interagency Guidance on Third-Party Relationships of June 2023 tells banks to manage partner risk through planning, due diligence, contract negotiation, ongoing monitoring and termination, and makes clear that using a third party does not diminish the bank's responsibility [13]. The Bank Service Company Act goes further: services performed for a bank are subject to regulation and examination as if the bank performed them itself (BK2) [14]. The statute reaches core processors and technology vendors directly, which is why BK2 sits at A4. Its limit is capacity rather than authority; it reaches far more providers than supervisors ever examine.

The failure mode that the guidance cannot fix is a stock. When a fintech offers accounts through a sponsor bank, customer funds commonly sit in a single custodial account for the benefit of many end users. Federal deposit insurance can pass through to each beneficial owner, but only if the custodial relationship is disclosed in the bank's account records and the ownership interests can be established from records that may be maintained by the custodian or a third party [15]. In the BaaS stack those records live at the middleware or the fintech (BK1). No rule obliged that tier to keep its ledger to the bank's standard, and so when Synapse, a middleware provider sitting between fintech programs and several partner banks, filed for bankruptcy in April 2024, the partner banks' records could not say who was owed what [16]. The FDIC's response in September 2024 was a proposed rule requiring banks themselves to maintain direct, reconciled records of beneficial ownership for custodial accounts with transactional features [17], which is to say a rule that would move the stock back inside the licensee. BK1 is one of two plates at A0.

Experiment E9 shows why the granularity of reconciliation, not its frequency, is the variable that matters. It simulates a year of 20,000 sub-ledger postings a day in a pooled account, with misallocations (credited to the wrong end user) and omissions (never posted to the sub-ledger) each occurring at five in a hundred thousand postings; 379 misallocations and 362 omissions totalling $163,318 result. Reconciling the pooled account against the sum of the sub-ledger daily, which is what an aggregate check does, catches every omission and no misallocation, because misallocations net to zero. The mean outstanding misstatement under daily aggregate reconciliation is $41,610; under daily per-owner reconciliation it is $447, about 93 times smaller. Reconciling the aggregate only every 90 days barely changes the picture ($51,326), because the error that matters was never visible to an aggregate check at all.

Capital is the purest stock in finance, and it does not travel. The Basel framework requires common equity Tier 1 of at least 4.5% of risk-weighted assets, Tier 1 of 6% and total capital of 8%, plus a capital conservation buffer of 2.5% in common equity, and a leverage ratio of Tier 1 capital to an unweighted exposure measure of at least 3% [18]. The EU implements this through the Capital Requirements Regulation [19], with the 2017 Basel III finalisation [20] applied through CRR3 [21]; US banks face a Tier 1 leverage minimum of 4% [22]. Every loan or deposit a partner originates lands on the sponsor's balance sheet (CA1), and the partner holds no capital for it. The United States is still settling the final Basel package: on 19 March 2026 the agencies re-proposed it with a single expanded risk-based approach for the largest firms and comments due on 18 June 2026 [23], estimating system-wide CET1 relief of $87.7 billion across the package [24].

The interaction between the two capital measures explains a quiet economic fact about BaaS. Experiment E3 computes where the leverage ratio binds. With the Tier 1 minimum plus buffer at 8.5% of risk-weighted assets, a bank whose risk-weighted assets are less than 35.3% of its exposure is constrained by the 3% Basel leverage ratio rather than by risk weights, and under the 4% US minimum the threshold rises to 47.1%. A stylised sponsor bank whose partners' deposits are parked in reserves and Treasuries, with a quarter of its balance sheet in partner consumer loans, has a density of 30%. On $10 billion of exposure its risk-based Tier 1 requirement is $255 million but the US leverage requirement is $400 million: leverage binds by $145 million. Each further billion of partner deposits placed at the central bank costs nothing in risk-based capital and $40 million under leverage. This is the mechanism by which deposit-gathering fintech programs consume sponsor capital even when the deposits carry no credit risk at all.

Non-bank licences have their own capital regimes, and they encode the act-versus-stock distinction directly. A payment institution under PSD2 must hold initial capital of €125,000 for most services and own funds calculated by one of three methods; Method B applies a sliding scale to one-twelfth of the previous year's payment volume, from 4% of the first €5 million down to 0.25% above €250 million [25]. An e-money institution needs initial capital of €350,000 and, under Method D, own funds of at least 2% of average outstanding e-money [26]. One measures a flow, the other a stock. Experiment E4 compares them. For a firm with €1 billion of annual volume, Method B requires €1,058,333, or 10.6 basis points of volume; at €100 billion the marginal rate falls to 2.2 basis points. Method D overtakes Method B once the float exceeds 63.5% of a month's volume at €1 billion, and only 20.6% at €10 billion. A wallet with €1 billion of annual volume that holds a month of customer balances needs €1,666,667 under Method D, 1.57 times the Method B figure. CA2 records that this capital, like the bank's, is held entirely by the licensee for its agents and distributors.

5Securities and insurance

Securities law attaches to the instrument. Under Howey, an investment contract exists where money is invested in a common enterprise with an expectation of profits from the efforts of others, regardless of the form the arrangement takes [27]. Because the test looks at economic substance, it follows the function into whatever wrapper a fintech chooses (SC1). Its weakness is that substance can depend on the manner of sale. In SEC v Ripple the Southern District of New York held that the company's institutional sales of XRP were investment contracts while its programmatic sales on exchanges were not, so the same token sat on both sides of the line depending on how it reached the buyer [28].

The conduct side of securities regulation reaches fintech through the broker-dealer. Regulation Best Interest requires a broker-dealer recommending a security to a retail customer to act in that customer's best interest and to identify and mitigate conflicts [29]. In 2023 the SEC proposed a dedicated rule for conflicts arising from predictive data analytics and similar technologies used to steer investor behaviour [30]; on 12 June 2025 it withdrew that proposal along with 13 others, stating that it did not intend to finalise them [31]. SC2 therefore rests on the general duty alone. It binds the broker-dealer and nobody else in the stack, so a nudge built into an interface the broker licenses from a vendor is reached only through the broker. The enforcement record shows the duty is real at that tier: in 2021 FINRA ordered Robinhood to pay about $70 million, then a record, for systemic supervisory failures including misleading customers and approving them for options trading inappropriately [32].

Insurance regulation splits along exactly the act-stock line. Solvency II requires an insurer to hold a solvency capital requirement calibrated to the value-at-risk of its basic own funds at a 99.5% confidence level over one year [33], and internationally the IAIS adopted its Insurance Capital Standard in December 2024 [34]. That capital is held by the licensed carrier (IN1), even when an insurtech or managing agent prices and binds the risk under delegated authority. Distribution is different. The Insurance Distribution Directive applies to anyone who distributes insurance, including ancillary intermediaries such as retailers and platforms selling cover alongside goods, and requires them to act honestly, fairly and professionally in the customer's best interests, to establish the customer's demands and needs, and to give standardised product information [35]. Embedded insurance at checkout is therefore reached in its own right (IN2), except where small ancillary sales fall within the Directive's exemption and the insurer or intermediary must ensure compliance instead.

Insurance AI governance in the United States follows the oversight model. The NAIC's model bulletin of December 2023 requires insurers to maintain a written programme for the responsible use of AI systems and states that insurers remain responsible for decisions made with systems supplied by third parties [36]; a 2026 study in the NAIC's own journal counts 24 states as having adopted it [37]. Colorado went further by regulation, requiring life insurers using external consumer data and predictive models to maintain a governance and risk-management framework and to test for unfair discrimination [38]. Both reach the model vendor at A2: through the carrier.

6Payments

Payment regulation in the EU is still the second Payment Services Directive and the second E-Money Directive. PSD2 requires a payment institution to safeguard funds received from payment service users, either by segregating them in a separate account or investment in secure low-risk assets, or by covering them with an insurance policy or comparable guarantee [25]; EMD2 applies the same safeguarding to funds received in exchange for e-money [26]. The duty belongs to the licensee (PY1). An agent or distributor that receives funds on the licensee's behalf carries no separate duty, so the moment of greatest exposure is the interval in which funds sit in a partner's account before reaching the safeguarding account. PY1 is a stock, and like every other stock in the census it sits at A1.

The replacement framework is agreed but not yet applying. The Commission proposed PSD3 and a directly applicable Payment Services Regulation in June 2023; political agreement followed on 27 November 2025 and the final compromise texts were published on 23 April 2026 [39]. The package moves conduct rules into the Regulation and folds e-money institutions into the payment-institution framework, so that a single licence covers both. It also introduces new fraud-liability and payee-verification rules and, notably for the attachment question, conduct obligations on very large online platforms and electronic communications providers where they are involved in presenting or distributing payment services [39], which is an attempt to reach the APP tier directly.

The United Kingdom has already made the most consequential payment-fraud decision of the decade. Under the Payment Systems Regulator's policy for authorised push payment scams, in-scope firms must reimburse victims of APP scams, with the cost shared equally between sending and receiving firms [40]. The requirement took effect on 7 October 2024 with a maximum reimbursement of £85,000 per claim [41]. Its attachment is distinctive: the obligation is a direction addressed to named payment service providers, which is why PY2 sits at A4. A consumer app built on a directed firm's licence is reached through that firm, and a scam that routes funds through a leg outside the scheme, such as a crypto on-ramp, escapes it.

Crypto payments add the travel rule. The EU's recast Transfer of Funds Regulation, applying from 30 December 2024, requires crypto-asset service providers to transmit originator and beneficiary information with each transfer of crypto-assets [42], implementing FATF Recommendation 16, which FATF amended again in June 2025 to strengthen payment transparency [43]. AM2 below codes it as A5 for the CASP and A0 for the self-hosted wallet, which has no obliged sender.

7Consumers

US consumer financial protection law is unusual in naming the service provider as a directly liable party. The Consumer Financial Protection Act prohibits covered persons and service providers from engaging in unfair, deceptive or abusive acts or practices, and defines a service provider to include anyone who provides a material service to a covered person in connection with a consumer financial product, such as designing, operating or maintaining it, or processing its transactions [44]. CP1 therefore sits at A5: the statute follows the function down the stack. Its one large carve-out is the merchant, whose extension of credit for its own goods is largely excluded from the Bureau's authority, so the APP tier of an embedded-credit product is mostly outside it.

Two recent orders show what the duty looks like when exercised against fintech. In October 2024 the CFPB ordered Apple and Goldman Sachs to pay more than $89 million between them over failures in the Apple Card's dispute handling and in how interest-free instalment financing was presented, naming the technology partner alongside the bank [45]. In January 2025 it ordered Block to pay up to $175 million in redress and penalties over Cash App's handling of fraud and disputes [46]. Whether such cases continue depends on enforcement appetite more than statutory reach, and that is where CP1 gives way.

The UK's Consumer Duty reaches the same distance by a different route. It requires firms to act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers and applies across the distribution chain to every regulated firm that can determine or materially influence those outcomes [47]. It does not reach unauthorised firms, which is why the 2026 extension to buy-now-pay-later matters. From 15 July 2026, interest-free instalment credit repayable in twelve or fewer instalments within twelve months, known as deferred payment credit, is regulated where it is offered by a third-party lender: the lender must be authorised, assess affordability, and meet the Consumer Duty, and customers gain the section 75 protection already available on credit cards [48,49]. The same product offered by the merchant itself stays outside. CP2 is the cleanest example in the census of a rule that fires because a function was unbundled, and it shows the corresponding exit: a merchant that brings the credit in-house leaves the perimeter.

The EU's equivalent arrives shortly. The second Consumer Credit Directive extends the consumer-credit regime to interest-free and low-value credit and strengthens creditworthiness assessment; it was adopted in October 2023 with application from 20 November 2026 [50].

8Privacy and data protection

Privacy regulation in US financial services rests on the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, which requires financial institutions to give privacy notices and opt-outs before sharing nonpublic personal information with nonaffiliated third parties [51]. Two features make it reach further than its reputation suggests. The statutory definition of financial institution is broad enough to cover most fintechs, and Regulation P limits what a recipient may do with information it receives: a nonaffiliated third party that obtains data under an exception may use and redisclose it only within that exception [52]. Because the reuse limit binds the recipient in its own right, PV1 sits at A5. The Federal Trade Commission's Safeguards Rule adds security requirements for non-bank financial institutions, including oversight of service providers and, since May 2024, notification to the FTC of security events affecting 500 or more consumers [53].

Open banking was meant to be the US's major data-access reform and is instead its clearest example of an obligation that exists without force. The CFPB's personal financial data rights rule of October 2024 required data providers to make covered data available through developer interfaces and required authorised third parties to certify to limits on collection, use and retention [54], a design that reaches aggregators and fintechs through a prescribed commitment rather than their own licence (PV2, A3). On 29 October 2025 a federal court in Kentucky enjoined the Bureau from enforcing the rule while it reconsiders [55], and on 6 August 2026 the Bureau sent a reconsideration proposal to OIRA [56]. Until a new rule exists, the screen-scraping and bilateral contracts the rule was meant to replace remain the operative regime.

In the EU, data protection attaches to role rather than licence. The GDPR binds processors directly: they must process only on documented instructions, implement appropriate security, notify the controller of breaches without undue delay, and are liable for damage caused by processing that infringes their obligations [57]. Every tier that processes personal data is therefore bound by its own law (DP1, A5). The regime gives way when roles blur, and the Court of Justice has been tightening its reading of the decisions it covers. In SCHUFA it held that a credit agency's automated establishment of a probability value can itself be an automated decision under Article 22 where a lender draws strongly on it [58], and in Dun & Bradstreet Austria it held that the right of access requires a meaningful explanation of the procedure and principles actually applied in automated credit assessment [59]. Payment-card data, by contrast, is governed largely by private ordering: the PCI Data Security Standard, now at version 4.0.1, is carried down the acquiring chain by card-scheme contracts (DP2, A3) [60]. The cost of falling short is not only contractual. In 2020 the OCC assessed an $80 million penalty against Capital One after a 2019 breach in its cloud environment, citing failures to establish effective risk assessment before migrating significant IT operations to the cloud [61].

The reason these regimes need to reach the data itself, and not only the institution holding it, is that financial data is unusually identifying. De Montjoye and colleagues showed in 2015 that four spatiotemporal points were enough to uniquely re-identify 90% of individuals in three months of credit-card records for 1.1 million people [62], a direct challenge to anonymisation by suppression of names in the k-anonymity tradition [63]. Experiment E8 reproduces the shape of that result on a synthetic population of 40,000 cardholders making 1,585,821 transactions across 1,500 merchants over 90 days. Knowing two merchant-and-day points makes 63.6% of cardholders unique, three makes 96.2%, and four 99.9%. Coarsening to merchant category and week slows the curve but does not stop it: four points still isolate 42.7% and six 80.8%. Data shared down a fintech stack in "de-identified" form is, for most purposes, personal data, which is why the instruments that follow the data (PV1, DP1) are the ones that matter.

9Financial crime

Anti-money-laundering law is built on customer due diligence, and its attachment rule is explicit. US banks must operate a customer identification programme and may rely on another financial institution for parts of it only under defined conditions [64]. FATF Recommendation 17 permits reliance on third parties for customer due diligence but states that ultimate responsibility remains with the relying institution [65], and the EU's new Anti-Money Laundering Regulation, directly applicable from 10 July 2027, carries the same principle into a single rulebook [66]. In a fintech partnership the program manager usually runs onboarding and a vendor runs document and biometric checks, so AM1 sits at A2: both are reached only through the bank's oversight.

The enforcement record is a catalogue of what happens when that oversight falls behind growth. In October 2024 the FCA fined Starling Bank about £29 million after it opened accounts for high-risk customers in breach of a voluntary restriction and failed to screen customers properly against sanctions lists as it grew [67]; in July 2025 it fined Monzo about £21 million for financial-crime control failures during a similar period of rapid growth [68]. In the United States, TD Bank pleaded guilty in October 2024 to Bank Secrecy Act violations in a resolution totalling about $3 billion across agencies [69], and Binance and its chief executive pleaded guilty in November 2023 in a resolution of $4.3 billion [70]. The common thread is a stock of risk (customers onboarded, transactions unreviewed) accumulating faster than the control function's view of it.

The EU is also building a supervisor to match the rulebook. The Anti-Money Laundering Authority in Frankfurt became operational on 1 July 2025 and took over the EBA's AML mandates on 1 January 2026 [71]; it will select around 40 high-risk cross-border groups for direct supervision during the second half of 2027, with direct supervision starting in 2028 [72].

Monitoring is where AML becomes a statistics problem, and the statistics are unforgiving. Experiment E5 models a transaction-monitoring detector as two normal score distributions separated by d', set to catch 90% of genuinely suspicious transactions. At d' = 3 (a good detector) and a prevalence of one in ten thousand, precision is 0.21%: an analyst reviews 477 alerts for every true case, and a book of ten million transactions a month generates about 143,144 analyst hours of review at twenty minutes per alert. A weaker detector at d' = 2 needs 2,626 alerts per true case; a much stronger one at d' = 4 needs 37. Pushing recall from 90% to 99% at d' = 3 raises the burden to 2,529 alerts per case. These are the economics behind the backlogs that recur in BaaS consent orders: a fintech partner that grows its transaction volume tenfold grows the bank's alert queue tenfold, and the obligation to clear it (RR1, below) stays with the bank.

10Reporting and resilience

Regulatory reporting is the least visible regime and the one most dependent on data the licensee does not hold. A US bank must file a suspicious activity report within the prescribed period after detecting facts that may constitute a basis for filing [73], but in a partnership the fintech sees the transactions first. RR1 sits at A2: nothing obliges the partner to surface what it sees except the partner agreement and the bank's oversight. Prudential reporting has the same shape at larger scale. The Basel Committee's principles for risk data aggregation and risk reporting, published in 2013, require banks to be able to aggregate risk data accurately and quickly across the group, including data held in outsourced systems [74]; a decade later the Committee reported that only two of the 31 global systemically important banks assessed were fully compliant with all principles [75].

Operational-resilience reporting is where the EU has gone furthest in reaching past the licensee. DORA, applying since 17 January 2025, requires financial entities to classify and report major ICT-related incidents, to include prescribed provisions in contracts with ICT third-party providers (including assistance with incidents, audit and access rights, and exit strategies), and creates a framework for the European Supervisory Authorities to oversee critical ICT third-party providers directly [76]. The ESAs designated the first critical providers on 18 November 2025 [77], 19 in all and including the largest cloud platforms [78]. RR2 records the resulting layered attachment: the bank reports (5), its processors are bound by prescribed contract terms (3), and its designated critical vendors are overseen directly (4). This is the most elaborate attempt in the census to make an obligation follow the function, and it is notable that it applies to a flow of incident information rather than to a stock. The EBA's earlier outsourcing guidelines, which DORA now overlays for ICT, had already required banks to secure audit and access rights in outsourcing contracts [79].

11Model governance

For fifteen years, model governance in US banking meant SR 11-7, the 2011 supervisory guidance that defined a model broadly, demanded effective challenge, and brought vendor models within scope while acknowledging that vendors may not disclose enough for full validation [80]. On 17 April 2026 the Federal Reserve, OCC and FDIC replaced it with revised interagency guidance that is more principles-based, tailored to a bank's model risk profile, and explicitly states that it does not establish enforceable legal requirements [81]. The same issuance withdrew the 2021 interagency statement on models supporting BSA/AML compliance [82]. MG1 sits at A2 with force recorded as guidance: vendor models are still in scope, and the vendor is still reached only through the bank's validation. The UK's equivalent, the PRA's supervisory statement SS1/23, took effect in May 2024 for banks with internal-model permissions and goes the other way, setting more prescriptive expectations including an explicit model inventory and tiering [83].

The US guidance draws one line that matters for the rest of this article. It states that generative and agentic AI models are novel and rapidly evolving and are not within its scope, and commits the agencies to a request for information on AI model risk [82]. MG2 records the consequence: there is currently no instrument in US bank supervision that says who validates a foundation model a bank did not build, or how. It sits at A0 alongside BK1, and both are stocks, an inventory of models in one case and a ledger of ownership in the other. General safety-and-soundness duties remain, and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework offers a voluntary structure [84], but neither attaches to the vendor.

Model validation depends on tests whose power is often lower than practitioners assume, and backtesting is the canonical example. The Basel traffic-light framework assesses a 99% value-at-risk model by counting exceptions over 250 trading days: four or fewer is green, five to nine yellow with an add-on to the capital multiplier, ten or more red [85]. Experiment E7 computes the zone probabilities exactly from the binomial distribution. A correctly specified model is green 89.2% of the time and red only 0.03%, so the framework rarely punishes a good model. But a model whose true exceedance rate is 2%, double what it claims, still lands green 43.9% of the time, and a model at 3% is green 12.8% and red only 22.1%. This is the known weakness of unconditional coverage tests that Kupiec formalised [86] and that Christoffersen extended to independence of exceptions [87]: with 250 observations the test has little power against moderate misspecification. Governance that relies on a single backtest is relying on a weak instrument.

12AI regulation

The EU AI Act classifies AI systems used to evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons or establish their credit score as high-risk, with an exception for systems used to detect financial fraud, and does the same for risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance [88]. The obligations attach by role. A provider, meaning whoever develops the system and places it on the market under its own name, must meet requirements on risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy and robustness; a deployer must use the system according to its instructions, assign human oversight to competent people, monitor it, and keep the logs it generates for at least six months [88]. AI1 therefore sits at A5 with the model vendor bound directly as provider. It is not yet operative: the Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, deferred the Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 [89]. A lender that substantially modifies a vendor's system, by fine-tuning for instance, can become its provider, a transition the Act defines but practice rarely notices.

US law reaches credit models through the creditor. The Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B require a creditor taking adverse action to give the applicant a statement of the specific principal reasons [90]. The CFPB has said that this applies however complex the model, that a creditor cannot cite the complexity or opacity of its algorithm as a reason for failing to give specific reasons [91], and that checking the closest box on a sample form is not enough if it does not reflect the actual reasons [92]. AI2 sits at A1: the vendor that computes the reasons owes the applicant nothing, and the method for computing them is left open. The economic literature shows why the stakes are not only procedural; algorithmic lending can reduce some forms of discrimination [93] while machine-learning models can widen gaps between groups even as they improve aggregate accuracy [94].

Experiment E6 shows that the method question is not academic even for the simplest possible model. It builds a linear logistic scorecard over eight correlated features for 20,000 applicants, declines the riskiest 30%, and computes each declined applicant's top four reasons by three defensible conventions: contribution relative to the best attainable value (the traditional "points below maximum"), relative to the population mean (which is what additive feature attribution such as SHAP reduces to for a linear model with independent reference [95]), and relative to the mean of approved applicants. The first two agree on the single top reason for 71.9% of declined applicants, on the same set of four for only 31.5%, and on the same ordered list for 6.7%. All three agree on the top reason for 71.9%. The model is fully transparent; the reasons still depend on a reference point the regulation does not specify. An opaque model inherits this indeterminacy and adds its own.

State AI law and insurance law add further layers. Colorado's insurance regulation and the NAIC bulletin, discussed above, follow the oversight model. The pattern across AI regulation is the same as across the older regimes: where a statute defines obligations by role, as the AI Act does, the vendor is reached directly; where it defines them by the regulated entity, as ECOA and the NAIC bulletin do, the vendor is reached through its customer.

14What follows

Read as a whole, fintech regulation is not lagging behind technology in general. It has been remarkably effective at following acts into new places: securities law follows the instrument, privacy and data-protection law follows the data, consumer protection names service providers, the AI Act binds the model vendor as provider, DORA reaches cloud platforms directly. What it has not learned to do is follow a stock. Capital, client money, the ledger of who owns what, and the inventory of models in use all remain obligations of the licensed entity, reached in the rest of the stack only through that entity's contracts. When a stock is held, recorded or computed outside the licensee, the obligation does not follow it, and the gap is invisible until the holder fails.

Three practical consequences follow for anyone building or supervising a fintech stack. First, map the stocks, not the activities. For each balance, ledger and model the product depends on, record which tier actually holds or computes it and whether any rule binds that tier; E9 shows that an aggregate check on a stock can look healthy indefinitely while the per-owner view drifts. Second, price the licensee's capital explicitly into partnership economics, because E3 and E4 show that the cost of a partner's growth falls on the sponsor through leverage or float-based own funds even when the partner's activity is low-risk. Third, treat explanation and monitoring methods as design choices that need owners, because E5 and E6 show that the outputs regulators look at (alert queues, adverse-action reasons) are determined as much by unregulated choices of threshold and reference point as by the underlying model.

For regulators, the pattern suggests where the next instruments are likely to appear and where they will be hardest to write. The FDIC's custodial-records proposal is a stock rule; so is any future requirement that a program manager hold capital or that a model vendor maintain an inventory a supervisor can inspect. They are harder than activity rules because a stock has to be held somewhere, and the only place regulation has ever required one to be held is on a licensed balance sheet.

The limitations are real. The census is 28 obligations chosen by the author, coded by the author, across three jurisdictions; a different selection could shift the means, although E2 suggests it would need to shift a great deal to close the gap. The ladder measures legal attachment, not enforcement, and A5 on paper can coexist with no enforcement in practice, as CP1's dependence on agency appetite shows. Several instruments discussed here are moving (the GENIUS rules, the 1033 rewrite, the Basel re-proposal, PSD3 and the PSR) and will need re-coding when they settle. And every quantitative experiment is a stylised model with stated assumptions, not a calibration to any institution's data.
