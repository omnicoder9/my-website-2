The bank, unbundled

A bank is usually described by what it is: a chartered institution that takes deposits and makes loans. Robert Merton argued three decades ago that the more durable description is functional. The functions a financial system performs (clearing and settling payments, pooling resources, moving resources across time and space, managing risk, producing information, and containing incentive problems) are stable; the institutions that perform them are not 1. Read through that lens, the thirteen areas in this article's brief are not thirteen technologies. Each is a relocation. A function that used to sit inside one firm, behind one licence and on one balance sheet, is moved somewhere else: into a software platform's checkout, a middleware company's database, a data aggregator, an instant rail, a smart contract, a model, an agent, a token register, a liquidity pool or a super-app.

The economic case for relocation is old and strong. Philippon's long-run measure puts the unit cost of financial intermediation in the United States at roughly two per cent of intermediated assets for more than a century, essentially untouched by the information technology that transformed every other industry 2. Fintech's promise was to lower that cost by unbundling the bank and letting each function migrate to whoever performs it most cheaply 3. Most of what this article describes is that migration in progress.

A function inside a bank, however, travels with two things that do not move automatically when the function does. One is a balance sheet that absorbs the loss when the function fails. The other is a licence, or a statute, that says whose loss it is. Diamond and Dybvig's account of bank runs is, at bottom, an account of why the second matters as much as the first: a claim is only as good as the arrangement that guarantees it will be honoured in the bad state 4. A relocated function can be faster, cheaper and better designed than the one it replaced and still leave that question unanswered.

So the organising question here is not what each new arrangement can do. It is this: when the relocated function fails, whose balance sheet is the loss on? There are five possible answers, and they are not equally good. The loss can land on a public balance sheet (a central bank or a deposit-insurance fund), on a regulated intermediary that a rule makes answerable, on a non-bank that has promised by contract or posted a reserve, on the end user, or on nobody who can be identified. The article codes 26 arrangements, two from each area in the brief, against those five answers, and runs nine small deterministic experiments to price the mechanisms that decide which answer applies.

The short version of the result is uncomfortable for the more optimistic accounts of the field. Of 26 arrangements, 18 have a balance sheet standing above the user, but only 13 of those anchors are operative today; four are adopted, proposed or in pilot, and one rests on a judicial analogy. Eight arrangements, 30.8 per cent, leave the loss with the user or with nobody. Every arrangement for which no instrument allocates the loss lands on the user or on nobody, which sounds tautological until one notices how many of the newest arrangements are in that position. And at the bottom of the stack, where money itself is being re-engineered, none of the three arrangements has an operative anchor above the user at all.

How the plates are coded

Each arrangement is recorded on a plate with a fixed set of fields: what moves, what it moves out of, what it moves into, and what happens to the loss when it fails. Each plate is then coded on three axes. The first is where in the financial stack the relocated function acts. Six layers are distinguished, from the bottom up: money (the claim itself and its issuer), settlement (the act that makes a transfer final), ledger (the record of who owns what), rail (the channels along which instructions and data travel), decision (choosing whether, when and how much), and interface (where the customer meets the service). Figure 1 places all 26 plates on that stack.

The second axis is the anchor: whose balance sheet bears the first loss when the relocated function fails for a reason internal to that function. Macroeconomic shocks that would hit any arrangement are excluded; a ledger that disagrees with the bank, a payment sent to a fraudster, a model that denies the wrong applicant, or a token that will not redeem at par are included. The five anchors are ordered by how firm they are, from P (a public balance sheet) through B (a regulated intermediary made answerable by rule) and K (a non-bank answerable by contract or by a reserve it holds) to U (the end user) and N (nobody identifiable). That order is used only to summarise; the codings themselves are categorical.

The third axis is the status of whatever puts the loss where it is: F if an operative instrument in force allocates it, A if the instrument is adopted, proposed or in pilot but not yet operative, J if the only support is a court extending an older rule by analogy, and D if no instrument allocates the loss and it simply stays where it lands. Each plate names its reference jurisdiction, because the same technology lands on different balance sheets in different places, and that variation is itself one of the findings.

Each plate carries a small gauge, the recourse route, that draws all three codings at once. The left column is the six-layer stack with the plate's layer filled. The right column holds the five anchors in order of firmness, with the plate's anchor filled in its colour. The line between them is the route the loss takes: solid when an operative instrument fixes it, dashed when the instrument is pending, dotted when a court has supplied it by analogy, and a faint broken line ending in an open circle when nothing does. The gauge is the same size and scale on every plate, so the plates can be compared at a glance.

Three limitations should be stated before the results. The codings are judgements, made against named sources and recorded in the closing ledger so they can be disputed. The two plates per area were chosen to show contrast where contrast exists, so the count of areas whose two plates land differently is partly a product of selection and is reported as illustration rather than as a statistic. And the experiments in the lab are mechanism models with illustrative parameters, stated in full; where a parameter is calibrated to a published figure the article says so, and calibration is never presented as a test.

Embedded finance

Embedded finance is the relocation of the interface. The phrase was popularised by the argument that every company with a customer relationship would eventually offer financial products inside its own software, because the software already holds the customer, the context and the data 5. Payment acceptance was the first function to move. A vertical software platform for restaurants, gyms or clinics onboards its merchants to card acceptance as a payment facilitator, sponsored by an acquirer, and the merchant never deals with a bank directly. The infrastructure providers that make this possible now process at a scale comparable with large banks; Stripe reported total payment volume of about $1.4 trillion for 2024, up 38 per cent on the year 6.

The anchor for embedded acceptance is contractual, and it holds. Card scheme rules require a payment facilitator to be sponsored by an acquirer, and the acquirer remains answerable to the scheme for the facilitator's sub-merchants 7; commercially, the acquirer passes that exposure down to the facilitator by contract, so the facilitator is first in line for its sub-merchants' chargebacks and fraud. The loss travels up a chain of contracts that ends at a regulated acquirer, which is why the plate is coded K with status F: a non-bank is first in line, by an instrument that is in force.

Credit was the second function to move into the checkout, and here the anchor was rewritten in 2026. Deferred-payment credit, the interest-free instalment product usually sold as buy now pay later, was exempt from UK consumer credit regulation when provided by a third-party lender. The Financial Conduct Authority's own figures put such lending at about £60 million in 2017 and more than £13 billion in 2024 8, used by around 11 million people 9. From 15 July 2026 third-party lenders must be authorised or hold a temporary permission, must assess affordability, and become subject to section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act, so that a consumer can claim against the lender when goods go wrong as they could on a credit card 10,11.

The detail that makes A2 worth a plate is what the rule leaves out. Deferred-payment credit provided by the merchant itself remains unregulated, and agreements made before 15 July 2026 stay outside the regime 10. The same product, presented on the same screen, therefore has two different anchors depending on whose balance sheet funds it: the regulated third-party lender, or the customer alone. The anchor did not follow the function into the checkout; a statute had to drag it there, eight years after the market began to grow, and the statute drew its line around the legal form of the lender rather than around the function.

Banking-as-a-Service

Banking-as-a-Service relocates the ledger. A licensed bank holds pooled deposits in a for-benefit-of account; a non-bank programme manager or middleware provider keeps the sub-ledger that says which end user owns which slice of the pool; and a fintech application sits in front of both. The arrangement lets a firm without a charter offer accounts, cards and transfers, and it lets a small bank earn deposits and fees from customers it never meets.

What the arrangement does not do is move the record of ownership into a place where a deposit-insurance fund can read it. Pass-through insurance covers each beneficial owner of a custodial account only if the bank's records, or records the bank can rely on, identify the owners and their interests 12. When the middleware is the only place those records live and the middleware fails, the deposit-insurance machinery has nothing to pass through to. That is what happened at Synapse Financial Technologies, which filed for Chapter 11 on 22 April 2024 13. The frozen funds belonged to more than 100,000 consumer accounts, with about $265 million owed to end users 14, against roughly $219 million actually held at the partner banks 15. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's complaint states that Synapse's records failed to match the banks' as early as September 2023 and puts the shortfall at between $60 million and $90 million 13; one of the partner banks was already under a Federal Reserve enforcement action for its fintech programmes 16.

Plate B1 is coded N with status D because, at the moment of failure, no party was first in line. The bank pointed at the middleware, the middleware at the bank, and the instrument that eventually paid was not a contract or a deposit-insurance fund but the Bureau's Civil Penalty Fund, which in November 2025 allocated $46,248,291, roughly half the estimated shortfall, after the Bureau secured a stipulated judgment with a nominal one-dollar penalty precisely so that the fund could be used 13,15. A public balance sheet did absorb part of the loss, but only after the fact and by improvisation. That is not an anchor in the sense this article uses; it is a rescue.

Experiment E2 in the lab prices the mechanism. A sub-ledger that drifts from the bank's ledger accumulates a gap proportional to the volume posted since the last reconciliation. With postings equal to 5 per cent of balances a day and one posting in two thousand creating a persistent overstatement, a pool reconciled daily carries a mean gap of 0.003 per cent of balances at the moment of failure; reconciled once a year, 0.445 per cent. The inverse calculation is more telling. Treating the reported $265 million owed against $219 million held as a 17.4 per cent gap that accumulated over the roughly 210 days between the first mismatch and the bankruptcy, the persistent error rate needed is 1.65 per cent of postings at 5 per cent daily turnover, or one posting in 60. The same error rate under daily reconciliation would bound the worst-case gap at 0.083 per cent, 210 times smaller. Figure 2 shows the relationship.

The regulatory response has aimed at exactly that lever. The FDIC's September 2024 proposal for custodial deposit accounts with transactional features would require banks to hold beneficial-ownership records and to reconcile against them no less frequently than daily 17. The banking agencies had already restated that a bank remains responsible for activities conducted through third parties 18, and in July 2024 they addressed deposit arrangements with third parties directly 19. Those instruments do not create a new balance sheet; they move the record back to where an existing one can see it.

The contrast with B2 is the whole point. A bank that exposes its own ledger through an API, so that each programme's end users hold deposits recorded on the bank's core, has relocated distribution without relocating the record. The deposit is the bank's own liability, insured directly, and the guidance on third-party relationships keeps the bank accountable for the programme 18. Same customers, same apps, same rails; a different layer moved, and the anchor stayed where it was.

Finance-as-a-Service

Finance-as-a-Service is the broader version of the same move, and the term is used loosely. Here it means the modular supply of financial operations (acquiring, issuing, payouts, core ledgers, fraud scoring) by specialist providers to banks and non-banks alike, so that a financial product can be assembled from rented components. Two plates capture its two distinct liability structures.

When the rented component is a licensed payment institution that holds customer money, the anchor is the institution itself, backed by safeguarding rather than insurance. Under PSD2, payment institutions must protect funds received from users either by segregating them in a separate account or by covering them with insurance or a comparable guarantee, and the funds are insulated from other creditors in insolvency 20. That is a K anchor in force: a non-bank, answerable by an instrument, with a reserve. It is weaker than a deposit guarantee, because safeguarding protects against the institution's other creditors rather than against loss of the safeguarded assets themselves, but it is defined in advance, which is more than B1 had.

When the rented component is infrastructure, the anchor does not move at all. The Digital Operational Resilience Act, which has applied since 17 January 2025, leaves financial entities fully responsible for compliance with their obligations whatever they outsource 21. On 18 November 2025 the European Supervisory Authorities designated the first critical ICT third-party providers under the Act, after collecting the registers of information that financial entities must keep on their ICT contracts 22; the list comprises 19 providers, including hyperscale cloud, data-centre, network and finance-specific technology firms 23. Designation brings direct oversight of the provider's risk management. It does not make the provider liable to a single customer of a single bank.

The result is a split that the Financial Stability Board's third-party toolkit describes in general terms: the authority can supervise the concentration, while the institution remains the party that answers for the outcome 24. For a customer, plate C2 is therefore as well anchored as a traditional bank, coded B with status F. For the system it is less comfortable, because 19 providers now sit underneath a very large number of B anchors, and a common failure at one of them would hit many balance sheets at once. The anchor is firm for the individual case and correlated across cases, which is a different risk from the one this census measures.

Open finance

Open finance relocates the rail: the customer's data, and in its stronger forms the customer's ability to instruct payments, are made available to third parties through standard interfaces. The European template was PSD2, which created regulated account-information and payment-initiation services and obliged banks to give them access 20. The United Kingdom pushed further through the Competition and Markets Authority's 2017 order requiring the largest banks to build common open APIs 25. India took a different route, building a licensed category of consent managers, the account aggregators, that move data without reading it 26. The two plates separate reading from writing, because the law separates them too.

In the United States the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau finalised its personal financial data rights rule under section 1033 of the Dodd-Frank Act on 22 October 2024 27,28, with compliance dates running from April 2026 for the largest providers to April 2030 for the smallest 28. The rule never reached its first compliance date in operation. In August 2025 the Bureau issued an advance notice reopening it, asking whether a third-party fintech can be a consumer's representative at all and whether data providers may charge for access 29; as of 2026 a federal court has enjoined enforcement and the rule is being rewritten 30. The question this article asks, who pays when shared data is misused or a credential leaks through an aggregator, was never settled by the 2024 text in any case; it was left largely to the contracts between banks and aggregators 27. Plate D1 is coded N with status A: an instrument exists, it is not operative, and even in its adopted form it did not supply an anchor.

Payment initiation in the European Union is the opposite case. PSD2 makes the account-servicing bank refund an unauthorised payment initiated through a third-party provider first, and then lets the bank recover from the provider if the provider was responsible 20. The customer deals with one party, the party is regulated, and the allocation between the bank and the initiator is settled behind the customer's back. That is a B anchor in force, and it was written in 2015, before the services it governs had any scale.

The European extension from payment accounts to savings, investments, pensions and insurance has stalled. The Commission proposed the financial data access regulation in June 2023 31; in April 2026 it circulated a non-paper on options for finishing the trilogue 32; and as of September 2026 no further trilogue had been scheduled and it was uncertain whether the file would be pursued in the current Commission term 33. So both of the world's largest attempts to extend open banking into open finance were, in the autumn of 2026, adopted or proposed and not in force. Reading has proved harder to anchor than writing, which is the reverse of what one might expect from the size of the harm, and the likely reason is that a misdirected payment has an obvious victim and an obvious amount while a misused dataset has neither.

Real-time finance

Real-time finance relocates settlement time. A fast payment, in the Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures' definition, is one in which transmission of the message and availability of funds to the payee happen in real time or near it, on as near a 24/7 basis as possible 34. The arrangements now in operation differ by orders of magnitude in use. India's Unified Payments Interface, launched in 2016, processed a record 24.51 billion transactions in August 2026 35, an average of 791 million a day 36. Brazil's Pix, launched by the central bank in November 2020, reached comparable ubiquity on a smaller population because the central bank built, operated and in effect mandated it 37. In the United States the Federal Reserve's FedNow service, launched on 20 July 2023, settled 8.4 million payments worth $853.4 billion in 2025, an average of $101,435 a payment 38; by its third anniversary more than 1,800 banks and credit unions were enrolled, and the private RTP network processed 128 million transactions in the first quarter of 2026 39. FedNow's network limit was raised to $10 million from November 2025 40. Figure 3 puts the daily volumes on one scale.

In the euro area, Regulation (EU) 2024/886 made instant credit transfers compulsory: payment service providers had to be able to receive them by 9 January 2025 and to send them, at no higher price than ordinary transfers and with verification of the payee's name, by 9 October 2025 41. The speed is now a legal requirement. The anchor for what goes wrong at that speed is not.

The failure that real-time payment changes is authorised push payment fraud: the customer is deceived into sending money to a fraudster's account, and the payment, being authorised, is not an unauthorised transaction under any of the consumer-protection regimes built around stolen cards and credentials. In the United States, Regulation E's liability limits apply to unauthorised transfers 42, and a transfer the consumer was tricked into making is authorised. On a rail that makes funds available in seconds, the recovery window that batch clearing used to provide by accident disappears.

Experiment E3 isolates that effect. With victims' report delays drawn from a lognormal distribution with a median of six hours, a one-hour freeze latency at the receiving bank, and mule accounts that move funds onward thirty minutes on average after they become available, a payment that is available immediately is recovered 0.40 per cent of the time. Delaying availability by one hour raises recovery to 2.90 per cent, by four hours to 32.35 per cent, and by a full day to 87.21 per cent. Even if every victim reported with a median delay of five minutes, instant availability would allow only 10.41 per cent to be recovered. Figure 4 shows the curve. The parameters are illustrative, but the shape is not sensitive to them: when funds are final before a human can notice the deception, recovery is a race the bank cannot win, and the loss stays wherever the rule leaves it.

The United Kingdom changed the rule instead of the rail. From 7 October 2024 the Payment Systems Regulator has required sending and receiving payment firms on Faster Payments to reimburse victims of authorised push payment scams, splitting the cost equally, up to £85,000 a claim, unless the customer was grossly negligent 43. In the first year, to 30 September 2025, 88 per cent of the money lost to in-scope scams, £173 million, was returned; around 269,000 claims were reported, of which 188,000 were in scope 44. An independent evaluation published on 1 July 2026 found in-scope fraud losses fell by about 21 per cent, roughly £73 million a year, while the share of confirmed losses reimbursed rose from 54 to 65 per cent by value 43.

Plates E1 and E2 are the cleanest pair in the census. The rail is identical in kind. The anchor is the user in one and the regulated intermediaries in the other, and the only thing that differs is an instrument written after the rail existed. The European Union is moving part of the way: the payment services regulation politically agreed on 27 November 2025 would make a payment service provider refund in full where a scammer impersonated that provider's own staff, and would make online platforms liable to providers that reimbursed victims if the platform had been notified of fraudulent content and failed to remove it 45,46. That narrows the gap without closing it, and it is not yet in force.

Programmable money

The idea that a contract could execute itself through code is older than any blockchain; Szabo described smart contracts in 1997 as protocols that embed contractual terms in the systems that carry out the transaction 47, and Ethereum made the idea general by putting a Turing-complete virtual machine on a public ledger 48. The useful distinction for finance was drawn by Lee in a Federal Reserve note: programmable payments attach logic to the conditions under which a transfer of ordinary money occurs, while programmable money embeds the logic in the money itself, so that the restriction travels with the unit wherever it goes 49. The two have very different anchors.

The Monetary Authority of Singapore's purpose-bound money design wraps a condition, such as spending only at approved merchants or releasing only on delivery, around an underlying digital money that is itself unchanged 50. Programmable money proper is a different object. If the unit of account's carrier can refuse to move, a loss caused by that refusal (a payroll that does not clear because a condition misfired, a benefit that expires, a balance frozen by a defective rule) is not a loss from a counterparty's default or from fraud. It is a loss caused by the money. No framework in the census allocates such a loss, and the most important public issuer contemplating retail digital money has chosen to exclude the design rather than allocate it: the European Commission's digital euro proposal provides that the digital euro shall not be programmable money 51, a position the European Parliament's committee restated when it adopted its mandate in June 2026 52. The Bank for International Settlements' own blueprint locates programmability in a unified ledger that combines tokenised central bank money with tokenised deposits and assets, where logic acts on transfers between instruments whose legal character is unchanged 53. Plate F2 is coded N with status D. The finding is not that programmable money is dangerous; it is that the institutions closest to issuing it have treated an unanchored design as a reason not to build it.

Autonomous finance

Autonomous finance relocates the decision from the customer to software acting on the customer's behalf. The term covers a spectrum from rules the customer writes (round up every purchase and sweep the change into savings) through goal-based automation (keep the current account above a buffer, pay the cheapest debt first) to fully delegated management. The two ends of that spectrum have opposite anchors, and the reason is a definition written in 1978.

Autonomy that is framed as the customer's authority lands on the customer.

AI-native financial services

AI-native financial services relocate the decision into a learned model and, increasingly, the interface into a generative one. Adoption is no longer the question. The Bank of England and Financial Conduct Authority's 2024 survey found 75 per cent of UK financial firms already using artificial intelligence, with a further 10 per cent planning to within three years 58, and the Financial Stability Board's 2024 assessment treated third-party concentration, correlated model behaviour, cyber risk and governance as the channels through which that adoption could matter for stability 59.

Agentic banking

Agentic banking relocates the decision and the interface together. An AI agent chooses what to buy or whom to pay and executes the transaction through credentials it holds on the customer's behalf. The industry's response has been a burst of protocols, each addressing one part of the trust problem. Google's Agent Payments Protocol, announced in September 2025, represents the user's authority as a chain of cryptographically signed mandates covering intent, cart and payment 68, and Google donated it to the FIDO Alliance in April 2026 69. OpenAI and Stripe's Agentic Commerce Protocol, launched the same month, standardises how an agent hands a merchant a scoped payment token at checkout 70. Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol, announced in October 2025, lets a merchant verify that an agent presenting a Visa credential is one the network recognises 71.

Tokenized financial infrastructure

Tokenisation relocates the register of ownership onto a programmable ledger. The Bank for International Settlements describes a continuum, from assets that are merely recorded on a distributed ledger, through tokens that are the legal record, to unified ledgers where tokenised money and tokenised assets settle against each other atomically 75. The legal question at each step is whether the token is the asset or a copy of a record held elsewhere, and the answer determines the anchor.

The cost of atomicity is less discussed. Reporting on the Agorá results described settlement in seconds once liquidity is locked 82, and that qualification carries the economics. The international standard for payment systems asks only that settlement be final by the end of the value date, and intraday or in real time where needed 83; real-time finality is a choice with a price. Deferred net settlement lets banks offset payments against each other and fund only their net positions; real-time gross settlement trades that liquidity saving for the elimination of settlement risk, a trade-off the central-bank literature has understood since the RTGS systems of the 1990s 84,85. Experiment E6 measures it on random payment flows between banks. With 20 banks exchanging 4,000 payments, deferred net settlement needs liquidity equal to 11.90 per cent of gross value, while real-time gross settlement with random arrival order and no queuing or recycling needs 20.09 per cent, 1.75 times as much; across 5 to 40 banks the ratio ranges from 1.68 to 2.05. Figure 6 shows the comparison. An atomic ledger removes credit risk between participants; it does not remove the need to pre-fund, and someone's balance sheet has to carry the extra liquidity. Liquidity-saving mechanisms can recover part of the gap, which is why liquidity-saving tools are among the next phase's work 86.

Digital currencies

Digital currencies relocate money itself, the bottom of the stack. Two very different objects share the label. Brunnermeier, James and Landau argued that digitalisation could unbundle the roles of money and redraw currency areas around platforms rather than states 87. A retail central bank digital currency is a liability of the central bank held directly by the public; a payment stablecoin is a liability of a private issuer that promises redemption at par and holds a reserve to back the promise. The anchors differ by construction, and the question for each is whether the construction has been finished.

Exploration of central bank digital currencies is near universal and launch is rare. The Atlantic Council's tracker counts well over a hundred countries and currency unions exploring a CBDC, representing nearly all of world output, but only a handful of fully launched retail systems, among them the Bahamas, Jamaica and Nigeria 88. The BIS's 2023 survey found that almost all central banks were doing some form of CBDC work 89. The technical design space has been mapped since at least 2020 90, and the central monetary concern, that a retail CBDC could drain deposits from banks in a crisis, was addressed early by proposals to tier remuneration or cap holdings 91.

Decentralized finance

Decentralised finance relocates every layer at once into public code: the ledger, the market, the credit decision and the settlement. A systematisation of the field describes the result as a stack of protocols, each composable with the others, operating without a custodian 108. The BIS's critique, that the decentralisation is often an illusion because governance and key holders concentrate control 109, and the Financial Stability Board's 2023 assessment that DeFi's vulnerabilities are largely the familiar ones of traditional finance, often amplified by its design 110, both bear on the anchor. If no one is in charge, no one is first in line.

Over-collateralised lending replaces the credit officer with a liquidation threshold. Super-apps relocate the interface in the other direction: instead of finance being embedded in other software, everything else is embedded around finance. The ledger

Collecting the 26 plates gives the census in Figure 11. Ten land on a regulated intermediary, five on a non-bank by contract or reserve, three on a public balance sheet, five on the user and three on nobody. Fifteen are fixed by an operative instrument, five by an instrument that is adopted, proposed or in pilot, one by judicial analogy, and five by nothing. The combination of layer and anchor separates 97.2 per cent of the 325 pairs of plates, against 78.2 per cent for the anchor alone; 19 of the 30 possible layer and anchor cells are occupied.

Four findings stand out. First, the anchor is not predicted by the layer. The rank correlation between how low in the stack a plate acts and how firm its anchor is comes to 0.120, and every layer from settlement to interface contains both a firm anchor and a user or nobody anchor. Technology does not decide who pays.

Second, the money layer is the least anchored. All three plates at the bottom of the stack (programmable money, a retail CBDC and a payment stablecoin) lack an operative anchor above the user. The CBDC and the stablecoin are on their way to firm anchors, public and contractual respectively, but neither is in force in the reference jurisdiction, and programmable money has been excluded rather than anchored. The newest forms of money are, as of October 2026, the ones whose loss allocation is least settled.

Third, the decision layer is where users carry the most. Seven plates act at the decision layer, and three of them (automated sweeps, an agent operating the account, and automated liquidation) land on the user. Two of those three are put there by an operative rule, the Regulation E definition of an authorised transfer, which dates from the Electronic Fund Transfer Act of 1978 42,54. The oldest operative instrument in the census is doing the most surprising work: it decides that when software decides, the user pays, provided the user handed the software the keys.

Fourth, anchors are written after failures, not before. Figure 12 places the instruments that set or reset the anchors on a timeline. The PBoC's reserve rule followed concerns about misappropriation of client funds 123; the UK reimbursement regime followed years of authorised push payment losses 43; the FDIC's custodial-account proposal followed Synapse 17; the GENIUS Act followed the stablecoin failures of 2022 and 2023 98,107; the UK deferred-payment regime followed a market that grew more than two hundred-fold before it was regulated 8. The instruments that were written before their markets existed, PSD2's allocation for payment initiation and the 1978 definition of authorisation, are the ones that have held, and one of them now does work its drafters could not have imagined.

Whose balance sheet

The thirteen areas in the brief are usually presented as a sequence of capabilities: money that moves instantly, data that flows with consent, contracts that execute themselves, models that decide, agents that act, tokens that settle atomically. Each capability is real, and the lab confirms that several are as large as their advocates say. What the census adds is a single question asked of every capability: when this goes wrong, whose balance sheet is the loss on?

The answer is not determined by the technology. The same instant rail puts the loss on the payer in the euro area and the United States and on the payment firms in the United Kingdom. The same checkout credit is regulated when a third party funds it and unregulated when the merchant does. The same tokenised settlement keeps its anchor because a legal review concluded that tokenisation changes nothing about what reserves and deposits are. The anchor is set by an instrument, and the instruments arrive late: in this census, typically after a failure has shown where the loss would otherwise land.

Three practical conclusions follow. For firms building in these areas, the relevant design question is not only what the function does but which balance sheet it sits on, and the cheapest time to answer it is before a regulator answers it after a failure, as the FDIC did for the ledger and the Payment Systems Regulator did for fraud. For policy makers, the census suggests that the gaps are concentrated, not general: at the money layer, where the new instruments are pending, and at the decision layer, where a definition of authorisation written for debit cards now governs software agents. And for users, the census is a reminder that the most important number in an agent mandate, a slippage tolerance or a sub-ledger reconciliation schedule is often the only liability limit they have.

Merton's functional perspective predicted that functions would migrate to whatever institutions perform them best 1. It did not predict that the obligation to bear the loss would migrate with them, and the evidence from these 26 arrangements is that it does not, unless someone writes the rule that carries it across.

        avail_cash = cash - locked
        need = f
        if need <= avail_cash:
            rem = (avail_cash - need) + locked * 0.0 + (1 - cash)
            # remaining holders: cash left + bills (marked at par) + locked marked at zero
            return rem / (1 - f) if f < 1 else float('nan')

        short = need - avail_cash
        # solve q*(1-kappa*q/2) = short

            q = short
        else:

            if disc < 0: return 0.0

        bills_left = (1 - cash) - q

        # remaining bills marked at post-sale price

        return bills_left * mark / (1 - f)
