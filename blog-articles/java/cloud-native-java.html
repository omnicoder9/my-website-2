1Who decides, and when?

The HotSpot virtual machine was built to make its most important decisions late and from the inside. It sizes its heap from the memory it finds, picks a garbage collector from the processors it counts, loads a class the first time something touches it, and compiles whatever turns out to be hot. Almost everything that makes Java fast in steady state follows from that design: the runtime waits until it knows, then commits.

A cloud-native platform is also a late decider, but it decides from the outside. The scheduler chooses a node, the kubelet restarts a container that stops answering, the autoscaler adds replicas, a proxy retries a call, and a functions platform decides whether an execution environment exists at all. The Cloud Native Computing Foundation's own definition lists the ingredients as containers, service meshes, microservices, immutable infrastructure and declarative APIs, all aimed at systems that are resilient, manageable and observable1; the twelve-factor methodology says the same thing as rules for the process: configuration in the environment, logs as event streams, fast startup, graceful shutdown, disposability2. The orchestration model descends directly from Google's Borg and Omega, where the unit of management became the container rather than the machine3,4.

Put a Java service on such a platform and there are two sets of late decisions acting on one process, and the question this article organises everything around is simply this: for each thing that has to be settled about a running Java service, who settles it, and at what point between writing the code and answering a request? The claim the rest of the article defends is that most of what goes wrong in cloud-native Java is neither side's decision taken alone. It is the interval between them: a value fixed at one moment by one owner that another owner can still change afterwards.

Six stations and five deciders

To make that question answerable, every decision is placed on a ruler of six stations, the moments at which a value can be fixed: source (written in code), build (compiled, linked, analysed or trained), image (frozen into the container image or a snapshot), manifest (declared in the deployment specification), start (resolved when the process launches) and run (changed while it serves). Five deciders can own a decision: the JVM through its ergonomics and runtime, the application or its framework, the platform (orchestrator, kubelet, kernel or functions control plane), the mesh or proxy, and the operator or delivery pipeline. The first two live inside the process; the other three do not.

Each decision in the article is a numbered plate with four fixed fields: what is decided, by whom, when, and how it breaks. Each plate carries a small gauge, the commit strip, that draws the six stations as cells. A solid glyph marks where the decision is fixed today, shaped by decider; a dashed glyph with an arc marks where it used to be fixed, if that has moved; a small outlined glyph below the strip marks a second owner acting on the same quantity. The hatched span is the plate's exposure: it runs from the station where the value is fixed to the latest station at which something it depends on can still change. Exposure zero means the decision is taken when everything it needs is known. Exposure greater than zero means the value is a bet on a future the owner cannot see.

Thirty-one plates cover the eight subjects the article was asked to treat: the container image, Kubernetes, configuration, service meshes, health checks, observability, deployment and serverless. They were coded by hand from the specifications and documentation cited on each; the coding is a judgement and the lab reports it as data, not as a measurement. Figure 1 is the whole census on one page.

Three regularities in that map carry the argument, and they are stated here so the sections that follow can be read as evidence for or against them. First, of the ten decisions that have moved, 8 moved earlier, toward the build, and every one of those moves was made for startup or footprint; only 2 moved later, and both of those moved out of the application into infrastructure. Second, the moves toward the build bought their speed by taking on exposure: the eight had a mean exposure of 0.00 stations before they moved and 2.62 after. Third, the owners inside the process carry most of the exposure: a mean of 1.86 stations across the 14 plates they own, against 0.65 across the 17 owned outside it.

2Docker and the image

A container image is the first place a Java service can commit to anything, and for most of Java's history the JVM ignored that it was in one. Before JDK 10 it read the host's memory and processor count, so a JVM given a 512 MiB limit on a 64-core, 256 GiB machine sized its heap and its thread pools for the machine. The fix, JDK-8146115, taught HotSpot to read the cgroup's memory limit and CPU quota and was backported to 8u1918; cgroup v2 support followed in JDK 159. Container awareness did not change when the JVM decides, which is still at start, but it changed what it reads.

What the JVM chose on this machine

The service's first log line records the JVM's view: one available processor, a maximum heap of 2,901 MiB, and the collectors Copy,MarkSweepCompact, which are the Serial collector's young and old generations. The sandbox exposes one vCPU and about 4 GB, and that single processor was enough to make the JVM abandon G1. The default heap ceiling is a fraction of the memory the JVM believes it has, 25% unless -XX:MaxRAMPercentage says otherwise10; the session ran with 75%.

Listing 3 probes the rule directly by overriding the two inputs. With one active processor the JVM chose Serial at every memory size; with two or four it chose G1 at every size; and the heap was exactly a quarter of the memory ceiling in every row. The memory half of the rule could not be exercised this way: HotSpot treats a machine as server class only with at least two processors and roughly 1,792 MB of physical memory, the rule under which JDK 9 made G1 the default11,12, and -XX:MaxRAM changes the heap arithmetic without changing what the server-class test reads, so the 1,791 and 1,792 MB rows are indistinguishable here. That is itself a small instance of the article's thesis: two JVM decisions that look like one read different inputs at the same station.

JDK 27 removes the processor half of this surprise. JEP 523 makes G1 the default in all environments, on the evidence that G1 is now competitive with Serial at every heap size in throughput, latency, footprint and startup; an application in a constrained container that never names a collector is the only one affected7,13. The same release turns on compact object headers by default, shrinking every object header, after JEP 519 made them a product feature in JDK 2514,15. Both are density changes aimed squarely at small containers.

Memory the heap does not own

A container's memory limit covers everything the process commits, not only the heap. Native Memory Tracking on the running service reports 44.6 MiB committed in total, of which the heap was 8.0 MiB; the other 36.6 MiB is class metadata, the shared class archive, compiled code, thread stacks, arenas and symbols. That is the floor for a service with almost no classes. Experiment E1 scales it by five, an assumption standing in for a framework application with several thousand classes, adds 3% of the heap for collector side data, and asks how much of the limit is left when the heap is full (Figure 2).

The two failure directions are asymmetric. At the 25% default a full heap on the measured floor leaves 67.1% of a 512 MiB limit unused, and 73.4% of a 4 GiB one; the budget is paid for and idle. Raising the percentage recovers that memory but moves the failure from waste to death: with the five-fold floor, 75% is safe only from 804 MiB upwards and 90% only from 2,506 MiB. When the limit is crossed the kernel's out-of-memory killer ends the process with SIGKILL; there is no OutOfMemoryError, no stack trace and no chance for a shutdown hook. That is why the Dockerfile below pairs a moderate percentage with -XX:+ExitOnOutOfMemoryError: a Java heap exhaustion becomes a clean, logged exit, while native growth is left headroom rather than a ceiling.

Deciding the image's contents at build time

jdeps reports that the service needs four platform modules: java.base,java.management,java.net.http,jdk.httpserver. jlink can link exactly those into a private runtime16. The linked runtime is 42.4 MiB against 328.8 MiB for the installed JDK (243.2 MiB without its jmods directory). The measured cost of that decision was unexpected: the linked runtime started more slowly than the full JDK, reaching readiness in 238 ms against 130 ms, because the JDK ships a default class-data-sharing archive17 and a freshly linked image does not. Regenerating one with --generate-cds-archive restores the startup (135 ms) at a size of 69.5 MiB. The runtime's contents moved from run to build (plate D03), and the startup it had been getting for free at run time had to be re-bought at build time too.

The larger move is Project Leyden's ahead-of-time cache18, the successor to application class-data sharing, which has let an archive hold application classes since JDK 1019. A training run records which classes the application loads and links and, from JDK 25, method profiles; production runs map the result instead of repeating the work20–22. JEP 516 in JDK 26 made the cache independent of the collector, so ZGC users can have it too, and cites the Spring PetClinic sample starting 41% faster with some 21,000 classes already loaded and linked23. On the reference service, one training run wrote a 25.2 MB cache and readiness fell from 130 to 77 ms, 41% less; the first answered request, which pulls in java.net.http, fell from 726 to 405 ms. The linked runtime with its own cache was fastest of all at 67 ms (Figure 3).

The cache is a bet that production will look like training. Listing 4 shows what happens when it does not: a cache trained against one runtime image and used with another is rejected with an error in the log, and by default the JVM simply starts without it. Only -XX:AOTMode=on turns the mismatch into a failure to start. The Inside.java guidance is explicit that the training run and all following runs must be essentially identical24. With JDK update releases now moving to a monthly cadence25, every patch is a new runtime and a new cache; a pipeline that rebuilds the image but not the cache will lose the startup gain silently, which is exactly the kind of quiet fallback the article's exposure measure is designed to flag.

Listing 5 assembles those decisions into an image. Each command it runs was executed in this build, but no container engine was available in the sandbox, so the file itself was not built here. Three details matter. The build is multi-stage, so the compiler, jdeps and the training run never reach the final image26. The training run uses the same linked runtime and jar that production will use, because the cache is checked against both. And the entry point uses the exec form, so the JVM is process 1 and receives SIGTERM directly rather than through a shell that may not forward it27. Tools such as Jib and Cloud Native Buildpacks, and Spring Boot's layered jars, make a further build-time decision about layer order, placing dependencies that rarely change beneath application classes that change on every commit, so a rebuild invalidates and ships only the top layer28–31.

3Kubernetes

Kubernetes asks a Java service two quantitative questions in the manifest and then enforces the answers at run time with mechanisms the JVM cannot see. Requests are what the scheduler reserves when it places a pod; limits are what the kernel enforces afterwards; and the relation between them sets the pod's quality-of-service class, which orders eviction when a node runs short of memory32,33. A JVM makes its own sizing decisions once, at start, from whatever those limits say. From that point there are two owners of one resource, binding at different stations (plates K01, K02, K05).

CPU limits are enforced per period, not on average

A CPU limit is not a speed. The kernel's CFS bandwidth controller gives a cgroup a quota of CPU time per period, 100 ms by default, and when the quota is spent every thread in the group waits for the next period34,35. A JVM starts several threads that do not serve requests: JIT compiler threads, collector threads and the like, sized at start from the processor count. During warm-up they are busy. Experiment E2 simulates the controller at one-millisecond resolution for a request needing 40 ms of CPU on one thread, arriving at a random point in the period (Figure 4).

Warm, the request takes its 40 ms at every quota. With a one-core limit and 150 ms of compiler and collector work per period competing, its median latency is 152 ms and its 99th percentile 187 ms, although the container's average utilisation over a second never exceeds its limit; at half a core, even 60 ms of background work pushes the median to 340 ms. With two cores the same work fits inside each period and nothing is throttled. The latency comes from the granularity of enforcement, which a utilisation graph averaged over a minute cannot show. That is why many operators set a memory limit but leave CPU with a request only, as in Listing 6; it is a trade of isolation for latency and should be made deliberately rather than by default.

Autoscaling on a signal the JVM distorts

The horizontal pod autoscaler computes a desired replica count as the current count multiplied by the ratio of the observed metric to its target, rounded up, and ignores ratios within a tolerance of the target36. CPU is the usual metric. A newly started JVM spends CPU compiling code, so a scale-out adds pods whose CPU is high for reasons that have nothing to do with load, and the next evaluation can read that as a reason to scale out again (plate K03). The autoscaler's stabilisation windows, scaling on request rate rather than CPU, and moving profile collection to the build with an AOT cache all weaken the loop; none of them is on by default.

Termination is a race against routing

When a pod is deleted, Kubernetes marks it terminating, runs any preStop hook, sends SIGTERM, waits up to the grace period (30 seconds unless set) and then sends SIGKILL37. In parallel, and asynchronously, the pod is marked as terminating in the EndpointSlices that tell proxies and load balancers where to send traffic38. Nothing orders these two paths. A process that closes its listener as soon as SIGTERM arrives will receive requests from components that have not yet heard it is leaving. The reference service handles this the way Listing 2 showed: readiness drops to 503 immediately, the process keeps serving for a configurable drain interval, and only then stops accepting. Spring Boot's graceful shutdown is the framework-level version of the same sequence39.

How long to drain depends on a distribution the application cannot observe. Experiment E4 assumes the last router stops sending traffic after 0.3 s plus a lognormal delay with a 1 s median, and 200 requests per second reaching the pod; both are assumptions, and the shape is the point (Figure 5). With no drain the expected loss is 300 failed requests per terminated pod, about 6,007 over a 20-pod rolling update. A 5 s drain reduces that to 1.09, and 8 s to 0.11, because the drain has passed the propagation's 99.9th percentile of 6.73 s. The drain and the service's own shutdown work must both fit inside the grace period, which is why the manifest checks DRAIN_SECONDS < GRACE_SECONDS < terminationGracePeriodSeconds and the build asserts that ordering when it validates the listing.

Resizing a pod whose heap was sized at start

In-place pod resize lets the platform change a running container's CPU and memory without recreating the pod. It reached beta and was enabled by default in Kubernetes 1.33 and became stable in 1.35; 1.36 extended it to pod-level resources as a beta, and 1.37 was released on 26 August 202640–43. For most processes this is simply good. For a JVM it creates a new instance of the article's central problem: the heap ceiling was computed at start from the old limit. Raising the memory limit gives the heap nothing; lowering it leaves a heap that can legally grow past the new limit. The honest configuration is the one in Listing 6: CPU may be resized in place, memory restarts the container so that the JVM can decide again. Pools sized from the processor count at start will not follow a CPU resize either, which matters less but is not nothing.

4Configuration management

The twelve-factor rule is short: configuration, meaning everything likely to vary between deploys, is stored in the environment and kept strictly separate from code2. Kubernetes supplies it through ConfigMaps and Secrets, exposed either as environment variables or as files in a mounted volume44,45. Those two exposures behave differently after start. Environment variables are fixed for the life of the process; a mounted ConfigMap or Secret is updated in place by the kubelet some time after the object changes, unless it was mounted with subPath44. Whether a rotated credential ever reaches the code depends on something the manifest does not say: whether the application reads the file again (plate C03).

Precedence is a decision too

Java frameworks do not read one source. Spring Boot resolves each property from an ordered list of well over a dozen sources, from defaults through packaged and external property files, profile-specific files, environment variables and system properties to command-line arguments, with later sources overriding earlier ones46. MicroProfile Config does the same with numeric ordinals, by default 400 for system properties, 300 for environment variables and 100 for a packaged microprofile-config.properties47. The manifest therefore does not decide a value; it contributes a candidate, and the framework decides at start which candidate wins (plate C01). An environment variable left behind by a base image or a debugging session silently outranks the file an operator edited.

Fail before listening

The most useful configuration decision is when an invalid value is noticed. The reference service resolves and validates every setting before it opens a socket, collects all problems rather than the first, and exits with status 78, EX_CONFIG in the BSD sysexits convention48. Listing 7 is that path, run with a non-numeric port and a drain longer than the grace period. A pod that fails like this never becomes Ready, so a rollout stops at the first bad replica instead of routing traffic to it. Spring Boot reaches the same place declaratively: a @ConfigurationProperties record annotated with @Validated is bound and checked while the context starts, and a constraint violation stops the context (Listing 8)46. Validation left until first use produces the opposite outcome, a pod that passes readiness and fails its first real request (plate C02).

Configuration that stopped being configuration

Ahead-of-time compilation moves some decisions that look like configuration to the build. Spring Boot's native-image support states the consequences plainly: the classpath is fixed at build time, beans cannot change at run time, and conditions such as @Profile and @ConditionalOnProperty are evaluated when the image is built rather than when it starts49. Quarkus has long distinguished build-time properties, fixed when the application is built, from run-time ones50. The environment variable in the manifest is still read; it just no longer chooses which components exist (plate C04). That is the largest exposure in the configuration family: three stations between the build that fixed the wiring and the start that was supposed to choose it.

5Service meshes

In a system of microservices, each independently deployable and talking to the others over lightweight network calls51, a service mesh moves the transport concerns of a call out of the process and into proxies the platform manages: encryption and identity, retries and timeouts, traffic splitting, and the telemetry of every hop. The classic form injects an Envoy sidecar into every pod. Istio's ambient mode, generally available since Istio 1.24 in November 2024, replaces that with a shared per-node layer-4 proxy, the ztunnel, and optional layer-7 waypoint proxies, explicitly to remove the per-pod resource cost and the need to restart applications when the mesh is upgraded52. For a Java service the attraction is subtraction: keystores, truststores, retry libraries and per-client timeout settings can all leave the application.

Identity moved out and later

Transport identity is one of only two decisions in the census that moved later rather than earlier. It used to be a keystore baked into the image or mounted at start; in a mesh it is a short-lived certificate issued to the workload and rotated by the mesh's certificate authority while the process runs, naming the workload with a SPIFFE identity rather than a host name53. Nothing in the Java code needs to know. The plate's failure mode is the residue of the old arrangement: an application that still terminates TLS itself, or pins a certificate, or traffic that bypasses the proxy and so bypasses the identity (plate M01).

Two owners of one retry

Retries are where a mesh most often adds a second owner rather than replacing the first. Istio retries failed requests by default, with its behaviour set per route54; Java HTTP and RPC clients commonly retry too; and a call that crosses an outbound sidecar and an inbound one can be retried at every hop. Experiment E5 computes the load reaching the bottom service exactly, for nested layers that each make up to three attempts (Figure 6).

When failures are rare the layers barely matter: at a 1% attempt failure rate every configuration sends about 1.01 calls per request. As the bottom service degrades they compound. At a 50% failure rate three layers send 2.0; at 90% they send 9.42; and in a full outage, when retries can do nothing, they send 27 for every request, and four layers 81. The load multiplies exactly when the service is least able to absorb it, which is the mechanism the Google SRE book describes for cascading failures and the reason the Amazon Builders' Library argues for retrying at a single point in the stack55,56. A retry budget, as Linkerd implements it, bounds extra attempts to a fraction of normal traffic; at 20% per layer the full-outage multiplier falls to 1.728 for three layers57.

Timeouts compound the other way. If the outer caller gives up at 800 ms and an inner layer allows 2 s per attempt, work continues for a request nobody is waiting for; the tail of a fan-out is set by its slowest component, so wasted work in the tail is expensive58. A bounded timeout at every integration point is one of the oldest stability patterns for production services59. The rule that falls out of the plates is not "use the mesh" or "use the library" but one owner per concern. Listing 9 gives the mesh the whole budget for one call: a total timeout of 800 ms that includes its retries, two retries of 300 ms each, and retry conditions limited to failures that are safe to repeat. The Java client for that call should then be configured not to retry at all.

Who starts first

A sidecar is useless if the application's first outbound call happens before it is ready, and startup code that fetches remote configuration or opens a connection pool does exactly that. Kubernetes' native sidecars, init containers with restartPolicy: Always that start before the application containers and keep running, became stable in 1.3341,60. That turned a race into a declaration in the manifest (plate M04). Ambient mode removes the question for layer 4, because the node proxy is already running when the pod starts.

6Health checks

Kubernetes asks three different questions of a container and acts differently on each answer61. A failing liveness probe gets the container killed and restarted. A failing readiness probe takes the pod out of the Service's endpoints, so it receives no new traffic, without restarting it. A startup probe, while it has not yet succeeded, suspends the other two, so that a slow boot is not mistaken for a hang. The platform owns the consequences; the application owns the endpoints; the manifest owns the timing. Three owners at three stations is the reason health checking goes wrong so often.

Frameworks model the same split. Spring Boot exposes liveness and readiness as separate health groups backed by application availability states, and its documentation cautions against making liveness depend on external systems, because a shared dependency failing would restart every instance at once62. MicroProfile Health defines liveness, readiness and startup checks as separately annotated procedures63. The reference service implements the three as three endpoints with deliberately different logic: liveness reports only that the process can answer; startup reports that initialisation has finished; readiness reports the lifecycle phase, and is the only one that changes during shutdown.

The startup budget

Before startup probes existed, a slow JVM was protected by initialDelaySeconds on its liveness probe. That turns boot time into a race with a fixed deadline: with an initial delay of 15 s, a period of 10 s and a failure threshold of 3, a process that has not answered by about 35 s is killed. Experiment E3 draws boot times from a lognormal distribution with a 20 s median, an assumption for a framework application on a constrained node, and counts the boots killed (Figure 7).

On an ordinary day the liveness-only budget kills 8.04% of boots, which already means about one pod in twelve restarts before it has served anything. During a rollout onto busy nodes, modelled as boots 1.8 times slower, it kills 52.72%, and each pod needs on average 2.12 attempts, each of which adds CPU load to the nodes that were already too slow. A startup probe of 24 checks at 5 s allows 120 s and kills 0.117% of the same slow boots, while liveness, once startup has succeeded, still detects a hung process within 20 to 30 s. The two concerns, how long booting may take and how quickly a hang is noticed, had been forced into one number; the startup probe separates them (plate H03).

What each endpoint may depend on

Readiness is the right place for a dependency, within limits: a pod that cannot reach its database should stop receiving traffic. But readiness is evaluated per pod and the dependency is shared, so a database outage makes every replica unready together and the Service has no endpoints; whether that is better than serving errors is a product decision, and it should be taken on purpose. Liveness must never depend on anything outside the process. A liveness probe that checks the database converts one dependency's blip into a synchronised restart of the whole fleet, which then boots, reconnects and loads the database at the same moment (plate H01). The Spring configuration in Listing 8 includes the database in readiness and excludes it from liveness for that reason.

7Observability

Observability in a cloud-native system is built on the assumption that no one will log in to the machine. Everything the operators will ever know about a process must leave it as a stream: logs, metrics, traces, and, for the JVM, flight recordings64. Each of those streams embodies a decision about what to keep, made by some owner at some station, and in each case the interesting failures come from making that decision too early.

Logs and metrics

The twelve-factor rule for logs is that the process writes an unbuffered event stream to standard output and never manages files; routing and storage belong to the environment2. The reference service writes one JSON object per line, with the event name, level and fields as keys, which is what makes the stream machine-readable without a parsing rule per message. Its /metrics endpoint speaks the Prometheus text exposition format65; in a framework application Micrometer produces the same output from instrumentation shared with other back ends66.

The decision that matters in metrics is the label set, and it is made in source code (plate O03). Every distinct combination of label values is a separate time series, and Prometheus's own guidance warns against labels with unbounded values such as user identifiers67. Experiment E6 counts: one request counter labelled by 20 routes, 4 methods, 8 status codes and 30 pods is 19,200 series. Replace the route with the raw request path, 5,000 distinct values, and it is 4,800,000; add a user identifier with 100,000 active values and it is 1,920,000,000. The reference service labels by the matched route, never the raw path, for this reason, and the comment in Listing 1 says so.

Traces, and when they are kept

Distributed tracing descends from Google's Dapper, which propagated identifiers across service boundaries and sampled aggressively to keep overhead negligible68. The W3C Trace Context recommendation standardised the propagation headers69, and the OpenTelemetry Java agent instruments common libraries by rewriting their bytecode as classes load, with no code change70. That moves the choice of instrumentation points from source to start (plate O01), and ties it to the versions of the libraries on the classpath: a library upgraded past what the agent supports stops producing spans without an error.

Sampling is the sharper decision. A head sampler decides at the root span whether a trace is kept, before anyone knows how the request will end71. Rare failures are therefore kept at the sampling rate, like everything else. Experiment E6 gives the arithmetic (Figure 8): at a 1% head rate, 299 failing requests of one kind must occur before there is a 95% chance that even one trace of them survives; at 0.1%, 2,995. A tail sampler in the OpenTelemetry Collector buffers spans and decides after the trace completes, so it can keep every error and a small share of successes72. Listing 10 configures the agent to keep everything at the root and leaves the decision to the collector; it trades collector memory and network for a decision taken when the outcome is known73.

The flight recorder and the process that is killed

JDK Flight Recorder records JVM and application events into in-memory and on-disk buffers at low overhead; it became part of OpenJDK in JDK 11 and gained event streaming in JDK 1474,75. It answers questions metrics cannot, such as which allocation site filled the heap or which lock a thread waited on. Its weakness in a container is the way containers die. dumponexit writes the recording when the JVM exits normally, including after SIGTERM, but a container that exceeds its memory limit is killed with SIGKILL and nothing runs (plate O04)10. Keeping the recording on disk in a volume that outlives the container, as Listing 10 does, and turning heap exhaustion into a clean exit with ExitOnOutOfMemoryError, keeps the evidence for the most common way a Java container disappears. JDK 27 adds in-process redaction of JFR data, which makes recordings easier to ship off the node7.

8Cloud deployment

Continuous delivery made deployment a routine, automated and reversible act rather than an event76, and the evidence gathered in Accelerate links frequent, small, automated releases with better stability rather than worse77. On Kubernetes the mechanics are declarative: a Deployment describes the desired state, and a rolling update replaces pods at a pace set by maxSurge, maxUnavailable and minReadySeconds78; GitOps adds that the desired state is versioned, pulled and continuously reconciled79.

What runs is a digest, not a tag

An image tag is a mutable pointer; a digest is the hash of the image manifest and identifies exactly one set of bytes31. A Deployment that names a tag resolves it when each node pulls, so two replicas of one revision can run different code if the tag moved between pulls. Pinning the digest, as Listing 6 does, moves that decision from the manifest back to the image (plate L01), and it is one of the few moves toward the build that carries no exposure: nothing later can change which bytes a digest names.

Ready is not warm

A rolling update routes traffic to each new pod as soon as its readiness probe passes. A JVM is ready long before it is warm: HotSpot starts by interpreting, profiles what runs, and compiles the hot paths in tiers, so the first minutes of a new pod's life are slower than the steady state (plate L02). minReadySeconds only delays counting a pod as available; it does not reduce its share of traffic. Progressive delivery tools such as Argo Rollouts can shift traffic by weight in steps, pausing for analysis between them80, which gives each wave of new JVMs a fraction of the load while they compile.

Leyden attacks the same problem from the build. AOT method profiling in JDK 25 stores profiles from the training run so the JIT compiler can start optimising immediately rather than waiting to collect its own22; that is plate L03, a decision that moved four stations earlier and took four stations of exposure with it, because production traffic may not resemble the training workload. The next step, caching compiled code itself, is tracked as a draft JEP that had reached Submitted status by August 2026 with no target release81. Until it ships, the JVM still compiles in production; it just knows sooner what to compile.

9Serverless Java

Serverless computing hands the platform the last decision a long-running service kept for itself: whether an instance exists at all82. The provider creates an execution environment when an invocation arrives and there is none idle, keeps it for a while, and reclaims it; the application is never told how long "a while" is (plate S01). For the JVM, whose strengths all arrive after warm-up, this is the least favourable arrangement possible.

How often the tail is a cold start

Microsoft's characterisation of the entire Azure Functions workload found invocation rates spanning eight orders of magnitude, and most applications invoked very rarely: 45% at most once an hour, and 81% at most once a minute83. Experiment E7 asks when the cold start stops being a rare event and becomes the tail. With one warm instance, Poisson arrivals at rate λ and an idle keep-alive T, the probability that an invocation finds no warm instance is e−λT, so more than 1% of invocations are cold, and the 99th percentile latency is the cold-start latency, whenever λT < ln 100 ≈ 4.605. With a 10-minute keep-alive, an assumption since providers do not publish it, that is any function invoked less than 0.461 times a minute, about 28 times an hour. A function invoked once an hour is cold on 84.6% of invocations (Figure 9). For almost half the applications in the Azure trace, then, cold-start time is not the tail of the latency distribution; it is the latency.

Three ways to move initialisation earlier

Every serious answer moves work from the invocation to some earlier station, and the three current answers differ in which station and what they give up.

Snapshot the initialised process. AWS Lambda SnapStart runs a function's initialisation once when a version is published, snapshots the memory and disk state of the execution environment, and restores new environments from that snapshot; it is available on published versions only, not with provisioned concurrency84. The same idea exists in OpenJDK as Project CRaC, coordinated restore at checkpoint, with an API for resources to prepare for a checkpoint and repair themselves after restore85. The price is plate S02: one snapshot seeds many environments, so anything meant to be unique that was created during initialisation, random seeds, identifiers, cached credentials, open connections, is cloned. AWS's guidance is to generate such values inside the handler or in an after-restore hook and to use cryptographically secure generators, which its runtimes reseed86,87. Listing 11 shows the pattern: prime code paths before the checkpoint, re-derive the environment identifier and the random source after restore.

Compile a closed world. GraalVM Native Image analyses the application from its entry points at build time, includes only what it finds reachable, can run static initialisers at build time and store the resulting heap in the executable, and produces a native binary that starts without a JIT warm-up88,89. Reflection, dynamic proxies, resources and serialisation are invisible to that analysis and must be declared as reachability metadata, which is why a shared repository of metadata for common libraries exists (Listing 12)90. The decision about what code exists moves from run time to build time (plate S03), and the failure is the purest example of exposure in the census: a path that works on the JVM and fails only in the native build, only when it is taken.

Train a cache and keep the JVM. Leyden's AOT cache, described in the image section, is the third answer and the most conservative: it keeps the JIT, dynamic class loading and every Java behaviour, and moves only loading, linking and profiling to a training run18,20,23. On the reference service it cut time to the first answered request by 44%. It narrows the cold start rather than removing it, and its exposure is the smallest of the three because a mismatched cache falls back to normal startup instead of failing.

The three are points on one trade: how early initialisation is fixed against how much of the world must be known at that moment. The snapshot fixes the most state earliest and has to repair uniqueness afterwards; the closed world fixes the code and has to be told about everything dynamic; the cache fixes the least and gives up the least.

10The ledger

Table 1 collects the 31 plates. Read down the station column and the census is lopsided toward run time: 11 decisions are fixed while the process runs, 6 at start, 5 in the manifest, 3 in the image, 5 at build and 1 in source. Read down the decider column and the platform owns the most, 11, followed by the application with 8 and the JVM with 6. A signature made of station, decider, whether the plate is exposed and whether it has a second owner distinguishes 95.1% of the 465 pairs of plates, against 89.9% for the subject family the article is organised by; there are 18 distinct signatures among 31 plates.

What the census says

The moves go one way. Of the 10 decisions that moved within roughly the last decade, 8 moved earlier: the runtime's contents, class loading and linking, configuration validation, the bean graph, artifact identity, the JIT profile, initialised state and reachable code (D03, D04, C02, C04, L01, L03, S02, S03). In 7 of those eight the previous owner was inside the process. Only transport identity and instrumentation points moved later (M01, O01), and both moved out of application code into infrastructure the operator controls.

Earlier costs exposure. Before those eight moves, each decision was taken at or after the last moment its inputs could change; the total exposure was 0. After them it is 21 station-steps, a mean of 2.62 (Figure 10). The one move that bought no exposure, pinning the image digest, is the one where nothing downstream can change the thing decided. Everywhere else, startup and footprint were bought with a bet that the world at run time will look like the world at build time, and every failure field on those plates describes the bet being lost: a module reached by reflection, a cache trained on a different runtime, a profile from the wrong workload, a seed cloned into a thousand environments.

Split ownership is common, and the order is not fixed. 18 plates have a second owner acting on the same quantity, and every one of those pairs one owner inside the process with one outside it. In 8 the inside owner binds first (D01, D02, K02, C03, M02, M03, H01, H02): the JVM sizes the heap before the platform resizes the limit, the application reads a secret before the platform rotates it, a client library's retry policy is written before the mesh's. In 6 the outside owner binds first (K01, K05, C01, M04, H03, L02), and in 4 both act at run time. The expectation this article started with, that the process always commits before the platform, is false for a third of the split plates; the stronger regularity is the exposure gap, 1.86 stations for decisions owned inside the process against 0.65 for those owned outside it.

Rules that follow

The plates reduce to a handful of rules that apply across all eight subjects. Give every concern exactly one owner, and make the other owner's setting inert: one retry policy, one deadline, one place where TLS terminates. When an inside owner must commit before an outside one can change the input, make the change restart the process so the inside owner can decide again, as the memory resize policy does. When a decision moves to the build for speed, make its failure loud: AOTMode=on in the environments where a missing cache matters, native-image tests that exercise reflective paths, a snapshot scanner for uniqueness. When the platform will act on what the process reports, keep each report to the one question it answers: liveness about the process, readiness about routing, startup about booting. And where the only safe moment is late, as with sampling, pay to wait for it.

11Method and limits

The reference service was compiled and run with OpenJDK 25.0.4.1 on Ubuntu 24.04 in a sandbox with one vCPU and about 4 GB of memory and no container engine; JDK 25 was installed for the build because the sandbox's default runtime, 21, has no javac and no AOT cache. Startup was measured as JVM uptime at the moment the server was listening and after the first request answered through java.net.http, logged by the service itself; each of six configurations ran 17 times, interleaved, and the first two rounds were discarded as page-cache warm-up, leaving 15. Medians and quartiles are reported; on a single shared vCPU the absolute numbers are specific to this machine and only the ratios should be carried elsewhere. Native memory was read with Native Memory Tracking after 200 requests. The ergonomics table was generated by running -XX:+PrintFlagsFinal under each setting.

The lab, lab.py (Listing 13), is 333 lines of standard-library Python with seed 20260922; it produced 74 lines of output, reproduced below, and was checked to produce byte-identical output under three values of PYTHONHASHSEED. Its inputs marked as assumptions (the five-fold non-heap scale, 3% collector overhead, background CPU demand, the boot-time and endpoint-propagation distributions, request rates, keep-alive windows) are illustrative, chosen to be plausible rather than fitted to any product; the experiments are there to show the shape of each trade, and each conclusion in the text is stated so that it survives a change of those constants by a factor of two. E1 uses the measured non-heap floor; E2 is a discrete simulation of CFS bandwidth control at one-millisecond resolution; E3 and E4 are Monte Carlo over 200,000 and 100,000 draws; E5, E6 and E7 are exact.

The census is the most judgement-laden part of the article. Each plate's station, decider, prior position, second owner and latest input station was coded by hand from the cited documentation; a reader who disagrees with a coding can change one line in plates.py and rerun E8. The census counts are therefore claims about this coding, not about the world, and they are offered as a structured way of reading the eight subjects rather than as a measurement of them. Listings that depend on Spring, Istio, AWS or GraalVM artefacts were not compiled, because the sandbox cannot reach Maven Central; YAML and JSON listings were parsed, and the Deployment's timing invariants were checked by the build.
