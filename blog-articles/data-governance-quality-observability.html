Three disciplines, routinely bought as one product, that answer different questions: who is accountable for this dataset, whether its contents satisfy the assertions people depend on, and whether the machinery producing it is still behaving as it did yesterday. 01 · The problem

Three questions that get confused for one

Every organisation that runs on data eventually discovers that it is running on three separate promises, and that it has usually only made one of them explicit.

The first promise is about authority: somebody is accountable for this dataset, knows what it is allowed to be used for, and can say who may see it. The second is about correctness: the values in it satisfy the assertions its consumers rely on — keys are unique, amounts are plausible, categories come from a known set. The third is about behaviour: the machinery that produces the dataset is doing today roughly what it did yesterday, and somebody will notice quickly when it stops.

These are data governance, data quality, and data observability. They are routinely bundled into one budget line and one platform purchase, which is how organisations end up with a catalogue nobody reads, a test suite that passes while the dashboard is wrong, and an alerting system that fires into an unmonitored channel. The disciplines answer genuinely different questions, they fail in different ways, and each one is close to useless without the other two. Policy without measurement is paperwork. Measurement without ownership is an unread alert. Telemetry without policy is a wall of graphs that nobody is obliged to act on.

The money involved is not trivial. Gartner's frequently cited estimate puts the average annual cost of poor data quality at $12.9 million per organisation1, and the regulatory tail is heavier still: in 2020 the US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency fined Citibank $400 million over deficiencies that included data governance, and in 2024 the same bank paid a further $136 million because it had not fixed them2, 3. But the cost that most teams actually feel is quieter — the standing discount that everyone applies to internal numbers, the fortnight of reconciliation before each board pack, the model that was quietly retired because nobody could explain its inputs.

This article treats the three as one control loop with three roles. Governance decides what must be true and who is answerable. Quality encodes those decisions as assertions and enforces them at specific points in a pipeline. Observability instruments the pipeline so that violations, including the ones nobody thought to assert, surface as signals. The evidence produced by the third feeds back into the first: an incident that nobody owned is a governance defect, not merely an engineering one.

02 · History

How the field arrived here, in three eras

The oldest of the three ideas is also the narrowest. When Codd set out the relational model in 1970, correctness was a property the database management system would guarantee: relations, keys, and integrity constraints meant that certain classes of nonsense could not be written down at all4. For two decades that framing held, and the working definition of data quality was operational: the schema either accepted your row or it did not.

It broke on contact with the data warehouse. Once organisations began combining records from systems that had never been designed to agree with one another, the interesting failures were all things no constraint could catch: a customer who exists twice under different spellings, a revenue figure that is internally consistent but three weeks stale, a field that is correctly typed and semantically meaningless. The decisive reframing came from Richard Wang and Diane Strong, whose 1996 survey work showed that data consumers judge quality along a far broader set of dimensions than the accuracy that information systems professionals were optimising, and organised those dimensions into intrinsic, contextual, representational, and accessibility categories5. Quality became fitness for use — a property of the relationship between a dataset and a task, not of the dataset alone6. Thomas Redman put the corollary bluntly for executives: the costs land on the whole enterprise, not on the database team7, and subsequent work turned the dimensions into assessable metrics rather than adjectives8.

The second era was written by regulators. Sarbanes–Oxley made executives personally attest to figures assembled by systems they had not designed, which made traceability a legal interest rather than an engineering nicety. Access control was standardised on the same trajectory: the role-based model that David Ferraiolo and Richard Kuhn proposed in 1992, arguing that discretionary controls suited to military multilevel security were the wrong instrument for commercial systems9, was unified with the Sandhu framework10 and adopted as ANSI/INCITS 359 in 200411, 12. Then the 2008 financial crisis produced the single most consequential data governance instrument of the period: the Basel Committee's BCBS 239, issued in January 2013, whose fourteen principles require systemically important banks to aggregate risk data accurately and completely, and to keep doing so under stress13. More than a decade later the Committee is still publishing implementation guidance on the same themes, with data lineage prominent among the unresolved challenges14, and European supervisors have issued their own guide on the subject15.

The third era is the one most practitioners live in, and it borrowed its vocabulary from site reliability engineering. As pipelines proliferated, the dominant failure mode stopped being the loud one — a job that crashes — and became the silent one: a job that succeeds while emitting garbage. Google's GOODS system, which catalogued the company's datasets by crawling rather than by asking anyone to register them, showed what metadata at that scale looked like16. Amazon's Deequ and the open-source Great Expectations project made assertions about data a build artefact, deliberately styling them as unit tests17. In 2019 Barr Moses named the resulting practice data observability, and the loss function it minimises data downtime18. Ownership decentralised in parallel: Zhamak Dehghani's data mesh made domain teams responsible for their own data products, held together by what she called federated computational governance19.

Nothing on that ledger has been retired. A platform built today enforces constraints in the storage layer, satisfies auditors with lineage, and emits telemetry to a monitoring system — which is precisely why the three practices keep colliding over the same tables, the same metadata, and the same on-call rota.

03 · Quality

Dimensions are measurements, not adjectives

The dimensional vocabulary is the field's most useful export and its most abused. Saying that a table should be accurate and timely commits nobody to anything. A dimension becomes a control only when it is expressed as a ratio with a denominator you can defend, a threshold somebody agreed to, and a named owner who is paged when the threshold is breached.

Six dimensions cover most practical work, and they map cleanly onto the characteristics enumerated in ISO/IEC 25012, which distinguishes inherent properties of the data from ones that depend on the system holding it20, 21; the complementary ISO 8000 series addresses how quality data is specified, verified, and exchanged between organisations22, while DAMA's body of knowledge supplies the surrounding operating model rather than a metric set23.

  • Completeness. The proportion of expected values that are present. The subtlety is entirely in the word expected: a null in an optional column is not a defect, and a row that never arrived cannot be counted by any check that only inspects rows that did.
  • Uniqueness. One real-world entity, one record. Duplicate keys are the classic replay artefact, and as the aviation case later in this article shows, non-unique identifiers in a reference dataset can be catastrophic even when every individual record is impeccable.
  • Validity. Conformance to a declared domain — a type, a range, a regular expression, an enumerated set, a currency code. This is the dimension most amenable to automation and the one that catches the largest share of upstream schema surprises.
  • Consistency. Agreement between facts that ought to agree: referential integrity across tables, a reconciliation between a fact table and the source system's own control totals, the same customer count in two marts.
  • Timeliness. Whether the data landed inside the window in which it was still useful. Timeliness is a joint property of the data and the decision it feeds; an hour is late for fraud scoring and early for a quarterly disclosure.
  • Accuracy. Whether the value corresponds to the real world it claims to describe. This is the hardest dimension to measure honestly, because doing so requires a reference you trust more than the data under test — a survey, a master record, a physical count. Teams that claim high accuracy scores are usually measuring validity and mislabelling it.

Two refinements matter more than adding a seventh dimension. The first is scope: a metric computed over a whole table hides the failure that matters. A completeness score of 99.5% is comfortable until you learn that every missing value belongs to the one market whose regulator is asking. Partition the metric the way the business partitions the decision. The second is the threshold. Setting every check to demand perfection guarantees that the alerts will be ignored within a month; setting them to whatever the data currently achieves guarantees they will never fire. The threshold is a negotiated service level, and negotiating it is what forces the conversation about who actually depends on the dataset.

04 · Enforcement

From assertions to control points

An assertion that runs somewhere is worth little; the value is in where it runs and what happens when it fails. The cost of a defect rises with every hop it survives, so the design question is how early a rule can be enforced and how forcefully.

Three control points cover most architectures. At the ingest gate, before anything is promoted out of the landing zone, checks are structural and cheap: does the payload match the agreed schema, is the row count within a plausible band, did it arrive inside the SLA. Failures here should block, because a bad batch that never enters the warehouse costs one re-run and a bad batch that does costs a backfill of everything downstream. In transformation, checks are semantic: primary keys unique and non-null, foreign keys resolvable, enumerations within their accepted sets, aggregates reconciling to their inputs. At the contract boundary, where a dataset is published for others to consume, checks defend the promises made to consumers: column-level guarantees, distribution stability, a freshness budget, and a veto on breaking schema changes.

What happens to failing rows is a governance decision disguised as an implementation detail. Dropping them produces a clean table and a silent completeness defect. Passing them through produces an honest table and a downstream incident. The defensible pattern is quarantine: failed records are written to a durable side channel with the assertion that rejected them and the run that produced them, so they can be corrected and replayed, and so the quarantine itself becomes the evidence trail an auditor asks for.

The tool landscape

Four families of tooling dominate, and they are complementary rather than competing. dbt puts tests beside the models they defend; the four generic tests it ships with — unique, not_null, accepted_values, and relationships — are compiled into SQL that returns failing rows, and cover a surprising fraction of real defects24. Great Expectations is Python-native and richer: an Expectation is a single assertion, a Suite bundles them for a dataset, and a Checkpoint binds a suite to a run and routes the results to actions and to generated documentation25. Soda takes the declarative route, with checks written in a YAML dialect intended to be readable by the people who own the data rather than only the people who move it26. Deequ, built at Amazon on Spark, was designed for the scale case: its authors framed the problem explicitly as unit tests for data and built incremental computation so that constraints can be re-verified on growing datasets without rescanning history17, 27.

Choosing between them matters less than the two decisions that surround them: which datasets are in scope at which tier, and whether a failure blocks. A common and workable pattern is tiered coverage — exhaustive assertions on the small set of certified, customer-facing assets; key and freshness checks on everything one hop upstream; anomaly monitoring only on the long tail28.

A minimal engine, end to end

The mechanics are simpler than the tooling suggests, which is worth demonstrating: the following module has no dependencies, computes five of the six dimensions over a batch, quarantines what fails, and then monitors volume with a baseline robust enough to survive the anomalies it is meant to catch.

@dataclass
class CheckResult:
    name: str
    dimension: str
    score: float
    threshold: float
    failing: list = field(default_factory=list)

    @property
    def passed(self) -> bool:
        return self.score >= self.threshold


def completeness(rows, column, threshold=0.99):
    bad = [r for r in rows if r.get(column) in (None, "")]
    score = 1 - len(bad) / len(rows) if rows else 0.0
    return CheckResult(f"not_null({column})", "completeness", score, threshold, bad)


def uniqueness(rows, key, threshold=1.0):
    seen, dupes = set(), []
    for r in rows:
        if r[key] in seen:
            dupes.append(r)
        seen.add(r[key])
    score = 1 - len(dupes) / len(rows) if rows else 0.0
    return CheckResult(f"unique({key})", "uniqueness", score, threshold, dupes)


def timeliness(rows, event_col, load_col, budget_hours=6, threshold=0.98):
    late = [r for r in rows
            if (r[load_col] - r[event_col]).total_seconds() > budget_hours * 3600]
    score = 1 - len(late) / len(rows) if rows else 0.0
    return CheckResult(f"latency <= {budget_hours}h", "timeliness", score, threshold, late)


def mad_z(value: float, history: list[float]) -> float:
    """Median absolute deviation z-score: robust to the outlier being tested."""
    med = statistics.median(history)
    mad = statistics.median([abs(h - med) for h in history]) or 1e-9
    return 0.6745 * (value - med) / mad


def volume_anomalies(counts, z_limit=3.5, min_history=4, window=6, min_rel=0.15):
    """Flag a day that is both statistically odd AND materially different."""
    findings = []
    for day in sorted(counts):
        history = [counts[d] for d in sorted(counts)
                   if d < day and d.weekday() == day.weekday()][-window:]
        if len(history) < min_history:
            continue
        med = statistics.median(history)
        z, rel = mad_z(counts[day], history), (counts[day] - med) / med
        if abs(z) >= z_limit and abs(rel) >= min_rel:
            findings.append((day, counts[day], med, z))
    return findings

Run against a batch with nine nulls, one replayed order, four malformed currency codes, six orphaned foreign keys, and thirty-one late arrivals injected, together with eight weeks of daily row counts containing a filter regression, a double-loaded partition, and a missed run:

BATCH  orders_daily  rows=2000  partition=2026-07-27

ASSERTIONS

  [FAIL] uniqueness    unique(order_id)              99.950%  (min 100.00%)     1 bad
  [PASS] validity      currency in ISO-4217 set      99.800%  (min 99.50%)     4 bad
  [PASS] validity      0 < amount < 10000           100.000%  (min 99.50%)     0 bad
  [FAIL] consistency   customer_id in dim_customer   99.700%  (min 100.00%)     6 bad
  [PASS] timeliness    latency <= 6h                 98.450%  (min 98.00%)    31 bad

  verdict: BLOCK — 2 of 6 assertions failed; 7 rows quarantined for replay

VOLUME MONITOR  (per-weekday median, MAD z-score, |z| >= 3.5)
  2026-07-09  volume               observed= 76,901  expected~127,801    -39.8%   z= -10.94
  2026-07-16  volume               observed=262,019  expected~126,891   +106.5%   z= +23.42
  2026-07-22  freshness: no rows   observed=      0  expected~133,160   -100.0%   z= -13.97

Three details in that output carry the argument. First, the two blocking failures are the ones whose thresholds are absolute — a duplicate key and an unresolvable foreign key are not tolerable at any rate, whereas nulls and late arrivals have a negotiated budget and consume it without stopping the pipeline. Second, only seven rows are quarantined out of two thousand, which is what makes blocking affordable: the correct response to a small, well-identified defect is to isolate it, not to reject the batch. Third, the volume monitor requires an anomaly to be both statistically extreme and materially large. Statistical significance alone would have flagged a dozen days of ordinary week-on-week growth; the relative-deviation gate is what separates a monitor from a nuisance.

05 · Governance

Who decides, who answers, and how the decision reaches the data

Data governance is the exercise of authority over data assets: deciding what data the organisation holds, what it may be used for, who owns it, how long it is kept, and how those decisions are enforced and evidenced. DAMA's body of knowledge places governance at the centre of its wheel precisely because it is not a discipline alongside architecture, quality, and security but the coordination layer over all of them23, 29. It is not a knowledge domain that produces artefacts; it is the thing that makes the other domains answerable.

In practice a governance programme consists of a small number of unglamorous mechanisms.

  • Ownership. Every asset above a materiality threshold has a named accountable owner — a person, not a team inbox — and a steward who does the day-to-day curation. The test of whether ownership is real is whether the owner receives the alert.
  • Classification. Every column carries a sensitivity label and a domain, applied at creation and propagated through lineage. Classification is the hinge on which the rest turns: access policies, masking rules, and retention schedules are all functions of it.
  • A glossary with teeth. A definition of active customer that lives in a wiki is documentation; one that is bound to the specific column implementing it, and that fails a build when the two diverge, is governance.
  • Retention and lifecycle. Data that should have been deleted is a liability that grows monotonically, and deletion is the control most often specified and least often verified.
  • A decision record. Exceptions, waivers, and threshold changes are logged with a rationale and an expiry. Most governance failures found in audit are not missing controls but undocumented deviations from them.

Centralised, federated, and computational

The historical model was a central governance body that reviewed everything, which scales to roughly the point at which the review queue becomes the bottleneck for every product team, after which it is routed around. The data mesh critique made this explicit and proposed the alternative: domain teams own their data as a product, with a federation of those owners setting global rules that apply to every product's interfaces, and — crucially — those rules implemented as platform capabilities rather than as documents30, 19.

The word doing the work there is computational. A policy that says sensitive columns must be masked in non-production environments is a wish. The same policy expressed as a platform default that reads the classification from the catalogue and applies the masking automatically, with the exception path requiring a logged waiver, is a control. The practical measure of governance maturity is the ratio between rules that execute and rules that are merely published.

This is also where governance stops being a purely internal matter. BCBS 239 requires banks to be able to aggregate risk data accurately and completely, on a largely automated basis to reduce error, and to do so under stress rather than only in calm conditions13 — requirements that cannot be met by a documentation exercise. The Basel Committee's own follow-up material, more than a decade after publication, still identifies governance of risk data aggregation, lineage, and cross-border issues among the persistent challenges14.

06 · Metadata

The catalogue: making the estate legible

You cannot govern what you cannot enumerate, and by the time an organisation notices it needs a catalogue, nobody knows how many tables it has. The realisation that the inventory must be built by machine rather than by asking people to register their datasets is what makes modern catalogues different from the data dictionaries that preceded them. Google's GOODS system illustrated the shape of the answer: crawl the storage layer, infer datasets and their relationships, and treat the resulting metadata as a post-hoc, best-effort index rather than a registry that producers are obliged to maintain16.

The open-source lineage of the idea is short and worth knowing, because the architectural differences between these systems still shape products sold today. Apache Atlas emerged from the Hadoop ecosystem as a governance-first metadata store with fine-grained classification. LinkedIn built WhereHows, then replaced it with DataHub, which was announced in 2019 and open-sourced in 2020 and which took a stream-based, event-driven approach to metadata ingestion31, 32. Lyft built Amundsen for discovery specifically, open-sourcing it in October 2019 after a year in production, with search ranked by usage so that heavily queried tables surface first — explicitly modelled on web search rather than on a library catalogue33, 34. Commercial platforms — Alation, Collibra, Atlan, and the catalogues built into cloud warehouses and lakehouses — converge on the same feature set with more attention to workflow and policy.

The distinction that matters when evaluating any of them is between passive and active metadata. A passive catalogue is a place people go to look things up, and it decays: descriptions go stale, owners leave, and trust in the catalogue erodes faster than trust in the data. An active catalogue is wired into the systems that produce and consume data, so that the same metadata drives access policy, masking, test generation, and deprecation notices. The second kind survives because it is on the critical path of something; the first kind is a documentation project with a search box.

07 · Lineage

Lineage answers the question that matters at three in the morning

Lineage is the record of where a dataset came from and where its contents go. Its value is almost entirely operational, and it is realised at two moments: before a change, when you need to know what will break, and after an incident, when you need to know what was poisoned and by what.

Table-level lineage gives blast radius: this source feeds four staging models, two marts, an executive report, a churn model, and a reverse-ETL job that writes into the billing system. That is enough to decide whether a schema change is routine or requires notice. Column-level lineage gives precision: the column being dropped feeds one derived attribute that is used in a filter nobody has looked at in a year, or alternatively feeds the definition of a regulatory metric. The difference between those two answers is the difference between a five-minute change and a change-advisory conversation, and only column-level resolution can tell them apart.

Lineage can be captured in three ways, with a clear ordering of reliability. Parsing SQL and transformation code gives design-time lineage that is cheap and complete for the code it can parse, and blind to anything dynamic. Instrumenting the runtime gives observed lineage: what actually read what, on which run, with which parameters. Manual curation gives whatever the last person who cared entered, and decays.

The runtime approach acquired a standard. OpenLineage, which grew out of the Marquez project started at WeWork around 2017 to build a map of job and dataset dependencies35, defines a run-centric event model: a job is a recurring process, a run is one execution of it identified by a UUID, and datasets are the inputs and outputs of that run. Events are emitted at start and completion, and the core schema is deliberately small, with everything else carried in facets — atomic, extensible pieces of metadata attached to a job, run, or dataset36. The specification is defined in OpenAPI and supports custom facets, which is why the same event stream can carry schema, column-level lineage, and data quality metrics without any of those being blessed by the core36, 37.

The project graduated within the LF AI & Data Foundation in 2023, by which point its integrations included Airflow, Spark, Flink, and dbt, and its consumers included Marquez as the reference implementation alongside commercial catalogues38, 39. The strategic point for an architect is that lineage stops being a feature of one vendor's catalogue and becomes a telemetry stream that any tool can emit and any tool can consume — the same shift that OpenTelemetry produced in application monitoring.

Two warnings from practice. Lineage that stops at the warehouse boundary is only half a graph: the incidents that hurt most involve a dashboard, a spreadsheet, or an operational system that consumes the data and is invisible to the pipeline. And lineage is not an audit trail. It tells you that a job read a table; it does not tell you that a human approved the change, which is a separate record with separate retention requirements.

08 · Access

Access control, and the attributes it depends on

Access control in a data platform differs from access control in an application in one respect that drives everything else: the objects are enormous, heterogeneous, and constantly derived from one another. Granting someone access to a table is easy; ensuring that they cannot reconstruct the same information from three joins of things they are permitted to see is the actual problem.

The workhorse model remains role-based access control. Ferraiolo and Kuhn's 1992 argument was that discretionary controls, in which the owner of an object decides who may use it, are a poor fit for commercial and civil government organisations, where permissions properly follow job function rather than personal relationship9. The model was formalised, unified with the Sandhu framework, and standardised as ANSI/INCITS 359 in 200411. Its strengths are administrative: roles are auditable, reviewable, and comprehensible to the people who must certify them each quarter. Its weakness is combinatorial. Once entitlement must vary by region, data sensitivity, project, and contract, the number of roles grows multiplicatively, and role explosion is the standard end state of a mature RBAC deployment.

Attribute-based access control answers that by moving the decision to request time. NIST's guidance defines ABAC as evaluating attributes of the subject, the object, the requested operation, and, where relevant, environmental conditions, against rules describing which operations are allowable for a given set of attributes40. In a data platform this is what enables row- and column-level policies expressed once and applied everywhere: analysts may read rows whose data_region matches their own, may see a salary column only if their clearance meets the column's sensitivity, and lose access automatically when a retention date passes.

The trade is a shift in where fragility lives. Under RBAC, a wrong role assignment is a visible administrative error someone will catch at the next access review. Under ABAC, a wrong attribute — a mislabelled region, a missing sensitivity tag on a newly created column — is a silent security defect, and its correctness is now a data quality problem in the metadata. This is the sharpest illustration of why these disciplines cannot be run separately: the governance catalogue supplies the attributes, the quality system must assert that they are populated and valid, and the access system enforces on them. Most mature platforms run both models — roles for coarse entitlement, attributes for fine-grained filtering — with policy evaluated by a decision point that is separate from the enforcement point in the query engine.

Encryption is the layer beneath, and the requirements are unremarkable but non-negotiable: transport encryption everywhere, encryption at rest with keys held in a managed service rather than the platform being protected, envelope encryption so that key rotation does not mean rewriting petabytes, and, for the most sensitive fields, application-level or client-side encryption so that the storage layer never holds plaintext. Under GDPR, encryption and pseudonymisation appear explicitly among the technical measures a controller is expected to consider when securing processing, and the European Data Protection Board's 2025 guidelines set out how pseudonymisation must be implemented to have the effect its users claim for it41.

09 · Privacy

Masking, de-identification, and the frontier nobody can move

Access control decides who sees a dataset. Privacy engineering decides what the dataset reveals about a person once someone is looking at it, and it is governed by a hard trade-off: every technique that reduces the chance of singling out an individual also reduces the analytic value of the data. There is no configuration that escapes this, only a choice of position on the curve, and that choice belongs to governance rather than to whoever is writing the transformation.

The legal frame sets the stakes. Under the GDPR, pseudonymised data — data whose identifiers have been replaced but which can still be re-attributed using additional information — remains personal data, and remains fully in scope; only genuinely anonymous information, where the individual is not or is no longer identifiable, falls outside the regulation, and the test considers all means reasonably likely to be used, by the controller or by anyone else42. That last clause is what makes casual anonymisation claims dangerous: the standard is not what your team can re-identify, but what anyone with plausibly available auxiliary data could. US health data takes a more prescriptive route: the HIPAA Privacy Rule offers de-identification either by expert determination, in which a qualified person documents that the re-identification risk is very small, or by the Safe Harbor method, which removes eighteen enumerated classes of identifier and requires no actual knowledge that the remainder could identify someone43, 44.

The techniques, roughly in order of protection:

  • Redaction and suppression. The column is not there. Unbeatable protection, zero utility, and the correct answer more often than teams like to admit.
  • Tokenisation. The value is replaced by a surrogate, with a vault holding the mapping. Referential integrity survives joins; the vault becomes the crown jewel.
  • Format-preserving masking. The surrogate looks like the original — a plausible card number, a valid postcode — which keeps downstream validation working and is invaluable for non-production environments.
  • Generalisation and k-anonymity. Values are coarsened — age to a band, postcode to its district — until every record is indistinguishable from at least k − 1 others on the quasi-identifiers. Effective against linkage attacks, and vulnerable when a group shares a sensitive value.
  • Differential privacy. Calibrated noise is added so that the presence or absence of any single individual cannot materially change what is released, with a formal privacy budget accounting for cumulative loss. The US Census Bureau's TopDown Algorithm applied this to the 2020 Census, producing privacy-protected microdata for the redistricting and demographic files — among the largest deployments of formal privacy by a national statistical office, and a useful public record of both the mechanism and the accuracy arguments it provoked45, 46.

Three operational lessons recur. Apply protection as far upstream as you can bear: a pipeline that lands raw identifiers and masks them on the way out has already created the copy that will be forgotten. Treat the classification metadata as production infrastructure, because a masking rule that keys off a sensitivity label is only as good as the label. And instrument the exceptions — the analyst who needs unmasked access for a fraud investigation is a legitimate case, and an unlogged one is how a defensible programme becomes an indefensible one.

10 · Observability

Instrumenting the machine, not just the output

The word is borrowed twice over. In control theory, observability is the property that a system's internal state can be reconstructed from its outputs — a formal notion introduced alongside controllability in Kalman's work on the general theory of control systems47. Site reliability engineering borrowed it for software, where it came to mean instrumenting a system richly enough to answer questions you did not anticipate when you deployed it. Data observability is the third borrowing, coined by Barr Moses in 2019 along with data downtime for the periods in which data is missing, wrong, or otherwise not usable18.

The framework that stuck is five pillars, deliberately parallel to the pillars of DevOps observability: freshness, distribution, volume, schema, and lineage48, 49. Their usefulness is that they are all computable from metadata, without reading the data itself or knowing anything about the business.

  • Freshness. When did this table last update, and is the gap since the last update longer than its history suggests it should be? Most incidents that consumers notice first are freshness incidents, because a stale dashboard looks exactly like a correct one.
  • Volume. How many rows arrived, relative to what this table normally receives at this time of week? Volume anomalies catch upstream filter changes, partial loads, and double loads — three of the most common silent failures.
  • Distribution. Are the values in a field still in their usual shape: null rate, cardinality, mean and quantiles for numerics, category mix for enumerations? This is where a unit change, a truncated join, or a new upstream source shows up.
  • Schema. Have columns been added, removed, renamed, or retyped? Schema changes are the clearest causal signal available, because they are discrete and attributable to a deploy.
  • Lineage. The connective tissue that turns four independent alerts into one incident with a root cause and a blast radius.

The distinction from data quality testing is worth stating precisely, because vendors blur it. A quality test encodes a hypothesis someone had: this column should never be null. An observability monitor detects departures from behaviour without anyone having had a hypothesis: this column has never before been 40% null on a Tuesday. Tests give you certainty about a small, chosen surface; monitors give you coverage of a large, unchosen one, at the cost of false positives. A programme that runs only tests will be blindsided by the failure nobody imagined. A programme that runs only monitors will accumulate anomalies without a definition of correct, and will discover during an audit that it cannot demonstrate any control at all.

Making monitors survivable

The engineering content of observability is almost entirely in avoiding alert fatigue, and three techniques do most of the work. Seasonality: baselines must be conditioned on day of week and, in many businesses, on hour of day and on the calendar; a fixed threshold either screams every Saturday or sleeps through a weekday collapse. Robust statistics: the mean and standard deviation are computed over a window that contains the anomaly you are trying to detect, which inflates the spread and hides it; the median and median absolute deviation do not, which is why the demonstration engine earlier uses a MAD-based z-score. Materiality gates: requiring a deviation to be both statistically extreme and large in relative terms is what keeps a very stable table from paging someone over a 3% wobble.

Beyond that, alerts must be routed to the owner recorded in the catalogue rather than to a shared channel, and they must be suppressed transitively — if a source table has failed, the twelve downstream tables that consequently failed should produce one incident, not thirteen. That deduplication is only possible with lineage, which is the practical reason lineage is counted as a pillar of observability rather than a governance artefact.

11 · Contracts

Service levels, contracts, and what an incident costs

Everything so far describes controls. What turns controls into an operating discipline is agreeing, in advance, what the guarantee is and how it will be measured — the same move that service level objectives made for software reliability.

A data service level objective is stated per dataset and per dimension, with a window and a target: this table is fresh within 45 minutes of the hour, 99% of the time, measured monthly; its primary key is unique with zero tolerance; its null rate on the customer identifier stays under 0.5%. The value of writing it down is not the number but the conversation, which forces the consuming team to say what they actually depend on and the producing team to say what they can actually sustain. A dataset with no consumer willing to state an objective is a dataset that can be deprecated, which is itself a useful finding.

A data contract is the stronger form: a versioned, machine-readable declaration of a dataset's schema, semantics, quality guarantees, and change policy, owned by the producer and enforced in the producer's own deployment pipeline. The critical difference from a test suite is where the enforcement sits. Tests in the consumer's warehouse detect a breaking change after it has shipped; a contract checked in the producer's CI prevents it from shipping. Schema registries in streaming platforms have enforced exactly this for years with compatibility rules, and the data contract movement is largely the generalisation of that idea to analytical datasets, with quality assertions and ownership attached.

The outcome metrics are the two that bound an incident: time to detection, the interval during which the organisation acted on numbers that were wrong, and time to resolution, which includes not only the fix but the backfill and the re-certification of everything downstream. Their product with incident count is data downtime, and it is the only aggregate worth reporting to an executive, because everything else — number of monitors, number of catalogued assets, test coverage percentage — is an input that can be inflated without improving anything. A programme that doubles its test count while its time to detection stays flat has bought nothing.

12 · Evidence

Four failures, and the control that would have caught each

Abstract arguments about data quality are unpersuasive; the public record is not.

A row limit, a contact tracing system, and 15,841 cases

Between 25 September and 2 October 2020, 15,841 positive COVID-19 test results in England were left out of the reported daily case figures and, more importantly, were not passed to contact tracers. The cause was a data engineering choice: Public Health England assembled laboratory results, which arrived as CSV files without incident, into Excel templates using the legacy XLS format, whose roughly 65,000-row ceiling meant that each template could hold only about 1,400 cases once each result expanded into several rows. When a template filled, further cases were simply omitted50. Economists later exploited the resulting geographic variation as a natural experiment, estimating that the tracing failure was associated with a very large number of additional infections and deaths51.

This is a completeness failure of the class that no row-level test can catch, because the missing records are absent from the very artefact being validated. The control that catches it is a volume reconciliation at the ingest gate: count the records the source claims to have sent, count what was loaded, and fail loudly when they differ. A monitor watching daily volumes against a seasonal baseline would also have flagged it — the truncation produced a systematic shortfall for eight consecutive days.

Two waypoints with the same name

On 28 August 2023, the UK's air traffic control flight planning system stopped processing flight plans automatically, and controllers moved to manual processing. The trigger was a single flight plan containing two geographically distant waypoints that happened to share an identifier — the code DVL, which denotes both Deauville in France and Devil's Lake in North Dakota. The system could not resolve the ambiguity and raised a critical exception52, 53. More than 2,000 flights were cancelled across two days; the UK Civil Aviation Authority's independent review estimated that over 700,000 passengers were affected and put the cost to passengers and operators at between £75 million and £100 million, issuing 34 recommendations54, 55.

This is a uniqueness failure in reference data, and it illustrates why uniqueness is the dimension least tolerant of a percentage threshold. Every individual record was valid; the defect existed only in the relationship between two of them, and only under a specific query. The general lesson is that identifier uniqueness must be asserted across the namespace in which the identifiers will actually be compared — and that systems consuming reference data they do not control need explicit handling for the ambiguity case rather than an exception path that stops the world.

A consent order that would not close

In October 2020 the OCC assessed a $400 million penalty against Citibank and issued a cease and desist order requiring comprehensive remediation of risk management, data governance, and internal controls2. Nearly four years later, in July 2024, the OCC amended that order and imposed a further $75 million penalty for failure to meet remediation milestones, while the Federal Reserve added $60.6 million; the Fed's finding referred specifically to continuing deficiencies in the bank's data quality management programme and inadequate control of data quality risks3, 56.

The instructive part is the duration. This is not a story about a bank that ignored a regulator; it is a story about how long it takes to retrofit data governance onto an estate assembled over decades of acquisitions, and about the fact that regulators increasingly treat data quality management as a named, examinable programme rather than as a component of general operational risk.

A model trained on bad ground truth

In 2022 Unity Software attributed a revenue shortfall of roughly $110 million, and a very large fall in market value, to the ingestion of bad data from a large customer that degraded its targeting models57. The mechanism here is different from the previous three: nothing was missing, duplicated, or malformed in a way a schema check would notice. The data was structurally fine and semantically corrupted, and the damage was done by a system that consumed it without any assertion about its distribution.

This is the failure mode that observability exists for, and specifically the distribution pillar. It is also the bridge to the last technical section, because machine learning systems consume data in the one way that defeats every control designed for human consumers: silently, continuously, and without anybody reading the numbers.

13 · AI

Why AI made all of this mandatory

A dashboard with a wrong number is read by a person who may notice that it looks odd. A feature pipeline with a wrong number is read by a model that will incorporate the error into its parameters and generalise it. Machine learning removes the last informal control that data quality programmes had been quietly relying on, which is human incredulity.

The specific failure modes are well documented. Training–serving skew: the transformation applied during training differs subtly from the one applied at inference, so the model sees a distribution it was never fitted on. Silent feature degradation: an upstream field becomes null or changes units, the pipeline succeeds, and accuracy decays slowly enough that it is attributed to drift in the world rather than to a defect. Label leakage and staleness: ground truth arrives late or is computed from something the model will not have at inference. The response developed in production ML systems is to treat data validation as a first-class pipeline stage with a schema that is versioned alongside the model, checking each batch against expectations and anomaly conditions before training or serving58 — the same idea as Deequ's constraint verification, applied to features rather than tables17.

Regulation has now caught up with that practice. Article 10 of the EU AI Act requires that training, validation, and testing datasets for high-risk AI systems be subject to appropriate data governance and management practices, covering among other things the data collection process and the origin of the data, the preparation operations applied to it — annotation, labelling, cleaning, updating, enrichment, aggregation — the examination of possible biases, and the identification of relevant gaps or shortcomings and how they will be addressed. The datasets themselves must be relevant, sufficiently representative, and to the greatest extent possible free of errors and complete for the intended purpose, with appropriate statistical properties59. Where personal data is involved, these obligations sit on top of the GDPR's existing requirements rather than replacing them60.

Read as an engineering specification rather than as law, Article 10 is a demand for exactly the three capabilities this article has described: lineage that records provenance and preparation, quality metrics with documented thresholds, and a governance record that shows who decided what and why. Organisations that have those capabilities for their analytical estate can extend them to their models. Organisations that do not will find that the compliance deadline arrives before the platform does.

14 · Practice

An adoption path, and the ways it goes wrong

The failure patterns are consistent enough to name.

  • The catalogue-first programme. Eighteen months are spent inventorying and describing assets before any control executes. The catalogue is complete on the day it is delivered and stale within a quarter, because nothing depends on it.
  • Coverage as the metric. Test counts and catalogued-asset percentages rise; time to detection does not move. Inputs are being reported because outcomes are harder to attribute.
  • Alerts without owners. Monitors route to a shared channel. Within six weeks the channel is muted, and the programme's actual detection latency is now the time until a customer complains.
  • Quality as a downstream cleanup team. A central team fixes defects in the warehouse rather than at the source, which is both unbounded work and a guarantee that the source will never improve.
  • Governance that only forbids. If every interaction with the governance function is a refusal, teams will route around it, and the estate that results will be genuinely ungoverned rather than merely inconvenient.

The sequence that tends to work inverts the usual order and is deliberately narrow at the start. Begin with the small set of assets that already carry consequences — the reported financials, the regulatory submission, the model that touches customers — and give each one a named owner. For those assets only, write down the service level: freshness, key uniqueness, the two or three column-level guarantees that consumers actually depend on. Implement those as blocking assertions at the gate closest to the source, with quarantine rather than deletion. Add freshness and volume monitors across everything else, since they are computable from metadata and cost almost nothing. Turn on runtime lineage so that alerts can be deduplicated and blast radius computed. Only then build outwards: classification and access policy driven by the catalogue, contracts at the boundaries that have proven contentious, and reporting that shows detection and resolution time rather than coverage.

Two organisational points determine whether any of this survives contact with a reorganisation. Ownership must be attached to a role that persists, and the alert must go to the owner: an accountability that generates no interruptions is not an accountability. And the exception path must be usable. Every governance regime that lasts has a fast, logged way to say yes, with a waiver and an expiry, because the alternative is not compliance but circumvention.

15 · Close

The promise underneath

The three disciplines converge on a single question that is really about institutions rather than technology: when somebody acts on a number, what entitles them to believe it? Governance answers that a named person is accountable for it and has decided what it may be used for. Quality answers that it satisfies assertions somebody wrote down and can be shown to have passed. Observability answers that the system producing it is behaving as it did when those assertions were last true, and that a deviation would be noticed by someone specific in a stated amount of time.

Each answer is partial and none of them is optional. What the historical record shows — from a spreadsheet that silently dropped cases in a pandemic, to two airfields four thousand miles apart sharing a three-letter code, to a bank paying half a billion dollars over data it could not aggregate — is that the failures are rarely exotic. They are ordinary lapses in ordinary controls, discovered late, in systems that everybody assumed somebody else was watching. The work of these three disciplines is mostly the unglamorous business of making sure that assumption is written down, tested, and instrumented, so that it stops being an assumption at all.
