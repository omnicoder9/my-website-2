The question every API answers

Kafka never learns whether your code did what a record asked. A broker knows that bytes were appended to a log and copied to other brokers; a consumer group knows the number it last wrote into an internal topic. Everything else, including whether an invoice was charged once, twice or not at all, is a claim made by somebody about a record, at some moment, on the strength of some evidence. This article reads the Kafka Java client, Spring for Apache Kafka and Spring Boot as a catalogue of those claims. Each API in the usual list (KafkaTemplate, @KafkaListener, producer and consumer configuration, error handlers, retry topics, dead-letter topics, transactions, batch and concurrent consumers, Boot auto-configuration) is one answer to a single question: when is a record done, and who gets to say so?

The question is worth organising an article around because Kafka's own documentation frames delivery guarantees as a choice between at-most-once, at-least-once and exactly-once, and then shows that which one you get depends on where the consumer records its position relative to where it processes1. The original Kafka paper made the same point in 2011 by declining to track consumption on the broker at all: a consumer's position is a single integer per partition, and the broker keeps records whether or not anybody has read them2. That design is why Kafka scales and why the integration code matters. The log is durable; what you do with it is not, unless you arrange for it to be.

Two devices carry the argument. The first is a done ladder of six rungs, each naming a different event that someone might treat as completion. D0 is handed off: send() has returned and the record sits in a client buffer. D1 is leader-acknowledged: one broker has appended it. D2 is replicated: every in-sync replica has it. D3 is passed by a batch commit: the consumer group's committed position has moved beyond it, at the granularity of a poll. D4 is passed by a record commit: the position moves record by record, after each is processed. D5 is committed with its output: the consumed offset and the records produced from it commit in one transaction. The rungs are ordered by how much of the system has to agree before the claim is made, not by how much better each one is; every rung costs something the one below does not.

The second device is a crash tape drawn for every configuration in the article. Twenty configurations, called plates, are each run through the same two scenarios by a small deterministic model in the lab at the end. In the crash scenario the process dies after handling the eleventh of sixteen records and before any commit that was due at that instant. In the fault scenario the sixth record cannot be handled at all (a poison record on the consumer side; a partition-leader failure on the producer side). Each of the sixteen cells shows what became of that record once the system recovered: settled once, handled twice, re-handled behind a fence that made the first attempt invisible, lost, skipped after its retries with only a log line to show for it, parked in a dead-letter topic, rerouted out of order, or stalled behind something that will never succeed. Figure 1 shows how to read one.

The headline result, developed in the ledger at the end, is that the two rows of the tape are controlled by disjoint settings. The listener's acknowledgement mode decides the crash row and nothing else; the error handler decides the fault row and nothing else; only a transaction reaches into both. And 9 of the twenty plates end at least one row with a record that is gone without trace beyond a log line, including the plate that represents a Spring Boot application with no Kafka configuration at all.

The log underneath

A Kafka topic is a named set of partitions; a partition is an append-only sequence of records, each identified by a monotonically increasing offset2,3. Jay Kreps's essay on the log remains the clearest statement of why this structure is useful: a totally ordered, replayable sequence of facts is the common substrate under databases, replication and stream processing4. Each partition has one leader broker and a set of followers; the followers that are caught up form the in-sync replica set, and a record is committed in Kafka's own vocabulary once every member of that set has it5,6. Since Kafka 4.0 the cluster's metadata lives in a Raft quorum inside Kafka itself rather than in ZooKeeper, which 4.0 removed entirely7,8.

Figure 2 places the six rungs of the done ladder on the path a record takes. The producer side ends at D2, because once the in-sync replicas have a record there is nothing further a producer can learn about it. The consumer side starts again from nothing: reading a record proves only that it was readable. D3 and D4 are written by the consumer into the internal __consumer_offsets topic. D5 is written by the transaction coordinator, a broker-side component that makes a set of produced records and a consumed offset visible together or not at all9.

Ordering is a per-partition property, so the choice of partition is a choice about which records may be reordered relative to each other. For a record with a key, the Java client's default partitioner takes the 32-bit MurmurHash2 of the serialised key bytes, clears the sign bit and reduces it modulo the partition count3,10. The lab ports that function and checks it against the six test vectors in Kafka's own unit tests; all six match. Over 100,000 distinct keys and 12 partitions the busiest partition receives 1.0193 times the mean, a chi-square of 7.99 on 11 degrees of freedom, so the hash is not the problem. Traffic is. When the same 12 partitions receive 200,000 records whose keys follow a Zipf distribution with exponent 1.1, the single hottest key carries 15.14% of all traffic and the busiest partition runs at 2.843 times the mean while the quietest runs at 0.497. A consumer group cannot parallelise a key, so that partition's consumer sets the group's throughput.

The other consequence of hashing modulo the partition count is that adding partitions remaps keys. Growing a topic from 12 to 16 partitions sends 75.08% of keys to a different partition; growing it to 13 moves 92.18%; doubling to 24 moves exactly the half whose hash bit selects the new range (50.14%). For the duration of the transition, old records for a key sit in one partition and new ones in another, and two consumers may process them concurrently. Records without a key take a different path: since KIP-480 the client fills a batch for one partition before moving on (the sticky partitioner), and KIP-794 corrected that scheme's tendency to send more records to slower brokers11,12.

The Java client

Every Spring abstraction in this article delegates to two classes in kafka-clients: KafkaProducer and KafkaConsumer. Understanding where each one declares completion is the whole of the framework's job description.

Producing

KafkaProducer.send() serialises the record, chooses a partition, appends it to an in-memory batch and returns a Future immediately; a background I/O thread ships batches to partition leaders. The acks setting decides what the leader waits for before answering: nothing (0), its own append (1), or the full in-sync replica set (all), with the topic's min.insync.replicas setting the smallest set the leader will accept5,13. The second setting matters as much as the first: an early independent fault-injection test showed that an in-sync set allowed to shrink to the leader alone can lose writes the leader had already acknowledged14. A dropped response is retried until delivery.timeout.ms expires. Retries are where duplicates come from: if the leader appended the batch and the response was lost, a naive retry appends it twice. The idempotent producer removes this by giving each producer a numeric identity and each batch a per-partition sequence number, which the broker uses to discard duplicates and reject gaps9.

Kafka 3.0 made acks=all and enable.idempotence=true the defaults under KIP-67915,16. The history has a wrinkle worth knowing: a configuration-validation bug meant idempotence was not in fact switched on by default in the first release that intended it, and a follow-up fix was needed17. The fix also made the client quietly turn idempotence off when it conflicts with explicit settings such as acks=1, which is why the listing below states both values instead of trusting the defaults. Kafka 4.0 raised the default linger.ms from 0 to 5 milliseconds under KIP-1030, on the reasoning that a zero linger sends one record per request under light load18.

The lab measures what these settings buy. It draws 4,000 batches of 25 records with independent fault probabilities: a request that fails before the leader appends it, a response lost after the append, and (under acks=1 only) a leader that acknowledges and then dies before its followers copy the batch. Figure 4 shows the results. With acks=1 the 2 failovers lose 50 acknowledged records outright, and because acks=1 disables idempotence the 16 lost responses produce 400 duplicates while the 12 failed requests are reordered behind batches already in flight. With acks=all but idempotence off, the losses disappear and the duplicates and reorderings do not. With the defaults, all three counts are zero.

The fourth bar is the one that matters for application design. Idempotence is scoped to a producer session. If the process restarts and resends whatever it had not seen acknowledged, the new producer has a new identity and the broker has no basis for recognising the resend: two in-flight batches that had in fact reached the log produce 50 duplicates. Plates 1 to 4 show the same thing on the tape. Treating send() returning as completion (plate P01) loses the two records still in the client buffer when the process dies. Awaiting the acknowledgement (P02) converts that loss into two duplicates, which is the better failure because it can be detected downstream. Lowering acks (P03) or disabling idempotence (P04) adds a failure to the fault row that the defaults do not have.

Consuming

KafkaConsumer is a pull client. The application calls poll() in a loop; each call returns up to max.poll.records records (500 by default) and also performs the group-membership work that keeps the consumer alive19. Since KIP-62 heartbeats are sent from a background thread, but a consumer that does not call poll() within max.poll.interval.ms is still considered failed and its partitions are reassigned20. The class is not thread-safe, so one consumer belongs to one thread3.

Position is written by committing offsets. With enable.auto.commit=true, the client commits the offsets returned by the previous poll each time it polls again after auto.commit.interval.ms; with it off, the application calls commitSync() or commitAsync(). Either way, Kafka's documentation is explicit that processing before committing gives at-least-once delivery and committing before processing gives at-most-once1. KIP-848's new group protocol, generally available since Kafka 4.0, moves partition assignment from a client-side leader to the broker and must be requested with group.protocol=consumer8,21; Kafka 4.3 began logging a recommendation to move off the classic protocol ahead of deprecating it22.

Plates P07 and P08 show that the plain consumer, used as above, has the same crash row whether the library commits or the application does: every record handled since the last poll is handled again. Its fault row is worse than anything in the Spring plates. An exception that escapes the loop closes the consumer; the restarted consumer resumes from the last committed offset, handles the five records before the poison again, and fails again. The partition never advances. The loop is the error handler, and an empty one stalls.

Spring for Apache Kafka and Spring Boot

Spring for Apache Kafka applies the usual Spring division of labour to the two client classes: a template for sending and message-driven POJOs for receiving23. The receiving half is where the framework earns its keep. A listener container owns the KafkaConsumer, runs the poll loop on its own thread, invokes your @KafkaListener method for each record or batch, commits offsets according to an AckMode, and routes exceptions to an error handler24. In ladder terms the container moves the D3/D4 decision from application code into the framework. The current line is Spring for Apache Kafka 4.1 (4.1.0 reached general availability on 9 June 2026, built on the 4.2.1 client) and it ships in Spring Boot 4.1, released the following day25,26.

Spring Boot adds auto-configuration: a KafkaTemplate, a ConsumerFactory, a ConcurrentKafkaListenerContainerFactory configured from spring.kafka.listener.*, and, if spring.kafka.producer.transaction-id-prefix is set, a KafkaTransactionManager wired into that factory27. Spring Boot 4 also changed packaging: a Boot 4 project needs the spring-boot-starter-kafka dependency explicitly, and the release notes for Spring for Apache Kafka 4.1.0 added a documentation note saying so25.

Two lines in that file change the crash row and the fault row respectively, and they are the subject of the next four sections. The plate below shows what you get without them. The default AckMode is BATCH: offsets are committed after all records from a poll have been handled, so a crash replays the whole poll. And when no error handler bean is defined, the container still has one: a DefaultErrorHandler that tries a failing record ten times with no delay and then logs it and commits past it28. That is a reasonable default for a framework, because the alternative is the stall in P07. It is also a data-loss policy that nobody wrote down.

KafkaTemplate and producer configuration

KafkaTemplate wraps a ProducerFactory and exposes send methods that return a CompletableFuture<SendResult<K,V>>29. The template adds no delivery guarantee of its own; it is a convenience over KafkaProducer with Spring's conversion and observation hooks. What it changes is ergonomics: because the result is a CompletableFuture, completion handling composes with the rest of a Spring application, and code that ignores the future is easy to spot in review. The default producer factory shares one thread-safe KafkaProducer across callers, the same sharing the Kafka documentation recommends for the raw client3,30.

Producer configuration in Boot goes under spring.kafka.producer.*, with anything Boot does not model passed through spring.kafka.producer.properties.*27. Serialisers deserve one comment. Spring for Apache Kafka 4.0 moved to Jackson 3 and deprecated the old JsonSerializer and JsonDeserializer in favour of JacksonJsonSerializer and JacksonJsonDeserializer, with the old classes marked for removal31. Type information travels in record headers, and the consumer will only instantiate types from packages it trusts. That trust check has itself been a vulnerability: CVE-2026-41731, published 10 June 2026 with a CVSS score of 8.1, found that the header mappers matched trusted packages by prefix, so trusting one package trusted all of its subpackages and let a hostile producer steer deserialisation into JDK types32. The listing above names one exact package rather than a wildcard for that reason.

@KafkaListener and consumer configuration

A @KafkaListener method is a POJO endpoint; the framework converts each record's value to the parameter type and can inject the key, partition, offset, headers or the raw ConsumerRecord33. The interesting attribute is the container's AckMode, which decides when the container commits. BATCH, the default, commits once the records from a poll have been handled; RECORD commits after each record; COUNT and TIME commit after a number of records or an interval; MANUAL and MANUAL_IMMEDIATE leave the decision to the listener through an Acknowledgment parameter24. The same container properties object carries the commit-related knobs (ackCount, ackTime, syncCommits) that tune the intermediate modes34. Spring for Apache Kafka 4.1 added an ackMode attribute to @KafkaListener, so a single listener can override the factory's mode without a separate factory bean35.

The ack mode is a price list. Figure 5 shows the lab's measurement, with the crash placed uniformly within a 500-record poll and always at the worst instant in the record's cycle (after the record's side effect, before any commit due then). BATCH replays a mean of 250.92 records per crash and issues 20 commit requests per 10,000 records; RECORD replays 1.00 and issues 10,000. The two intermediate COUNT settings sit on the same straight line in log-log space: halving the replay window doubles the commit traffic. No setting reaches zero, because the record in flight when the process dies is always replayed. That residual is the reason every Kafka consumer should be written as an idempotent receiver, a pattern that predates Kafka by years36,37.

Plates P10 and P11 have identical tapes. The difference is who decides. Under RECORD the container commits after your method returns normally; under MANUAL_IMMEDIATE your code calls acknowledge(), which matters when a method returns before its work is finished (an asynchronous call, say) or when some records should not advance the position at all. Spring for Apache Kafka also supports acknowledging records out of order and committing only the contiguous prefix, so asynchronous processing does not have to fall back to BATCH to stay correct24.

Three consumer settings deserve to be set deliberately. auto.offset.reset decides where a group with no committed position starts; the default latest silently skips everything already in the topic, which is surprising for a new service reading an existing stream19. max.poll.records sets the size of the replay window under BATCH and the time budget per poll. And isolation.level=read_committed, which only matters if someone writes to the topic transactionally, hides records from aborted transactions (the default is read_uncommitted)9,19.

Error handlers

When a listener throws, the container hands the exception to a CommonErrorHandler; since version 2.8 one interface covers both record and batch listeners28. The default implementation, DefaultErrorHandler, works by seeking. For a record listener it commits the offsets of records that succeeded earlier in the poll, then seeks the consumer back to the failed record and to the first unprocessed record of every other partition, so the next poll redelivers them28. A BackOff decides how many times and how far apart; when it is exhausted, a recoverer is called and the record's offset is committed. The default back-off is FixedBackOff(0L, 9), ten attempts with no delay, and the default recoverer logs the record at ERROR28.

The handler classifies exceptions. Some are treated as fatal and go straight to the recoverer without retrying: DeserializationException, MessageConversionException, ConversionException, MethodArgumentResolutionException, NoSuchMethodException and ClassCastException, on the reasoning that a retry will not change the bytes28. Your own validation exceptions belong in the same list. The documentation also carries a warning that is easy to miss: the handler only sees exceptions that extend RuntimeException. An exception that extends Error bypasses the handler, terminates the consumer and closes its connection, and the application can continue to report itself healthy with a listener that no longer consumes28.

Two properties of the default handler matter when choosing a back-off. First, it retries on the consumer thread, so a delay stops the whole partition and every other partition that thread owns. The default back-off does not use a delay, which is why it is cheap. Second, a long delay can exceed max.poll.interval.ms; the framework's ContainerPausingBackOffHandler pauses the container instead of sleeping for exactly that case28. The lab measures the first property in the section on retry topics. The plate below is the handler as shipped.

Dead-letter topics

A dead-letter channel is the place a messaging system puts what it cannot deliver; the pattern is older than Kafka and appears under that name in Hohpe and Woolf36. Kafka itself has no dead-letter mechanism for ordinary consumers (Kafka Connect added one for its own pipelines in KIP-298)38, so in Spring it is a recoverer. DeadLetterPublishingRecoverer publishes the failed record to another topic using a KafkaTemplate. By default the destination is the original topic name suffixed with -dlt, on the same partition number, which means the dead-letter topic must have at least as many partitions as its source28. The published record carries headers naming the exception class, cause, message and stack trace, and the original topic, partition, offset, timestamp and consumer group, so a later process can decide what to do with it28.

Plugging the recoverer into the handler changes exactly one cell of the tape: the logged skip becomes a parked record (P13). The crash row does not move. That is the disjointness in its plainest form, and it is worth stating because the two are often discussed as if a dead-letter topic made a consumer reliable. It makes a failure retrievable. It has no bearing on duplicates.

Deserialisation failures need one more piece. If the value deserialiser throws inside poll(), the failure happens before the container has a record to hand to anyone. ErrorHandlingDeserializer wraps the real deserialiser, catches the exception, and delivers a record whose value is null and whose headers carry the failure; the container then raises a DeserializationException, which the error handler classifies as fatal and sends to the recoverer on the first attempt28,39. The recoverer restores the original bytes as the dead-letter record's value. If the dead-letter template uses a JSON serialiser it will fail to write those raw bytes, so the documentation recommends a template whose value serialiser can handle both objects and byte[], such as a DelegatingByTypeSerializer40.

A dead-letter topic is a queue of decisions nobody has made yet. It needs an owner, an alert on its lag, a retention period longer than the time anyone takes to look, and a replay tool. Uber's account of building retry and dead-letter queues on Kafka is explicit that dead-lettered records were merged back into processing by republishing them, which is only safe if the consumer is idempotent41. The DeadLetterPublishingRecoverer exposes header-management options for exactly this case, because each republish appends another set of exception headers and a record that cycles often enough eventually fails with RecordTooLargeException28.

Retry topics

Blocking retries hold the partition. For a transient failure (a downstream service briefly unavailable) that is often the wrong trade: one slow record delays every record behind it. The non-blocking alternative, which Uber described in 2018 and Spring for Apache Kafka now provides as @RetryableTopic, moves a failed record to a separate retry topic with a timestamp, and a separate consumer on that topic pauses its partition until the record is due41,42. A chain of such topics implements a back-off; the last hop is a dead-letter topic. With a 1,000 ms initial delay, a multiplier of 2 and four attempts, the framework creates -retry-1000, -retry-2000, -retry-4000 and -dlt topics and the consumers for them42. The documentation recommends AckMode.RECORD for these containers and states plainly that the strategy gives up Kafka's ordering guarantee for the topic42. It is not supported for batch listeners28.

The lab compares four strategies on one partition: 100,000 records at 40 per second, 20 ms of work each (80% utilisation), with 495 transient failures that succeed on the third attempt and 54 poison records. Figure 6 shows the 99th-percentile latency of the records that never failed. The shipped default (FixedBackOff(0, 9)) costs healthy records almost nothing: p99 110 ms, because ten immediate attempts take 200 ms. A one-second blocking back-off is a different system. With two retries, each transient failure costs two seconds of a partition that only has 20% spare capacity, the queue never recovers (by Little's law the number of records waiting is the arrival rate times the time each waits, so a growing wait means a growing backlog43), and the healthy p99 reaches 611 s. Retry topics hold the healthy p99 at 20 ms because the main consumer handles each record once, whether it fails or not.

The cost is ordering. Of the 549 rerouted records in the run, the lab counts 113 later records with the same key (out of 500 keys) that were processed before the retried record they followed. If the records are commands against the same entity (a debit followed by a closure, say), that is a correctness problem, not a latency statistic.

There is a second, less obvious cost. A retry chain covers an outage only as long as its total delay. Figure 7 takes failures that begin uniformly during a downstream outage and asks what share of them exhaust every attempt before the outage ends. Ten blocking attempts one second apart span nine seconds; the 1, 2, 4 second chain spans seven. For a ten-second outage 30.0% of failing records reach the dead-letter topic under the retry chain against 10.0% under blocking retries; for a sixty-second outage the figures are 88.3% and 85.0%. Neither strategy is an outage strategy. If downstream outages longer than the chain are expected, the right tool is pausing the container (or the partition) until the dependency recovers, not adding attempts.

Batch consumers

Setting batchListener on the container factory (or spring.kafka.listener.type=batch in Boot) delivers the whole poll to one method call as a List27,33. Batch listeners suit bulk sinks: one database round trip per poll instead of one per record. They also move the hardest question in error handling into application code, because the container cannot know which element of a list caused an exception.

The framework offers two answers28. If the listener throws BatchListenerFailedException carrying the failed record or its index, the error handler commits the offsets before that index, retries from the failed record onward, and on exhaustion recovers only that record and redelivers the rest. If the listener throws anything else, the handler falls back to retrying the whole batch from memory, pausing the consumer between attempts, and when the attempts are exhausted it calls the recoverer for every record in the batch.

The lab puts one poison record at a uniformly random position in a 500-record batch with ten delivery attempts. Figure 8 shows the result as a function of the poison's position. With BatchListenerFailedException the listener is invoked 509 times per batch on average (the 500 records plus nine retries of the poison) and nothing is lost. With a plain exception it is invoked 2,505 times, because everything before the poison is handled again on every attempt, and an average of 249.5 records that were never handled at all are passed to the recoverer. With the default logging recoverer, they are logged and their offsets committed. One bad record silently discards half a batch of good ones. Plates P16 and P17 show the difference on the tape.

Concurrent consumers and rebalancing

A ConcurrentMessageListenerContainer with concurrency set to c creates c child containers, each with its own KafkaConsumer and thread, all in the same consumer group24. Partitions are the unit of parallelism, so concurrency above the partition count leaves threads idle and concurrency that does not divide the partition count leaves the busiest thread with more than its share. Figure 9 computes relative throughput for 12 equally loaded partitions: 5 threads are no faster than 4 and 7 to 11 are no faster than 6, because some thread still owns 3 or 2 partitions respectively. Above 12 the extra threads idle.

The classic range assignor makes this worse when one listener subscribes to several topics, because it assigns each topic independently and gives the remainder to the same first members every time. With three topics of four partitions and five consumers, the range assignment is [3, 3, 3, 3, 0]: the fifth consumer receives nothing from any topic. A uniform assignment of the same twelve partitions gives [3, 3, 2, 2, 2]. With six consumers the range assignor still leaves two idle and the busiest at 3 partitions, against 2 under a uniform assignment.

Concurrency does not change what done means. Each child container commits for its own partitions under the same AckMode, which is why plate P18 has the same tape as P09. It changes the blast radius of a failure (a stalled partition stops one thread rather than the whole listener) and it changes how often the group rebalances, because every container restart, deployment or scale event changes group membership. Rebalancing is where the crash row is most often exercised in practice: partitions are revoked from one member and assigned to another, and whatever the first member handled but did not commit is handled again by the second. Spring's ConsumerAwareRebalanceListener exposes the revocation callback so that pending offsets can be committed before partitions move44.

How much moves in a rebalance depends on the protocol. Under the original eager protocol every member revokes every partition before reassignment. Incremental cooperative rebalancing (KIP-429) revokes only the partitions that actually move, and the KIP-848 protocol makes the same incremental behaviour the default and moves the computation to the broker21,45. Figure 10 counts the revocations when a twelve-partition group grows by one member: the eager protocol stops all 12 partitions every time, while the incremental protocols stop 6 when going from one member to two and 1 when going from seven to eight. Static membership (KIP-345) addresses the other common cause, restarts, by letting a member that returns with the same group.instance.id within the session timeout reclaim its partitions without a rebalance at all46.

Some workloads want parallelism that partitions cannot give: many workers on few partitions, with per-record acknowledgement and no ordering requirement. After an early-access release in 4.0 and a preview in 4.1, Kafka 4.2 declared share groups (KIP-932, "queues for Kafka") production-ready8,47,48; members of a share group receive records from the same partitions concurrently, acknowledge or release each record individually, and the broker counts delivery attempts49. Spring for Apache Kafka supports share consumers, including the renew() acknowledgement that extends a record's lock during long processing50,51. Share groups trade away ordering entirely, so they are a different answer to the question in this article, not a faster version of the same one.

Transactions

Kafka transactions, introduced by KIP-98 in 2017, let a producer write to several partitions and commit a consumer offset as one atomic unit, with consumers in read_committed mode never seeing records from aborted transactions9,52. A producer with a stable transactional.id fences any older instance with the same identifier, so a zombie cannot commit after its replacement has started. KIP-447 made the fencing work from the consumer group's metadata rather than requiring one producer per input partition, which is what made transactions practical for consumer applications53, and KIP-890 added server-side checks against a class of hanging and zombie transactions, with its second phase completed in Kafka 4.08,54.

Spring for Apache Kafka exposes this through KafkaTransactionManager. When a listener container has one, it begins a transaction before invoking the listener, any KafkaTemplate sends join that transaction, and on success the container adds the consumed offsets with sendOffsetsToTransaction() before committing55. Since version 3.0 only the EOSMode.V2 fencing of KIP-447 is supported55,56. In Boot, setting spring.kafka.producer.transaction-id-prefix is enough to create the transaction manager and associate it with the auto-configured factory27,57.

The Spring reference states the guarantee precisely: for a read, process, write sequence the sequence completes exactly once, while the read and the processing are still at-least-once55. Plate P19 shows what that sentence means. In the crash row all eleven handled records are handled again, but their earlier outputs belong to an aborted transaction that read_committed consumers never see; the tape draws them as fenced. That fence covers only what the transaction covers. A database row written, an email sent or an HTTP call made from inside the listener is outside Kafka's transaction and is duplicated exactly as in P09. Joining Kafka's transaction to a database transaction is a distributed commit, which Kafka does not provide58,59; the standard workaround runs the other way round, writing the event into the database inside the business transaction and relaying it to Kafka afterwards (a transactional outbox)60.

The fault row of P19 is the price of the guarantee. With a transactional batch listener the container cannot tell which record in the list failed, so after a rollback it re-fetches and reprocesses the whole batch; by default no recovery is possible and a poison record repeats forever, each attempt aborting the work done on the records before it28. Since version 3.2 ContainerProperties.setBatchRecoverAfterRollback(true) lets the after-rollback processor give up on the entire batch after its maximum failures, which replaces the stall with a recovered batch. For record listeners, the DefaultAfterRollbackProcessor plays the part the DefaultErrorHandler plays without transactions, including recovery to a dead-letter topic inside a new transaction28.

Transactions also cost throughput and latency, and both depend on how much work each transaction covers. Figure 11 uses an illustrative model in which each transaction pays a fixed 10 ms of coordination and each record 0.2 ms of work, arriving at 5,000 per second. One record per transaction runs at 1.96% of the non-transactional rate and three of every four offsets in the output partitions are spent on commit markers, because each transaction writes one control record to each partition it touched. At 500 records per transaction the efficiency is 90.91% and markers fall to 0.60%, but a read_committed consumer now waits a mean of 59.9 ms for each record, because nothing is visible until its transaction commits. The model's constants are assumptions; the shape (a knee where fixed cost and visibility delay cross) is not, and it is why the commit interval of a transactional application is a latency setting.

Testing the contract

The tapes in this article are claims about configurations, and claims about failure behaviour are cheap to make and expensive to discover wrong in production. Spring for Apache Kafka's test module provides an embedded broker (@EmbeddedKafka, which since Kafka 4.0 is always the KRaft implementation) and KafkaTestUtils for reading what an application wrote; Boot applications pull it in through spring-boot-starter-kafka-test, and the embedded broker publishes its address as spring.kafka.bootstrap-servers by default61. The test below pins down the fault row of the configuration from the error-handler section: a poison record must arrive on the dead-letter topic, at the same partition, with the original offset in its headers.

The same harness can pin down the crash row, by stopping the listener container between a record's side effect and its commit and asserting how many times the side effect ran after restart. Kleppmann's treatment of exactly-once processing is a useful antidote here: the property is a statement about the observable effects of processing, so the test has to observe effects, not offsets62.

The ledger

Figure 12 places all twenty plates by their two rows. Read along a row and the crash outcome varies while the fault outcome stays fixed; read down a column and the reverse. Among the Spring consumer plates, the crash row takes exactly three values, 11 replayed records for poll-level commits, 1 for record-level commits and 11 fenced for a transaction, and it is determined by the ack mode and transaction manager alone. The fault row is determined by the error handler and listener type alone. No error-handler setting changes the crash row, and no ack mode changes the fault row.

Four findings follow from the census and the experiments behind it.

The defaults are at-least-once with a logged skip. A Spring Boot application with no Kafka configuration (P20) replays a poll after a crash and, after ten immediate attempts, logs and commits past a record it cannot handle. 9 of the twenty plates end a row with a record that is gone apart from a log line or was never written; on the consumer side every plate without a recoverer is among them. A recoverer is the smallest change that removes silent loss, and it costs one bean.

Only one plate is clean in both rows, and it is a producer. The transactional KafkaTemplate (P06) has neither duplicate nor loss in either scenario. No consumer configuration achieves that. The nearest are record-level commits with a dead-letter or retry-topic recoverer (one replayed record per crash) and the transactional listener (no visible duplicates, but a poison batch stalls until a recoverer is added). Exactly-once applies to Kafka outputs; for every other side effect the consumer must be idempotent37,55.

The plain exception in a batch listener is the most expensive line in this article. It turns one poison record into an average of 249.5 unprocessed records handed to the recoverer, and with the default recoverer they are logged and committed. BatchListenerFailedException reduces that to zero.

Blocking retries are cheap until they have a delay. The default immediate back-off costs healthy records a p99 of 110 ms; a one-second back-off on a partition at 80% utilisation produced a backlog with a healthy-record p99 of 611 s. Retry topics remove the backlog and trade ordering for it (113 same-key overtakings in the run). Neither covers an outage longer than its chain.

Read as a checklist, the ledger says: state acks and idempotence explicitly and await the send future (D2, not D0); choose the ack mode by the size of replay you can tolerate and make the listener idempotent for the remainder; give the error handler a dead-letter recoverer and classify your own non-retryable exceptions; throw BatchListenerFailedException from batch listeners; use retry topics only for keys whose order does not matter; size concurrency to divide the partition count and prefer the KIP-848 protocol; and reach for transactions when the outputs that must not duplicate are Kafka records, remembering that the outbox, not the transaction, is the answer when the outputs are rows.
