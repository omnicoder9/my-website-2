Frame 01 · the problemWhy crosscutting resists objects

Every argument for aspect-oriented programming begins with the same observation, and it is worth restating precisely rather than sloganistically: some requirements do not decompose along the same axis as the rest of the system. Object-oriented decomposition is a single hierarchy. A requirement that must hold across the hierarchy has nowhere to live in it.

The canonical statement of this is Kiczales and colleagues at Xerox PARC in 1997, who named the phenomenon and proposed a language mechanism for it. Their formulation was that a programming system offers a decomposition, that decomposition is good at capturing some properties and bad at capturing others, and the properties it is bad at capturing are exactly those that crosscut the units the decomposition provides.1,2 Kiczales coined the term crosscutting for precisely this.3 The claim was not that object orientation is wrong. It was that a second, orthogonal decomposition is sometimes needed, and that hand-rolling it produces two well-known pathologies.

Scattering is one concern implemented in many places. Transaction demarcation in a service layer of forty classes is scattered across forty classes. Tangling is many concerns implemented in one place. A method that opens a transaction, checks an authority, starts a timer, writes a log line and then does three lines of arithmetic is tangled: five vocabularies compete for the reader's attention, and the one the method is named after is outnumbered.

Here is what tangling looks like when a real method has been asked to carry five concerns. The business meaning is one line. The other seventeen are infrastructure that the method is not about.

Three things are wrong with that method, and only one of them is aesthetic. First, the business logic is a minority of the text, which raises the cost of every future read. Second, the infrastructure is duplicated: forty sibling methods contain forty slightly different copies of it, and the differences between them are undocumented and mostly accidental. Third, and worst, the correctness of the whole system now depends on an unenforceable convention. Nothing fails if a new method forgets the authority check. Nothing warns you if a developer commits before the audit record is written rather than after. The requirement “every command is authorised, audited, timed and transactional” exists only in prose.

A crosscutting concern implemented by convention is a requirement with no representation in the program. Aspect-oriented programming is the proposal that such requirements should have one.

The AOP answer is to give the second decomposition a first-class unit — the aspect — and a way to say where it applies that does not require editing the places where it applies. What makes AOP genuinely different from ordinary indirection is the second half of that sentence. A decorator, a template method, a servlet filter and a delegating wrapper can all factor out repeated infrastructure. What they cannot do is quantify: state a predicate over program points and have the behaviour attach to every point that satisfies it, including points written by someone who has never heard of the aspect. That property — sometimes called obliviousness, and always contentious — is the source of both AOP's power and its bad reputation.

Spring's position on this argument is worth stating up front, because it explains almost every design decision in Spring AOP. Spring is not trying to be a general aspect language. It is trying to provide, in the phrasing of its own reference documentation, an AOP implementation good enough for the crosscutting concerns that occur in enterprise applications, integrated with its IoC container, and free of any requirement for a special compiler or a special class loader.4,5 Where a full weaver would be more powerful, the documentation says so and points at AspectJ.6 That restraint is the whole story of the sections that follow.

Frame 02 · lineageFrom a PARC research language to @Retryable in core

The technology has a legible history, and knowing it explains several otherwise arbitrary details — not least why a framework with no dependency on the AspectJ compiler nevertheless requires aspectjweaver on the classpath.

1997–2001: AspectJ

The 1997 ECOOP paper introduced the idea; AspectJ, developed by the same group at PARC, made it a language. The 2001 ECOOP paper describing AspectJ set out the join point model, pointcuts, advice and inter-type declarations in essentially the form the industry still uses.7 AspectJ moved to the Eclipse Foundation, where it remains an active project; the language and its compiler are still maintained and still track new Java releases.8,9 By 2001 Kiczales was already writing about AOP as an established practice with a growing tool ecosystem rather than a proposal.10 The reference material of that era — the Programming Guide, the Semantics appendix — is still the authoritative description of the join point model that Spring borrows.11

2002–2004: Spring, interception, and the AOP Alliance

Spring grew out of infrastructure code published with Rod Johnson's Expert One-on-One J2EE Design and Development in late 2002; it became an open-source project in early 2003 and reached 1.0 in March 2004.12 Its central argument — that the services EJB provided through a heavyweight container could be delivered to plain objects by a lightweight one — requires exactly the AOP capability under discussion: declarative transactions on a POJO are advice applied to a method you did not write specially.13

Spring 1.x AOP looked nothing like today's. There were no pointcut expressions. You wrote a MethodInterceptor, paired it with a Pointcut to form an Advisor, and declared a ProxyFactoryBean per proxied bean in XML. The join point was already restricted to method invocation, field interception was explicitly declined as a violation of encapsulation, and the interception interfaces were deliberately taken from the AOP Alliance, a cross-project effort to standardise interception so that aspects could move between frameworks.14,15 That decision has outlived the alliance itself: org.aopalliance.intercept.MethodInterceptor is still the interface every Spring advice ultimately implements, and it is still the interface Guice's interception uses too.16,17

2006: the @AspectJ style

Spring 2.0 is the pivot. It adopted the annotation style introduced by AspectJ 5, and with it the AspectJ pointcut expression language, while keeping the runtime purely proxy-based.18 The reference documentation of the period is unusually explicit about the split: Spring interprets the same annotations as AspectJ, using an AspectJ-supplied library for pointcut parsing and matching, but the AOP runtime is still Spring's own and there is no dependency on the AspectJ compiler or weaver. This is the origin of the classpath requirement that confuses newcomers — org.aspectj:aspectjweaver is needed for expression parsing, not for weaving.19,20 The same release introduced typed advice parameters, replacing the Object[] of the 1.x interceptor API, and the schema-based <aop:config> namespace for teams that preferred XML.21 The structure has been stable ever since; the 4.3 reference chapter reads almost identically to the 7.0 one.22

2022–2026: ahead-of-time, and more advisors in core

Two recent movements matter for anyone operating Spring AOP today. The first is ahead-of-time processing and GraalVM native images, which remove the ability to generate bytecode at run time and therefore force proxy creation into the build.23 The second is the steady migration of advisor-based features into the framework itself. Spring Framework 7.0 reached general availability on 13 November 2025 with a Java 17 baseline, Jakarta EE 11, and an explicit focus on Java 25;24,25 Spring Boot 4.0 followed on 20 November 2025, and Boot 4.1 shipped in June 2026.26,27 Among the additions is a set of core resilience features — @Retryable and @ConcurrencyLimit, enabled by @EnableResilientMethods — which are, mechanically, more advisors on the same chain that already carries @Transactional.28,29 Notably, the throttling half of that feature is implemented by ConcurrencyThrottleInterceptor, a class that has existed for programmatic use with the Spring AOP framework since version 1.0. The framework's answer to virtual threads, twenty-one years later, is an interceptor from the first release.30,31

The practical consequence for a working engineer is that the amount of AOP you should write yourself has fallen steadily for twenty years, while the amount of AOP running in your application has risen. Both trends are healthy. Neither reduces the need to understand the machinery, because the failures are the same whether the advisor was written by you or by Broadcom.

Frame 03 · vocabularySeven terms, defined so they stay defined

AOP terminology is unintuitive, and Spring's own documentation has said so since 2004 — while declining to invent friendlier words, on the grounds that a private vocabulary would be worse.14 The terms are worth learning exactly, because most confused AOP code is confused about one of them.

Join point. A point during the execution of a program at which advice could, in principle, run. In the full AspectJ model this includes method calls, method executions, constructor calls and executions, field reads and writes, exception handler execution, static and object initialisation, and advice execution itself. In Spring AOP a join point is always a method execution, on a Spring bean.32 Note the distinction between call and execution: a call join point exists at the caller, an execution join point exists at the callee. Spring only ever has the latter, which is the root cause of the self-invocation behaviour discussed later.

Pointcut. A predicate that matches join points. In Spring, pointcut expressions are written in the AspectJ pointcut language and evaluated by AspectJ's parser and matcher, then applied to Spring's much smaller join point set.33 A pointcut is a first-class, nameable, composable thing; treating it as an incidental string inside an annotation is the most common structural mistake in real aspect code.

Advice. The action taken at a matched join point. Spring supports before, after returning, after throwing, after (finally) and around advice.34 All five reduce to around advice over a proceed() call, a fact this article will lean on repeatedly.

Aspect. The module that groups pointcuts and advice for one concern. In the @AspectJ style it is an ordinary class annotated @Aspect; in Spring it is also an ordinary bean, with dependencies injected the ordinary way.35 By default an aspect is a singleton, which means advice must be stateless with respect to the invocation; the perthis and pertarget instantiation models exist if you genuinely need per-target aspect state, and are rarely the right answer.36

Advisor. A Spring-only term with no AspectJ equivalent: a small self-contained aspect consisting of exactly one advice and one pointcut.18 This is the currency of framework code. Spring Security's method security, the transaction interceptor and the caching interceptor are all advisors, not @Aspect classes.37

Target and proxy. The target is the object being advised; the proxy is the object handed to callers in its place.38 These are two distinct objects with distinct identities. Spring's pointcut language deliberately lets you match on either — this() binds the proxy, target() binds the object behind it — a distinction that does not exist in AspectJ, where at an execution join point both refer to the same object.33

Weaving. Linking aspects with the objects they advise, at compile time, load time or run time.32 Spring AOP weaves at run time, during bean creation, by wrapping. Everything else in this article follows from that choice.

Two further terms complete the set. Introduction, also called an inter-type declaration, adds a new interface and implementation to an advised type; Spring supports it in a narrowed form through @DeclareParents.39 And auto-proxying is the container behaviour that makes all of this ergonomic: at bean creation, Spring asks whether any advisor's pointcut matches the bean's type, and if so replaces the bean with a proxy carrying the matching advisors.40

Frame 04 · the modelOne join point kind, and why that is a feature

The single most useful sentence in the Spring AOP documentation is this: Spring AOP only supports method execution join points for Spring beans.33 Almost every surprising behaviour in production is a corollary of it.

The reasoning is mechanical rather than philosophical. Spring's weaving mechanism is a wrapper object. A wrapper receives control when someone invokes a method on the wrapper. It cannot receive control when a field is read, because field reads are not dispatched. It cannot receive control when a constructor runs, because the object does not exist yet. It cannot receive control when the target calls itself, because that call never reaches the wrapper. AspectJ can do all three because it rewrites bytecode: the advice is inserted at the shadow itself, not in front of the object.

The full AspectJ pointcut language contains designators Spring simply does not implement, and the documentation lists them explicitly: call, get, set, preinitialization, staticinitialization, initialization, handler, adviceexecution, withincode, cflow, cflowbelow, if, @this and @withincode.33 Encountering any of these in a Spring pointcut expression produces a parse or match failure, not silent success, which is one of the few places the framework fails loudly.

There is a second restriction inside the first, and it is the one that bites: on Spring beans. An object created with new is not advised, no matter how well it matches a pointcut. A domain entity constructed by Hibernate is not advised. A record returned from a factory method is not advised. If the aspect you want operates on the domain model rather than the service layer, proxy-based AOP is structurally the wrong tool and load-time weaving is the right one — which is precisely why Spring ships spring-aspects with @Configurable support for dependency-injecting objects the container did not create.41

What the restriction buys

It is worth being explicit that the narrow model is not merely a limitation to be worked around. Three real properties follow from it.

  • The build is unchanged. No special compiler, no agent, no post-processing step. javac output runs unmodified, which means every debugger, profiler, coverage tool and bytecode-reading library behaves normally.5
  • The failure surface is bounded. An aspect can only affect calls that cross a bean boundary, so the blast radius of a badly written aspect is the set of public service methods — large, but enumerable, and visible in a stack trace.
  • The mental model is checkable at run time. Because proxies are objects, you can ask a running application what it actually did: AopUtils.isAopProxy, AopProxyUtils.ultimateTargetClass, and casting to Advised to read the advisor chain.42 Woven bytecode offers no equivalent introspection.

A weaver can advise anything and tells you nothing. A proxy can advise one thing and tells you everything. For infrastructure that has to be operated at three in the morning, the second trade is usually the better one.

Frame 05 · weavingFour moments at which bytecode can change

Weaving is the step that makes an aspect take effect. The interesting variable is when, because that choice determines what can be advised, what tooling still works, and where the failure shows up when it goes wrong.

Compile-time weaving

The AspectJ compiler ajc compiles Java sources and aspects together, emitting class files with the advice already inlined at each matched shadow. This gives the full join point model, no runtime indirection, and errors at compile time. It costs you a non-standard compiler in the build, and it means the code your debugger shows is not the code that runs.11

Post-compile (binary) weaving

The same compiler can weave an already-compiled jar. This is the answer when the code you need to advise is a third-party dependency you cannot recompile. It also explains a recurring puzzle with aspect libraries: a class compiled by javac and annotated @Aspect is not a complete AspectJ aspect until ajc has finished it, which is why some annotation-driven library aspects work under Spring AOP and under load-time weaving but silently do nothing under compile-time weaving unless the library itself is woven.

Load-time weaving

A JVM agent transforms classes as the class loader reads them, driven by META-INF/aop.xml. Spring integrates this through @EnableLoadTimeWeaving and a LoadTimeWeaver abstraction; AspectJ weaving is activated by default when an aop.xml resource is on the classpath.43,44 In environments where no specific LoadTimeWeaver implementation applies, Spring provides InstrumentationLoadTimeWeaver, which needs the general-purpose spring-instrument.jar agent, auto-detected by the usual @EnableLoadTimeWeaving and <context:load-time-weaver/> setups.41

LTW gives you the full join point model with a stock compiler, at the cost of a JVM launch flag and a measurable increase in class-loading time. It also has a real ergonomic cost that is easy to underrate until you hit it: stepping through woven code in a debugger is materially harder than stepping through proxied code, a complaint the Spring issue tracker has carried since 2013.45 Use it when you must advise domain objects, and confine it to that.

Proxy weaving: what Spring AOP actually does

Spring's auto-proxy creator runs as a bean post-processor. For each bean, it asks every registered advisor whether its pointcut matches the bean's type; if any do, it builds a proxy carrying the matching advisors in precedence order and returns the proxy instead of the bean.40 Nothing is rewritten. A new object is created.

Frame 06 · proxies$Proxy42 or $$SpringCGLIB$$0, and what each costs

Spring AOP creates the proxy with either a JDK dynamic proxy or CGLIB, which is repackaged inside spring-core. If the target implements at least one interface, a JDK dynamic proxy is used and all implemented interfaces are proxied; if it implements none, a CGLIB proxy is created as a runtime-generated subclass.47

The consequences, enumerated

The reference documentation lists the constraints of forcing CGLIB proxying, and every one of them is a production incident somebody has already had.47

  • final classes cannot be proxied, because they cannot be extended.
  • final methods cannot be advised, because they cannot be overridden.
  • private methods cannot be advised, for the same reason.
  • Methods that are not visible — package-private methods declared in a parent class in a different package — cannot be advised, because they are effectively private.
  • The proxy instance is created through Objenesis, so the target constructor is not called twice; on a JVM that does not allow constructor bypassing you may see double invocations and matching debug log entries.
  • CGLIB proxying interacts badly with the Java Module System: you cannot proxy a class from java.lang on the module path, and the workaround flag --add-opens=java.base/java.lang=ALL-UNNAMED is not available for modules.

The JDK side has its own failure mode, and it is the more insidious one because it appears as a ClassCastException at injection time rather than as missing behaviour. A JDK proxy implements the target's interfaces and is not an instance of the target class. Any code that injects or casts to the concrete class fails the moment an advisor starts matching that bean. This is why Spring Boot changed its default: Boot's auto-configuration configures Spring AOP to use CGLIB proxies, and spring.aop.proxy-target-class must be set to false to get JDK proxies instead.48,49,50 It is also why a subtle class of Boot bugs exists around ordering: the property that switches proxy strategy has to be applied before the beans in question are created, and a Spring Boot issue documents exactly this cascade producing a 404 because a controller was proxied by interface and its request mappings became invisible.51

Unified settings, and a new escape hatch in 7.0

A detail worth internalising: proxy settings are not per-annotation. Multiple <aop:config/> sections collapse into a single unified auto-proxy creator at run time, applying the strongest proxy settings any of them specified, and the same is true of @EnableAspectJAutoProxy, @EnableTransactionManagement and related annotations. Setting proxyTargetClass=true anywhere forces CGLIB proxies everywhere. As of 7.0 this unification extends to individual proxy processors such as @EnableAsync.47

Spring Framework 7.0 adds the first real per-bean control over this: @Proxyable on a @Bean method or @Component class, with @Proxyable(INTERFACES) or @Proxyable(TARGET_CLASS) overriding the global default, and @Proxyable(interfaces=…) to restrict the proxy to a chosen subset of interfaces rather than all of them.47 For codebases that have spent a decade fighting a global proxy-target-class setting, this is the most practically useful AOP change in the release.

Kotlin: final by default meets subclass proxies

Kotlin makes every class and member final unless declared open, which collides directly with subclass proxying. The remedy is the kotlin-spring compiler plugin, a preconfigured all-open preset that opens types annotated or meta-annotated with @Component, @Async, @Transactional, @Cacheable and @SpringBootTest, which by meta-annotation covers @Service, @Repository, @Controller and @Configuration.52,53,54 There is a second Kotlin-specific trap in the same area: Kotlin treats all exceptions as unchecked, but a proxy does not, so a checked exception thrown through a proxied method is wrapped in UndeclaredThrowableException unless the function is annotated @Throws.53

The low-level API is still there, and still useful

Underneath @Aspect sits an API that predates it and remains the right tool for library code, for tests, and for anything that needs to construct a proxy without a container: ProxyFactory for programmatic proxies, ProxyFactoryBean for declarative ones, AspectJProxyFactory for applying an @Aspect class to a single object, and TargetSource for controlling where the target instance comes from on each invocation — hot-swappable targets and pooled targets are both TargetSource implementations.55,56,57,58,59

Frame 07 · pointcutsThe designator catalogue, and how matching really works

A pointcut declaration has two parts: a signature — a name and any parameters, expressed as a void method — and an expression, given by @Pointcut.33 Separating the two is not a style preference. It is the difference between a pointcut you can name, reuse, compose and unit-test, and a string literal duplicated across eleven annotations.

The this/target split deserves a second look because it is Spring-specific. AspectJ has type-based semantics and at an execution join point both refer to the same object. Spring is proxy-based and differentiates: this is bound to the proxy, target to the object behind it.33 The practical consequence: with a JDK dynamic proxy, this(ConcreteServiceImpl) never matches, because the proxy is not an instance of the implementation class. Under CGLIB it does. A pointcut written with this() can therefore change meaning when someone flips proxy-target-class.

Static and dynamic matching, and why it matters for cost

Spring's Pointcut API is deliberately split into a ClassFilter and a MethodMatcher, and the MethodMatcher reports whether it is a runtime matcher.60 If it is not, matching happens once, at proxy creation, and the result is cached. If it is — which is the case for args(), @args(), this() and @target() with runtime semantics — the three-argument matches method is evaluated on every invocation, with the actual arguments.

This is the one place where a pointcut can be a measurable performance problem. A pointcut of @annotation(com.acme.Audited) costs nothing per call; a pointcut of args(com.acme.LargeCommand) costs an instanceof per call on every method of every matched bean. The rule that follows is simple: put a static designator first and let it do the rejecting. @within(Service) && args(Command) is cheap because the class filter eliminates almost everything before any argument is examined. A bare args(Command) is not.

A second, larger cost lives at start-up rather than at call time. Every advisor's pointcut is evaluated against every candidate bean during context refresh. In an application with thousands of beans and a dozen advisors, sloppy expressions — execution(* *(..)) being the pathological case — turn into measurable start-up latency and, worse, into proxies around beans nobody intended to advise, including infrastructure beans whose proxying causes strange downstream failures. Scope aggressively, and always with a package or annotation predicate.

Composition

Expressions combine with &&, || and !, and named pointcuts compose like any other predicate. Composition is where the pointcut library pattern pays off: auditableCommand() is defined once as the conjunction of “in a service bean”, “is a command method” and “is not opted out”, and every aspect that needs that set refers to the name. When the definition of a command changes, one file changes, and one test asserts the new match set.

One caveat on negation, because it produces the most confusing failures in practice: !@annotation(X) combined with a runtime designator does not always mean what a reader expects when the annotation is on the interface method rather than the implementation. Java annotations are not inherited from interfaces — the reason Spring's own guidance is to annotate concrete classes rather than interfaces for @Transactional, since with class-based proxies or AspectJ weaving the settings on an interface are simply not seen.61,62 The same rule applies to your own annotations, and it is not optional advice.

Choosing between them

The Spring documentation's guidance is to use the least powerful form that does the job, and the reason is not purity: the weaker forms are harder to get catastrophically wrong.34 An @Around that forgets to rethrow swallows every exception in the application. An @Around that returns the wrong type produces a ClassCastException in the caller with a stack trace that points nowhere useful. An @Before can do neither.

There is one asymmetry worth knowing when you do reach for @Around: the advice method must declare Throwable, must return Object (or the specific return type when the pointcut is narrow enough), and must return the value from proceed(). If the advised method returns void, returning null is correct. If it returns a primitive, the boxing is handled for you, but returning null produces a NullPointerException at the unboxing site inside the proxy — a failure that reads as if the target method returned null, which it did not.

Binding context rather than reflecting on it

Advice can take typed parameters bound by the pointcut, which is the feature Spring 2.0 introduced to replace Object[] and which most code still fails to use.18 Binding the annotation instance with @annotation(myAnn), or the first argument with args(cmd, ..), removes an entire class of reflective code from the aspect body and moves the type errors to compile time. Use argNames when the compiler is not retaining parameter names.

Aspect instantiation and state

An @Aspect bean is a singleton by default, so any field in it is shared across every advised invocation in the application. Advice must therefore be re-entrant and thread-safe, and per-invocation state belongs in a local variable or a scope object. AspectJ's perthis and pertarget models are supported for the genuine cases where an aspect needs state per advised object, at the cost of one aspect instance per matched bean.36 In fifteen years of production Spring, the number of aspects that legitimately need this is small; the number that acquire mutable fields by accident is not.

Frame 09 · the chainproceed() is recursion, not a callback

Everything above is vocabulary. This is the mechanism. When a call arrives at a Spring proxy, the proxy does not run five pieces of advice in a loop. It hands the invocation to the first interceptor in the chain, and each interceptor holds the remainder of the chain as a continuation it may or may not invoke.

The contract is the AOP Alliance's: an advice implements MethodInterceptor and receives a MethodInvocation, on which it calls proceed() to continue down the chain toward the join point. Most interceptors call it and return its value. An interceptor may instead return a different value, or throw, rather than proceeding at all.14,16 Spring's implementation, ReflectiveMethodInvocation, keeps an index into the advisor list: each call to proceed() advances the index, and when the index reaches the end of the list it invokes the join point itself.

Three consequences follow, and all three matter operationally.

  • Stack depth is proportional to advisor count. Five advisors on a recursive service method is ten extra frames per level of recursion. This is a real cause of StackOverflowError in deeply recursive advised code, and it is why the stack traces in incident reports are so long.
  • Order is nesting, not sequence. An advisor is not “run third”; it contains everything after it. Whatever the third advisor does in a finally block happens after the fourth, fifth and the method itself have all completed.
  • A MethodInvocation is single-use. Calling proceed() twice from one @Around re-enters the rest of the chain from the current index — which is exactly what a retry advisor wants, and exactly what a careless one does by accident.

Writing at this level is unfashionable but appropriate for anything that ships as a library. It avoids the aspectjweaver dependency, it produces an advisor you can order precisely, and it is what the framework itself does. Spring's own advice API includes MethodBeforeAdvice, AfterReturningAdvice, ThrowsAdvice and introduction advice on top of the alliance interface, all reducible to the same chain.64,65 Spring also ships several ready-made interceptors that are worth knowing before writing your own: CustomizableTraceInterceptor for placeholder-driven method tracing, PerformanceMonitorInterceptor, DebugInterceptor and ExposeInvocationInterceptor, which publishes the current MethodInvocation as a thread-local.16

Frame 10 · precedenceOrdering is a correctness property

Most teams treat advisor order as cosmetic until an incident teaches them otherwise. It is not cosmetic. Each ordering decision is a claim about what must still be true when the thing inside it fails.

Spring follows AspectJ's precedence rules. The highest-precedence advice runs first on the way in, so of two @Before advices the higher-precedence one runs first; on the way out from the join point the highest-precedence advice runs last, so of two @After advices the higher-precedence one runs second. Where two advices in different aspects match the same join point and you have not specified otherwise, the order of execution is undefined. Precedence is declared by implementing Ordered or annotating the aspect @Order, and the aspect returning the lower value has the higher precedence.34

Two refinements are easy to miss. First, precedence is a property of the aspect, not of an advice method: you cannot give two advice methods in the same aspect different orders. Second, ordering within a single aspect is determined by advice kind, and Spring had to engineer it deliberately. The AspectJPrecedenceComparator originally mimicked the AspectJ compiler by relying on Class#getDeclaredMethods() returning methods in source order — a guarantee Java 7 removed. Since Spring cannot recover source order without reading bytecode, the framework implemented a reliable invocation order for ‘after’ advice methods declared within a single aspect instead.66 The practical rule: rely on advice kind for ordering within an aspect, never on the order you happened to type the methods.

The orderings that are actually load-bearing

The security-versus-transactions ordering is the one with framework-level guidance attached. Spring Security's migration guidance has advised setting @EnableTransactionManagement(order = 0) to give transaction management higher priority than method security, and the Security team has an open discussion about warning when the configured precedence disagrees — explicitly acknowledging that in practice an application may have other AOP modules to consider that Security cannot know about.67 The right conclusion is not to copy an order value from a migration guide. It is to decide, for your application, what should be true when the layer inside fails, and then to assert the resulting chain in a test.

Retry has the same character. Retry advice outside the transaction advisor gives each attempt a fresh transaction, which is nearly always what “retry the operation” means. Retry inside the transaction advisor causes every attempt to share a single transaction that may already be marked rollback-only, so the retries are guaranteed to fail. Now that @Retryable lives in the framework core rather than a separate project, this ordering question arrives in applications that never made a conscious decision about it.28

Write down, for each advisor in your chain, the sentence “this must still happen if everything inside it fails”. The advisors for which that sentence is true belong on the outside. That is the whole of ordering.

Frame 11 · failureThe catalogue of things that silently do not happen

Spring AOP's characteristic failure is not an exception. It is the absence of behaviour. The advice does not run, nothing is logged, and the system behaves as though the annotation were a comment. What follows is the complete catalogue, in rough order of how often it appears in production.

1. Self-invocation

Once a call reaches the target object, any method calls it makes on itself — this.bar(), or the implicit bar() — are invoked against this and not against the proxy, so self-invocation does not give the advice a chance to run.47

Spring's documentation ranks the remedies, and the ranking is worth respecting: refactor so the self-invocation does not happen; failing that, inject a self-reference and call through it; and as a last resort, and “highly discouraged”, use AopContext.currentProxy(), which requires exposeProxy and couples the class to Spring AOP.47,68

2. Non-public, final, and static methods

A CGLIB proxy advises by overriding. private methods, final methods, methods on final classes, and package-private methods declared in a parent class in another package cannot be overridden, so they are not advised.47 static methods are not dispatched through the instance at all. In every case the annotation is accepted and ignored.

3. Annotations on interfaces

Java annotations are not inherited from interfaces. Spring's own guidance is therefore to annotate concrete classes and their methods rather than interfaces: you may place @Transactional on an interface, but it only works as expected with interface-based proxies, and with class-based proxies or the weaving-based aspect the settings are not recognised and the object is not wrapped in a transactional proxy at all.61 The same reasoning applies to any custom annotation your aspects match on.

4. Objects the container did not create

An entity constructed by Hibernate, a value object built by a factory, a lambda, a record instantiated inline: none is a Spring bean, so none is proxied. Aspects over the domain model require weaving.41

5. Calls that bypass the proxy by construction

Registering the target object itself somewhere — passing this to an event listener registry, a callback, a ThreadLocal, or capturing it in a lambda inside a constructor — hands out an un-advised reference. Everything that arrives through that reference is un-advised for the lifetime of the application.

6. The proxy is not ready yet

Advice cannot run before the proxy exists. Code in a constructor or in @PostConstruct runs during bean creation, before the auto-proxy creator has wrapped the bean, so a @Transactional method called from initialisation code is not transactional.69

7. Two advisors, one of which is not where you think

An aspect registered in a child context does not advise beans in the parent. An advisor whose pointcut matches an infrastructure bean can proxy something the framework then fails to introspect. Beans created before the auto-proxy infrastructure is initialised — a classic hazard with BeanPostProcessor implementations and their dependencies — escape proxying entirely, usually with an INFO-level message about not being eligible for auto-proxying that nobody reads.

Making the silence loud

The uniform fix for this entire catalogue is not vigilance. It is a start-up check. Ten lines in a BeanPostProcessor convert the most common half of the list from a silent runtime no-op into a context refresh failure, which surfaces in CI.

Frame 12 · practiceLogging, done so it survives a code review

Logging is the first aspect everyone writes and the one most often written badly. The bad version logs entry and exit of every method in the application at INFO, prints arguments with toString(), and is deleted six months later when someone traces a log-volume bill to it.

The useful version starts from a different question: what does an aspect know that the method body does not? Exactly three things. It knows the signature without the method having to name itself. It knows that the invocation is a boundary, so it can bracket state. And it knows both exits, so it can guarantee cleanup. Anything that does not depend on one of those three facts belongs in the method.

Four decisions in that listing are the whole difference between a logging aspect that lasts and one that is reverted.

  • It logs commands, not methods. The pointcut is a named, curated set — not execution(* *(..)). Log volume is a design parameter, chosen once, in one file.
  • Redaction is declared at the parameter. A @Sensitive annotation on the parameter is checked reflectively by the aspect, so the knowledge of what is sensitive lives with the thing that is sensitive, not in a denylist inside the aspect.
  • Arguments are bounded. An un-truncated toString() on a collection is an unbounded log line and, for a lazily-initialised JPA collection, a database query issued by your logging.
  • It rethrows. Advice that catches and does not rethrow changes program semantics. Logging must never do this.

On the failure path, log the exception type and let the boundary handler log the stack trace once. An aspect that logs full stack traces at every layer produces the same trace three times, and the on-call engineer reading it cannot tell whether that means three failures.

Frame 13 · practiceSecurity: use the advisors that already exist

Method security is the strongest argument for AOP in the Spring ecosystem, and also the strongest argument for not writing your own aspect. Spring Security's method security is implemented exactly as this article has described — as advisors on the same chain — and the documentation traces the sequence explicitly: among the proxy's other advisors it invokes an AuthorizationManagerBeforeMethodInterceptor matching the @PreAuthorize pointcut, which calls PreAuthorizeAuthorizationManager, which uses a MethodSecurityExpressionHandler to parse the SpEL expression and build an evaluation context from a MethodSecurityExpressionRoot carrying a Supplier<Authentication> and the MethodInvocation. If evaluation passes, Spring AOP proceeds to invoke the method; if not, the interceptor publishes an AuthorizationDeniedEvent and throws an AccessDeniedException.70

Every piece of that is a component you can replace. The published extension point is to disable the built-in behaviour with @EnableMethodSecurity(prePostEnabled = false) and publish your own Advisor beans built from AuthorizationManagerBeforeMethodInterceptor and AuthorizationManagerAfterMethodInterceptor with your own AuthorizationManager, marked @Role(ROLE_INFRASTRUCTURE).70 That is a far better place to put a custom authorisation rule than a hand-written @Aspect, because it inherits the ordering constants, the event publication and the exception translation.

Why a hand-rolled security aspect is a bad idea

The catalogue in the previous section is the answer. A security aspect that silently fails to apply is not a bug; it is an authorisation bypass. Every entry in that catalogue — self-invocation, a final method, an annotation on an interface, an object the container did not create, a call through a captured this — becomes a way to reach a protected method without the check. This is why method security should be treated as defence in depth behind a request-level filter chain, never as the only enforcement point, and why the proxying configuration matters so much that Spring Boot has an issue documenting a case where method security caused JDK proxies to be used and broke request mapping as a side effect.51

Frame 14 · practiceTransactions: the aspect that justified the framework

Declarative transaction management is the reason most Spring applications have an AOP proxy at all, and it is the canonical demonstration of the model: an EJB-style container service, delivered to a plain object, by wrapping.69,71 The mechanism is a TransactionInterceptor in the advisor chain, driven by a TransactionAttributeSource that reads @Transactional from the target class.72

Reading the interceptor's shape answers most transaction questions directly. Rollback rules are attributes of the advice, not of the exception: by default a transaction is rolled back on RuntimeException and Error and committed on a checked exception, which is a policy choice inherited from EJB and adjustable per method.62 Propagation is a decision made by the interceptor before proceed(), which is why REQUIRES_NEW between two methods of the same bean does nothing at all — there is no proxy hop at which the decision could be made.73

Two integrations are worth knowing because they solve problems people otherwise solve badly with custom aspects. Transaction-bound events let a listener run after commit rather than at publish time, which is the correct mechanism for “send the email only if the order really was placed”.74 And @Transactional can be applied by AspectJ rather than by a proxy, using mode = AdviceMode.ASPECTJ and the spring-aspects library, which removes the self-invocation restriction entirely — at the cost of adopting a weaver.75 The documentation on advising transactional operations also covers the composition case directly: adding your own advice around a transactional method, ordered relative to the transaction advisor.76

Frame 15 · practiceAuditing: two mechanisms answering two questions

Auditing is where teams most often build an aspect that duplicates something the framework already does better, because “audit” names two different requirements that need two different mechanisms.

“What changed, and who changed it?” is a data question, and the right mechanism is entity-level. Spring Data provides @CreatedBy, @LastModifiedBy, @CreatedDate and @LastModifiedDate, populated by AuditingEntityListener registered as a JPA entity listener and enabled with @EnableJpaAuditing, with the current principal supplied by an AuditorAware bean.77 This runs on persistence lifecycle callbacks, which means it is immune to every failure in the self-invocation catalogue: it does not matter which method reached the repository.

“What was attempted, by whom, and did it succeed?” is an invocation question, and that is genuinely an aspect. The join point is the command; the audit record is a fact about the invocation, not about a row.

The design decision that matters here is placement relative to the transaction advisor, and it is a real trade-off rather than a best practice. An audit advisor inside the transaction writes its record in the same unit of work, so the audit row and the change commit or roll back together — correct for “what changed”, and wrong for “what was attempted”, because a rolled-back attempt leaves no trace. An audit advisor outside the transaction survives the rollback and records the attempt, at the cost of no longer being atomic with the change it describes. Regulated environments usually want both, which means two advisors at two orders, not one advisor with a flag.

Frame 16 · practicePerformance monitoring, and the cost of the mechanism itself

Timing is the textbook @Around, and it is also the aspect most thoroughly commoditised. Micrometer ships the aspects; the framework ships the observation abstraction; Spring Boot registers them for you.78

The registration story has been evolving and is worth getting right for your version. Micrometer's @Timed support has been an AspectJ-style aspect since 2018, registered as a TimedAspect bean, with the original pull request already warning that it overlaps with the @Timed handling in WebMvcMetricsFilter and should not be mixed with it.79 Boot's auto-configuration of ObservedAspect was proposed separately, and the maintainers initially declined to rush it precisely because of that overlap history.80 Boot 3.2 auto-configured AspectJ handlers for observation annotations, and a follow-up issue records the conclusion that the mere presence of AspectJ and Micrometer on the classpath is not a strong enough signal to enable them.81 The current documented behaviour is explicit and opt-in: set management.observations.annotations.enabled to true and put org.aspectj:aspectjweaver on the classpath to enable scanning of @Observed, @Timed, @Counted, @MeterTag and @NewSpan.82

The one job a hand-written timing advisor still does better than an annotation is outcome classification. A business rule violation is not a success and is not an error, and only your code knows which exceptions belong in which bucket. That decision determines the cardinality of the resulting metric, which is the single most expensive mistake available in this area: a tag derived from a method signature is bounded; a tag derived from an exception message, a tenant id or a URL path is not.83

What the mechanism itself costs

Two costs, at two different times, and they are commonly confused.

At start-up, every advisor pointcut is matched against every candidate bean, and every matched bean gets a generated proxy class. This is the cost that shows up as slower context refresh and larger metaspace. It scales with beans × advisors, and it is the reason a sloppy execution(* *(..)) is expensive even if the advice body is empty.

Per invocation, the cost is one virtual dispatch into the proxy plus one ReflectiveMethodInvocation allocation plus one frame per advisor plus, for runtime pointcuts, a match evaluation per call. For a service method that touches a database this is unmeasurable. For a method called ten million times a second in a tight loop it is not, and the correct response is to move the pointcut so it does not match that method, rather than to reject AOP as a technique.

Frame 17 · testingProve the wiring, not only the logic

Aspect bugs divide cleanly into two populations, and they need two different kinds of test. Either the advice body is wrong — a logic bug, testable without a container — or the advice never ran — a wiring bug, invisible to every unit test you can write.

The second population is the dangerous one, because a wiring bug produces a green suite. A test that calls the service method directly on a hand-constructed instance passes whether or not the annotation would have been honoured in production. This is the single most common reason a transactional boundary reaches production without a transaction.

Four levels, in increasing cost, and a healthy suite has all four:

  • Pointcut unit test. Instantiate AspectJExpressionPointcut, set the expression, and assert matches(Method, Class) for a method that should match and one that should not. No container, milliseconds, and it fails the moment somebody renames a package.60
  • Advice unit test. Build a proxy with AspectJProxyFactory or ProxyFactory over a hand-written stub target and exercise the advice directly.57
  • Wiring test. In a real context, assert AopUtils.isAopProxy, assert the ultimate target class, and assert the advisor chain read from Advised contains the interceptors you claim in the order you claim.42
  • Behavioural test. Exercise the boundary from outside — through MockMvc, or through a repository assertion after a forced rollback — so the assertion is about observable behaviour rather than about a mock.84

Two testing hazards specific to AOP. First, mocking: @MockitoBean replaces the bean including its proxy, so a test that mocks a service is a test with no advisors on that service. That is usually what you want, and it is occasionally the reason a test passes that should not. Second, @Transactional on the test method itself rolls back at the end, which means an assertion about commit behaviour inside a test transaction is asserting nothing; the framework's test transaction support is a separate mechanism from the application's.84

Frame 18 · ahead of timeProxies in a world without a runtime

GraalVM native image removes the assumption every proxy mechanism has relied on since 2004: that you can generate a class while the program is running. The classpath is fixed at build time, there is no lazy class loading, and reflection, resources, serialisation and dynamic proxies must all be declared in advance.85 Spring's answer is ahead-of-time processing: the framework generates the assets GraalVM needs during the build, including the proxy classes.23

The failure mode is specific and easy to recognise. When a proxy is needed that AOT did not anticipate, the native binary fails at start-up with an UnsupportedOperationException stating that CGLIB runtime enhancement is not supported on native image, and advising that Spring AOT processing be enabled to pre-generate the named $$SpringCGLIB$$0 class at build time — a real report from Spring Modulith running on Spring Boot 4.0 illustrates it exactly.86 The historical trajectory is worth noting: early Spring Native did not support class proxies at all and forced spring.aop.proxy-target-class to false, which is why interface-based proxying still tends to be the smoother path under AOT.87

The operational rules that follow are short.

  • Prefer interface-based proxies for anything constructed dynamically, and register the JDK proxy interface list explicitly — including SpringProxy, Advised and DecoratingProxy, which Spring adds to every proxy it creates.
  • Avoid pointcuts whose match set depends on runtime state; AOT must be able to determine it statically.
  • Run the native build in CI. Under AOT, a proxying mistake that used to be a runtime surprise becomes a build failure — which is an improvement only if the build actually runs.

The broader point is that AOT is pushing Spring's whole ecosystem toward decisions made at build time. Spring Framework 7.0's release notes describe a generation focused on this direction alongside the Jakarta EE 11 and JSpecify moves,88 and Spring Boot 4's AOT-generated repository implementations are the same idea applied to Spring Data. For AOP specifically, the consequence is modest but real: the more of your advisor topology is decidable at build time, the less of it can surprise you at three in the morning.

Frame 19 · modelRun the semantics yourself

Arguments about advice ordering are usually settled by someone running an experiment. Here is a smaller experiment than a Spring context: a dependency-free Python model of the three rules that decide what happens at an advised call. It is not Spring, and it is not a simulation of Spring's internals — it is the semantics, written out at a size a person can hold in their head.

The interesting part is what the trace shows without any framework in the way: the nesting produced by order values, the difference between the two exits, the fact that a non-public method is still reached but is not advised by the advisor that requires public visibility, and the self-invocation gap sitting in the middle of an otherwise correct call.

Three predictions the model makes, all of which hold in a real Spring application. First, the advisor with the lowest order value logs first and last. Second, the @AfterReturning-style advisor produces no output on the exceptional path while the @After-style advisor produces output on both. Third, the inner call in case 1 reaches the target directly, so nothing in the chain observes it — and routing the same call through the proxy in case 4 produces the full chain. If your mental model disagrees with any of those, the model is a cheaper place to fix it than production.

Frame 20 · judgementWhen not to reach for it

AOP's reputation problem is deserved, and it comes from a specific failure: aspects that change behaviour readers cannot predict from the code in front of them. The mitigation is not to avoid AOP. It is to confine it to concerns where the surprise is the point.

Four heuristics that hold up in practice.

If it changes what the method computes, it does not belong in an aspect. Cross-cutting infrastructure — a transaction, a timer, an authorisation check, an audit record — is invisible to the method's contract. Rewriting arguments to alter business results, or substituting return values, is legal and is almost always the wrong tool: the reader of the method has no way to discover it.

If the concern applies to three methods, write a method. Quantification only pays when the match set is large enough that enumerating it is a maintenance burden, and open enough that new members should be included automatically. Three call sites is not that. Spring's own guidance on choosing a declaration style makes the same point in a different register — use the simplest thing that expresses the intent.6

If a silent non-application is a security or correctness failure, add a second layer. Everything in the failure catalogue applies to your aspect too. Method security is defence in depth behind a filter chain; an audit aspect is a complement to database-level auditing, not a replacement for it.

Prefer the annotation the framework ships. @Transactional, @Cacheable, @PreAuthorize, @Observed, @Async, @Retryable and @ConcurrencyLimit are all advisors somebody else has already ordered, documented, tested against the proxy edge cases, and taught the AOT engine about.89,90,91,28 The cache abstraction in particular is worth reading before writing a caching aspect, because it has already solved the problems — key generation, conditional caching, sync semantics — that a hand-written one discovers slowly.90

And a fifth, which is really a documentation practice: for every aspect that ships, write the five fields used in the cards throughout this article — pointcut, advice type, order, invariant, failure mode. If any field cannot be filled in confidently, the aspect is not ready. The reference documentation's own worked example and its further-resources page are a reasonable model for what “documented aspect” should look like.92,93
