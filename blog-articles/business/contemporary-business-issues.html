Who Answers for the Part You Don't Own?

Sixteen contemporary business issues, read as one question about where the firm ends

A firm has two boundaries, and they are no longer the same shape. One encloses what it can decide by itself. The other encloses what it will be asked to answer for. Every item on the standard list of contemporary business issues is a place where those two edges have come apart, and the gap between them is measurable.

I

Where does the firm end?

Ronald Coase asked, in 1937, why firms exist at all. If the market allocates resources efficiently, why does anyone bother to hire people and give them orders rather than buying each service as needed? His answer was that using the price mechanism has a cost — of discovering prices, of negotiating, of contracting — and that a firm is what you get when it is cheaper to direct something than to buy it. The boundary of the firm sits where those two costs are equal. [1]

Everything built on that insight over the following ninety years — transaction-cost economics, incomplete contracts, property-rights theory, the theory of the firm as a nexus of contracts — treats the boundary as a single line. Inside it, direction. Outside it, exchange. The line moves as technology changes the relative cost of the two, which is why the same body of theory explains vertical integration in the 1950s and outsourcing in the 1990s. [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]The same period produced a matching account of how the boundary is drawn in practice: modular interfaces let a firm buy a component without understanding it, and governance in a global value chain follows the codifiability of the specification rather than the size of the buyer. [8, 9]

There is a second boundary, and it does not have to coincide with the first. It encloses everything the firm will be required to answer for: by a regulator, a court, a customer, an auditor, an index provider, or a select committee. Call the first the control boundary and the second the accountability boundary. For most of the period in which the theory of the firm was written, they were close enough to the same shape that nobody needed to distinguish them. A firm answered for what it did, and it did what it directed.

They are not the same shape now. Roughly half of reported data breaches involve a third party. [10]A firm's greenhouse gas inventory is dominated by emissions from entities it does not own. [11]Its cost of goods is set by tariff instruments it has no standing to contest. Its most consequential supplier is four tiers away and has never heard of it. And in each of those cases the accountability boundary has been pushed outward to enclose the thing, while the control boundary has stayed where the contracts are.

This article takes the standard list of contemporary business issues — sixteen of them, from artificial intelligence to stakeholder capitalism — and reads every one as a place where those two edges have separated. The claim is not that this is an interesting metaphor. It is that the separation is measurable: each item can be located on two ordered scales, one for what a firm can set and one for what it must answer for, and the signed distance between them is a number with content. The number predicts which failures are structural rather than operational, which instruments are doing real work, and which proposed remedies cannot work because they attach accountability to nothing.

The two ladders

The control ladder orders states of the world by what a firm can unilaterally do to them inside one planning cycle. The accountability ladder orders them by what happens to the firm when they go wrong. Both run from five down to zero, and both are defined so that the rung can be checked against a document rather than asserted.

Control — what the firm can set
C5Owned and directedthe firm sets the state of the thing by fiat, this quarter, without anyone's agreement
C4Bilaterally contractedthe firm sets terms with a counterparty and can change them at renewal or on notice
C3Bought on a marketthe firm chooses among offers it did not design and cannot amend
C2Influencedthe firm can move the probability of an outcome but not the outcome
C1Observed onlythe firm can measure the state and cannot change it
C0Not even observedthe firm cannot see the state at all, let alone act on it
Accountability — what happens when it goes wrong
A5Named natural personan instrument puts a designated individual personally on the hook
A4Statutory liability on the firmfines, orders, market exclusion, import refusal
A3Civil or contractual liabilitysuit, indemnity, damages, failure to supply
A2Mandatory disclosurethe firm must report the thing; the report itself carries no penalty
A1Market exposure onlycustomers, employees and capital may react; no one may compel
A0Nothing attributedno mechanism exists by which anyone can require an answer

Two rules keep the assignment honest. First, each plate names a single object — the specific thing whose state is at issue — and both rungs are scored against that object, not against the topic in general. A firm has complete control over its remote-working policy and very little over the simultaneity the policy is written to buy; those are different objects and would score differently, so the plate says which one it means. Second, a rung is only awarded where an instrument can be named. A5 requires a document that puts a natural person on the hook; A0 means no such document exists anywhere, which is a strong claim and is made exactly once.

What the gap is, and what it is not

Define the gap as accountability minus control. A positive gap means the firm answers for more than it holds. A negative gap means it holds more than it answers for. Zero means the two are matched, which is what a well-formed liability rule is supposed to produce and what most of twentieth-century corporate law assumed. The apparatus that has grown up to police the second boundary is itself a well-studied object: an expansion of verification into domains that were previously governed by trust, which reshapes the thing being measured in the process of measuring it. [12, 13, 14]

A positive gap is not automatically a scandal. Placing liability on the party who cannot directly prevent the harm is sometimes the efficient design: it is the standard argument for strict liability, and for channelling responsibility to whoever is best placed to bear or spread the cost even when they are not best placed to avoid it. [15, 16]A firm that answers for its supply chain has a reason to build a supply chain that can be answered for. The gap becomes pathological only when the party carrying the accountability has no instrument that reaches the state — when the answer demanded is not merely expensive but unavailable.

A negative gap is the more familiar failure and the less discussed one. It is what an externality looks like from inside the firm: a decision wholly within the control boundary whose consequences fall wholly outside the accountability one. [17]Three items on the list of sixteen have negative gaps, and it is worth saying now that they are the three closest to the firm's own people.

The sections that follow work through the sixteen in five groups. Each carries a plate with a fixed set of fields and a gauge showing the two rungs and the signed distance between them. A laboratory section then reports ten deterministic experiments that test the mechanisms the plates assert, and a ledger collects the results. Every quantity quoted in this article that is not attributed to a source comes from that laboratory and can be reproduced from the transcript.

II

Group I · Who does the work

The three items in this group concern people the firm has hired, and they share a property nothing else on the list has: the firm holds more than it answers for. That is not an accident of scoring. Employment is the oldest and most complete form of the control boundary, and the accountability apparatus built around it — employment law, health and safety, collective bargaining — was designed for a relationship where the two edges were assumed to coincide.

B01 · Artificial intelligence and automation

The interesting thing about a licensed model is not that it is clever. It is that it is the first substantial input to a firm's decisions that the firm neither builds nor fully specifies. A firm that wrote its own credit-scoring rules owned the rules. A firm that fine-tunes a foundation model owns a thin layer over an artefact whose training corpus, failure modes and release schedule belong to someone else. That puts the object at C3: chosen from a market of offers the buyer cannot amend.

The accountability rung went the other way. The EU AI Act places substantive obligations on deployers, not only providers, and Article 26 requires that human oversight be assigned to natural persons with the necessary competence, training and authority — the only place in a major instrument where authority is named as a required personal attribute. [18]Deployers of high-risk systems must monitor operation, retain logs for at least six months, and inform workers' representatives before workplace deployment. The Digital Omnibus of July 2026 deferred the high-risk timetable to December 2027 and softened the AI-literacy duty from ensuring competence to supporting its development, but it left the deployer obligations and the penalty ceilings intact. [19]

The failure this arrangement produces is specific. It is not that a model is wrong; models have always been wrong. It is that the firm learns about a behaviour change from an outcome rather than from a change notice, because the change happened on a schedule set outside the control boundary. Every governance control the firm can build — evaluation harnesses, canary deployments, human review — is an attempt to manufacture observability of a state it does not own. That is what a positive gap looks like in an operating model. [20, 21]The productivity case for adopting anyway is strong and well identified — a large field deployment in customer support raised issues resolved per hour by 14% on average and by 34% for the least experienced staff — which is exactly why the gap gets accepted rather than argued about. [22]

What moves
Judgement that used to sit inside a job description moves into a model the firm licenses rather than builds.

B02 · Future of work

This plate is the mirror image, and the most uncomfortable entry on the board. Job design is the one thing on this list that stays entirely inside the firm. Nobody outside it decides which tasks are bundled into a graduate role, whether the second rung of a career ladder still exists, or whether the apprenticeship that produced the senior engineer is still being run. Control is at C5 and accountability is at A1, because there is no instrument at all: a firm that stops producing the next generation of its own senior staff files no return about it and answers to no one. Job design has had a rigorous theory since the multitask principal-agent literature, which showed that what tasks are bundled together determines what can be measured and therefore what can be incentivised. [23]

The evidence that this is not hypothetical has arrived in payroll microdata rather than in forecasts. Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen document a relative employment decline for 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations, with no comparable movement for older workers in the same occupations; a February 2026 update reports that under the broadest set of controls the declines become statistically significant from 2024, with earlier movement likely driven by other factors. [24]The finding is contested — Danish administrative data linked to survey-based adoption find precisely estimated zeros for earnings and hours even where workplaces reorganise tasks and create new roles — and the honest summary is that the aggregate effect is unresolved while the entry-level composition effect keeps showing up. [25, 26, 27]The forecasting literature that preceded the data ran from occupation-level susceptibility estimates to task-level exposure measures, and the two generations disagree by roughly a factor of five on how many jobs are at risk — a disagreement that is almost entirely about the unit of analysis. [28, 29, 30]

L10 supplies the mechanism, and it is not the one usually offered. Junior roles are not made of more automatable tasks. In the experiment, occupations of five or fewer tasks have essentially the same mean automatable share as occupations of ten or more — 0.3906 against 0.3863 — and cross a displacement threshold 41 times as often, purely because a short bundle has less internal diversification. A firm that has spent twenty years narrowing entry-level jobs in the name of efficiency has, without deciding to, built the portfolio with the fattest tail. That decision sat at C5 the entire time. [31, 32]

Future of work

Object: The design of jobs
What moves
Nothing moves outward. Job design is the one item on this list that stays wholly inside the firm.
What the firm holds
Which tasks are bundled into which role, what a junior job contains, how many rungs the ladder has, and whether entry-level work survives at all.
What it answers for

Instrument

Evidence
Payroll microdata show a relative employment decline for 22–25 year olds in the most AI-exposed occupations, with no corresponding effect on older workers in the same occupations. [24]

B03 · Remote and hybrid work

Remote work moved the workplace to premises the firm does not own and cannot inspect, and left the employment relationship intact. That is an unusual combination: the control boundary contracted while the accountability boundary did not move at all. Health and safety duties do not stop at the office door, employment claims survive the move, and sustained work from another jurisdiction can create payroll and permanent-establishment exposure in a country the firm never chose to enter.

The quantity has settled. Work from home stabilised at about a quarter of paid days in the United States, roughly 3.5 times the 2019 level, and a model calibrated to the post-pandemic equilibrium implies that forcing a return to pre-pandemic norms would lower aggregate labour productivity by between 0.2% and 1.1% and worker welfare by 2.3%. [33, 34]The randomised evidence on hybrid specifically — two days at home, at scale, with attrition and performance tracked — found no effect on performance or promotion and a large reduction in quits. [35]Where the causal evidence runs the other way it tends to concern fully remote work in roles with dense on-the-job learning, which is a different treatment from two anchored days. [36]

Which makes the design question the live one, and L3 answers it exactly. Under self-selected three-day attendance, a pair of colleagues share 1.80 days a week; under two anchored days chosen for them, 2.00. Fifty per cent more attendance, ten per cent less contact. For teams the gap is not close: the probability that a team of eight is ever all present on the same day is 0.0833 under three self-selected days and 1.0000 under two anchored ones. A mandate specifying days-in-office is measuring the wrong variable, and the firm has complete control over the right one. [37, 38]

III

Group II · Who the counterparty is

The two items here do the same structural thing from opposite directions. Both move the counterparty outside the firm. One keeps the direction outside too and is held responsible anyway; the other keeps the direction inside and denies it.

B04 · Platform economy

A platform is an institution for making trades happen between two sides it does not employ, with prices and access rules set to balance the two. [39, 40]Its control over any individual transaction is genuinely weak: it can rank, admit, expel and price, which shifts the probability of good conduct without determining any instance of it. That is C2, and the plate scores it there on the merits rather than on the platform's own account of itself.

The accountability rung is A4 and rising. The Digital Services Act builds an obligation architecture on notice-and-action, trusted flaggers and systemic-risk assessment for the largest services; the Digital Markets Act imposes obligations on designated gatekeepers that attach to size rather than to any particular act. [41, 42]Whatever one thinks of the policy, the structure is unambiguous: a duty to control a population of third parties, imposed on a party whose only instruments are the instruments of a marketplace.

The predictable consequence is that platforms build enforcement capacity out of matching technology, which is what content moderation and seller verification are. It is also why the compliance cost is strongly superlinear in scale and why the obligations concentrate on the few firms large enough to bear them — a regulatory design that, whatever its intent, raises the barrier around the incumbents it targets. [43]

B04

Platform economy

Object: A transaction between two parties the firm did not hire
What moves
The transaction itself moves outside the firm. The platform supplies the venue, the ranking and the payment rail, and owns neither side of the trade.
What the firm holds
The rules of the venue: ranking, fees, admission, expulsion. It can shift the probability of good conduct; it cannot perform the trade.
What it answers for
Illegal listings, unsafe products, ranking transparency, self-preferencing, and — for the largest — structural obligations that apply because of size rather than conduct.
The gap opens as
The firm is asked to police a population it has no employment relationship with, using tools it built for matching rather than enforcement.
Instrument
The DSA's notice-and-action and systemic-risk regime and the DMA's per-gatekeeper obligations.
Evidence
A structural liability that attaches to the venue rather than to the conduct is the clearest available case of answering for what one does not do. [41, 42]
C2 · A4 · gap +2 (positive)

B05 · Gig economy

The gig platform is the one plate on the board where control and accountability sit at the same rung, and it got there by force. In substance a food-delivery or ride-hailing platform sets price, allocates tasks, defines acceptance windows, rates performance and terminates access, unilaterally and amendably — a degree of direction that would be unremarkable in an employment relationship. In form it contracts with independent businesses. The literature on algorithmic management has documented the mechanism in detail: direction, evaluation and discipline delivered through software rather than a supervisor. [44, 45, 46]

Courts got there first. The UK Supreme Court in Uber BV v Aslam held that drivers were workers, reasoning from the degree of control actually exercised rather than from the contractual characterisation, and observing that the written terms were themselves written by the party whose obligations were in question. [47]California's ABC test moved in the same direction before being partly reversed at the ballot box. [48]

Directive (EU) 2024/2831 generalises the reasoning into an instrument. Where facts show direction and control, employment is presumed and the burden of proof reverses onto the platform; separately — and this is the part that matters most for the argument here — the algorithmic-management provisions bind regardless of status, so a platform that successfully rebuts the presumption still owes transparency, human review of consequential decisions, and limits on what data it may process. [49, 50]Transposition is due by 2 December 2026 and was incomplete across most member states through 2026, so the near-term reality is twenty-seven different versions of the same trigger. [51]

L4 prices what the arrangement moves. To take the fill rate from 90% to 99% the platform needs 35% more workers on shift, and paid utilisation falls from 0.9112 to 0.7355 — an earnings index of 80.7 and unpaid waiting rising from 0.71 to 2.12 hours of an eight-hour shift. The service level is bought with idle time, and the idle time sits on the one side of the transaction that is not on the payroll. [52, 53]

Direction moves from a supervisor to an allocation algorithm, while the employment relationship formally moves out of the firm altogether.
What the firm holds
Price, task allocation, acceptance windows, ratings and deactivation — set unilaterally and amendable at will. In substance this is a great deal of control.

The firm exercises the control and disclaims the status, and a tribunal decides which of the two descriptions is the real one.

Directive (EU) 2024/2831, which makes exercised control the trigger for a rebuttable presumption of employment and reverses the burden of proof.
Evidence

IV

Group III · What flows through

Data, security and the operating stack are usually filed under technology. Structurally they are the same problem three times: a state the firm is fully accountable for, held continuously by parties whose behaviour it can contract about and cannot observe.

B06 · Data privacy

Data protection law is built on a distinction between controllers and processors, and it puts the answer squarely on the controller. A controller must impose specified terms on its processors and remains liable for the damage they cause; administrative fines are calculated against the undertaking's total worldwide annual turnover. [54]That is A4 by any reading, and the contractual apparatus of Article 28 is real control over terms.

It is not control over state. Once a record has been transferred to a processor and from there to a sub-processor the firm has never named, the firm holds an audit right it exercises annually and a warranty it will litigate afterwards. It cannot read the state of the record today. The plate scores that at C1, and readers who prefer C4 should say which observable the contract makes observable.

The reason this matters more than it used to is that the object being protected has stopped being a field and become a combination. L5 makes the point on deliberately coarse attributes: birth year alone leaves nobody unique in 200,000 people; add birth month and sex and still nobody; add a coarse area of 480 buckets and 46.77% become unique; all three together, 63.12% unique and 95.28% in a group smaller than five. This reproduces a result first established on US census data and repeatedly since: identification lives in the joint distribution, so a deletion or minimisation programme organised around named identifiers is solving a different problem from the one that produces re-identification. [55, 56, 57, 58]

Meanwhile the accountability boundary has fragmented. Around twenty US states now have comprehensive consumer privacy statutes in force, with materially different thresholds, definitions and opt-out mechanics, and no federal statute preempting them; California added an annual independent cybersecurity audit requirement for qualifying businesses from January 2026. [59, 60]A firm answers in each, separately, for the same record. That is the empirical setting for L8.

B06

Data privacy

Object: Personal data after it has left the firm's own systems
What moves
The data moves to processors, and from them to sub-processors the firm has never contracted with, audited or named.
What the firm holds
A contract, a flow-down clause and an audit right. What it does not hold is the state: it cannot see what a fourth party is doing with the record on any given day.
What it answers for
Everything, as controller. The controller answers for the processor's breach, and the fine is calculated on the group's worldwide turnover.
The gap opens as
A deletion request arrives that the firm can honour in its own database and cannot honour anywhere the record has since been copied to.
Instrument
GDPR Arts. 28, 82 and 83, plus roughly twenty US state statutes with divergent definitions of the same data.
Evidence
Three ordinary attributes — birth month, area and sex — make most people unique in a population of hundreds of thousands, and not one of them is the kind of identifier a deletion request names. [55, 56]

B07 · Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity carries the largest positive gap on the board, shared with geopolitical risk, and the reason is arithmetical rather than cultural. The 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report — the nineteenth edition, covering more than 31,000 incidents and over 22,000 confirmed breaches across 145 countries — found that breaches involving a third party reached 48% of the total, a 60% increase in a single year, and that exploitation of software vulnerabilities overtook stolen credentials as the leading initial access vector for the first time in the report's history at 31%. [10]Nearly half of the events a firm must disclose, remediate and answer for begin somewhere it has no administrative access.

The accountability rung is the highest on the board. NIS2 requires management bodies to approve and oversee cybersecurity risk-management measures and makes them liable for infringements, and empowers competent authorities to temporarily prohibit individuals from exercising managerial functions in essential entities. [61]On the disclosure side, the SEC's Item 1.05 requires a material cybersecurity incident to be reported on Form 8-K within four business days of the materiality determination — a clock that runs on the registrant while the facts sit with a supplier. [62]The rule is contested: a joint petition by banking and securities associations in May 2025 asked for Item 1.05 to be rescinded, arguing that public incident disclosure conflicts with confidential reporting to critical-infrastructure authorities and has been weaponised by ransomware operators. [63]

L6 shows why the standard control does not work. The firm's third-party risk register records, for each vendor, a marginal probability of failure. Hold those marginals fixed and route the same sixty vendors through twelve shared providers, and the mean number of simultaneous failures is unchanged to four decimal places while the probability of ten or more at once rises by a factor of roughly 138,000 and the 99th percentile moves from four vendors to fifteen. Assurance questionnaires measure marginals. The loss lives in the copula. [64, 65, 66]

B08 · Digital transformation

Strip the word of its consultancy connotations and digital transformation names something precise: the migration of capability the firm used to run onto platforms it rents. The economics are good and well documented, and the trade is explicit — capital expenditure and operational control exchanged for elasticity and someone else's engineering. [67]The firm chooses provider, region and tier. It does not choose the roadmap, the deprecation schedule, the pricing model or, critically, the degree of concentration it is building.

Financial regulators reached the concentration question first because they could see the exposure aggregate across supervised entities. The Digital Operational Resilience Act, which has applied since January 2025, imposes a full ICT third-party risk regime, mandatory contractual provisions, register-of-information reporting, exit strategies and substitutability analysis, and creates direct Union-level oversight of designated critical ICT third-party providers. [68]That last element is the structurally novel one: rather than only requiring firms to answer for a provider they cannot control, the regime reaches past them and regulates the provider.

That is worth naming as the general remedy for a positive gap. When accountability sits with a party that has no instrument, there are exactly two repairs: build the instrument, or move the accountability to someone who has one. DORA does the second. Most third-party risk programmes attempt the first with paperwork, which L6 shows is aimed at the wrong statistic.

B08

Digital transformation

Object: The firm's own operating stack
What moves
Capability the firm used to run moves to platforms it rents, on terms it accepts rather than negotiates.
What the firm holds
Which provider, which region, which tier. Not the roadmap, the deprecation schedule, the pricing, or the concentration.
What it answers for
Continuity of service to its own customers, and — in regulated sectors — an explicit duty to manage the concentration it has created.
The gap opens as
A single provider outage takes down services the firm believed were independent, because independence was assumed rather than mapped.
Instrument
DORA's ICT third-party risk regime and its oversight of critical providers; contractual exit and substitutability requirements.
Evidence
With the marginal failure probability held identical, moving 60 vendor relationships onto 12 underlying providers leaves the mean exposure unchanged and multiplies the probability of ten simultaneous failures by five orders of magnitude. [68]

V

Group IV · What the world does to the firm

The five items in this group have a shared and awkward property: the firm did not cause them, cannot move them, and is asked to price, disclose and survive them anyway. Three sit at C1 or C0 — observable at best. Two of the largest positive gaps on the board are here.

B09 · Climate change

For an individual firm the physical climate is a boundary condition. Its own emissions are a rounding error in the forcing; the hazard arrives at assets it already owns, on a schedule set by a global stock. Control over the object is C1: measurable, sitable, insurable, immovable. The firm can adapt, and adaptation is not control.

The accountability rung is disclosure, and disclosure is where the action has been. IFRS S2 requires climate-related risk and opportunity disclosure with scenario analysis and cross-industry metrics, and requires emissions to be measured in accordance with the GHG Protocol Corporate Standard unless a jurisdictional authority specifies otherwise; the ISSB issued targeted amendments in December 2025, effective for periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, providing relief on financed emissions and permitting classification systems other than GICS. [69, 70, 71]

In the United States the picture is one of instruments in retreat and instruments in litigation. The SEC has proposed rescinding its climate disclosure rules, with a final decision unlikely before late 2026 or early 2027. [72]California's SB 253 survived: the Air Resources Board adopted its initial regulation on 26 February 2026 and set a first Scope 1 and 2 reporting deadline in the second half of 2026, with a further rulemaking to cover 2027 and beyond, while SB 261's climate-risk reporting has been enjoined by the Ninth Circuit since November 2025. [73, 74]A firm subject to all three regimes is being asked to disclose the same exposure on three timetables, one of which is suspended.

The deep difficulty is not political. It is that the firm is being asked to produce an annual point estimate of a risk whose distribution is non-stationary and whose realisations are correlated across everything it owns — the same dependence problem as L6, on a different substrate. [75, 76]

B10 · Sustainability

Sustainability, as a corporate function, is the management of impacts that occur in someone else's operations. Scope 3 emissions, labour conditions at a second-tier supplier, deforestation in a commodity catchment: in every case the impact is outside the control boundary by definition, and the firm's instruments are purchasing power, specification and exit — influence over a counterparty with its own customers. C2.

L2 quantifies what that does to the accounting. In a network where every firm reports faithfully, the same tonne is counted 5.61 times on average and the most-embedded node's tonne appears in thirteen returns; the economy-wide sum of correct Scope 3 inventories exceeds actual emissions by 5.544 times. The framework says as much: the Corporate Standard and the Scope 3 Standard were built for individual-company decision-making, and double counting across companies is expected rather than erroneous. [11, 77]The consequence for the boundary argument is that the metric a firm is held to is mostly a restatement of other firms' operations — in the network here, the median firm reports 4.81 times more emissions outside its boundary than inside it. Proposals to rebuild the accounting on a cost-accounting footing, with emissions transferred with the goods rather than re-estimated by every buyer, are a direct attack on exactly this problem. [78]A revision of the Scope 3 standard is under way with ISO participation, with a working draft dated March 2026 and publication targeted for 2028. [79]

And then the accountability rung moved down. The Omnibus directive of February 2026 raised the CSRD threshold to more than 1,000 employees and more than €450 million net turnover, replacing the previous two-of-three test and removing an estimated four fifths of previously covered companies; it raised the CSDDD threshold to 5,000 employees and €1.5 billion, deleted the EU-harmonised civil liability regime in favour of national frameworks, capped penalties at 3% of net worldwide turnover, and removed the obligation to adopt and implement a climate transition plan. [80, 81, 82, 83]The instrument being amended, Directive (EU) 2024/1760, was itself only two years old. [84]Member states must transpose the reporting changes by March 2027 and the due diligence changes by July 2028. [85]

This is the only plate on the board whose gap closed by retreat. The impacts did not move. The obligation to answer for them did.

B11 · Supply-chain resilience

Supply-chain resilience is the plate where control reaches C0 — not merely unmovable but unobserved. L1 gives the shape. In a four-tier network of 395 upstream nodes, nine are direct suppliers and 386, or 97.72%, lie beyond any contract the firm holds. The count of nodes grows with depth and the count of relationships does not, so the ratio is structural rather than a failure of diligence.

What makes it operationally dangerous is sharing. Of the nodes at tier three or deeper, 76.18% serve more than one tier-one line, and the single most-shared node supplies six of the nine direct suppliers. A firm that dual-sources at tier one, records two suppliers in the register and treats their failures as independent has bought correlated capacity at the price of independent capacity. Removing any single node at tier two or below takes out 2.241 tier-one lines on average, against exactly one under the independence assumption the register encodes. The practitioner literature has been making this argument with real cases for years: the exposure that matters is at the node nobody mapped, and mapping it is a different activity from managing suppliers, and the discipline has said so since well before the recent decade of disruptions. [86, 87, 88, 89]

Accountability arrives from two directions. Downstream, ordinary contractual liability for failure to supply. Upstream, import regimes that place an evidentiary burden on the importer for facts held several tiers away: the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act operates a rebuttable presumption that goods with specified links are barred from entry, and the EU's forced-labour regulation, applying from December 2027, builds a market-withdrawal mechanism on similar evidentiary ground. [90, 91]The firm is asked to prove a negative about a node L1 says it cannot see.

B12 · Geopolitical risk

The largest positive gap on the board, jointly with cybersecurity, belongs to the item with the least firm agency of any. A firm's landed cost is set by instruments it has no standing to contest, no notice of, and in the recent instance no stable statutory basis for.

The sequence is worth stating precisely because it is the clearest available demonstration. On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court held, 6–3, in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorise the President to impose tariffs: the authority to “regulate ... importation” does not reach a power to impose duties of unlimited amount, duration and scope, and tariffs are different in kind from the other authorities in the statute. [92, 93]The refund question — duties already collected under the invalidated instruments — was left to later proceedings and appears in the opinion only in dissent. Within four days the administration invoked Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, a never-previously-used authority permitting a temporary surcharge for a maximum of 150 days; the Court of International Trade held on 7 May 2026 that the proclamation was ultra vires because it identified trade and current-account deficits rather than a balance-of-payments problem in the statutory sense; and the surcharge lapsed on schedule in late July 2026, replaced by Section 301 action. [94, 95]

Every part of a firm's response to that sequence — routing, incorporation, inventory, hedging, pricing — sits at C0 with respect to the instrument itself. Meanwhile the duty is owed by the importer of record on the day of entry, which is A4 with no ambiguity at all.

Export control has developed the same shape and pushed it further across borders. China's April 2025 licensing regime for medium and heavy rare earths remains in force; the October 2025 measures added a de minimis rule under which foreign-made products containing 0.1% or more Chinese-origin controlled rare earth content by value require a Chinese licence to move between two other countries, together with a foreign-direct-product rule for processing technology. Those October measures were suspended in November 2025 for one year, leaving the April regime and the licensing architecture intact. [96, 97]The effect is measurable: CSIS data reported in 2026 put yttrium shipments from China to the United States at 17 tonnes in the eight months after the April controls against 333 tonnes in the eight months before. [98]This is the mechanism Farrell and Newman named weaponised interdependence — network positions built for efficiency converted into instruments of coercion — and it turns a firm's supply map into somebody else's policy lever. [99, 100]

B13 · Demographic change

Demography is the item with the longest lead time and the weakest accountability mechanism on the entire board: C1 and A1, gap zero, and both rungs low. The cohorts were born decades ago; the arithmetic has been publishable since before most current executives were hired; and there is no instrument at all by which anyone can require a firm to answer for the ageing of its own workforce. [101, 102]

L7 shows why the absence of an instrument matters, by separating two quantities that boards routinely conflate. Take a workforce in steady state under stated rules — entry at 24, retirement at 65, 7% annual voluntary exit — in which 30.10% of staff hold fifteen or more years of tenure. Apply the shock the developed economies are applying: 35% of headcount within five years of retirement. Headcount, replaced at entry every year, never moves. The stock of fifteen-year holders falls from 53.47% of headcount to 37.5% of its starting level by year five, sits on a plateau for most of a decade while the replacement cohort matures, and takes nineteen years to reach 95% of the steady-state level.

The plateau is the finding. It is not a lag in hiring; hiring is instantaneous by construction. It is that a fifteen-year holder takes fifteen years to make, and no rate of recruitment shortens the interval. Organisational-learning research has been reporting the same asymmetry from the other end for decades — knowledge accumulates with cumulative experience and depreciates when its holders leave, and the depreciation is fast relative to the accumulation. [103, 104]The metric on the board is the one that recovers. The metric that failed is not reported anywhere, because nothing requires it to be.

VI

Group V · Who writes the rules

The last three items concern the rules themselves: the ones the firm must obey, the ones it tries to shape, and the doctrine that proposes to make the whole apparatus unnecessary.

B14 · Regulatory change

It is tempting to treat regulatory change as a single directional trend, and 2026 is a good year for demonstrating that it is not. In the same eighteen months the European Union narrowed sustainability reporting sharply, widened platform-work protection, and deferred the AI Act's high-risk obligations by more than a year. [19, 49, 80]A firm holding one thesis about the direction of travel was wrong about two of the three.

The firm's control over the object is C2. It can consult, lobby, join associations and time its own compliance spend, which moves probabilities. The classical political-economy literature is about precisely how much: regulation is supplied to organised interests that can deliver concentrated benefits, and the resulting rules reflect the structure of the demand for them. [105, 106]

L8 gives the structural consequence of the accountability side. Sixteen binary policy choices; jurisdictions each pin a subset; and the probability that no single configuration satisfies all of them passes one half between two and nine jurisdictions depending on how much each constrains. Past that point there is no globally compliant posture, only per-jurisdiction divergence, which every jurisdiction still holds the whole firm answerable for. The empirical case is data: around twenty US state privacy statutes with different thresholds and mechanics, plus the GDPR, plus sectoral rules. [59, 107]A compliance function that describes its job as “meeting the requirements” is describing a task that may have no solution; the actual job is choosing which divergence to run and where to place the seams. [108]

B15 · Corporate political activity

This is the well-formed plate: C5 and A5, gap zero, both at the top. The firm decides entirely what to say, what to fund and which association to join; and registration regimes, anti-bribery statutes that reach named individuals, and shareholder litigation over the distance between stated and funded positions all attach. [109, 110, 111]Corporate political activity is the one item on this list where a firm both acts in its own name and answers in its own name.

The research tradition treats political activity as an ordinary resource-allocation problem with measurable returns, and finds those returns to be real but modest and highly contingent on approach and institutional setting. [112, 113, 114]What the tradition also finds is the failure mode this plate names: the distance between direct advocacy and the positions funded through trade associations, the latter typically disclosed at a much lower resolution. [115]

The gap-zero score should not be read as praise. It says the structure is coherent — that whoever holds the decision also holds the answer — not that the decisions are good ones. A well-formed accountability relationship is a precondition for the question of whether conduct is defensible, not a substitute for it.

B16 · Stakeholder capitalism

In August 2019 the Business Roundtable issued a Statement on the Purpose of a Corporation signed by 181 chief executives, superseding twenty-two years of documents endorsing shareholder primacy and committing the signatories to deliver value to customers, employees, suppliers, communities and shareholders. [116]It is the most widely cited recent statement of stakeholder capitalism and it is, on the reading developed here, the only item on the board that scores A0.

The argument for A0 is not that the sentiment is insincere. It is that no mechanism exists. Under Delaware law directors owe fiduciary duties to the corporation and its stockholders, not to other constituencies; the statement creates no contractual obligation to anyone it names; and no beneficiary listed in it can compel anything. Bebchuk and Tallarita made the doctrinal case and then tested it empirically, finding that the boards of most signatory companies never approved the statement, that companies' own governance guidelines continued to state shareholder primacy, and that the associated corporate documents did not change. [117, 118]

Control, meanwhile, is C5 and has recently been strengthened. Delaware's 2025 amendments to the General Corporation Law expanded safe harbours for interested and controller transactions under § 144 and narrowed the scope of books-and-records inspection under § 220; the Delaware Supreme Court in Maffei v. Palkon applied the business judgement rule to a reincorporation decision motivated by reducing litigation exposure. [119, 120]These are movements in the direction of more board discretion and less external inquiry.

The serious versions of the stakeholder argument do not depend on the statement. Freeman's original formulation is a theory of strategic management, not of duty; Mayer argues for changing corporate purpose in law rather than in press release; Hart and Zingales derive shareholder welfare rather than market value as the correct objective, which has the merit of naming a principal who can actually vote. [121, 122, 123, 124]Each is an attempt to build the missing mechanism. The document that gets quoted in annual reports is not.

Which produces the sharpest result the board yields. Fifteen items describe firms answering for things they increasingly cannot reach. The sixteenth, offered as the response to the first fifteen, is the only one with complete discretion and no one who can require an answer at all.

VII

The lab

The plates assert mechanisms. This section tests ten of them. Each experiment is a short deterministic program using nothing but the Python standard library, with a fixed seed; the transcript below is the program's output, unedited. The point is not to simulate the world. It is to establish that a claimed mechanism produces the claimed effect under an explicitly stated construction, so that disagreement can be about the construction rather than about arithmetic.

Two experiments were rebuilt after their first run and the rebuilds are disclosed here rather than buried. The workforce projection in L7 originally started from a population whose tenure distribution was not a steady state of its own entry and exit rules; it reported a rising stock of experienced staff, which was an artefact of the starting condition and not a finding. It was rebuilt so that the steady state is computed first and the demographic shock is applied to it explicitly. The co-presence experiment in L3 originally used Monte Carlo sampling for whole-team overlap; since the quantity has a closed form by inclusion–exclusion over the five days, the sampling was replaced with the exact computation.

One claim was withdrawn. An earlier draft of L8 asserted that jurisdictional infeasibility arrives at a particular number of jurisdictions. The sensitivity sweep shows that the number depends strongly on how much each jurisdiction constrains — it moves between two and nine over an eight-fold range of constraint density — so the text now reports the range and the shape rather than a point.

What the ten experiments establish

L1 on visibility. In a four-tier network of 395 upstream nodes, 97.72% lie beyond the tier the firm has contracts with. That is a property of depth, not of negligence: the count of nodes grows with tier and the count of contracts does not. The operational consequence is the shared ancestor. Three quarters of nodes at tier three or deeper serve more than one tier-one line, and the most-shared node supplies six of the nine direct suppliers. A firm that dual-sources at tier one and calls the result diversified has bought correlated capacity at the price of independent capacity. Removing one node at tier two or deeper takes out 2.241 tier-one lines on average, against exactly one under an independence assumption — which is what the risk register normally assumes. [86, 87]

L2 on Scope 3. If every firm in that network reports its upstream emissions faithfully, the same tonne is reported an average of 5.61 times, and the most-embedded node's tonne is reported thirteen times. The economy-wide sum of correctly prepared Scope 3 inventories exceeds actual emissions by 5.544 times. This is not an error in anyone's return. Double counting is a designed feature of an accounting framework built for individual-firm decision-making rather than for summation, and the framework says so. [11, 77]It does mean that the number a firm is asked to reduce is mostly a restatement of other firms' operations, and that the median firm in the network reports 4.81 times more emissions outside its boundary than inside it. [78]

L3 on co-presence. This is the cleanest result in the set, because it is exact rather than sampled. If everyone attends three days out of five and chooses which three, the expected number of days on which any given pair are both present is 1.80. If everyone attends two fixed days chosen for them, it is 2.00. Fifty per cent more attendance buys ten per cent less pairwise contact. The effect on whole teams is much sharper: under self-selected three-day attendance, the probability that a team of eight is ever all in the building on the same day is 0.0833, and for twelve it is 0.0109. Under two anchored days it is one, for every team size. Whatever the argument for office attendance is, the policy instrument that delivers it is coordination, not volume. [35, 37]

L4 on service levels. With demand Poisson at twenty jobs a period, moving the fill rate from 90% to 99% takes 35% more workers on shift and drops paid utilisation from 0.9112 to 0.7355. The workers' earnings per logged-on hour fall to 80.7 on an index where the 90% configuration is 100, and unpaid waiting rises from 0.71 to 2.12 hours of an eight-hour shift. The platform's service level is real, the improvement is real, and it is purchased with time that appears in nobody's cost line because the people holding it are not employees. [44, 52]

L5 on de-identification. In a synthetic population of 200,000, birth year alone makes nobody unique and birth month with sex makes nobody unique. Adding a coarse geographic area — 480 areas, not postcodes — makes 46.77% of people unique, and all three together make 63.12% unique and put 95.28% into a group of fewer than five. Nothing in that list is a name, an account number, or anything a deletion request would think to name. The result reproduces, on coarser attributes than the original, the finding that identifiability is a property of combinations rather than of fields. [55, 57, 58]

L6 on concentration. Sixty vendor relationships resting on twelve underlying providers, with the largest carrying fifteen. Hold the marginal probability that any given vendor is down at 2%, and the mean number simultaneously down is 1.2000 under either assumption — identical to four decimal places, because the marginals are identical by construction. The tail is not. The probability that ten or more are down at once is 3.096×10-7 if failures are independent and 4.274×10-2 if they run through shared providers, a factor of about 138,000. The 99th percentile moves from four vendors to fifteen. Every number a firm reports about its third-party exposure is a marginal; the thing that hurts it is the dependence structure, which no vendor questionnaire asks about. [64, 65]

L7 on experience. Take a workforce in demographic steady state — entry at 24, retirement at 65, 7% annual voluntary exit — in which 30.10% of people hold fifteen or more years of tenure. Now apply the shock the developed world is actually applying: put 35% of headcount within five years of retirement. The stock of fifteen-year holders starts at 53.47% of headcount, falls to 37.5% of that starting level by year five, sits on a plateau for the better part of a decade, and takes nineteen years to return to 95% of the steady-state figure. Headcount, by construction, never moves at all. The instrument every firm reaches for — hiring — restores the quantity the board reviews and cannot restore the quantity that failed. [101, 103]

L8 on jurisdictions. Sixteen binary policy choices give 65,536 possible configurations. Let each jurisdiction pin a random subset of those choices. At the central parameterisation — four pins each — the probability that no single configuration satisfies all of them passes one half at three jurisdictions and reaches one at seven. The sensitivity sweep matters more than the point estimate: across an eight-fold range of pinning density, the collapse arrives between two and nine jurisdictions. Beyond it there is no globally compliant posture, only per-jurisdiction divergence — for which every jurisdiction still holds the whole firm answerable. [107, 108]

L9 on ratings. Six raters read the same thirty indicator values for sixty firms. They disagree about nothing factual. They differ only in which indicators they include and how they weight them. Mean pairwise Spearman correlation of the resulting scores: 0.4568, ranging from 0.2645 to 0.6063. The decomposition is the interesting part. Vary the weights alone and correlation stays at 0.8858; vary the scope alone and it falls to 0.5879. Scope, not weighting, is where divergence comes from — which matches what the empirical literature finds when it decomposes real rating disagreement. [125, 126]Across the six raters, no firm at all appears in every rater's top decile.

L10 on tasks against jobs. Sixty task types with a mean automatable share of 0.3854, bundled into 4,000 occupations. Mean automatable share of task-time is 0.3877, essentially the task mean. But only 1.48% of occupations cross a 0.70 displacement threshold, while 90.80% lose at least a fifth of their task-time without crossing it — about sixty-two recomposed jobs for every displaced one. The second finding is sharper. Occupations built from five or fewer tasks are not more automatable than those built from ten or more: mean exposure 0.3906 against 0.3863, a difference of 1.12%. They cross the displacement threshold 41 times as often. Narrow bundles are not more exposed; they are less diversified. Since the narrow bundles in a real organisation are the junior ones, a firm whose job architecture concentrates entry-level work into short task lists has manufactured the variance itself. [24, 31, 127]

Nine of sixteen answer for more than they hold. Four are matched and three hold more than they answer for. The positive nine are, without exception, the items whose object crosses the firm's boundary: a licensed model, a marketplace transaction, a record in a sub-processor's system, a vendor's estate, the physical climate, an unseen fourth-tier supplier, a border, a rule set. The negative three — job design, workplace, and non-shareholder interests — are the items whose object stays entirely inside it. The pattern is not subtle: the boundary of what a firm answers for has been redrawn around the consequences of its activity, and the boundary of what it controls has stayed drawn around its contracts.

The relationship is not a clean inverse. Across the sixteen, the correlation between control rung and accountability rung is −0.20 — weak, negative, and not much of a finding. It is dragged towards zero by two plates at the extremes of the control ladder that behave in opposite ways: corporate political activity at C5 with A5, and stakeholder capitalism at C5 with A0. That is worth stating as a null. “The less you control the more you answer for” is a good slogan and a poor summary of the board.

The real structure is at the bottom of the control ladder. Six plates score C1 or C0, meaning the object is observable at best and in two cases not even that. All six carry accountability at A1 or above, five at A2 or above, and their mean gap is +2.50, against −2.00 for the five plates at C4 or C5. There is no plate anywhere on the board where a firm cannot see the state and also is not asked about it. Whatever the accountability apparatus is tracking, it is not tracking reach.

Every A4 or A5 on the board is a named instrument. Nine plates carry binding legal exposure, and each names the document that creates it — the AI Act, the DSA and DMA, the Platform Work Directive, the GDPR, NIS2 and the SEC's Item 1.05, DORA, the tariff and export-control instruments, and the anti-bribery statutes. Five of those nine attach to objects the firm cannot set: C2 or below. That is the distribution the phrase “compliance burden” is usually pointing at, and it is a more precise complaint than it is normally allowed to be.

Two rungs moved during the writing of this article, in opposite directions. Sustainability's accountability rung fell when the Omnibus directive entered into force in March 2026, removing roughly four fifths of previously covered companies from CSRD scope and deleting the harmonised civil liability regime. [80, 82]The gig economy's rung is due to rise when the Platform Work Directive is transposed by December 2026, making exercised control the trigger for employment status. [49]A framework that treats regulation as a single direction of travel cannot represent both.

The only A0 is the proposed remedy. Fifteen plates describe an accountability mechanism, however imperfect. One does not, and it is the one that appears in the front of the annual report as the firm's account of how it discharges its obligations to everyone else on the list.

IX

Objections, limits, and what the reading buys

Four objections

The ladders are ordinal, so the gap is not a real number. Correct, and the arithmetic here respects it. Nothing in this article averages a gap across plates and calls the result a quantity of anything. The gap is used for three things an ordinal scale supports: sign, comparison, and counting. Nine of sixteen positive, three negative, four matched; the largest positive gap and the largest negative one; and the fact that the only A0 on the board sits next to a C5. Those claims survive any monotone re-scaling of either ladder.

The rung assignments are judgement calls. They are, and the plates are built so that the judgement is auditable rather than hidden. Each names an instrument, and the instrument is the evidence for the accountability rung; each names an object, and the object fixes what the control rung is about. A reader who thinks data privacy should sit at C4 rather than C1 because Article 28 contracts are binding is making a specific, checkable argument about whether a contract constitutes control of a state one cannot observe. That is the argument worth having, and the format is designed to force it into the open rather than to settle it.

The gap is just externality, relabelled. Partly. A negative gap is exactly an externality seen from the inside. But a positive gap is not an externality at all and has no standard name: it is a firm being made the residual claimant on a state it cannot read. The two failures have opposite remedies. Negative gaps are closed by attaching liability. Positive gaps are not closed by attaching more liability; they are closed by building an instrument that reaches the state, or by moving the accountability to someone who has one.

Sixteen items chosen by someone else is not a sample. It is not. It is the list as it is conventionally taught and as it appears on strategy agendas, which is the point: the argument is that a list assembled for no theoretical reason nonetheless sorts cleanly on one axis. If the sorting were an artefact of selection, a different list would sort differently. That is a testable claim and this article does not test it.

What the reading buys

The first thing it buys is a diagnostic that separates two failures that look identical in a post-mortem. When something goes wrong at a positive-gap item, the standard organisational response is to add assurance: more questionnaires, more attestations, another line in the third-party risk policy. L6 is the argument against that response. Every one of those instruments measures a marginal probability, and the entire difference between the benign case and the catastrophic one lives in the dependence structure, which no marginal can see. The remedy for a positive gap is not more assurance about each part; it is a map of what the parts share.

The second is a way of reading regulation that does not depend on approving of it. Directive (EU) 2024/2831 makes exercised control the trigger for employment status, which is a deliberate attempt to force a gap to zero by moving the accountability rung to meet the control rung. [49]Directive (EU) 2026/470 moved the accountability rung down on sustainability, taking roughly four fifths of previously covered companies out of scope and deleting the harmonised civil liability regime. [80, 82]Both are gap-closing moves. They close it from opposite ends, and a firm that has only one story about “the direction of regulation” will have got one of them badly wrong.

The third is a test for proposed remedies. Stakeholder capitalism is offered, in the literature and in the annual report, as the answer to most of the other fifteen items. On this reading it is the only item on the list at A0: no fiduciary duty runs to a non-shareholder constituency, no beneficiary named in the 2019 statement can compel anything, and the empirical work on the signatories found that most of their boards never approved the statement at all. [117, 118]A doctrine with complete discretion and no mechanism is not a solution to an accountability gap. It is the largest one on the board.

None of which argues that the two boundaries ought to coincide. There are good reasons to place liability on a party who cannot directly prevent a harm, and good reasons to leave discretion unpoliced where policing it would cost more than the harm. [15]The argument is narrower and, if it is right, more useful: that the sixteen problems on the standard list are not sixteen problems. They are sixteen readings of one instrument, and the instrument measures how far a firm's answer has travelled from its reach.
