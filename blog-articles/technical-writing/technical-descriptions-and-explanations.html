What Can the Reader Do Afterwards?

Technical description and explanation, read as fourteen acceptance tests

A technical writer sits down with an object and a reader, and has to decide what kind of sentence to write. Handbooks answer this with a list: definition, description, classification, comparison, mechanism, process, cause and effect, overview, component, function, concept, example, analogy, and the choice of abstraction that governs the rest[1],[2]. The list is stable across half a century of textbooks, which is a good sign that it is tracking something real. What the list does not say is what makes the items different from each other, or how you would know that you had picked the wrong one.

The usual answer is that they differ in subject matter. Descriptions are about things and processes are about events; mechanisms are about the insides and functions are about the purposes. This cannot be right, because the same subject matter takes all fourteen treatments. A pressure relief valve can be defined, described, classified, compared with a rupture disc, explained mechanically, narrated as a process, blamed for an incident, placed in a system overview, described as a component, given a function, used to illustrate a principle, shown as an example, likened to a fuse, and pitched at any of four levels of detail. Nothing about the valve selects among these. The reader does.

The better answer is that each mode answers a different question and, in doing so, promises the reader a different competence. That promise is testable. If a definition has worked, the reader can take an instance the document never mentioned and say whether the term covers it. If a mechanism has worked, the reader can answer a question about a change nobody has made. If an analogy has worked, the reader can carry the source into the target and also knows where to stop carrying it. These are acceptance tests, in the ordinary engineering sense: statements of what would count as the text having done its job, written before the text is written. Standards for user documentation have been converging on exactly this posture. ISO 24495-1 defines plain language by whether readers can find, understand and use the information rather than by any property of the sentences[3],[4], and ISO/IEC/IEEE 26513 makes the review of information for users a matter of testing it against tasks[5].

The question this article asks

Every one of the fourteen modes can be stated as a promise about the reader's subsequent competence. Order those competences by inferential strength and you get a ladder. At the bottom, the reader can pick the thing out of a lineup. At the top, the reader can take the account to a system this document never described. Each mode sits at one rung: the highest rung it can reach without borrowing from another mode.

The ladder is not an invention of this article. It is the manipulationist criterion for explanation, which holds that an explanation is worth the name when it answers a range of what-if-things-had-been-different questions[6],[7]; it is the difference Marr drew between levels that answer what and levels that answer how[8]; and it is the distinction the educational literature keeps rediscovering between knowing that a device works and knowing how, which shows up in performance data as the difference between operators who can follow a procedure and operators who can recover from a state the procedure did not anticipate[9],[10].

The question, then, is not what is this text about? but what can the reader do afterwards, and how would anyone know?

None of this is new as an idea, only as a working rule. Philosophy of science spent the second half of the twentieth century arguing about what an explanation has to supply, moving from the covering-law model, on which an explanation is a derivation from laws and initial conditions[11],[12], through statistical-relevance and causal-process accounts[13],[14], to the position that explanations are judged by what they let you do with them[6],[15]. The psychological literature added the warning that the feeling of having understood is a poor guide to having understood[16]. Instruction had reached the same place from the other direction, with taxonomies of educational objectives that grade material by the competence it targets rather than by its content[17],[18]. What the technical-writing literature inherited instead was the readability formula, which counts syllables and sentence lengths and was known to be measuring the wrong thing within a decade of being adopted[19],[20],[21]. The systems and software vocabulary standard still has no entry for most of the fourteen modes[22].

Two things follow, and both are measured later in this article. The first is that mismatches between the mode used and the question asked are not style problems. They are category errors with a determinate cost, and the cost can be computed: a description used as a mechanism gets the sign of an intervention wrong; a taxonomy with two criteria at one level sorts a minority of its own subject matter; a set of positive examples cannot narrow a concept past a fixed floor no matter how many are supplied.

The second is less comfortable. Rank the fourteen modes by how high they reach and then rank them by how cheaply a claim in that mode can be checked, and the two orders run against each other. The modes that promise least are the ones a script can audit. The modes that promise most — conceptual explanation, analogy, the choice of abstraction — can be audited only by a reader who already possesses the competence the text was supposed to supply. This is the reason technical documentation is full of mechanically enforced rules about terminology and numbering and nearly empty of rules about explanation.

How to read the plates

Each of the fourteen modes gets a plate. The fields are the same every time. Answers is the reader's question in canonical form. Commits to is what the writer is asserting by choosing this mode. Acceptance test is what the reader can do if the text worked. Presupposes is what the reader must already have for the test to be passable at all. Fails as is the characteristic degenerate form: not a bad instance of the mode, but a different mode wearing its clothes.

The gauge on each plate is an inference staircase. Six treads, one per rung. The filled tread is the highest rung the mode reaches unaided; the hatched treads below it are the competences the mode borrows from the reader or from a neighbouring section rather than supplying itself. A mode with a high tread and a lot of hatching is expensive: it works only for a reader who already has most of what it needs.

A word about the presupposes field, because it is where most real documents fail. What a reader must already have is not a fixed quantity; it is negotiated, and the negotiation is the ordinary business of cooperative communication — say as much as is needed and no more, and assume the other party is trying to be relevant[23],[24],[25]. A document cannot negotiate. It has to guess, once, for everybody, and the plates record what each mode must guess right for its acceptance test to be passable at all.

One rule governs the assignment, and it is worth stating because it is what keeps the ladder from being a matter of taste. A mode scores rung I4 only if it supports arbitrary interventions on the variables it names, not one particular intervention. That is why functional explanation sits at I3 rather than I4: a function statement licenses exactly one counterfactual — remove the part and the capacity goes — and is silent about every other change you could make to the same part.

GROUP I

Fixing the referent

M01Definition

01DefinitionGROUP I · Fixing the referent
Answers
What does this term pick out, and what does it exclude?
Commits to
That a stated superordinate concept plus a stated delimiting characteristic together fix the extension of the term.
Acceptance test
The reader can take an instance the text never mentioned and say whether the term applies.
Presupposes
Every term used in the definition is either primitive for this audience or defined elsewhere in the same vocabulary.
Fails as
The definition circles back to the term through some chain of other entries, or smuggles in a requirement.
evidence [26],[27],[28]

A definition in technical work is not a gloss and not an etymology. It is a licence to substitute. ISO 704 sets the standard form: name the superordinate concept, then give the characteristics that delimit this concept within it, and prefer this intensional form to every alternative because it is the only one that exposes where the concept sits in a concept system[26],[29]. The logician's version of the same requirement is older and sharper. A definition is admissible when it satisfies eliminability — every sentence containing the defined term has an equivalent that does not — and non-creativity, meaning it licenses nothing about the old vocabulary that was not licensed before[30],[27],[31].

The form itself is Aristotle's, and the tradition that carries it into modern terminology work runs through the long argument about whether definitions report usage, stipulate it, or do something in between[32],[33]. Carnap's notion of explication is the useful middle term for technical work: you take a vague concept in use and replace it with a precise one that does the same job better, and you accept that the replacement is a proposal rather than a discovery[34],[35]. Terminology practice institutionalises exactly this, which is why its handbooks treat the concept system as the primary object and the term as a label attached to it[36],[37].

Both criteria are properties of a vocabulary, not of an entry. This is the part that gets lost. A drafter checks a definition by reading it, and reading it is a local operation, so circularity that runs through three other entries is invisible. Experiment L1 makes the cost of that blindness concrete. In a twenty-four-term brake vocabulary over seven declared primitives, every term unfolds to primitives, at a mean depth of 2.125 and a maximum of 4. Then one entry is rewritten in a form that any editor would wave through — retardation: the reduction in motion produced by a brake — and 5 terms lose their eliminability, not one. The defect is in the graph, not the sentence.

The standards writers know this, which is why the ISO/IEC drafting rules forbid definitions that contain requirements or that take the form of a sentence continuing the term[28],[38]. It is also why a definition is one of the very few things in a technical document that a program can audit without a reader: the dependency graph is right there in the text. Hold on to that, because almost nothing else in this article has the property.

Two failure modes are worth separating from circularity because they look like precision. The first is definition by extension where an intension was wanted — listing the members instead of stating the membership condition, which is exactly right for a closed set such as the seven fault codes and exactly wrong for anything a future release might add[26]. The second is the definition that quietly does normative work: a compliant unit is one that has passed acceptance testing is a requirement wearing a definition's clothes, and the reader who tries to use it to sort an instance finds that the sorting has been deferred to somebody else[28],[39].

There is a limit worth naming. The genus-and-differentia form assumes that membership has necessary and sufficient conditions, and for a great many technical concepts it does not. Family resemblance is the rule rather than the exception in natural categories[40],[41], and terminology theory has spent thirty years arguing about how much of Wüster's programme survives that observation[42],[43]. The practical consequence is narrow but real: when a concept resists intensional definition, the honest move is to say so and switch modes — to classification, or to examples and counterexamples — rather than to produce a definition whose delimiting characteristic is doing no work.

M02Description

02DescriptionGROUP I · Fixing the referent
Answers
What is there, and what would I see, measure or touch?
Commits to
That the stated attributes hold of the object, at the stated tolerance, under the stated conditions.
Acceptance test
The reader can hold the text against the object and find every stated feature, and can locate a named feature without being told where it is.
Presupposes
A shared vocabulary of parts and a shared frame of reference for orientation and units.
Fails as
Attributes chosen for salience rather than discrimination, so the description fits several objects equally well.
evidence [44],[45],[46]

Description is the mode with the weakest promise and the widest use. Its acceptance test is physical: the reader holds the text against the object and every stated feature is there, at the stated tolerance, in the stated place. Nothing about future states is claimed. Nothing about causes is claimed. What the reader gains is the ability to recognise the thing and to find a named part on it, which is rung I1 and no higher.

The weakness is the point. A description is the only mode whose acceptance test can be run by someone with no understanding of the subject at all, holding a ruler. This is why it carries the regulatory weight it does: it is what an inspector can check, what a requirements document can be held to, and what a specification means by shall[46],[47],[39],[48].

The characteristic failure is not vagueness but salience. Writers describe what is striking, and what is striking is rarely what discriminates. A description that opens with the housing colour and the overall dimensions has told the reader nothing that separates this unit from the four adjacent part numbers, while omitting the keyway offset that does. The discipline that corrects this is a discrimination test: for each object in the family, find the smallest set of attributes that no sibling shares, and check that the description contains it. It is dull, it is mechanical, and almost nobody does it.

Two conventions in the field encode the lesson. The first is controlled language: ASD-STE100, now in Issue 9 and formally a standard rather than a specification, restricts writers to about nine hundred approved words, each approved for one part of speech and one meaning, precisely so that a description cannot silently vary its own vocabulary between one paragraph and the next[49],[50]. The second is reference designation. IEC 81346 structures a technical system by aspect — what an object does, what it is, where it sits — and gives each aspect its own designation, so that a component named by function does not have to be renamed when the product changes[51]. Both are answers to the same discovery: description is only checkable if the vocabulary is fixed first.

The editing tradition has its own version of the discrimination rule, arrived at through style rather than logic. Put the information the reader needs to interpret a sentence before the information that is new; keep the subject of a sentence and the actor in it the same thing; and prefer the concrete noun to the nominalisation that hides it[52],[53]. None of these is a rule about description as such, but each of them is a rule about whether a reader can hold a description against an object and check it, which is what the mode's acceptance test requires[54],[55].

Where description shades into something stronger is in what the reader does with the spatial arrangement. Give a reader a labelled exploded view and an ordered part list and they will start forming expectations about what touches what, which is a step toward mechanism they were not licensed to take[56],[57]. That is not always a problem, but it is worth knowing that a description is doing it, because the inferences it invites are the ones nobody has checked.

M03Classification

03ClassificationGROUP I · Fixing the referent
Answers
What kinds are there, and which kind is this one?
Commits to
That one criterion divides the subject at each level, and that the resulting classes are exclusive and exhaustive over the stated domain.
Acceptance test
The reader can place a novel instance in exactly one class, and can say when an instance falls outside the scheme altogether.
Presupposes
A stated domain of application and a stated criterion of division at every level.
Fails as
Two criteria applied at one level, so instances land in several classes at once or in none.
evidence [26],[58],[59]

Classification promises that the reader can sort. Hand them an instance the document never listed and they place it in exactly one class, or they recognise that it falls outside the scheme. Everything else about a taxonomy is bookkeeping.

The rule that makes the promise keepable is very old and is stated in current terminology standards as a requirement: divide by one criterion at one level[26]. Break it and the failure is not gradual. Experiment L2 enumerates a pump catalogue of 24 feature combinations and applies two schemes to it. A division by a single criterion sorts 100.0% of the space into exactly one class, which is not a good result but a structural guarantee. A scheme whose four classes come from four different criteria — positive-displacement, electric, submersible, sealless, each of them a perfectly sensible category — sorts 29.2% into exactly one class, leaves 8.3% in no class, and puts 62.5% into two or more, with some instances in all four at once.

Notice what that number means. Such a scheme is not a taxonomy with a few awkward edges. It is a taxonomy for a 29.2% minority of its own subject matter, and a reader who trusts it will misfile most of what they meet. Catalogues that grew by accretion look exactly like this, and the symptom in the field is a support queue full of people who cannot find their own equipment in the index.

Two refinements save classification from being a merely formal exercise. The first is that the criterion must be chosen for the reader's task, not for the object's structure. Ontology work states this as the requirement that a taxonomy commit to an identity criterion and stick to it[59],[60], and insists that a shared scheme is a specification of a conceptualisation, written down as one before it can be shared[61]; the classification literature states it as the observation that every scheme encodes a set of interests and silently disadvantages whatever it cannot see[58]. The second is that one level of the hierarchy is psychologically privileged. Rosch's basic level — the rung at which most features are shared within a class and few across classes — is where people name things spontaneously, learn fastest and remember best[62],[63],[64]. A document whose headings sit above or below that level will be formally correct and hard to use, and the effect is measurable in how experts and novices sort the same problems: novices group by surface appearance, experts by the principle that governs the solution[65].

The mode's honest limit is that it cannot decide what the classes are for. A classification is answerable to a purpose that the classification itself cannot state, which is why the strongest documentation of a taxonomy always begins with a sentence naming what decisions the scheme is meant to support.

M04Comparison

04ComparisonGROUP I · Fixing the referent
Answers
How do these differ, on what basis, and which should I choose?
Commits to
That the named attributes are the ones that matter, measured the same way for every candidate, and weighted as stated.
Acceptance test
The reader can predict which candidate wins under a weighting the text did not use, and can say what would have to change for the ranking to flip.
Presupposes
A common measurement procedure, a stated basis of comparison, and candidates drawn from one class.
Fails as
An unstated basis, so the verdict cannot be wrong; or a single composite score standing in for the weighting that produced it.
evidence [66],[67],[68]

Comparison is the mode readers want most and writers control least. Its acceptance test is unusual because it looks past the text: the reader should be able to predict which candidate wins under a weighting the document never applied, and to say what would have to change for the ranking to flip. If the document has not made that possible, it has expressed a preference and called it an analysis.

What makes the test passable is a stated basis: the same attributes, measured the same way, for candidates drawn from one class, with the weighting in the open. Similarity is not a property of a pair of objects but of a pair plus a set of features and a weighting over them, which is why judged similarity is famously asymmetric and context-dependent[66]. The performance literature learned the same lesson expensively. Fleming and Wallace showed that summarising normalised benchmark ratios with an arithmetic mean can reverse the ranking that the geometric mean gives on the same data[67]; Smith showed that the single number depends on which machine you normalise to[68]; and the resulting convention — report the components, name the reference, state the weighting — is now standard practice in architecture[69].

Experiment L3 puts numbers on the residual freedom. Three controllers, five attributes, min-max normalisation, and every weight vector on a 0.05 grid over the simplex: 10,626 weightings in all. Controller A wins on 38.4% of them, Controller C on 33.9%, Controller B on 27.1%, and none of the three is dominated. A document that reports one winner without a basis has selected a point in that space and hidden the selection.

The experiment also produced a null result worth reporting, because it inverts a common worry. Under equal weights, all four defensible normalisations — raw ratios, min-max, z-scores, and ranks — pick the same winner. The disagreement in this case does not come from the scaling; it comes entirely from the weights. That is the more uncomfortable finding, because scaling is a technical choice a writer can defend on technical grounds, and weighting is a statement about what the reader cares about, which the writer usually does not know.

Two structural warnings belong with the mode. First, no aggregation rule over three or more criteria has all the properties one naively wants of it, which is not a defect of any particular method but a theorem about aggregation[70],[71]. Second, the presentational habit of collapsing to a composite score — a five-star rating, a weighted index, an analytic-hierarchy number — is the failure mode, not the solution, because it publishes the output of a weighting while withholding the weighting[72]. The usable form is a table where the reader can see the components and do their own arithmetic.

GROUP II

Making it move

M05Mechanism

05MechanismGROUP II · Making it move
Answers
How does it work, and what happens if I change this part of it?
Commits to
That the named entities and activities are organised so that they produce the behaviour, and that the account supports interventions on any of them.
Acceptance test
The reader can answer a question about a change nobody has made yet, including a change the text never mentions.
Presupposes
Component descriptions, a decomposition, and a process account that fixes the normal case.
Fails as
A narrative of the normal case presented as a mechanism, which answers what happens next and inverts what happens if.
evidence [73],[6],[74]

Mechanism is where the ladder jumps. Everything before it can be checked by observing the system in its ordinary condition. A mechanism claims something stronger: that the reader can answer a question about a change nobody has made yet. Machamer, Darden and Craver's formulation — entities and activities organised such that they produce regular changes from start to finish[73],[75],[76] — earns its keep because it makes that jump explicit. The account has to name what the parts are and what they do, because it is the doings that the reader will later intervene on.

The manipulationist criterion states the acceptance test directly: an account explains to the extent that it answers what-if-things-had-been- different questions[6]. Notice what that rules out. A perfectly accurate account of how the system behaves in its ordinary condition is not a mechanism, because it fixes only the observed regime, and a reader who reads it as a mechanism will get interventions wrong. Not approximately wrong.

Experiment L4 constructs the smallest honest case. A maintainer can raise coolant flow; operators raise it exactly on the machines that already run hot; running hot causes bearing failure. The observational table is correct and is the sort of table a good description would contain: failure occurs in 26.0% of intervals where flow was raised against 8.8% where it was not, an association of 17.2 points in the direction of harm. The causal effect of raising flow, computed on the same model, is 13.8 points in the direction of protection. Association and effect have opposite signs, and the error a reader makes by treating the table as causal is 31.0 percentage points[77],[78].

The audit in that experiment is the part worth keeping. Eight queries were put to the observational table. All 4 observational queries came back exactly right. Of the 4 interventional queries, one came back right — and it is the one that conditions on the duty cycle as well as on the setting. The descriptive account works causally precisely when the reader already supplies the mechanism from outside. That is the whole difference between the two modes in a single line.

The other half of the mechanist literature is worth having because it takes the pressure off the word itself: what makes something a mechanistic explanation is the practice of decomposing and localising, not a metaphysical thesis about machines[79]. And the reader-side counterpart is the mental model, the internal account a person runs to predict what a device will do. That literature is candid about its own limits — the term covers several different things, models are typically incomplete and unstable, and people run them badly[80],[81],[82] — which is the correct expectation to have of what a theory-of-operation chapter can install.

What this means for writing is unglamorous. A theory-of-operation chapter earns its rung by naming the variables that can be changed and stating what each change propagates to, which is closer to a device topology than to a narrative[74],[83]. And the mode has a well-documented occupational hazard: readers systematically believe they understand mechanisms they cannot in fact produce, an illusion of explanatory depth that is specific to mechanisms and does not appear for facts or procedures[84],[85]. A document that leaves the reader feeling that they understand the machine has done nothing at all, since that feeling is exactly what the illusion produces. The test is the intervention question, asked out loud.

M06Process explanation

06Process explanationGROUP II · Making it move
Answers
What happens, in what order, and how do I know a stage has finished?
Commits to
That the stages occur in the stated order, that each has a stated entry and exit condition, and that nothing material happens between them.
Acceptance test
The reader can be dropped into the middle of the process, say which stage is running, and say what comes next.
Presupposes
Named participants, a stated granularity, and an observable marker for each stage boundary.
Fails as
Steps whose order is required but not stated, leaving the reader a large space of executions that all look like compliance.
evidence [86],[87],[88]

A process explanation promises prediction under normal operation: dropped into the middle, the reader can say which stage is running and what comes next. It does not promise intervention. The qualitative-physics tradition built machinery for exactly this competence, deriving the possible behaviours of a device from a description of its processes and their influences without any numerical model[86],[89], and the lesson that transfers to prose is that the useful unit is not the step but the stage boundary: what starts a stage, what ends it, and how the reader can tell.

The measurable property of a process description is how much of the execution it leaves open. Experiment L5 takes a six-action caliper service with a genuine partial order among the actions. An unordered list of the six actions leaves 720 orderings the reader could construct, of which 3 actually work: a reader who reconstructs an order gets a working one 0.42% of the time. Numbering all six removes all 9.49 bits of that ambiguity. But grouping the six into three named subgoals — isolate, replace, restore — with the actions left unordered inside each group removes 62% of the bits and lifts the chance of a working execution to 25%, a factor of 60, without adding a single instruction.

That result is the quantitative shadow of a well-replicated instructional finding. Labelling subgoals in a worked procedure improves transfer to novel problems substantially, and it does so because the label gives the learner a unit to encode instead of a run of undifferentiated actions[87],[90]. The same structure is what makes procedural documentation survive contact with a distracted reader: people do not read procedures linearly, they sample them, and a sampled text is recoverable only if it is chunked[91],[88],[92].

The mode's characteristic failure is the required order that is not stated. It is invisible in review because the reviewer knows the order, which is the curse of knowledge operating on exactly the population least able to detect it[93],[94],[95]. The countermeasure is mechanical: for each pair of steps, ask whether swapping them breaks anything, and if it does, say so in the text rather than relying on the numbering to imply it. Numbering implies sequence to a reader who reads in sequence, and that reader is rarer than the numbering assumes.

A second failure is subtler and shows up in the standards. A process description and a procedure are different artefacts: one says what the system does, the other says what the reader should do. S1000D keeps them in different data module types with different schemas, and the information code that opens a data module records which kind of information is inside[96],[97]. Documents that blur the two produce a text that is neither predictive nor executable — the reader cannot tell whether a sentence is reporting or instructing.

M07Cause and effect

07Cause and effectGROUP II · Making it move
Answers
Why did this happen, rather than something else?
Commits to
That the named factor made the difference, against a stated contrast case and a stated set of background conditions.
Acceptance test
The reader can say which change would have prevented the outcome, and which would not, without having seen either.
Presupposes
A mechanism, or at least a stated causal structure; and a contrast class the explanation is answering against.
Fails as
A single cause selected from an INUS cluster and reported as the cause, with the selection criteria left out.
evidence [98],[99],[100]

Causal explanation shares its rung with mechanism and almost none of its checkability. The acceptance test is a counterfactual the reader has never seen tested: which change would have prevented the outcome, and which would not. In a laboratory that is answerable by experiment. In an incident report it is answerable by nobody, because the system that produced the outcome has been dismantled, repaired, or is running again with a patch on it.

The first discipline is contrast. Explanations answer why-this-rather-than- that, and the that is never in the question as asked[99],[101],[102]. Why did the pump fail? has different correct answers depending on whether the contrast is the other pump, the same pump last week, or the pump as designed. A report that does not state its contrast case is producing a sentence the reader cannot evaluate, and the same demand has resurfaced in work on machine explanation, where contrastive framing turns out to be what people actually want when they ask why[103].

The formal machinery for the second half of that test is now standard. The distinction between seeing and doing is what the do-operator was introduced to mark, and the surrounding framework supplies rules for when an interventional quantity can be recovered from observational data at all[78],[104],[105]. The statistical tradition made the same point more austerely: an attribute you cannot in principle manipulate is not a cause in the sense that supports the counterfactual[106]. That is a stronger position than most incident reporting can afford, but it is a useful test to apply to a sentence before publishing it.

The second discipline is about selection. Causes come in clusters of conditions each of which is insufficient but necessary within a set that is unnecessary but sufficient — Mackie's INUS structure[98],[107],[108]. An investigator picks one member of the cluster and reports it as the cause. The selection is legitimate and unavoidable; what makes it explanation rather than assignment is stating the criterion by which it was made. Root cause is the phrase that hides this step, which is why systems-safety work has spent two decades arguing that the chain-of-events model with a single root is the wrong model for accidents in tightly coupled systems[100],[109],[110]. Dekker's version of the objection is the sharpest: human error is a label applied at the end of the search, not a finding that terminates it[111],[112].

The classic documentary failure sits at exactly this seam. The Columbia board found that the foam-strike analysis had been presented in a slide hierarchy where the qualifiers migrated into sub-bullets and the headline asserted what the analysis did not support, and it said so in the report itself[113],[114]. The Challenger commission's minority appendix made the parallel point about the treatment of margin[115],[116],[117]. Neither is a story about bad writers. Both are stories about a format in which the contrast case and the selection criterion have no place to go[116].

What a writer can actually do is modest and worth doing. State the contrast. State the background conditions being held fixed. Say which of the INUS cluster you selected and why. And distinguish, in the text, between the factor that made the difference on this occasion and the factor whose removal would most reduce the recurrence rate — they are different questions and they routinely have different answers[118],[6].

A system overview looks like the most ambitious mode and is in fact one of the humblest. Its promise is navigational: given a symptom or a question, the reader can name the region responsible, and can read the rest of the document in an order where nothing is used before it has been introduced. That is rung I1. An overview that tries to explain how the system works has stopped being an overview and become a mechanism at a coarse grain, which is a legitimate thing to write and a different thing to check.

What makes an overview true or false is not the boxes but the grouping. Parnas's criterion is that a module should hide a design decision likely to change, so that when the decision changes the damage is confined[119],[122]. Simon's is that a system worth describing hierarchically is nearly decomposable: interactions within a subsystem are strong and interactions between subsystems are weak, so the short-run behaviour of a part is approximately independent of the rest[120]. Both are claims about which grouping is correct, not about how to draw it.

Both are also older than software. Alexander's argument about design was that the right decomposition is the one whose parts have few and weak connections across the cut, and that a decomposition inherited from vocabulary rather than from the structure of the problem will not have that property[123]; the product-architecture literature restates it as the mapping between functional elements and physical chunks[124],[125].

Experiment L6 tests two overviews of the same appliance: 18 modules, 28 dependencies, unchanged between them. One groups by the design secret each region hides; the other groups the way the source tree happens to be laid out — drivers, core, utils, services, security — which is how most overviews are actually written. The functional grouping cuts 18 of the 28 dependencies against the incidental grouping's 22, and confines 3 of six candidate changes to a single region against 2. Those are differences of degree. The difference in kind is elsewhere: quotient the dependency graph by each grouping and the functional one is acyclic, so a reading order exists and the experiment prints it, while the incidental one has all 5 of its groups in a single strongly connected component. Under that overview there is no order in which the sections can be written such that each uses only what has already been introduced. Every section forward-references, and the reader who starts at the top never reaches solid ground.

This is the useful reframing. An overview is not a picture of the system; it is a claim that a particular set of regions makes the system's behaviour and its likely changes local, and that claim has a decidable consequence about document order. Architecture description standards approach the same point from the other side, by requiring that a description declare the concerns and stakeholders each view addresses, so that a reader can tell what the grouping was chosen to make easy[121],[126],[127].

Two honest caveats. The first is that the decomposition a document presents is almost never the one the system was built by, and the standard advice is to present the rational one anyway, marked as a reconstruction, because the reader needs a structure and the development history is not one[128]. The second is that a written overview is a lossy record of something that lived in the builders' heads, and Naur's argument that a program is the residue of a theory its authors held explains why every overview reads as thinner than the understanding it came from[129],[130].

There is a second-order effect that argues for putting the overview first even when it is thin. Prose that arrives without a frame is very hard to retain: Bransford and Johnson's participants given an unlabelled passage recalled little and understood less, and the same passage with a title supplied first was both comprehensible and memorable[131],[132]. An overview is an advance organiser with a dependency graph attached. Both jobs matter, and the second one is checkable.

M09Component descriptions

09Component descriptionsGROUP III · Taking it apart
Answers
What is this part, and how do I tell it from its neighbours?
Commits to
That the part is identified, located within a stated whole, and distinguished from the parts it could be confused with.
Acceptance test
The reader can find the part on the object, name what it connects to, and order the right replacement.
Presupposes
A system overview that has already fixed the whole, and a designation scheme that survives redesign.
Fails as
A part list that describes each item as if the reader arrived at it directly, with no statement of which whole it belongs to.
evidence [133],[51],[56]

Component descriptions are description with a whole attached. The reader should be able to find the part on the object, name what it connects to, and order the right replacement. That last clause is what separates the mode from plain description: a component description is answerable to an identity scheme, and the scheme has to survive redesign.

The mode's difficulty is that part-of is not one relation. Winston, Chaffin and Herrmann distinguish six — component-integral object, member-collection, portion-mass, stuff-object, feature-activity, place-area — and only some of them are transitive[133]. A document that treats all of them as one inherits a transitivity it cannot support: the gasket is part of the pump, the pump is part of the coolant loop, therefore the gasket is part of the coolant loop reads fine and produces wrong answers about change impact and about who owns the maintenance interval.

Industrial practice solved this by refusing to have a single decomposition at all. IEC 81346 structures a technical system by aspect and gives each its own reference designation: what an object does carries an equals sign, what it is carries a minus, where it sits carries a plus, and the same physical object appears in all three structures under different designations[51]. The point is not notational tidiness. It is that a functional parent and a physical parent are different parents, and a document that has only one parenthood will describe a component correctly under one aspect and misleadingly under the other.

The psychological finding that matters here is that parts and categories are entangled. People decompose objects into parts at the basic level and use the parts to justify the category, so a component vocabulary is quietly doing classification work[56],[63]. This is why a component description that introduces a part name never used elsewhere is worse than it looks: it has created a category with one member and no criterion.

Two practical conventions follow, and both show up in the aerospace specifications. Every component description states its containing assembly before it states anything else, which is what a standard numbering system enforces by making the assembly part of the identifier[96]. And the information code that opens a data module records whether the module describes what the component is made of or what it does — S1000D allocates 041 to descriptions of how a component is made and 042 to descriptions of its function, under a general 040 for descriptions in general[97]. The mode of description is coded as an axis independent of the subject, which is the practice this whole article is arguing for, already implemented and in daily use since long before anyone framed it this way.

M10Functional explanations

10Functional explanationsGROUP III · Taking it apart
Answers
What is this for, and what would be lost without it?
Commits to
That the part contributes a stated capacity to a stated containing system, and that the contribution is the reason the part is there.
Acceptance test
The reader can predict what the system stops being able to do when the part is removed, and can recognise a substitute that would serve.
Presupposes
A containing system whose capacity is already stated, and a description of the part that does not presuppose the function.
Fails as
Function stated as a restatement of behaviour, so the account cannot distinguish what the part does from what it is for.
evidence [134],[135],[136]

A functional explanation says what a part is for. Its acceptance test is a subtraction: the reader can say what the containing system stops being able to do when the part is removed, and can recognise a substitute that would serve. Note the second clause, because it is what distinguishes function from behaviour. Behaviour is what the part does; function is the contribution the part makes to a capacity of the whole, and two components with quite different behaviours can have the same function[134].

Philosophy has two accounts of what makes a function statement true and they disagree about something a technical writer has to decide. On the causal- role account, a function is whatever contribution the part makes to a capacity of the containing system that the analyst has chosen to explain[134]. On the etiological account, a function is what the part is there for — what explains its presence, whether by selection or by design[135],[137]. For engineered artefacts the second is tempting because designers do have intentions, and the literature on the dual nature of technical artefacts is essentially about the difficulty of holding the physical description and the intentional description in one frame[138],[139].

The practical consequence is that a function statement is answerable to a stated containing system, and changing the container changes the truth value. A relief valve's function relative to the pump is to cap discharge pressure; relative to the plant it is to prevent a release; relative to the compliance regime it is to satisfy a code requirement. All three are correct and they license different subtraction tests. Documents that do not name the container produce function statements that cannot be wrong.

Engineering design has done more with this than philosophy, because it had to. The functional basis is a controlled vocabulary of about eight primary function classes and three flow classes — material, energy, signal — assembled so that a design can be described as a function structure before any component is chosen[140],[136],[141]. Function-behaviour-structure frameworks make the same separation explicit and add the observation that these are three different descriptions of one artefact, connected by inference and not by identity[142],[143],[144]. Rasmussen's abstraction hierarchy makes it operational for operators, running from functional purpose down through abstract function and generalised function to physical form, with why-questions answered by moving up and how- questions by moving down[145],[146].

That hierarchy is also the reason this mode sits at I3 and not I4. A function statement licenses exactly one counterfactual — remove the part and the capacity goes — and says nothing about what happens if you halve its setpoint, reverse its orientation or run it at twice the temperature. Those questions need the mechanism. The characteristic failure of the mode is to present the single licensed counterfactual as though it were a general one, which produces documents whose readers can recite what everything is for and cannot predict what any of it will do.

M11Conceptual explanations

11Conceptual explanationsGROUP IV · Reaching the reader
Answers
What is the idea here, and where else does it hold?
Commits to
That there is a principle the particular case instantiates, and that the principle has a stated scope.
Acceptance test
The reader meets a system this document never described and correctly says whether the principle applies to it.
Presupposes
At least one worked case, and an audience with somewhere to attach the principle.
Fails as
A restatement at a higher pitch of abstraction with no scope condition, which no instance can fail to satisfy.
evidence [147],[132],[148]

Conceptual explanation is the mode with the highest promise and the least apparatus. It asserts that there is a principle the particular case instantiates, and that the principle has a scope. Its acceptance test is transfer: the reader meets a system this document never described and correctly says whether the principle applies. Nothing weaker will do, because a principle that never has to decide a new case is doing no work.

The documentation frameworks have converged on giving this mode its own place rather than letting it leak into the others. Diátaxis separates explanation from tutorial, how-to and reference on the grounds that they serve different reader states, and its central claim is that the failure mode is mixing them on one page[148]. Information Mapping made the same move a generation earlier by typing every block of content — concept, procedure, process, structure, fact, classification — and forbidding blocks of different types to run together[149],[150]. DITA turned the typing into schemas, with concept, task and reference as distinct topic types with distinct content models; version 1.3 remains the current OASIS standard while 2.0 has been in draft for years[151],[152]. ISO/IEC/IEEE 26514 carries an equivalent list in its information architecture: conceptual, instructional, reference, troubleshooting[44].

Four independent traditions arriving at nearly the same partition is evidence that the partition is real. It also raises the obvious question of why the boundary needs enforcing at all, and the answer is in the cognitive literature: conceptual material and procedural material compete for the same working memory during a task, so an explanation embedded in a procedure is not neutral, it is a cost paid at the worst possible moment[153],[154],[155].

Whether the explanation is worth that cost is one of the better-studied questions in the area, and the answer is conditional. Kieras and Bovair gave one group a rote procedure for operating a control panel and another group a model of how the device worked; the model group learned faster, retained better, and — the part that matters — could simplify and repair procedures the rote group could only execute[9]. Smith and Goodman found the same for written instructions with an explanatory schema[10]. Mayer's review of model-based instruction reports the effect concentrated on transfer problems rather than on retention of the taught material itself[147]. And the effect reverses with expertise: material that helps a novice reliably slows an expert, because the expert must integrate the explanation with a schema they already have[156].

There is one lever that costs the writer almost nothing, which is to make the reader produce the explanation instead. Prompting learners to explain worked material to themselves improves understanding substantially over reading it twice, and the gain shows up on exactly the transfer items that define this rung[157]. Instructional-design frameworks build this in as a stage rather than an option — present the generality, present the instance, then require the learner to generate[158],[159].

The characteristic failure of the mode is the scopeless principle. A sentence pitched one level above the case, containing no condition that any instance could fail, feels like an explanation and licenses nothing. The system is designed for resilience is such a sentence. The repair is not more words; it is a scope condition, which is the only part of a conceptual explanation the reader can ever test.

M12Examples and counterexamples

12Examples and counterexamplesGROUP IV · Reaching the reader
Answers
What counts as one of these, and what does not quite count?
Commits to
That the cases shown are on the sides the text says they are on, and that the near cases are near in the dimension the writer means.
Acceptance test
The reader can classify a case that lies between the ones shown, and can say which feature decided it.
Presupposes
A concept boundary the writer has already fixed, even if only implicitly.
Fails as
Only positive cases, which cannot exclude any hypothesis more general than the one intended.
evidence [160],[161],[162]

Examples and counterexamples do a job no amount of prose does: they fix a boundary. The reader who has seen enough cases can classify one that lies between them and say which feature decided it. The mode's rung is I2 — sorting — and its interest lies in the fact that its efficiency is exactly computable.

The framework is Mitchell's: treat the set of hypotheses consistent with the examples so far as a version space, and watch it shrink[161],[163]. Experiment L7 runs this over a small conjunctive concept space — 256 hypotheses over 81 objects, with a target the writer has in mind — and produces the structural result first, because it is exact rather than statistical. There are 4 hypotheses consistent with every positive instance of the target simultaneously. It follows that a text showing only correct cases cannot get the reader below 4 candidate concepts no matter how many cases it shows, and the simulation confirms it: after sixteen positives the space is still sitting at exactly 4.

The comparison is where the practical advice lives. Starting each regime with one correct instance and then continuing: more positives settle the concept in 0.0% of runs, random negatives in 0.0%, a random mixture of instances in 53.3% after a mean of 10.10 examples, and alternating a positive with a near miss — a negative that differs from a positive in exactly one attribute — in 98.9% after 6.64. The near-miss regime reaches a version space of about one by the eighth example while the random mixture is still above four.

Winston's near-miss method is fifty years old and this is what it was for: a negative example one feature away from a positive identifies which feature is doing the work, and no positive example can[160]. The learning- theory version is that queries chosen by a teacher are dramatically more efficient than samples drawn at random[164],[165]. The mathematician's version is Lakatos, whose whole dialogue is an argument that concepts are formed by the pressure of counterexamples rather than by definition[166],[167].

Two cautions. First, worked examples are a different device from boundary examples and obey different rules: they reduce load for novices, and the benefit shrinks and then reverses as expertise grows[168],[162],[169],[156]. Second, a counterexample only teaches if the reader can see which dimension it varies. A near miss presented without the contrast made explicit is just another negative, and the version-space collapse in the experiment depends entirely on the near miss being near in the dimension the writer meant.

M13Analogies

13AnalogiesGROUP IV · Reaching the reader
Answers
What is this like, and how far does the likeness run?

That a stated set of relations in a familiar source holds, with stated substitutions, in the unfamiliar target.
Acceptance test
The reader can use the source to answer a target question the text never asked, and can name at least one inference the source licenses that the target refuses.
Presupposes
Genuine familiarity with the source, and an explicit object correspondence.
Fails as

evidence [170],[171],[172]

An analogy asserts that a set of relations holding in a familiar source holds, under a stated substitution, in an unfamiliar target. Structure-mapping theory is precise about what gets carried: relations rather than attributes, and systems of interconnected relations in preference to isolated ones[170],[173],[174]. The acceptance test has two halves and documents almost never state the second. The reader should be able to use the source to answer a target question the text never asked — and should be able to name at least one inference the source licenses that the target refuses.

Experiment L8 audits the most-used analogy in technical explanation. Encode the hydraulic model of a DC circuit as 14 relations over pressure, flow, pipes, pumps, tanks and breaks; encode an object correspondence; then write down independently what is true of the electrical target. Of the 14 relations whose arguments map, 8 are true of the target and 6 are false — a transfer rate of 57.1% and a leak rate of 42.9%. The leaks are not exotic. Charge escaping from a break in a wire; charge as a substance with quantity and location; charge pooling at the bottom; a conductor that can be emptied; current wearing out along its path; current consumed by the resistor. Every one of them is a sentence a reader can form from the analogy alone, and every one is a documented misconception in physics education[171],[172].

The important part of that number is not its size but its invisibility. The 8 true relations and the 6 false ones arrive with the same warrant, in the same sentence, from the same source. Nothing in the analogy marks the boundary, because an analogy is precisely a device for transferring structure without first checking it. Gentner and Gentner's finding sharpens this: readers who use a flowing-fluid model and readers who use a moving-crowd model make systematically different errors on the same circuit problems, each model failing where its own structure misleads[171].

The literature's repair is to state the disanalogy. Explicitly mapping the correspondences and then explicitly listing where the mapping stops is what distinguishes a teaching analogy from a decorative one, and the models built for classroom use — teaching-with-analogies, bridging analogies — all include a step for it[175],[176],[172]. It is the cheapest high-value sentence in technical explanation and it is almost always missing.

There is a deeper reason it is missing, which is that the leak audit costs exactly what the analogy was substituting for. To list the disanalogies you must already have the target theory. A writer who has it can check the analogy; a reader who has it does not need the analogy. That gap is the mode's permanent condition, not a fixable defect, and it is why analogy sits at the top of the ladder and the bottom of the checkability scale. The same observation applies to the metaphors that have become invisible through use — memory, gate, handshake, inheritance — where the mapping was never stated and so the disanalogies cannot be[177],[178].

M14Levels of abstraction

14Levels of abstractionGROUP V · Choosing the grain
Answers
At what grain is this account true, and where does that grain fail?
Commits to
That the account holds fixed a stated set of variables, and that questions about the suppressed variables are out of its scope.
Acceptance test
The reader can say which questions this level cannot answer, and can name the level to descend to for each of them.
Presupposes
At least two levels that make contact, and a way of checking one against the other.
Fails as
A level presented without its scope condition, which is exact until it is not, with nothing in the text marking the transition.
evidence [179],[8],[180]

The last mode is not a mode. It is the choice that governs the other thirteen: which variables the account holds fixed, and which it suppresses. A level of abstraction is an explicit commitment to a set of observables and to the questions those observables can answer[179]. Marr's three levels made the point for information-processing systems — computational, algorithmic, implementational, each answering questions the others cannot[8] — and Newell's knowledge level made it for agents[181]. The engineering tradition arrived independently at the same commitment through abstract data types: the representation is hidden, the operations are the interface, and the proof obligation is that the abstraction relation holds[182],[183],[184],[185].

The programming tradition also supplies the vocabulary technical writers borrow without noticing: an abstraction barrier is a promise about which details the code above it may depend on, and the discipline of stating that promise is what makes the barrier real rather than decorative[186].

The acceptance test at this level is unusual because it is about the text rather than the subject. The reader should be able to say which questions this account cannot answer, and name the level to descend to for each. That is the only mode in the fourteen whose test is a statement about scope, and it is the one documentation most reliably omits.

Experiment L9 measures what omission costs, on a write path with four levels. The file level says a record of R bytes costs R bytes. The block level says the device writes whole pages. The flash-translation level adds garbage collection at the current utilisation. The device level is a simulated page-mapped controller with greedy collection, and it is the ground truth[187],[188]. Across twelve workloads the file level is exact on 2 of them and wrong by more than a tenth on 9; its worst case is a 512-byte random write on a device 95% full, where the true cost is 2.27 pages against a predicted 0.125 — a factor of 18.2.

Two things about that number matter more than its size. First, the file level does not degrade gracefully into wrongness. It is exact on every sequential workload at every fill level and then wrong by more than an order of magnitude, with nothing in the model marking the transition. This is what Spolsky named the law of leaky abstractions and it is a structural property, not a quality defect: the level is correct until the suppressed variable starts to matter[180],[189].

Second, and less expected, the level with the most detail has the largest mean error. The flash-translation model is off by a mean of 74.5% against the block model's 16.8% and the file model's 53.2%, and it is 285% high on skewed workloads. The reason is instructive: it is the only one of the three making a quantitative claim about something it cannot observe — the shape of the workload. A more detailed account is not a more accurate one. It is an account that has taken on more commitments, each of which can be wrong[190],[191]. The information-theoretic reading is the same one that lets experiment L5 price a process description in bits: a description is a code, and a code that resolves more possibilities is longer and more exposed[192].

For a writer the operational advice is short. Say what the level holds fixed. Say what it therefore cannot answer. Give the reader the name of the next level down. That is roughly what the elaboration tradition prescribes as a sequencing strategy — start at the level that is whole and simple, then refine — with the addition that each level should carry its own scope condition rather than waiting for the next section to supply it[193],[194],[195].

The lab

Nine experiments, standard library only, deterministic, run at build time, output embedded verbatim below. Every number quoted in the sections above and in the figures comes from this run. The point of the lab is not to simulate technical writing, which would be silly. It is that several of the claims made about these modes are claims about structures — dependency graphs, partitions, hypothesis spaces, weight simplices, causal models — and structures can be measured rather than asserted.

Two experiments were rebuilt after their first run and the rebuilds are disclosed here rather than buried. L1's first version tried to induce a circular definition by redefining friction in terms of brake; the graph turned out not to close, the audit reported no change, and the experiment was measuring nothing. The published version redefines retardation, which does close the loop. L7's first version gave each teaching regime only examples of its own kind, so the negative-only and near-miss-only regimes could never collapse a version space at all and scored zero by construction — an artefact of the setup, not a finding. The published version starts every regime with the same opening move, one correct instance, which is what any real document does, and then varies what comes next.

Nothing in the lab is exotic. The heaviest machinery is a strongly-connected-components pass over a seven-node quotient graph[196]; everything else is enumeration.

L3 also produced a null result that is reported as a null: under equal weights, four different normalisation schemes agree on the winner. The disagreement lives entirely in the weighting.

Fourteen plates, sorted by the rung they reach unaided. The checkability column records what it would take to find out whether a claim in that mode is wrong: C3 a program can audit the text itself; C2 an inspector can compare the text against the object; C1 only a reader can tell, by attempting the acceptance test; C0 only a later failure will.

What the ledger says

The counts are the argument. Of the fourteen modes, 5 can be audited by a program operating on the text alone, 5 require access to the object, and 4 can only be checked by running a reader. Every one of the C3 modes sits at rung I2 or below. Every mode at rung I4 or above is C2 or C1. Across the fourteen plates the correlation between reach and checkability is -0.67 — negative, and not weakly.

That is not a coincidence and it is not a defect of the trade. The modes that reach highest are exactly the modes whose acceptance tests quantify over cases the document does not contain. A definition can be audited because its promise is about a vocabulary that is entirely present in the text. A classification can be audited because exclusivity and exhaustiveness are properties of stated criteria over a stated domain. An analogy cannot be audited without the target theory, and a conceptual explanation cannot be audited without a reader who has somewhere to attach it. The property that makes a mode powerful — that it licenses inferences about what the text does not say — is the same property that puts its correctness out of the text's reach.

Three consequences follow for practice, and they are not the ones the style guides emphasise.

Enforce what can be enforced, and stop pretending the rest is enforced. Terminology closure, single-criterion division, stated comparison bases, cited contrast cases, stated abstraction scope: all mechanically checkable, none checked in most pipelines. Controlled language and structured authoring already automate the vocabulary half[49],[151],[150]. Nothing stops a build from failing on a circular definition, an overlapping taxonomy or a comparison table with no stated weighting, and the experiments above are, among other things, demonstrations that these checks are cheap.

For the modes that cannot be enforced, buy the check. Reader testing is the only acceptance test that exists for the top of the ladder, which is why the standards for reviewing information for users are built around task-based evaluation rather than inspection[5],[197],[198]. The minimalist tradition went further and made the reader's attempt the design method rather than the validation step[199],[200]. A document whose central claims are conceptual and analogical and which has never been put in front of a reader has not been reviewed. It has been proofread.

Name the mode. The single cheapest improvement available is to make the mode explicit — in the heading, in the topic type, in the information code. S1000D has done this since long before the frameworks caught up, coding descriptions of construction and descriptions of function as different information types on an axis independent of the subject[97],[96]. DITA does it with topic types[151]. Diátaxis does it with four quadrants and one rule about not mixing them[148]. All three are, in the terms of this article, ways of declaring an acceptance test at the top of the page so that the reader knows which one to run.

A reader from rhetoric will notice that this whole argument is a claim about genre. The modes are recurrent forms that arose because they answer recurrent situations, which is what a genre is, and the reason the fourteen are stable across textbooks is that the situations are[201],[202]. That tradition has been arguing for decades that technical writing is not the neutral transmission of content but a set of moves with social consequences[203],[204], and the acceptance-test framing is one way of making the consequence concrete: the mode you choose determines what your reader will be able to do, and choosing it silently does not make the choice go away. It is also why the newest of the standards keep moving in this direction — the plain-language series has extended into science writing, and the documentation standards have added guidance for teams shipping continuously, both of which are attempts to keep the acceptance test attached to the text as it changes[205],[206].

There is a version of this argument that overreaches, and it is worth marking the boundary. Nothing here shows that the high modes are less valuable than the low ones. The evidence points the other way: models beat rote procedures on transfer, subgoal labels beat undifferentiated steps, analogies do real work for readers who have nothing else to attach the material to[9],[87],[147]. The claim is narrower. It is that the value and the verifiability of these modes come apart, and that a profession which measures what it can measure will drift toward the bottom of the ladder without ever deciding to. The fourteen modes in every handbook's list are not fourteen styles. They are fourteen different promises about what the reader will be able to do, and only five of them can be checked without asking.
