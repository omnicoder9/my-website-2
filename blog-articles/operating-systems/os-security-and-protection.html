1  The question

An operating system is asked to keep promises about a machine that is mostly running other people's code. The standard way of teaching what it does about this is a list: goals, then models, then mechanisms, in roughly the order they were invented. The list is accurate and it hides the only question that decides whether any item on it will help you. Every protection mechanism buys a property, and every one of them buys that property on condition that something else is intact. The mechanisms differ enormously in what that something else is.

Address space layout randomisation and secure boot are both described as security features and they are not the same kind of object. Randomisation is void the moment the protected program discloses one pointer — a condition about the application itself, the thing the mechanism was supposed to be protecting you from. Secure boot is void only if the firmware or its key hierarchy is subverted; it survives total compromise of the kernel, of every daemon, and of every application on the machine. Both are true statements about a computer. Only one of them is a statement you can still rely on after you have lost.

So the question this article asks of every mechanism is: what has to be right for this to hold? Specifically, what is the least privileged component whose compromise, on its own, voids the guarantee as stated. That component is the mechanism's weak link, and naming it turns a flat vocabulary into something with a shape. It also reorders the syllabus in a way that is uncomfortable: the mechanisms taught first and mentioned most — file permissions, stack canaries, randomised layouts — sit at the very shallowest level, and the ones that survive deepest make the narrowest claims.

Thirty-two mechanisms are worked through below, each on a fixed set of five fields, each placed on a six-level reliance ladder, each carrying a small gauge that draws its dependency as a span resting on piers. Eight experiments, run at build time on this machine against the Linux kernel's own source of truth, supply every number quoted. The article's own claim about itself is in the record block at the top: a measurement, not an assurance. The standard treatments this reorders rather than replaces are Anderson's survey, Bishop's and Gollmann's textbooks, Jaeger's monograph on operating system security specifically, and the operating-systems texts that carry the syllabus this article follows.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6

2  What the goals actually promise

Confidentiality, integrity and availability are not mechanisms and they are not requirements either. They are predicates over states of a system, and the interesting content of each is in the quantifier that a textbook usually leaves out. Confidentiality is not "information is secret"; it is that no unauthorised subject learns the information, which commits you to a definition of learning that has to cope with inference, aggregation and the timing of a cache line. Integrity is not "data is correct"; a correctness-preserving system and a modification-preventing system are different things, and Clark and Wilson's 1987 paper is essentially the observation that commercial practice wants the first while the military models deliver the second.7, 8, 9, 10

Availability is the odd one. It is stated with the other two in every introduction and then almost nothing in the protection vocabulary is about it.11, 12 Of the thirty-two mechanisms below, four carry an availability claim, and in all four it is a side effect of an isolation boundary that was drawn for confidentiality or integrity: a container's control group, a hypervisor's memory assignment, the page table, and general attack-surface reduction. Nothing in the list exists in order to keep a system answering. The mechanisms that do — admission control, quotas, replication, rate limiting, backpressure — are taught under scheduling and reliability, and nobody files them under protection. That is a fact about the field's history rather than about the goal, and it is worth stating plainly before spending eleven sections on the other two. It is also visible in the control catalogues: the families in NIST's control set that bear on availability are contingency planning and incident response, neither of which is a mechanism a kernel implements.13

Two further goals are usually smuggled in. Authenticity is the property that a claimed origin is the real one, which is integrity applied to the binding between a message and a principal rather than to the message. Accountability is the property that an action can afterwards be attributed to a principal, which is the goal that audit, attestation and non-repudiation serve and which no preventive mechanism serves at all.9, 1, 2 Keeping accountability separate matters here, because it is the only goal in the article whose mechanisms are honest about not stopping anything.

Authentication and authorisation are frequently run together and they are two different questions asked of two different objects. Authentication asks whether a claimant is the principal they claim to be, and answers with evidence. Authorisation asks whether a named principal may perform a named operation on a named object, and answers from a policy. The clean statement is Lampson's access matrix: rows are subjects, columns are objects, and the cell holds the rights. Everything in section 6 below is a way of storing that matrix without writing it down — by column, which is an access control list; by row, which is a capability list; or by a rule that computes the cell on demand.14, 15, 16

One result from that period deserves to be quoted more often than it is. Harrison, Ruzzo and Ullman showed in 1976 that for a general discretionary protection system the question can this right ever leak to this subject? is undecidable.17 Not expensive; undecidable. It becomes decidable only under restrictions that real systems do not satisfy. Every access control model in use is therefore a decision about which safety question to make answerable by refusing to ask the general one, and the differences between the models in section 6 are best read that way.

3  Where the vocabulary came from

Multics, begun in 1965, is where most of this article's furniture was built.18 It had segmentation with per-segment access, a hardware ring structure with eight protection levels and gates for controlled transfer between them, access control lists on every segment, and an explicit model of principals.19 A modern kernel uses two of those eight rings and has otherwise not improved on the design. What Multics did not have was an implementation that matched it, which is the point of the 1974 evaluation Karger and Schell ran against it for the Air Force: the flaws they found were in the code and the configuration and not in the architecture, and they included a trap door planted in the compiler that survived recompilation.20, 21, 22

Anderson's 1972 planning study gave the field the reference monitor and the three conditions it must meet: it must mediate every access, it must be tamper-proof, and it must be small enough to be verified.23 Half a century of operating system security is best read as a series of retreats from the third condition. Saltzer and Schroeder's 1975 survey then stated the eight design principles — economy of mechanism, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation, open design, separation of privilege, least privilege, least common mechanism, psychological acceptability — of which the sixth is now quoted constantly and the first almost never, although the first is the one the others depend on.16

The formal models arrived alongside. Bell and LaPadula gave confidentiality a lattice with no read up and no write down; Biba gave integrity its dual; Denning showed that any such policy is a lattice; Lampson had already observed in 1973 that a program confined by such a policy can still signal through the resources it shares with everything else.24, 25, 26, 27 The Orange Book of 1983 turned the models into evaluation classes and, in doing so, fixed the word mandatory in its current sense: a policy the owner of the data cannot relax.11 Its successor, the Common Criteria, dropped the fixed classes in favour of a security target the vendor writes, which made evaluation possible for products that were not military operating systems and made comparison between evaluations much harder.28, 29

Two lines then diverge. One is the policy line: type enforcement in 1985, domain and type enforcement for UNIX in 1995, the Flask architecture in 1999 separating the policy decision from the enforcement mechanism, and SELinux and the Linux Security Modules interface in 2001 and 2002.30, 31, 32, 33, 34 The other is the exploitation line, which begins in public with the Morris worm in 1988 and with the 1996 Phrack article that taught a generation how a stack frame is laid out.35, 36 Everything in section 8 — canaries, non-executable pages, randomised layouts, control-flow integrity — is a response along that second line, and the striking feature of its history is how short the interval is between a mitigation shipping and its first general bypass.

The third line is newer and is the reason the ladder below has six rungs rather than three. Starting in the late 2000s the trust anchor moved off the processor and into a separate device: a Trusted Platform Module holding measurements, firmware that verifies signatures before any operating system runs, a security processor holding disk keys. That is a genuine improvement — it is the only way any of the plates in section 9 can survive the kernel — and it introduced a new class of dependency that the older vocabulary has no words for, on a component most owners of a machine cannot inspect, update, or replace.

4  The reliance ladder

A running system is a stack of components, each of which can rewrite the state the one above it depends on. The ladder below orders six of them by privilege, deepest first, and a plate's rung is the least privileged of these whose compromise, on its own, voids the guarantee as the mechanism states it.

Three properties of the rule are worth stating because they are what keep it from being an opinion. First, it is about sufficiency, not necessity: everything deeper than the rung must also be intact, which is why the gauge draws every pier from the rung leftward as solid. The rung is the shallowest thing that has to be, not the only thing. Second, it is assigned against the guarantee as stated, not against the property people hope for. A non-executable page really does mean the processor will not execute that page; that the attacker did not need new instructions is a separate observation, and it goes in the plate's fails as field rather than changing the rung. Third, defeating the mechanism by a route that is not a compromise of any component — a design bypass, a covert channel, a confused deputy asked politely — is a failure mode and not a rung.

Cutting across the ladder is a second, independent property: what the guarantee does when it holds. Enforced means a check must pass and cannot be passed without authorisation. Raised cost means the barrier is statistical — entropy, a work factor, a reduced surface — and can in principle be crossed by an attacker willing to pay. Recorded means the mechanism prevents nothing at all and produces evidence. These are orthogonal to depth: measured boot is recorded at rung 4, auditing is recorded at rung 1, and a stack canary is raised cost at rung 0.

The five fields on each plate are fixed. Guarantees is the property, phrased the way the mechanism's own documentation would phrase it. Enforced by names the component that performs the check. Voided by names the rung. Survives names what the guarantee holds through, which is the field that most often surprises. Fails as names the characteristic way the mechanism disappoints when nothing at all has been compromised.

5  Naming the principal

Access control cannot start until something has decided who is asking, and the decision is made in a part of the system that most treatments skip over. On Linux and every other Unix, the kernel's answer to "who is this process" is a 32-bit integer. It has no opinion about usernames, groups by name, home directories or password policy; all of that is a convention maintained by user-space libraries reading files and directory services. The consequence is that the entire authentication apparatus sits at rung 1 and not at rung 2.37, 38

The plates in this group therefore share a shape. Each one improves what an attacker must steal — a hash rather than a password, a hardware-held key rather than a hash, a short-lived ticket rather than a long-lived secret — and none of them moves the decision. The verifying process sees the credential and reaches the verdict, and if it is wrong the guarantee is gone regardless of how good the cryptography beneath it was.

P01

User and group identities

integrityaccountabilityrung L1 · enforced
guarantees
Every request carries a principal the kernel can name, and that name is fixed for the life of the process.
enforced by
The kernel's credential structure. The number-to-name mapping is not the kernel's; it lives in files and directory services above it.
voided by
Any privileged user-space component that writes the mapping: the password file, the name service switch, the directory server behind it.
survives
Compromise of an unprivileged application, which cannot change the identity it was given.
fails as
Collision and reuse. Two names for user 0; a deleted account whose number is recycled to a new person who inherits the old one's files.

The kernel does not know your username. It knows a 32-bit integer, and everything above that is a convention maintained in user space. A writable name service configuration is therefore not a weak authentication policy, it is an absent one.37, 38, 5

P02

Password verification and the work factor

confidentialityaccountabilityrung L1 · raised cost
guarantees
A claimant who does not know the secret cannot present it, and an attacker holding the stored verifier pays a tunable cost per guess.
enforced by
The verifying process and its password-hashing function.
voided by
The process that verifies, which necessarily handles the plaintext, and the file it reads.
survives
Theft of the verifier file on its own, for as long as the work factor outruns the attacker's hardware.
fails as
Offline cracking at a rate the defender never measured. The work factor is set once and the attacker's hardware is not.

Morris and Thompson introduced both the salt and a deliberately slow hash in 1979, and set out the arithmetic that still governs: the cost per guess is a constant multiplier and the size of the secret is an exponent. E5 measures both on this machine and finds that 600,000 PBKDF2 iterations are worth 19.19 bits, which four random lower-case characters very nearly match at no cost to the verifier.39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46

P03

Possession-based and multi-factor authentication

confidentialityaccountabilityrung L1 · enforced
guarantees
The claimant demonstrates control of a private key that never leaves the authenticator, bound to the relying party's identity.
enforced by
The authenticator hardware, plus the relying party's verification of the signature it returns.
voided by
The relying party. The key is safe; the decision that the signature was acceptable is an ordinary piece of software.
survives
Theft of the verifier database, and phishing of a shared secret, since there is no shared secret to phish.
fails as
Attacks that skip the authentication entirely and target the session it establishes.

This plate sits at the same rung as the password it replaces. Moving the secret into hardware changes what an attacker must steal; it does not change who decides that the proof was good.47, 48, 49, 44

P04

Pluggable authentication stacks

accountabilityrung L1 · enforced
guarantees
The policy about who may log in is written once and applied identically by every service that authenticates.
enforced by
A library loaded into each service's own address space, running with that service's authority.
voided by
Any module in the stack. Each one sees the credential and each one runs as the service.
survives
Nothing below it. A stack is a conjunction of trusted components, not a defence in depth.
fails as
Control flags. A module marked optional that has silently stopped working is indistinguishable, from outside, from one that is passing.

The abstraction was designed so that adding an authentication method would not require recompiling every service. The security consequence is that every service now imports the union of every method's defects.50

P05

Session credentials and caches

confidentialityaccountabilityrung L0 · enforced
guarantees
After one successful authentication, later requests carry a token that stands in for the principal without re-presenting the secret.
enforced by
The service that issued the token and checks it on each use.
voided by
The application holding it. A bearer credential is readable by whatever can read the process that holds it.
survives
Nothing. Portability across requests is the feature.
fails as
Theft and replay. The credential outlives the authentication event that justified issuing it, and nothing in the protocol notices.

Kerberos was designed in the knowledge that a ticket is a secret in the client's hands, which is why tickets expire. Expiry bounds the damage; it does not move the rung.51, 52, 53

Two of the plates deserve a remark the fields do not have room for. The password work factor is the oldest deliberate cost function in computer security, introduced with the salt by Morris and Thompson in 1979 for exactly the reason it is used now, and it is routinely mis-sold. Experiment 5 measures it on this machine: 600,000 PBKDF2 iterations cost 174.07 ms per verification, are worth 19.19 bits of difficulty, and are very nearly equalled by four extra random lower-case characters that cost the verifier nothing.39, 45 The work factor is a constant multiplier against something the attacker must search exponentially. It is worth having and it is not where the leverage is. The complementary finding from the empirical side is that users hold a small number of secrets across a large number of accounts and that the rational response to a password policy is reuse, which is why the verifier's work factor and the user's entropy have never been independent variables.54, 55, 49

The second remark is about what NIST's 2025 revision of its authentication guideline actually changed. It withdrew composition rules and mandatory periodic rotation, required verifiers to screen against known-compromised secrets, and reoriented the requirements around length and around authenticator types that do not produce a shared secret at all.44 Read through this article's lens, that is a coherent move: the guidance stopped trying to make the rung-1 verifier stronger and started trying to remove the class of credential whose theft the rung-1 verifier cannot prevent.

6  Writing the rule

The seven plates here are the article's clearest illustration that a difference in expressiveness is not a difference in reliance. A permission word and a compiled SELinux policy both end in a kernel check. What separates them is who may change the rule, and that is what the ladder measures.

P06

Discretionary access control

confidentialityintegrityrung L0 · enforced
guarantees
An attempt to open an object is checked against the object's owner, group and permission word before it can succeed.
enforced by
The kernel's inode permission check, on every path resolution.
voided by
Any process running as the object's owner. The owner may relax the mode at will, so the rule is only as strong as every program that user runs.
survives
Compromise of a different user's processes.
fails as
Delegation. The model can say who may read a file and has no way to say that the reader may not pass it on.

Harrison, Ruzzo and Ullman proved in 1976 that for a general discretionary system, the question of whether a given right can ever leak to a given subject is undecidable. The permission word is not a weak answer to that question; it declines to ask it. E3's census of this machine finds 98,617 of 98,633 files readable by every local account, in an image nobody misconfigured.37, 14, 15, 17, 38

P07

Access control lists

confidentialityintegrityrung L0 · enforced
guarantees
The rule can name individual principals and groups rather than only owner, group and other.
enforced by
The kernel, reading an extended attribute or security descriptor.
voided by
The owner, exactly as above, plus whoever holds the right to change the list, which the list itself confers.
survives
Nothing the owner does not survive.
fails as
Accumulation. Lists grow by exception and nothing prunes them, so the effective rule becomes a thing no one can state.

Access control lists solve expressiveness and not review. Saltzer and Schroeder's economy of mechanism is a requirement about what a human can check, and a descriptor with forty entries and inheritance fails it whatever the kernel does with it.56, 57, 16

P08

Users, groups and role-based access control

confidentialityintegrityaccountabilityrung L1 · enforced
guarantees
Permissions attach to roles rather than to people, and a principal exercises only the roles currently activated.
enforced by
A policy decision point, in practice a privileged service that owns the role definitions.
voided by
The administration interface. A role is a name for a set of permissions, and whoever edits the set writes the policy.
survives
Application compromise. A program cannot grant itself a role it does not hold.
fails as
Role explosion, and creep in the set behind a stable name. The audit reads the same in year five and means something else.

The 1992 formulation was explicit that roles are an administrative convenience layered over an access matrix, not a new kind of enforcement. That is why this plate sits one rung deeper than discretionary control: the rule has been moved out of the user's hands, and into somebody else's.58, 59, 60, 61

P09

Mandatory access control and lattice models

confidentialityintegrityrung L2 · enforced
guarantees
No subject may move information from a higher label to a lower one, whatever the owner of the data wants.
enforced by
A reference monitor that mediates every access and cannot be bypassed.
voided by
The kernel implementing the monitor. There is nothing else left.
survives
Any amount of misbehaviour by the owning user and their programs. That is what the word mandatory is doing.
fails as
Covert channels. The lattice constrains the objects and not the timing, the cache, the disk arm or the error code.

Anderson's 1972 report names the three properties a reference monitor must have -- complete mediation, tamper-proofing, and being small enough to verify -- and every mechanism in this section is a partial answer to the third. Bell and LaPadula give confidentiality, Biba gives its dual for integrity, and Clark and Wilson observe that commercial integrity is about well-formed transactions rather than about a lattice at all.24, 25, 26, 27, 23, 11, 7, 62

P10

Type enforcement and SELinux

confidentialityintegrityrung L2 · enforced
guarantees
Every subject and object carries a type, and the permitted combinations of subject type, object type, class and permission are enumerated in advance.
enforced by
Security-module hooks in the kernel, checked against a compiled policy and a decision cache.
voided by
The kernel. A write that reaches the page cache or the block layer without passing a hook is outside the policy's reach entirely.
survives
Root in user space. A compromised privileged process is still confined to the domain it was launched in.
fails as
Unbounded policy. The kernel's own class map declares 97 object classes and 242 permission names; policy at that granularity is generated by tooling and reviewed by nobody.

Type enforcement separates the policy from the mechanism, which is Flask's contribution and the reason the Linux Security Modules interface exists at all. The limit of the approach was demonstrated in July 2026 by a cross-device-link race in XFS that overwrote protected files at the block layer on hosts running SELinux in enforcing mode: the hooks were intact and the write never went past one.30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 63, 64, 65

P11

Path-based confinement and AppArmor

confidentialityintegrityrung L2 · enforced
guarantees
A named executable is confined to an enumerated set of paths, capabilities and network operations.
enforced by
The same security-module hooks, against a per-executable profile written in terms of file names.
voided by
The kernel, and specifically anything that changes what a name resolves to.
survives
Root in user space, within the profile's coverage.
fails as
Aliasing. The subject of the rule is a string and not an object, so bind mounts, hard links and a private mount namespace are all ways of making the same inode answer to a different name.

The path-versus-label argument is the oldest live disagreement in Linux access control, and both sides are describing the same trade. A label is attached to the object and survives renaming, at the cost of having to be assigned to every object. A path is legible without tooling, at the cost of being a claim about the namespace rather than about the thing.66, 67, 34, 68, 69

P12

Capabilities as divided root

integrityrung L1 · enforced
guarantees
A privileged program holds a named subset of the superuser's powers rather than all of them at once.
enforced by
Per-capability checks scattered through the kernel.
voided by
Whoever composes the set -- and, more often, by the arithmetic, since several of the names are the superuser by a short route.
survives
The application, once the bounding set is fixed and new privileges are refused on exec.
fails as
A count mistaken for a measure. E4 finds 19 of this kernel's 41 capabilities reach full root through a documented step or short chain, and that the conventional container set drops 66% of the names while keeping 47% of the root-reachable ones.

These are not capabilities in the sense Dennis and Van Horn meant. An object capability is an unforgeable reference that carries authority to one thing; a Linux capability is a bit in a mask that switches off one class of check. The vocabulary collision has cost the field thirty years of confusion.70, 71, 72, 73, 74

The discretionary plates sit at rung 0 for a reason that is easy to state and easy to forget: the owner may relax the rule, and every program the owner runs is the owner. A web browser running as you can change the permissions on your files. That is not a defect in the implementation, it is the definition of discretionary, and it is why the census in experiment 3 is worth looking at. On this container's system hierarchies, deduplicated by inode, 98,617 of 98,633 regular files are readable by every local account, and the model's 4,096 expressible permission words are represented by 14 distinct values. Two of those values cover 99.5% of everything. The expressiveness is not being used, because the thing being expressed is a default.

Mandatory access control moves the rule out of the owner's hands and into the kernel's, which buys exactly one rung and it is the most valuable rung purchase in the article: a compromised privileged process remains confined. What it does not buy is completeness. In July 2026 a race in XFS's copy-on-write path was shown to overwrite protected files at the block layer on default enterprise installations running SELinux in enforcing mode, stripping the root account's password protection and persisting across reboots.65 The policy was not bypassed. The write never passed a hook, because it never went through the layer the hooks are attached to. That is precisely what rung 2 means, and the same reading explains three other Linux escalations disclosed in the same quarter, all of which reached their goal by writing into the page cache and therefore never consulted a file permission.75, 76, 77

Capabilities are the plate where counting misleads most, and experiment 4 quantifies it. Parsing this kernel's own header gives 41 capabilities. Coding the documented escalation steps between them — loading a module, writing to raw device memory, attaching to a privileged process, writing the shadow file, setting file capabilities on a binary — and taking the transitive closure gives 19 of the 41 that reach the full set again, seven of them in a single step. The conventional container default keeps 14 capabilities, which reads as having dropped 66% of them, and 9 of the 14 are in the root-reachable set: 47% of the authority that matters, retained. The caveat matters and is stated in the lab: in a real container those capabilities sit inside a user namespace, a seccomp filter and a mandatory policy, each of which cuts specific edges in that graph. The point is not that containers are insecure. It is that the number of capabilities dropped is not a measurement of anything.

7  Splitting the program

If the rule cannot be made tight, the other move is to make a successful attack worth less. This group contains the article's only rung 3 plate and, more interestingly, the transition point on the ladder: three plates at rung 0 that depend entirely on the program's own discipline, followed by four whose guarantee survives the complete compromise of the thing they constrain.

P13

Least privilege

confidentialityintegrityrung L0 · enforced
guarantees
At each moment a program holds only the authority the operation in front of it requires.
enforced by
The program itself, by relinquishing what it no longer needs.
voided by
The program. A missed drop, an unchecked return value, an ordering mistake between the user and group identifiers.
survives
Nothing. The principle has no enforcement point of its own; it is a discipline that other mechanisms make possible.
fails as
The drop that did not happen, or happened in the wrong order, or was reversible because the saved identifier was left in place.

Saltzer and Schroeder state it in 1975 as one of eight design principles, alongside economy of mechanism, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation, open design, separation of privilege, least common mechanism and psychological acceptability. Half a century later it is the only one of the eight that is routinely quoted, and it is the one with no enforcement point.16, 1, 78

P14

Privilege separation

confidentialityintegrityrung L0 · enforced
guarantees
The code that parses untrusted input runs with no privilege, and reaches a small privileged monitor only over a narrow, explicitly enumerated channel.
enforced by
The program's own architecture, resting on the kernel's process boundary.
voided by
The design -- specifically, by what the monitor is willing to do when asked.
survives
Complete compromise of the parsing process, which is the entire point of the arrangement.
fails as
A monitor whose interface is as powerful as the privilege it holds back. Splitting the process without narrowing the request moves the bug and keeps the consequence.

Provos, Friedl and Honeyman's 2003 retrofit of OpenSSH is the canonical instance, and its most useful result is negative: the monitor's interface had to be redesigned several times before the unprivileged half could not simply ask for what it wanted.78, 79, 80, 81

P15

Set-user-ID execution

integrityrung L0 · enforced
guarantees
An unprivileged caller can cause a specific privileged operation to happen without holding the privilege to perform it directly.
enforced by
The kernel, at the moment of exec.
voided by
The set-user-ID program, which now acts for a principal whose trustworthiness it must decide for itself.
survives
Nothing. The mechanism exists to carry authority across the boundary.
fails as
The confused deputy. The program has authority of its own and a request from someone else, and no way to tell whose authority it is using.

E3's census of this machine finds 11 set-user-ID binaries totalling 538,168 bytes -- half a megabyte of code that any local account can cause to run as user 0, by design, on an image nobody customised. Hardy's 1988 note names the failure mode and points out it is the reason capabilities were proposed in the first place.37, 82, 73

P16

System call filtering

integrityrung L2 · enforced
guarantees
A thread cannot invoke a system call outside an allow list installed before the untrusted work began.
enforced by
A bytecode filter evaluated by the kernel on the system call path, irrevocable once set.
voided by
The kernel. The filtered process cannot lift its own filter, so its compromise does not void the guarantee.
survives
Total compromise of the filtered process.
fails as
A count of names. E8 finds the conventional container profile leaves 300 of 386 entry points open, and one of the open ones is ioctl, behind which the kernel's own registry lists 131 distinct command namespaces that no syscall count touches.

This is the first plate in the article whose guarantee survives the compromise of the thing it constrains, and the reason is structural: the restriction is one-way. Irreversibility, not granularity, is what moves a mechanism down the ladder.83, 84, 85, 86, 87, 88, 89

Per-subsystem indirection inside a single shared kernel.

The kernel, which is shared. Every namespace is a different view of the same code.

User-space compromise in another tenant.

The escape. A defect in a subsystem that no namespace virtualises is an escape from all of them simultaneously.

P19

Virtual machine isolation

confidentialityintegrityavailabilityrung L3 · enforced
guarantees

enforced by
The hypervisor, with two stages of address translation in hardware underneath it.
voided by
The hypervisor and, in practice, its device model.
survives
Complete compromise of the guest kernel. This is the only plate in the vocabulary of which that is true.
fails as
The device model: a large body of code parsing guest-controlled data at the host's privilege, which is the same shape of problem one level down.

Popek and Goldberg's 1974 conditions are about correctness of virtualisation and not about security, but they imply the property that matters here: every sensitive instruction traps. Firecracker's design argument is precisely the size of the remaining device model, and gVisor's is that a kernel written in a memory-safe language in user space is a cheaper boundary than a second kernel.96, 97, 98, 99, 100

What separates the two halves is not granularity and not sophistication. It is irreversibility. A program that drops privilege can, in principle, be made to regain it — through a saved identifier left in place, through an exec that re-applies a set-user-ID bit, through a helper that will do the privileged thing on request. A program that installs a seccomp filter with no-new-privileges set cannot remove it; a thread that applies a Landlock ruleset cannot widen it. The kernel offers no interface for either, which is why those plates sit two rungs deeper than least privilege despite being, in some sense, less thorough. If you want a single design rule out of this article, it is that a restriction you can undo is worth about one rung less than one you cannot. The research tradition that took this furthest went past irreversibility to unforgeability: EROS built a whole system on capabilities as the only naming mechanism, seL4 produced a functional-correctness proof of a kernel small enough to admit one, and software fault isolation showed that a boundary can be enforced by rewriting the code rather than by a hardware domain at all.101, 102, 103 None of the three is in the standard syllabus, and all three are arguments about Anderson's third condition rather than about a mechanism.

Set-user-ID is the counterexample that proves the vocabulary needs the distinction. It is a mechanism for deliberately carrying authority across a boundary, and Hardy named the resulting failure mode in 1988: a program with authority of its own, acting on a request from someone else, with no way to tell whose authority it is exercising.82 The census in experiment 3 finds 11 such binaries on this unmodified image, totalling 538,168 bytes of code that any local account can cause to run as user 0 on demand. Half a megabyte is small by modern standards and enormous by Anderson's third condition.

Containers belong in this group rather than in a group of their own, because that is what they are: a bundle of rung-2 restrictions applied at once. Namespaces change what a process can see, control groups change what it can consume, seccomp changes what it can call, and a mandatory policy changes what it can touch. Each is real. None of them changes the fact that the kernel is shared, which is why the correct comparison is not container against virtual machine in the abstract but the size of the two interfaces. Experiment 8 measures one side of that: the x86-64 table exposes 386 system calls to a 64-bit process, the conventional container profile blocks 86 of them, and 300 remain. One of the 300 is ioctl, behind which the kernel's own registry documents 131 distinct magic numbers, each a separate command namespace.87, 88, 86 A profile that reports blocking 22% of system calls is reporting a percentage of names.98, 99

8  Defending the address space

This group is the one with the longest public record of failure, and the reason is visible in the ladder. Two of its five plates are at rung 0, meaning their guarantee is void if the protected program leaks one value; they are also the two that are described as security features in release notes. The other three are at rung 2 and their guarantees have held exactly as stated for decades, while the property practitioners wanted from them has not.

P20

P22

P23

Stack canaries and compiler hardening

guarantees

voided by
The application, through a leak of the value or a write that does not cross it.
survives
Nothing that can read memory, and nothing that can choose where to write.

Precision. Any defect that lets the attacker pick an address rather than a length walks past the check without touching it.

StackGuard in 1998 and the 1996 Phrack article that provoked it bracket the moment when memory corruption became an engineering discipline rather than an anecdote. P24

Control-flow integrity

integrityrung L2 · enforced
guarantees
An indirect branch may only land on a target in a set computed in advance, and a return may only go back to the site that called.
enforced by
Compiler instrumentation for forward edges; a hardware shadow stack or pointer authentication for backward edges.
voided by
The kernel, which holds the shadow stack's mappings and the pointer-authentication keys.
survives
Application compromise, for backward edges, when the hardware is doing the work rather than the compiler.
fails as
Precision. A forward-edge set computed from function signatures is large enough to leave usable gadgets, and a data-only attack never takes a wrong branch at all.

Abadi and colleagues' 2005 formulation is a complete answer to control-flow hijacking and an incomplete answer to exploitation, because those are different problems. Counterfeit object-oriented programming makes the gap concrete: every call in the attack is to a legitimate virtual method of the right type.122, 123, 124, 125, 126, 127, 128

The history is compressed and instructive. The non-executable stack patch and the return-into-libc technique that defeats it were published weeks apart in 1997.109 Randomised layout shipped in 2001 and Shacham and colleagues showed in 2004 that the 16 bits available on 32-bit Linux fell to brute force in about four minutes.113, 114 Return-oriented programming generalised return-into-libc to arbitrary computation in 2007.110 Fine-grained code randomisation was answered by just-in-time code reuse within a year.116 Forward-edge control-flow integrity, proposed in 2005, was answered by counterfeit object-oriented programming in 2015, in which every call is to a legitimate virtual method of the correct type.122, 124

None of this means the mitigations were pointless. Microsoft's vulnerability-trend data shows the cost of exploitation rising steadily across the same period, and the categories of bug that were once trivially exploitable became engineering projects.112 What it means is narrower and more useful: these mechanisms raise cost, the cost is denominated in bits, and the bits evaporate discontinuously. Experiment 1 puts a number on the discontinuity. At 28 bits — the mmap randomisation this host actually reports — a blind attacker re-randomised on every attempt expects 134,217,728 probes, which at this machine's measured 23,757 probes per second is 1.57 hours. An attacker facing a service that forks from an unchanged parent, who can distinguish a crash from a hang, learns the address one byte at a time in 514 expected probes: 0.022 seconds. Identical entropy; a factor of 261,124. The variable is not the mitigation, it is whether the application returns one bit of feedback.

Experiment 2 generalises the point into the arithmetic that defence-in-depth arguments usually get wrong. Take five mitigations and multiply their bypass probabilities and you get 92.38 bits of nominal difficulty. Two of the five — layout randomisation and the stack canary — depend on the same precondition, that the attacker cannot read memory. Give the attacker a 20% chance of one disclosure and the stack is worth 10.70 bits. The ratio has a closed form that does not depend on how the other three are scored at all: it tends to q × 284, because a leak cancels exactly the secrecy-backed terms and nothing else. Layers add bits only where they fail for different reasons, and two mitigations defeated by the same primitive are one mitigation.

It is worth naming what changed underneath all of this in 2018. Meltdown and Spectre established that the page table entry can be correct, the architectural check can be performed, and the data can arrive anyway, because the machine that executes is not the machine the architecture describes.107, 108 Kernel address randomisation had already been shown derandomisable from user space through the timing of the translation lookaside buffer.119 Rowhammer had shown in 2014 that reading memory you own can change memory you do not.129, 130 Rung 5 is empty in this article's ledger, which is a statement about the vocabulary and not about the machine: nothing in the standard protection list is stated as a guarantee that only the silicon could void, and everything in it quietly assumes the silicon behaves as documented.

9  Before and beneath

The deepest plates in the article are also the narrowest. All four say something about a machine that is off, starting, or being checked from outside, and none of them says anything about a machine that is running. That is not a weakness; it is what makes them survivable. A property about the state of a powered-down disk cannot be voided by a kernel bug, because there is no kernel.

P25

Secure boot

integrityrung L4 · enforced
guarantees
Firmware transfers control only to an image whose signature chains to a key in the platform's allowed database and is not in the forbidden one.
enforced by
The firmware, before any operating system exists.
voided by
The firmware and the key hierarchy above it.
survives
Total compromise of the kernel, of every privileged daemon, and of every application. The property is about what starts, not about what then happens.
fails as
A signed component with a defect, which is a signed bypass. The only remedy is revocation, and revocation is a list that has to be kept current.

This is the sharpest illustration of the article's thesis available. The Microsoft certificates that underwrite third-party Secure Boot on almost all x86 hardware began expiring on 24 June 2026 for the key exchange key and 27 June 2026 for the third-party bootloader authority, with the Windows production authority following on 19 October 2026. Machines still boot. What expired was the ability to add anything to the forbidden list -- the mechanism kept its guarantee and lost its remedy.131, 132, 133, 134, 135, 136, 137, 138

P26

Measured boot and attestation

integrityaccountabilityrung L4 · recorded
guarantees

Nothing. It measures; it does not stop. A measured boot will faithfully record the compromise and continue.
voided by
The core root of trust for measurement, which is the one component that measures itself.
survives
Everything above the firmware, in the weaker sense that a compromise leaves a record even when it succeeds.

P27

voided by
The key custodian: the security processor that releases it, or the person who types the passphrase.

Everything, while the machine is off. Nothing at all, once it is running and unlocked.

This plate rewards being read as a dependency claim rather than as a feature. 'The disk is encrypted' is true and says nothing about a running laptop, which is the state laptops are stolen in.142, 143, 144, 145, 146, 147, 148

P28

File and per-user encryption

confidentialityrung L2 · enforced
guarantees
A file's contents and name are unreadable without a key held for one particular user.
enforced by
The filesystem, with keys in the kernel's keyring.
voided by
The kernel, which holds the key for as long as anyone is logged in.
survives
Other users on the same mounted filesystem -- a case whole-device encryption does not address at all, because at that layer the device is either unlocked or not.
fails as
Metadata. Sizes, timestamps, link counts and directory structure stay in the clear, and often that is the interesting part.

Two mechanisms with the same word in their names sit two rungs apart, because they answer different questions. One is about a device somebody carried away; the other is about a user on a machine that is running.149, 150, 145, 151, 152

Secure boot is the plate this article was, in a sense, written for, because 2026 supplied the demonstration. The Microsoft certificates that underwrite third-party UEFI Secure Boot on essentially all x86 hardware carried fifteen-year lifetimes issued in 2011. The key exchange key authority expired on 24 June 2026, the third-party UEFI authority that signs Linux shim on 27 June 2026, and the Windows production authority follows on 19 October 2026.136, 138 Machines with the old certificates enrolled continue to boot, and Red Hat has shipped shim binaries signed with both the 2011 and 2023 chains so that either enrolment works.137 What stopped is narrower and worse: without a valid key exchange key, a device can no longer receive updates to the forbidden signature database. The guarantee — only signed images run — is intact. The remedy for a signed image that turns out to be a bypass is not.

That matters because signed bypasses are the characteristic failure of this plate and they are not rare. BootHole in 2020 was a buffer overflow in GRUB2, signed by the third-party authority and therefore trusted on effectively every Secure Boot machine, Windows included; remediation was and is a revocation entry.133 BlackLotus in 2023 was a bootkit sold in the wild that defeated Secure Boot on fully patched Windows 11 by exploiting a signed, not-yet-revoked component.134 LoJax in 2018 had already shown firmware implants surviving operating system reinstallation and disk replacement.135 A root of trust whose only remedy is a revocation list is exactly as strong as the mechanism that distributes the list, and that mechanism turns out to have an expiry date.

Measured boot answers a different question and is the article's only deep plate with a recorded posture. A platform configuration register is a running hash: it cannot be set, only extended, so it identifies one sequence of one set of measurements. Experiment 7 makes two consequences concrete. The same six components extended in reverse order give an entirely different value, and a single flipped bit anywhere in the chain moves 128.06 of 256 register bits, so a near-miss is indistinguishable from an attack. That precision is what makes the record trustworthy and it is also why sealing a key to it is painful: a modest fleet with three firmware versions, four option-ROM sets, three shims, five bootloaders, twelve kernels and twenty initial ramdisks has 43,200 legitimate configurations to enumerate, and a monthly kernel cadence adds 3,600 more every month.139, 140, 141 The standard escape — authorising policies by signature rather than enumerating them — works, and replaces a combinatorial problem with a signing key that now has to be protected.

Disk encryption is the plate whose common description is most misleading relative to its rung. "The disk is encrypted" is a claim about a powered-off device. The keys for a running one are in DRAM, which retains contents long enough to be recovered after a cold restart; they are reachable over any interface offering direct memory access; and a machine that resumes from suspend without re-deriving the key has, for these purposes, never been off.146, 147 Placing it at rung 4 is correct for the guarantee as stated and is also the reason the guarantee needs restating whenever someone cites it about a stolen laptop. Per-file encryption sits two rungs shallower and answers the different question of one user's files on a machine somebody else is also using.150, 149

Four plates remain: the two that prevent nothing, and the two about the kernel that every rung-2 plate in the article stands on.

P32

Kernel attack surface and memory safety

confidentialityintegrityavailabilityrung L2 · raised cost
guarantees
Fewer reachable defects, because there is less reachable code and less of it is written in a language that permits memory errors.
enforced by
Build configuration, compiler options, interface gating, and language choice for new subsystems.
voided by
The kernel. This is a property of the kernel, about the kernel.
survives
Nothing, which is the point: it is not an isolation boundary but a reduction in how often the boundaries below fail.
fails as
Logic. The 2026 crop reached root through the userspace crypto socket interface, twice through IPsec transform handling, through ptrace's credential check and through a filesystem copy-on-write race. Several are deterministic logic errors that no memory-safety measure addresses, and one was reported as working with near-certainty on unpatched hosts.

Android's data is the strongest evidence for the language argument: the proportion of memory-safety vulnerabilities fell as the proportion of new memory-unsafe code fell, without rewriting what was already there. It is also the clearest statement of the limit -- the measure is about new code, and the kernel a running system depends on is mostly old.168, 169, 170, 171, 112, 75, 76, 77, 65, 172, 173

The audit plate is where a widely repeated claim falls apart under measurement. Hash-chaining a log is supposed to make tampering evident: each entry's digest covers the previous one, so removing an entry invalidates everything after it. Experiment 6 builds a 400,000-entry chain on this machine in 0.200 seconds, deletes entry 100, and re-forges the entire chain in 0.251 seconds. The result is internally consistent and the deletion is invisible to anything running on the host. A year of records at fifty events per second is 1,577,880,000 links and re-forges in 788.8 seconds. Tamper-evidence is not a property of the hash function; it is a property of whether a digest has left the machine and reached a verifier who still has it. With an anchor every k entries, an attacker who takes root at a uniformly random moment can rewrite k/2 entries in expectation, and nothing more — which makes the anchoring interval, not the algorithm, the security parameter.155, 156, 157

The same experiment measures a less interesting and more common failure. The kernel's audit queue has a default backlog of 8,192 records and a userspace daemon that drains at a finite rate. Modelled against a two-second burst with a 5,000-per-second drain, arrivals at 6,000 per second are absorbed entirely, at 20,000 per second 54.52% of the burst is discarded, and at 100,000 per second 90.90% is. No attacker is required. Any mechanism whose posture is recorded has a completeness property that is worth measuring before its integrity property is worth arguing about.158, 153

Rootkits and kernel integrity close the article because they are the statement of what the ladder is for. A rootkit is not a category of malware; it is a claim about which rung the attacker has reached.166 Once attacker code is executing in the kernel, every plate at rung 2 or shallower in this ledger — twenty-eight of thirty-two — is void simultaneously, including the two whose job was to notice. Module signature enforcement and the lockdown policy exist to keep a privileged user from becoming a kernel, and lockdown is best read as an enumeration of the paths by which they otherwise could.164, 165 On platforms where a hypervisor holds the kernel's page tables, this plate moves to rung 3 and the enumeration stops being the defence, which is the strongest argument in the article for virtualisation as a security technology rather than a packaging one.100, 167

Memory safety is the other half of the answer and its evidence is good. Android's published data shows the proportion of memory-safety vulnerabilities falling as the proportion of new memory-unsafe code fell, without rewriting the existing base.170, 171 Rust has been in the Linux kernel tree since 2022.169 The limit is also visible. Of the Linux local privilege escalations disclosed between April and July 2026, one was a nine-year-old deterministic logic error in the userspace crypto socket interface, two were in IPsec transform handling, one was a logic flaw in ptrace's credential check that let an unprivileged user read root-owned files, and one was a filesystem race.75, 76, 77, 65 Several are logic, not memory. All of them reached root, and the mechanisms in sections 6 and 7 that were running at the time did what the ladder says they do at rung 2, which is nothing. The delivery problem is its own topic: mainline moved from 6.19 in February 2026 to 7.2 in August, a cadence at which the fixed kernel and the deployed kernel are rarely the same object.174, 173, 175, 176

11  The lab

Eight experiments, stdlib-only Python, seed 20260911, run at build time on the machine that produced this document. Three take their inputs from the Linux kernel's own source of truth — the x86-64 system call table, the capability header, the SELinux class map and the ioctl registry — and one takes them from this container's filesystem. The rest are exact computation or measurement. The complete output is reproduced verbatim below the discussion.

E1, randomisation against an oracle. This host reports randomize_va_space=2 and mmap_rnd_bits=28. A loopback accept-read-close loop measures 23,757 probes per second, which is a generous upper bound on what a network-facing service would give an attacker. Blind search of 28 bits expects 134,217,728 probes (1.57 hours here); byte-at-a-time search against a forking parent expects 514 (0.0216 seconds). The 16-bit case that took about 216 seconds on 2004 hardware takes 1.38 seconds here, which is the less interesting number: hardware got faster, and that is not an argument about randomisation.114, 115

E2, composition. The stipulated parameters are arbitrary and the result is not, which is the point of computing the closed form. Two of the five modelled mitigations hold secrets worth 28 and 56 bits. A single disclosure sets both terms to one and leaves the other three untouched, so the ratio of the leaky to the independent probability tends to q × 284 for small q, and the numerical sweep agrees with the prediction to six significant figures at q = 0.20. Multiplying bypass probabilities is only valid under an independence assumption that an information-disclosure primitive violates by construction.

E3, the authority census. 98,633 regular files and 11,780 directories under /etc, /opt, /srv, /usr and /var, following no symlinks and deduplicating by inode. Fourteen distinct permission words appear of the 4,096 the model can express; the eight commonest cover 99.99% of all 110,413 objects, and two of them cover 99.5%. 98,617 files — 99.98% — are readable by every local account. Eleven set-user-ID binaries totalling 538,168 bytes run as user 0 at any account's request. One world-writable regular file exists and no world-writable directory lacks the sticky bit. This is a stock image that nobody configured badly; the numbers describe the default, which is the point.

E4, divided root. The header gives 41 capabilities with CAP_LAST_CAP at CAP_CHECKPOINT_RESTORE. Seven reach root in a single documented step: loading a module, raw device I/O, tracing a privileged process, rebooting into a chosen kernel, overriding discretionary checks, becoming user 0 directly, and the broad administrative capability.70, 71 Adding documented chains — changing a file's owner or mode to obtain the override, setting file capabilities to obtain the administrative one, making a device node to obtain raw I/O — brings the transitive closure to 19 of 41. The conventional container default keeps 14 names, 9 of which are in that closure. The caveat is in the lab output and belongs here too: in a real container these sit inside a user namespace, a seccomp filter and a mandatory policy, and each cuts particular edges. 12  The ledger

The mean rung is 1.469. 16 of the 32 mechanisms — exactly half — are void once a single component no more privileged than a user-space daemon is compromised. Twelve more are void once the kernel is, which is twenty-eight of thirty-two gone at rung 2. Four survive the kernel: one hypervisor plate and three that are about a machine which is not running. Rung 5 is empty.

That last fact is the one that took the longest to be sure of, and it is a statement about the vocabulary rather than about hardware. Nothing in the standard list of protection mechanisms is phrased as a guarantee that only the silicon could void — there is no entry for "the MMU translates correctly" or "AES-NI computes AES". Those are assumptions, held by every plate at once, and the microarchitectural results of the last decade are the discovery that assumptions at that level are the kind of thing that can be wrong. A protection vocabulary with no rung-5 entries has no way to express what Meltdown broke, which is why the response to it had to be built out of kernel page-table isolation and compiler barriers: mechanisms at rung 2, defending a property that belongs at rung 5.

Three other regularities are worth recording. The first is that the mechanisms most often described to non-specialists sit shallowest. File permissions, stack canaries and randomised address spaces are the three items a general audience is most likely to have heard of, and all three are at rung 0, meaning each is void if the program it protects misbehaves in one specific way. The second is that the deep plates are narrow. Every plate at rung 4 makes a claim about starting or about being powered off, and none about running. Depth and scope trade against each other with unusual consistency here, and the trade is not a defect: it is what it costs to be able to say something that a kernel bug cannot take away.

The third is about posture. All three recorded plates — attestation, auditing, integrity monitoring — produce evidence rather than prevention, and two of the three sit at rung 1, which is to say the evidence is generated by a component with roughly the privilege of the attacker it is meant to catch. That is not an argument against logging. It is an argument that the value of a log is determined almost entirely by where it goes, a point experiment 6 makes quantitatively and which the design of every serious audit architecture since the Orange Book has assumed without always saying.11, 155

What follows from the ladder, if anything does, is a short list of questions to ask of a protection claim. Which component's compromise is sufficient to void it? Is the restriction reversible by the thing being restricted? Does the guarantee survive the compromise of what it constrains, or merely of somebody else? Is there a second mechanism here that fails to the same primitive as the first, in which case there is one mechanism? And is the property being claimed the property that was stated, or the property that was hoped for?16, 23, 1

None of that is a reason for pessimism about any particular mechanism. Twenty-five of the thirty-two are enforced, most of them work, and the ones at rung 0 have demonstrably raised the cost of exploitation for thirty years. It is a reason to stop reading a protection list as a list. Each item is a conditional, and the conditions are the part that varies.
