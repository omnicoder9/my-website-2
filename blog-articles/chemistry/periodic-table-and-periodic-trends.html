Chemistry Series

The Periodic Table and Periodic Trends

How the elements are arranged into periods, groups and blocks, what distinguishes the major families, and why atomic size, ionization energy, electron affinity, electronegativity, metallic character and oxidation state vary as they do.

Abstract

The periodic table arranges the 118 known elements in order of atomic number so that elements with analogous outer-electron configurations fall in the same column.1,2 This article describes the table in three parts. The first sets out its architecture: the seven periods, the eighteen IUPAC groups, the s, p, d and f blocks, the division into metals, nonmetals and metalloids, and the collective categories of main-group elements, transition elements, lanthanoids and actinoids. The second surveys five families (the alkali metals, alkaline-earth metals, chalcogens, halogens and noble gases) whose chemistry shows periodicity most clearly. The third develops the periodic trends quantitatively, starting from measured atomic and ionic radii, ionization energies and electron affinities, then treating electronegativity scales, effective nuclear charge and shielding as the explanatory framework, and ending with metallic character and oxidation states. Ionization energies are taken from the NIST Atomic Spectra Database,3 electron affinities from the critical compilation of Andersen, Haugen and Hotop,4 ionic radii from Shannon5 and covalent radii from Cordero and co-workers.6 Electron configurations themselves are developed in the companion article Quantum Chemistry and Electronic Structure and are used here without re-derivation.

1 Structure

Mendeleev's 1869 table ordered the then-known elements by atomic weight and grouped them by chemical analogy, leaving gaps for undiscovered elements.7,8 His predictions for "eka-aluminium", "eka-boron" and "eka-silicon" were borne out by the discoveries of gallium (1875), scandium (1879) and germanium (1886), which established the periodic system as a predictive rather than merely descriptive tool.8 Ordering by atomic weight nevertheless left anomalies: argon (standard atomic weight 39.95) precedes potassium (39.098), cobalt (58.933) precedes nickel (58.693) and tellurium (127.60) precedes iodine (126.90).9 Moseley's 1913–1914 X-ray measurements showed that the square root of the frequency of an element's characteristic Kα line rises linearly with an integer, the atomic number Z, and that ordering by Z removes all three inversions.10,11 (The physics of Moseley's law is treated in the companion article Atomic Theory and Atomic Structure.) The modern statement of the periodic law is therefore that the properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic number. The underlying reason is quantum-mechanical: similar outer-shell electron configurations recur at regular intervals of Z, and the outer electrons govern chemical behaviour.8,12

The form of the table recommended by IUPAC is the 18-column "medium-long" form shown in Figure 1, in which the two series of fourteen f-block elements are printed below the main body to keep the page a practical width.1,13 Inserting those rows in place would give the 32-column long form, which is topologically identical.8

1.1 Periods

A period is a horizontal row of the table. The period number equals the principal quantum number n of the outermost (valence) s subshell being occupied, so each period begins with an element that has a single electron in a new ns subshell (hydrogen or an alkali metal) and ends with a noble gas whose ns and, from period 2 onward, np subshells are full.12,15 The lengths of the periods are fixed by the order in which subshells fill, the (n + l) or Madelung ordering, together with the capacity 2(2l + 1) of each subshell (Table 1).8 Because seven periods are now complete, with oganesson (Z = 118) closing period 7,16,2 the period lengths run 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32.

Within a period, nuclear charge rises by one unit per element while the added electrons enter the same shell, where they screen one another only partly. Worked example 1

Locate selenium (Z = 34). Its ground-state configuration is [Ar]3d104s24p4.3 The highest principal quantum number occupied is 4, so selenium is in period 4. Its differentiating electron enters a p subshell, so it is a p-block element, and it has six electrons in its valence ns and np subshells, which places it in group 10 + 6 = 16.19 Selenium is therefore a chalcogen, beneath sulfur.

1.2 Groups

A group is a vertical column. IUPAC numbers the groups 1 to 18 from left to right.19,13 This numbering was introduced in 1988 to replace two incompatible older conventions, the North American "CAS" scheme and an older European scheme, which both used Roman numerals with A and B suffixes but assigned the suffixes to different columns (Table 2).19 Elements of a group share the same number and type of valence electrons, so their chemistry is related; in the s and p blocks the resemblance is often close, while in the d block similarities along a period can be as important as those down a group.15,12

Two placements are conventional rather than dictated by configuration. Hydrogen (1s1) is placed in group 1 but is not an alkali metal, and helium (1s2) is placed in group 18 with the noble gases, whose chemistry it shares, although its configuration is of the s2 type.1,8 The composition of group 3 has also been debated: whether it should contain Sc, Y, La and Ac or Sc, Y, Lu and Lr.20 An IUPAC task group reported in 2021 that there is no objective way to adjudicate between the two, but recommended the Sc–Y–Lu–Lr form as a convention because it keeps all elements in increasing order of Z, avoids splitting the d block, and gives f-block rows of fourteen elements, matching the fourteen electrons an f subshell holds.14 That form is used in Figure 1. The 2022 IUPAC table itself still leaves the question open by placing the full fifteen-element lanthanoid and actinoid series below group 3.1

1.3 Blocks

The blocks group elements according to the type of subshell (s, p, d or f) that receives the last electron in the aufbau sequence, the "differentiating electron".12,8 The width of each block, in columns, is the capacity of the corresponding subshell, 2(2l + 1): 2 for s, 6 for p, 10 for d and 14 for f (Figure 1). Block membership is a property of the idealized filling order; a number of d- and f-block atoms have ground-state configurations that deviate from it (Cr and Cu, for example), as explained in the quantum chemistry article.3

1.3.1 s-block

The s block comprises groups 1 and 2 (valence configurations ns1 and ns2) plus hydrogen and helium.12 Apart from hydrogen, the s-block elements are reactive metals with low first ionization energies that form compounds almost exclusively in the group oxidation state (+1 or +2), predominantly ionic in character, with beryllium the main exception because its small, highly polarizing Be2+ ion favours covalent bonding.15

1.3.2 p-block

The p block comprises groups 13 to 18 (valence configurations ns2np1 to ns2np6).12 It is the only block that contains metals, metalloids and nonmetals together: the boundary between them runs diagonally from boron to astatine, so that metallic character increases down each group (section 3.8).15,21 Heavier p-block elements show a growing preference for an oxidation state two below the group maximum, the inert-pair effect (section 3.9).15,22

1.3.3 d-block

The d block comprises groups 3 to 12, in which (n − 1)d subshells are filled in periods 4 to 7.12 All d-block elements are metals. Because the (n − 1)d and ns orbitals lie close in energy, most of them form compounds in several oxidation states, many of which are coloured and paramagnetic.12,15 Horizontal similarities are strong: Fe, Co and Ni, for example, resemble one another more than they resemble the heavier members of their own groups.15

1.3.4 f-block

The f block contains the two inner-transition series in which 4f (period 6) and 5f (period 7) subshells are filled.12 The 4f orbitals are buried beneath the filled 5s and 5p subshells and take little part in bonding, so the 4f elements are chemically very similar and dominated by the +3 oxidation state; the 5f orbitals are more extended in the early actinides, which accounts for their much wider range of oxidation states.15,12

Worked example 2

The number of elements in a complete period follows from the blocks it crosses. Period 6 spans the 6s block (2 elements), the 4f block (14), the 5d block (10) and the 6p block (6): 2 + 14 + 10 + 6 = 32, in agreement with Table 1. Period 3 crosses only 3s and 3p: 2 + 6 = 8, because the 3d subshell does not begin to fill until after 4s, in period 4.8

1.4 Metals

Most elements are metals. Macroscopically a metal is lustrous, malleable and ductile, and conducts heat and electricity well; the defining physical criterion is that its electrical conductivity is high and decreases as temperature rises, because conduction is by delocalized electrons in partly filled bands whose motion is scattered more strongly by lattice vibrations at higher temperature.23,24 Chemically, metals have low ionization energies and electronegativities, form cations, and give basic oxides and hydroxides (section 3.8).15 Herzfeld showed in 1927 that whether an element is metallic in the condensed phase can be predicted from two atomic quantities: when the molar refraction R (a measure of atomic polarizability) equals or exceeds the molar volume V, the valence electrons are no longer bound to individual atoms and the solid is a metal.25 Edwards and Sienko showed that the ratio R/V divides the periodic table into metals and nonmetals with few errors and that the dividing line it gives runs diagonally through the p block in the familiar position.24

1.5 Nonmetals

The nonmetals occupy the upper right of the table together with hydrogen: H, C, N, O, P, S, Se, the halogens F, Cl, Br and I, and the noble gases.15,12 They are poor conductors of heat and electricity in their normal forms (graphite is an exception within the layers), have high ionization energies and electronegativities, tend to gain or share electrons, and form acidic or neutral oxides.15 At room temperature eleven elements are gases, all of them nonmetals (H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2 and the six noble gases), and bromine is the only nonmetal that is a liquid.26 Nonmetal elements often exist as discrete molecules (O2, P4, S8) or as covalent network solids (diamond), and the energy cost of breaking those bonds, rather than any ionic lattice, governs their physical properties.15

1.6 Metalloids

Metalloids are elements whose properties are intermediate between those of metals and nonmetals. IUPAC does not define the category, and published lists vary; a survey of 194 lists by Vernon found that boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium are the six elements most commonly classified as metalloids.21 They lie along the diagonal boundary between metals and nonmetals in the p block. Characteristically, metalloids look metallic but are brittle, are semiconductors or semimetals rather than true metals, and form amphoteric or weakly acidic oxides.21,15 Silicon and germanium, for example, are semiconductors with band gaps at 300 K of about 1.11 eV and 0.66 eV respectively, so that their conductivity, unlike a metal's, rises with temperature as electrons are thermally excited across the gap.23 Classification at the boundary depends on the criterion used: polonium is usually counted a metal, and calculations predict that condensed astatine would be a monatomic metal, in contrast to the diatomic lighter halogens.21,27

1.7 Main-group elements

IUPAC designates the elements of groups 1, 2 and 13 to 18, except hydrogen, as main-group elements.13 These are the s- and p-block elements. Their chemistry is governed by the valence ns and np electrons, so their usual oxidation states can be predicted from the group number: for groups 1 and 2 the group number itself, and for groups 13 to 17 the group number minus 10 as a maximum and, for nonmetals, the group number minus 18 as a minimum (section 3.9).12 The older term "representative elements" is broadly synonymous.12

1.8 Transition metals

IUPAC defines a transition element as "an element whose atom has an incomplete d sub-shell, or which can give rise to cations with an incomplete d sub-shell".13 On this definition the elements of groups 3 to 11 are transition elements, but zinc, cadmium and mercury in group 12 normally are not, because both the atoms (n − 1)d10ns2 and their only common ions (M2+, d10) have filled d subshells; the term "d-block element" includes them.13,12 A 2007 matrix-isolation report of HgF4, which would contain Hg(IV) with a d8 configuration, was offered as evidence that mercury is a true transition metal,28 but a subsequent matrix-isolation study did not reproduce the observation, and the question remains unsettled.29

The characteristic features of transition-metal chemistry follow from the partly filled d subshell: multiple accessible oxidation states (manganese, for example, forms well-characterized compounds in every state from +2 to +7); coloured compounds from d–d electronic transitions; paramagnetism from unpaired d electrons; the formation of coordination complexes; and widespread use in catalysis, as with iron in ammonia synthesis and vanadium(V) oxide in sulfuric acid manufacture.15,12

Worked example 3

Is scandium a transition element? Sc is [Ar]3d14s2, so the atom has an incomplete 3d subshell and satisfies the IUPAC definition even though its compounds are almost all Sc(III), d0.13,15 Zinc, [Ar]3d104s2, forms Zn2+ ([Ar]3d10) and so satisfies neither clause. Copper, [Ar]3d104s1, has a filled 3d subshell in the atom, but Cu2+ is d9, so copper qualifies through the second clause.3,13

1.9 Lanthanides

The lanthanides are the fifteen elements from lanthanum (Z = 57) to lutetium (Z = 71). IUPAC prefers the name "lanthanoids", because the suffix -ide normally denotes a negative ion, and notes that lanthanum is included by common usage even though the name means "like lanthanum".13 Scandium, yttrium and the lanthanoids together are the rare-earth metals.13 Because 4f electrons are shielded by the filled 5s25p6 subshells, the lanthanoids are chemically very similar, occur together in minerals, and are notoriously difficult to separate; the +3 oxidation state dominates, with Ce(IV), Eu(II) and Yb(II) as the important exceptions, each of which reaches an empty, half-filled or filled 4f subshell.15,12 The 4f electrons shield one another and the valence electrons poorly, so ionic radii shrink steadily along the series, from 103.2 pm for La3+ to 86.1 pm for Lu3+ (six-coordinate).5,30 This "lanthanide contraction" (section 3.7) is why hafnium, the element after the series, has almost the same ionic radius as zirconium above it (71 pm for Hf4+ and 72 pm for Zr4+), and why the two are among the hardest pairs of elements to separate chemically.5,15

1.10 Actinides

The actinides (IUPAC: actinoids) are the fifteen elements from actinium (Z = 89) to lawrencium (Z = 103).13 All are radioactive. Only thorium and uranium occur in substantial quantities in nature; the elements beyond uranium are produced artificially by neutron capture or by nuclear reactions in accelerators.15 In contrast to the lanthanoids, the early actinides display a wide range of oxidation states (uranium commonly +3 to +6, as in the uranyl ion UO22+; neptunium and plutonium up to +7), because the 5f orbitals are more extended and closer in energy to the 6d and 7s orbitals than 4f orbitals are to 5d and 6s; from americium onward the chemistry becomes more lanthanoid-like, with +3 predominant.15,12 Relativistic effects are large in this region. The first ionization energy of lawrencium, measured one atom at a time in 2015, is 4.96 eV, the lowest of any actinide, consistent with a weakly bound 7p1/2 valence electron outside a filled 5f14 core.31

2 Major families

IUPAC approves collective names for several groups of like elements: alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr), alkaline-earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra), pnictogens (N, P, As, Sb, Bi), chalcogens (O, S, Se, Te, Po), halogens (F, Cl, Br, I, At) and noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn), together with the lanthanoids, actinoids and rare-earth metals.13 The superheavy elements that complete groups 1, 2 and 16 to 18 in period 7 are too short-lived for their chemistry to be established in bulk and are not discussed further here.

2.1 Alkali metals

The alkali metals of group 1 have the valence configuration ns1 outside a noble-gas core. Their single valence electron is weakly bound (Table 3), so they are the most electropositive elements, are found in nature only as M+ compounds, and form compounds almost exclusively in the +1 state.15,3 They are soft, low-density metals whose melting points fall down the group as metallic bonding weakens with increasing atomic size.26,15

All react with water to give the hydroxide and hydrogen, 2M(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H2(g), with vigour increasing down the group.15 High-speed imaging has shown that the explosive character of the reaction for sodium–potassium alloy is initiated by a Coulomb explosion: rapid electron transfer to water leaves the metal surface positively charged, and electrostatic repulsion fragments it, exposing fresh metal faster than the hydrogen produced could otherwise allow.33 With oxygen the products change down the group: lithium gives mainly the oxide Li2O, sodium the peroxide Na2O2, and potassium, rubidium and caesium the superoxides MO2, the larger cations stabilizing the larger anions in the lattice.15

Lithium is anomalous in ways that resemble magnesium, its diagonal neighbour: like magnesium it combines directly with nitrogen to form a nitride (Li3N), and its carbonate decomposes on heating to the oxide. The resemblance reflects the similar charge-to-radius ratios of Li+ and Mg2+ (76 pm and 72 pm, six-coordinate).15,5 Francium is intensely radioactive; its first ionization energy (4.073 eV) is higher than caesium's, a reversal of the group trend attributed to relativistic stabilization of the 7s orbital.3,22

Worked example 4

Using 1 eV = 96.485 kJ mol−1 (the product of the elementary charge and the Avogadro constant),34 the molar first ionization energy of sodium is 5.139 eV × 96.485 kJ mol−1 eV−1 = 495.8 kJ mol−1, and that of caesium is 3.894 × 96.485 = 375.7 kJ mol−1. Removing the valence electron from caesium costs about 24% less energy than from sodium, which is consistent with caesium's much more violent reaction with water.3,15

2.2 Alkaline-earth metals

The group 2 elements have the valence configuration ns2 and form compounds in the +2 state. Although their second ionization energy is roughly double the first, the high lattice and hydration energies of the small, doubly charged M2+ ions more than repay it, so M+ compounds are not formed in bulk.15,12 They are harder, denser and higher melting than the neighbouring alkali metals because two electrons per atom contribute to metallic bonding.15,26

Reactivity increases down the group. Beryllium does not react with water even at red heat, magnesium reacts only slowly with hot water or steam, and calcium, strontium and barium react readily with cold water to give hydroxides.15 Beryllium is the anomalous member: Be2+ is so small (27 pm, four-coordinate) and polarizing that beryllium compounds are largely covalent, and BeO and Be(OH)2 are amphoteric, whereas the oxides and hydroxides of the heavier members are basic.5,15 Two solubility trends illustrate the periodicity within the group: the hydroxides become more soluble down the group while the sulfates become less soluble, so that BaSO4 is insoluble enough to be used as a radiocontrast agent despite the toxicity of soluble barium salts.15 Radium is radioactive.

Worked example 5

The successive ionization energies of magnesium are 7.646, 15.035 and 80.144 eV.3 The ratio I2/I1 = 2.0, but I3/I2 = 5.3, because the third electron must be removed from the 2p subshell of the neon-like core, which is far closer to the nucleus and much less shielded. The sharp jump after the second electron identifies magnesium as a group 2 element, and shows why Mg(III) compounds do not exist.12

2.3 Chalcogens

The group 16 elements, oxygen, sulfur, selenium, tellurium and polonium, have the valence configuration ns2np4. The name, approved by IUPAC, derives from Greek roots for copper (and, by extension, metals) and "to produce", and has been interpreted as "ore former", since many metal ores are oxides and sulfides.35,13 The group spans the full range from nonmetal to metal: oxygen and sulfur are nonmetals, selenium is usually classed as a nonmetal although its grey form is a semiconductor, tellurium is a metalloid, and polonium is a radioactive metal.15,21

Oxygen differs from the heavier members in the ways typical of the first element of a p-block group. It is a diatomic gas with strong π bonding (O=O) and is paramagnetic, with two unpaired electrons; sulfur instead catenates through single bonds into rings and chains, of which the crown-shaped S8 ring of ordinary rhombic sulfur is the most stable.15 Oxygen is the second most electronegative element (χP = 3.44) and rarely exceeds the −2 state except in compounds with fluorine, whereas sulfur, selenium and tellurium, with accessible higher oxidation states, form compounds from −2 to +6, as in H2S, SO2 and H2SO4 or SF6.32,15 Hydrogen bonding, which is significant only for the very electronegative first member, explains why water boils at 100 °C while H2S boils at about −60 °C.26,12

2.4 Halogens

The halogens of group 17 (F, Cl, Br, I, At) have the valence configuration ns2np5, one electron short of a noble-gas configuration. They are the most reactive nonmetals, exist as diatomic molecules X2, and readily gain an electron to form halide ions X−; chlorine has the highest electron affinity of any element (section 3.4).15,4 Their physical state at room temperature changes down the group, from gaseous F2 and Cl2 to liquid Br2 and solid I2, as London dispersion forces grow with molecular size and polarizability.26,15

Oxidizing power decreases down the group, as the standard reduction potentials show: E° = +2.87 V for F2/F−, +1.36 V for Cl2/Cl−, +1.07 V for Br2/Br− and +0.54 V for I2/I−.26 Consequently each halogen displaces the heavier halides from solution: Cl2 + 2Br− → 2Cl− + Br2.15 Fluorine's exceptional reactivity has two sources: the F–F bond is unusually weak (bond dissociation enthalpy about 159 kJ mol−1, against about 243 kJ mol−1 for Cl–Cl), because repulsion between lone pairs on the two small atoms is large, and bonds from fluorine to other elements are very strong.15,26 Fluorine, the most electronegative element, shows only the −1 oxidation state in its compounds, whereas chlorine, bromine and iodine reach +7 in species such as ClO4− and IF7.15,12 Astatine is radioactive and available only in trace amounts; calculations predict it to be metallic in the condensed phase.27

Worked example 6

Will bromine oxidize iodide ion? For Br2 + 2I− → 2Br− + I2, E°cell = E°(Br2/Br−) − E°(I2/I−) = 1.07 − 0.54 = +0.53 V.26 Then ΔrG° = −nFE°cell = −2 × 96 485 C mol−1 × 0.53 V = −102 kJ mol−1, so the reaction is strongly spontaneous. The reverse, iodine oxidizing bromide, is not.36

2.5 Noble gases

The group 18 elements have filled valence shells (1s2 for helium, ns2np6 for the rest). They are monatomic gases with the highest first ionization energies of their periods; helium's, 24.587 eV, is the highest of any element (Figure 2), and all have electron affinities that are negative, meaning the anions are not bound.3,4 Their only interatomic attractions are weak dispersion forces, so their boiling points are very low and increase with atomic size, from 4.2 K for helium to 211 K for radon; helium is the only element that does not solidify at atmospheric pressure, however low the temperature.26,15 Argon was isolated from air by Rayleigh and Ramsay in 1894–1895, and the other stable members followed within a few years.37,15

Long regarded as chemically inert, the heavier noble gases were shown to form compounds in 1962, when Bartlett noted that the first ionization energy of xenon was almost the same as that of molecular oxygen, which he had already found to be oxidized by PtF6 to O2+[PtF6]−, and correctly predicted that xenon would react likewise.38 Xenon is now known to form XeF2, XeF4, XeF6, the oxide XeO3 and many related species with xenon in oxidation states +2 to +8; krypton forms KrF2.15,12 Reactivity decreases sharply up the group as ionization energy rises: the first neutral argon compound, HArF, was made in a solid argon matrix in 2000 and survives only at cryogenic temperatures, and helium and neon form no stable neutral compounds under ordinary conditions.39,12

3 Periodic trends

Periodic trends are systematic variations of atomic properties with position in the table. The general pattern is that properties change monotonically across a period and down a group, with the steepest changes at the ends of periods and characteristic irregularities where a subshell begins or reaches half-filling. The trends are measured properties of free atoms (ionization energies, electron affinities) or of atoms in compounds (radii, electronegativities), and they are explained by two linked quantities, effective nuclear charge and shielding, which are discussed in sections 3.6 and 3.7 after the observable trends they account for.12,36

3.1 Atomic radius

An atom has no sharp boundary, because the electron density decays smoothly with distance from the nucleus, so atomic radii are defined operationally from measured internuclear distances.36,12 Three definitions are common. The covalent radius is half the distance between two identical atoms joined by a single covalent bond, refined in practice to a set of radii whose sums best reproduce many measured bond lengths; Cordero and co-workers derived such a set for elements 1 to 96 from a large body of experimental bond distances in crystal structures.6 (Pyykkö and Atsumi have published a complementary set derived for elements 1 to 118 from experimental and computed bond lengths.40) The metallic radius is half the distance between nearest neighbours in a metal lattice, and the van der Waals radius is half the closest distance between non-bonded atoms in adjacent molecules, as compiled by Bondi and extended to the whole main group by Mantina and co-workers.41,42 Comparisons should be made only within one definition.

Two trends dominate (Figure 3). Across a period, radii decrease: covalent radii fall from 128 pm for lithium to 57 pm for fluorine, because each added electron enters the same shell while nuclear charge rises by one unit, so the effective nuclear charge on the valence electrons increases and draws them closer.6,17 Down a group, radii increase: from 128 pm for lithium to 244 pm for caesium, because each step adds a shell with a larger principal quantum number and the extra nuclear charge is largely cancelled by the additional inner electrons.6,12 The decrease across the d block is gentler than in the s and p blocks, because the electrons are added to an inner (n − 1)d subshell that shields the outer ns electrons fairly effectively.12 One consequence of the poor shielding of the d electrons that are inserted before gallium is that gallium (122 pm) is essentially the same size as aluminium (121 pm) above it, even though it has 18 more electrons.6,15

Worked example 7

Covalent radii are approximately additive. Using r(C, sp3) = 76 pm and r(Cl) = 102 pm from the Cordero set,6 the predicted C–Cl bond length in chloromethane is 76 + 102 = 178 pm, in good agreement with the measured value of about 178 pm.26 Additivity fails when the bonded atoms differ greatly in electronegativity, because the bond then acquires ionic character and contracts.12

3.2 Ionic radius

Ionic radii are obtained by partitioning measured cation–anion distances in ionic crystals between the two ions. The partition requires one reference value; the standard compilation, by Shannon, takes O2− as 140 pm in six-fold coordination and derives "effective ionic radii" as functions of charge, coordination number and, for transition-metal ions, spin state.5 (Shannon also tabulated "crystal radii", 14 pm larger for cations and 14 pm smaller for anions, which correspond more closely to the minimum in the electron density between ions.5)

The main regularities are as follows.5,12

  • Cations are smaller than their parent atoms and anions larger. Removing the valence electron from sodium (covalent radius 166 pm) leaves Na+ at 102 pm; adding an electron to chlorine (102 pm) gives Cl− at 181 pm.6,5
  • Down a group, radii of ions of the same charge increase: Li+ 76, Na+ 102, K+ 138, Rb+ 152 and Cs+ 167 pm; F− 133, Cl− 181, Br− 196 and I− 220 pm (all six-coordinate).5
  • In an isoelectronic series, radius falls as nuclear charge rises, because the same number of electrons is held by a larger charge (Table 4).
  • For one element, higher charge means a smaller ion: high-spin Fe2+ is 78 pm and high-spin Fe3+ 64.5 pm.5
  • Radius increases with coordination number: Na+ is 99 pm in four-, 102 pm in six- and 118 pm in eight-fold coordination.5
  • Spin state matters for d-block ions: six-coordinate Fe2+ is 78 pm high-spin but 61 pm low-spin, because in the low-spin ion the electrons occupy only the t2g orbitals, which point between the ligands.5,12

Worked example 8

Estimate the Na–Cl distance in rock-salt NaCl, in which both ions are six-coordinate. Summing Shannon radii gives 102 + 181 = 283 pm.5 The measured cubic lattice parameter of NaCl is 564.0 pm, and the Na–Cl distance is half of it, 282 pm, in close agreement.26

3.3 Ionization energy

The first ionization energy I1 is the minimum energy required to remove an electron from a gas-phase atom in its ground state, X(g) → X+(g) + e−; successive ionization energies I2, I3, … refer to removal from the cation of the preceding charge.36,43 It is always positive. Values are known to high precision from spectroscopy, mostly from the convergence limits of Rydberg series, and are tabulated in the NIST Atomic Spectra Database.3

Figure 2 shows the dominant pattern. I1 increases across a period, from a minimum at each alkali metal to a maximum at each noble gas, because effective nuclear charge rises while the valence shell stays the same. I1 decreases down a group, because the valence electron is further from the nucleus and better shielded: from helium (24.587 eV) through neon (21.565 eV) and argon (15.760 eV) to xenon (12.130 eV), and from lithium (5.392 eV) to caesium (3.894 eV).3 Caesium has the lowest first ionization energy of all the stable elements.3

Two small reversals within each p-block period are diagnostic of subshell structure.12,36

  • Group 2 to group 13: I1(Be) = 9.323 eV exceeds I1(B) = 8.298 eV, and I1(Mg) = 7.646 eV exceeds I1(Al) = 5.986 eV.3 The electron removed from boron or aluminium is a p electron, which penetrates the core less than an s electron of the same shell, is better shielded, and is therefore higher in energy and easier to remove.
  • Group 15 to group 16: I1(N) = 14.534 eV exceeds I1(O) = 13.618 eV, and I1(P) = 10.487 eV exceeds I1(S) = 10.360 eV.3 Oxygen's fourth p electron must pair with another in the same orbital; the additional electron–electron repulsion, and the loss of exchange stabilization relative to a half-filled p3 subshell, make it easier to remove.

Successive ionization energies always increase, since each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion, but they jump abruptly when an electron must be taken from a noble-gas core. Sodium's first two ionization energies are 5.139 and 47.286 eV, aluminium's first four are 5.986, 18.829, 28.448 and 119.992 eV.3 The position of the jump identifies the number of valence electrons and hence the group, as in worked example 5.

3.4 Electron affinity

The electron affinity Eea of an atom is the energy released when an electron is attached to the gas-phase atom, X(g) + e− → X−(g); equivalently, it is the energy needed to detach the electron from the anion, and it is positive when the anion is bound.43,4 (Thermochemical tables often list the corresponding enthalpy change of attachment, which has the opposite sign.36) Electron affinities are measured mostly by laser photodetachment threshold spectroscopy; Andersen, Haugen and Hotop have critically evaluated the data for all atoms.4

Electron affinity becomes broadly larger across a period, reaching a maximum at the halogens, whose anions have noble-gas configurations, and then falls abruptly to the noble gases, whose extra electron would have to enter the next shell (Table 5).4,12 The irregularities mirror those in ionization energy. The group 2 atoms (ns2) and nitrogen (2p3) have little or no affinity, because the added electron must enter a higher-energy p subshell or pair in a half-filled one; the heavier alkaline-earth atoms Ca, Sr and Ba bind an extra electron only very weakly.4 The trend down a group is less regular than for ionization energy. In particular, the elements of period 2 have smaller electron affinities than those of period 3 in groups 14 to 17, so that chlorine, not fluorine, has the largest electron affinity of any element (3.613 against 3.401 eV). The added electron in the compact 2p shell of fluorine experiences stronger repulsion from the electrons already present.4,12

Second electron affinities are always negative: once an anion has formed, adding another electron to it is opposed by electrostatic repulsion. The oxide ion O2− is not bound in the gas phase; it exists in solids only because the lattice energy of the resulting ionic crystal more than compensates.12,36

3.5 Electronegativity

Pauling defined electronegativity as "the power of an atom in a molecule to attract electrons to itself".44 Unlike ionization energy or electron affinity it is not a directly measurable property of an isolated atom, and several scales exist that quantify it in different ways. They agree on the overall pattern: electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group, so that fluorine is the most electronegative element and caesium and francium the least (Table 6).32,45

Pauling scale

Pauling noticed that the dissociation energy of a heteronuclear bond A–B exceeds the mean of the A–A and B–B bond energies, and attributed the excess Δ to ionic resonance arising from unequal sharing.46 He set the difference in electronegativity proportional to the square root of the excess:

|χA−χB|=(eV)−1/2DAB−12(DAA+DBB)(1)

Only differences are defined, so the scale is anchored by assigning a value to one element; the widely used values are those revised by Allred in 1961 from improved thermochemical data, with χP(F) = 3.98 and χP(H) = 2.20.32

Worked example 9

For HCl, the bond dissociation enthalpies at 298 K are D(H–Cl) = 431.4 kJ mol−1, D(H–H) = 435.8 kJ mol−1 and D(Cl–Cl) = 242.6 kJ mol−1.26 The excess is Δ = 431.4 − ½(435.8 + 242.6) = 92.2 kJ mol−1, or 92.2/96.485 = 0.956 eV.34 Equation (1) then gives |χCl − χH| = √0.956 = 0.98, close to the difference between the tabulated values, 3.16 − 2.20 = 0.96.32

Mulliken, Allred–Rochow and Allen scales

Mulliken proposed an absolute scale built from free-atom quantities: the electronegativity is the average of the ionization energy and the electron affinity, χM = ½(I + Eea), the energy an atom gains from holding an electron balanced against the energy it loses in releasing one.47 (Strictly, Mulliken used valence-state rather than ground-state values.47) With ground-state data, χM(F) = ½(17.423 + 3.401) = 10.41 eV and χM(Cl) = ½(12.968 + 3.613) = 8.29 eV.3,4

Allred and Rochow defined electronegativity as the electrostatic force exerted by the nucleus on a valence electron at the covalent radius, χAR = 0.359 Zeff/r2 + 0.744, with r in ångström and Zeff from Slater's rules (section 3.6); the constants were chosen to bring the values onto the Pauling range.48 Allen defined electronegativity as the average one-electron energy of the valence-shell electrons in the ground-state free atom, χspec = (mεp + nεs)/(m + n), where εs and εp are the multiplet-averaged s and p ionization energies obtained from atomic spectra and m, n are the numbers of p and s valence electrons; this "configuration energy", scaled to Pauling-like units, rests entirely on spectroscopic data and assigns values to the noble gases.45,49

Electronegativity differences predict bond polarity. A difference near zero (C–H, 0.35) gives an essentially nonpolar bond, a moderate difference (O–H, 1.24) a polar covalent bond, and a large difference (Na–Cl, 2.23) a predominantly ionic one; the ionic–covalent transition is gradual rather than sharp.32,12

3.6 Effective nuclear charge

In a many-electron atom each electron is attracted by the nucleus and repelled by every other electron. The net effect on a given electron can be represented as attraction by a reduced nuclear charge, the effective nuclear charge

Zeff=Z−S(2)

where S is the shielding (or screening) constant contributed by the other electrons.17,36 Slater gave in 1930 a set of empirical rules for S, intended for constructing simple analytic orbitals.17 Electrons are grouped as (1s)(2s,2p)(3s,3p)(3d)(4s,4p)(4d)(4f)(5s,5p)…, and for an electron in an ns or np group:

  • electrons in groups to the right contribute nothing;
  • each other electron in the same (ns,np) group contributes 0.35 (0.30 within 1s);
  • each electron in shell n − 1 contributes 0.85;
  • each electron in shell n − 2 or lower contributes 1.00.

For an electron in an nd or nf group, each other electron in the same group contributes 0.35 and every electron in a group to its left contributes 1.00.17

Clementi and Raimondi later derived more accurate values by fitting single-exponential (Slater-type) orbitals to self-consistent-field wavefunctions; their Zeff values for atoms with up to 36 electrons were extended to 86 electrons in a sequel.18,50 Both sets show the key trend (Table 7): across a period, Zeff on the valence electrons rises by roughly 0.65 to 0.7 per element, because each added electron in the same shell cancels only about a third of the added nuclear charge, whereas down a group, Slater's rules give a nearly constant Zeff for the outermost electron, the extra nuclear charge being cancelled by the extra inner shell; the more accurate Clementi–Raimondi values rise slowly down a group.17,18

Worked example 10

Apply Slater's rules to the 2p electron of nitrogen, 1s22s22p3. The other four electrons of the (2s,2p) group contribute 4 × 0.35 = 1.40 and the two 1s electrons 2 × 0.85 = 1.70, so S = 3.10 and Zeff = 7 − 3.10 = 3.90.17 For the 3s electron of sodium, 1s22s22p63s1, the eight n = 2 electrons contribute 8 × 0.85 = 6.80 and the two 1s electrons 2 × 1.00 = 2.00, so S = 8.80 and Zeff = 11 − 8.80 = 2.20. Although sodium's nucleus carries 11 units of charge, its valence electron experiences an effective charge only about half that felt by nitrogen's, which is why sodium's first ionization energy (5.14 eV) is far below nitrogen's (14.53 eV).3

Worked example 11

Why does potassium place its nineteenth electron in 4s rather than 3d? For a 4s electron in [Ar]4s1, the eight n = 3 electrons contribute 8 × 0.85 = 6.80 and the ten inner electrons 10 × 1.00 = 10.00, so Zeff(4s) = 19 − 16.80 = 2.20. For a 3d electron in [Ar]3d1, all eighteen electrons of the argon core lie in groups to its left and contribute 1.00 each, so Zeff(3d) = 19 − 18 = 1.00.17 The 4s electron feels the larger effective charge, consistent with the observed [Ar]4s1 ground state.3

3.7 Shielding

Shielding (or screening) is the reduction of the nuclear attraction on an electron by the electron density lying, on average, between it and the nucleus.36,12 Inner-shell electrons shield outer ones effectively, as Slater's coefficients of 0.85 and 1.00 express, while electrons in the same shell shield one another weakly (0.35), because each spends much of its time no closer to the nucleus than the others.17 Shielding also depends on orbital shape. For a given n, an s electron has appreciable probability density close to the nucleus (it penetrates the inner shells), a p electron less, a d electron less still; so for a given shell the shielding experienced decreases, and the orbital energy is lowered, in the order s < p < d < f in screening and s > p > d > f in penetration.36 This is why the subshells of a many-electron atom are no longer degenerate, and why the dips in ionization energy at boron and aluminium occur (section 3.3). The radial distributions behind penetration are shown in the quantum chemistry article.

The converse effect, the poor ability of diffuse, non-penetrating d and f electrons to shield the electrons outside them, produces two of the most important irregularities in the periodic table.12,15

  • The d-block (scandide) contraction. The ten 3d electrons inserted between calcium and gallium shield the 4s and 4p electrons incompletely, so gallium is barely larger than aluminium (section 3.1), and its first ionization energy (5.999 eV) is slightly higher than aluminium's (5.986 eV) rather than lower.3,6 Clementi–Raimondi effective charges show the same thing: Zeff on the valence electron of gallium is markedly larger than for aluminium.18
  • The lanthanide contraction. The fourteen 4f electrons shield even less well, so radii contract across the lanthanoids (section 1.9) and the 5d elements after them are almost the same size as their 4d congeners; Zr/Hf, Nb/Ta and Mo/W are pairs of near-twins in chemistry.5,30,15

For the heaviest elements, relativistic effects add to the poor shielding by d and f electrons: the 6s and 6p1/2 orbitals contract and are stabilized, contributing to the high ionization energies of gold and mercury, the liquid state of mercury, the colour of gold and the inert-pair effect in thallium, lead and bismuth.22

3.8 Metallic character

Metallic character, the extent to which an element shows the physical and chemical properties of metals, correlates with low ionization energy and low electronegativity. It therefore decreases across a period and increases down a group, so that the most metallic elements are at the lower left of the table and the least metallic at the upper right, with the metalloids on the diagonal between.15,12 In the condensed phase the transition is governed by Herzfeld's criterion (section 1.4), and the "metallization" of an element with increasing atomic size down a group, as from nonmetallic carbon through semiconducting silicon and germanium to metallic tin and lead in group 14, reflects the growing polarizability of the atoms relative to their volume.25,24

The acid–base character of the oxides is a convenient chemical index. Across period 3 the highest oxides change from strongly basic Na2O and basic MgO, through amphoteric Al2O3, to acidic SiO2, P4O10, SO3 and Cl2O7, the acidity increasing with the electronegativity and oxidation state of the element.15,12 Down group 15, the oxides change from acidic (N, P) through amphoteric (As, Sb) to basic (Bi).15

Worked example 12

Predict the products of dissolving Na2O and SO3 in water. Sodium is strongly metallic, so its oxide is basic: Na2O + H2O → 2NaOH. Sulfur is a nonmetal in a high oxidation state, so its oxide is acidic: SO3 + H2O → H2SO4. Aluminium, between them, gives Al2O3, which dissolves in both strong acid (to give Al3+(aq)) and strong base (to give aluminate, [Al(OH)4]−).15

3.9 Oxidation states

IUPAC defines the oxidation state of an atom as "the charge of this atom after ionic approximation of its heteronuclear bonds", the bonding electrons being assigned to the more electronegative partner in each bond.51 In practice it is calculated with the familiar rules that the oxidation states in a species sum to its charge, that fluorine is −1, and that oxygen is usually −2 and hydrogen +1 (−1 in metal hydrides).51,12

Oxidation states are periodic because they reflect the number of valence electrons available.12,15

  • s block: the group number, +1 for group 1 and +2 for group 2.
  • p block: a maximum of the group number minus 10 (+3 for Al, +4 for Si, +5 for P, +6 for S, +7 for Cl), reached with the most electronegative partners, fluorine and oxygen; and, for nonmetals, a minimum of the group number minus 18 (−4 for C, −3 for N, −2 for O, −1 for halogens), corresponding to a filled octet.
  • Inert-pair effect: down groups 13 to 15 the state two below the maximum becomes increasingly stable, so that Tl(I), Pb(II) and Bi(III) are more stable than Tl(III), Pb(IV) and Bi(V). This is attributed to the poor shielding and relativistic stabilization of the 6s2 pair, together with the weaker bonds formed by large atoms, which pay back less of the energy needed to involve it.15,22
  • d block: the maximum oxidation state equals the group number as far as manganese in the first transition series (Sc(III), Ti(IV), V(V), Cr(VI), Mn(VII), as in MnO4−) and as far as group 8 in the second and third series, where ruthenium and osmium reach +8 in RuO4 and OsO4.15 Beyond the middle of each series the maximum falls, because the increasing effective nuclear charge binds the d electrons more tightly. The highest oxidation state known for any element, +9, was established in 2014 for iridium in the cation [IrO4]+, identified in the gas phase by infrared spectroscopy.52
  • f block: +3 predominates for the lanthanoids and later actinoids; the early actinoids show a wide range (section 1.10).15

Worked example 13

Find the oxidation state of manganese in KMnO4 and of chromium in Cr2O72−. In KMnO4: +1 (K) + x + 4(−2) = 0, so x = +7, which equals the group number of manganese (7) and corresponds to formal removal of all seven 3d and 4s valence electrons. In Cr2O72−: 2x + 7(−2) = −2, so x = +6, again the group maximum.51,15 Both ions are strong oxidizing agents because those maximal states are readily reduced.15
