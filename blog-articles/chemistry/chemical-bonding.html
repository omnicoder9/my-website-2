Chemical Bonding

How atoms are held together in ionic solids, covalent molecules and metals: the energetics of ion formation and lattice energy, the length, strength and polarity of shared-electron bonds, the bookkeeping of Lewis theory, the continuum from nonpolar to ionic bonds, and the electron-sea and band descriptions of metals.

Abstract

A chemical bond exists between two atoms or groups when the forces between them are strong enough to form an aggregate stable enough to be treated as an independent species.1 This article treats the three limiting kinds of bond in five parts. The first develops ionic bonding quantitatively, from the energetics of forming isolated ions through the structures of ionic crystals to the lattice energy, computed electrostatically from the Born–Landé, Born–Mayer and Kapustinskii equations and obtained thermochemically from Born–Haber cycles. The second covers covalent bonding: the shared electron pair, bond order, and the measured lengths and energies of single, double and triple bonds, including the distinction between a bond dissociation energy and a mean bond energy. The third sets out Lewis theory as a working tool (Lewis symbols and structures, the octet rule, formal charge, resonance) together with its three classes of exception. The fourth quantifies bond polarity through electronegativity differences, ionic character and molecular dipole moments. The fifth describes metallic bonding with the free-electron (electron-sea) model and band theory. Thermochemical values are taken from the CODATA key values2 and the NIST-JANAF tables,3 molecular geometries and dipole moments from the NIST Computational Chemistry Comparison and Benchmark Database (CCCBDB),4 ionization energies from the NIST Atomic Spectra Database5 and electron affinities from Andersen, Haugen and Hotop.6 Electronegativity scales and the periodic trends in ionization energy and electron affinity are developed in the companion article The Periodic Table and Periodic Trends; molecular shapes, valence-bond hybridization and molecular-orbital theory are developed in Molecular Structure. Both are used here without re-derivation.

Modern bonding theory begins with two papers of 1916. Kossel explained the formation of salts by the transfer of electrons from one atom to another until each attains the configuration of a noble gas, the resulting ions being held together electrostatically.7 Lewis, in the same year, proposed that two atoms may instead hold a pair of electrons in common, and that atoms tend to surround themselves with eight valence electrons.8 Langmuir extended and popularised Lewis's ideas in 1919, introducing the terms "octet" and "covalence".9 The quantum-mechanical basis of the shared-pair bond was supplied in 1927 by Heitler and London's treatment of the hydrogen molecule,10 and Pauling's synthesis of these ideas, including the concepts of resonance and electronegativity, organised much of twentieth-century structural chemistry.1 Real bonds lie on continua between the ionic, covalent and metallic limits; the limits are nevertheless the natural starting points, and each is treated in turn below.

1 Ionic bonding

An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It is non-directional: each ion attracts all oppositely charged ions around it, so ionic compounds form extended three-dimensional lattices rather than discrete molecules.11 Bragg's 1913 X-ray analysis of rock salt showed directly that each sodium is surrounded symmetrically by six chlorines, and each chlorine by six sodiums, with no identifiable NaCl molecule.12

1.1 Ion formation

A cation forms when an atom loses one or more electrons, and an anion when an atom gains them. The energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom is its ionization energy I; the energy released when a gaseous atom captures an electron is its electron affinity Eea.13 Both are properties of isolated atoms, and their trends across the periodic table are treated in the companion article The Periodic Table and Periodic Trends. For bonding the key point is that electron transfer between isolated atoms is always endothermic: the smallest first ionization energy of any element (caesium, 3.894 eV) exceeds the largest electron affinity (chlorine, 3.613 eV).5,6 What makes ionic compounds stable is the electrostatic energy released when the ions come together, not the electron transfer itself.

Worked example 1 · Energetics of a gaseous Na⁺Cl⁻ ion pair

The first ionization energy of sodium is 5.1391 eV and the electron affinity of chlorine 3.6127 eV.5,6 With 1 eV per particle = 96.485 kJ mol−1,14 forming separated Na+(g) and Cl−(g) from the atoms costs (5.1391 − 3.6127) × 96.485 = +147.3 kJ mol−1. Bringing the two ions from infinity to the measured internuclear distance of gaseous NaCl, r = 236.1 pm,4 releases the Coulomb energy

EC=−NAe24πε0r=−1.389×10−4 J m mol−12.361×10−10 m=−588 kJ mol−1

The point-charge attraction outweighs the cost of electron transfer by about 440 kJ mol−1 (less the short-range repulsion between the ion cores, which this estimate omits). Assembling the ions into a crystal releases still more energy, as section 1.4 shows.

Kossel's rule that main-group ions adopt noble-gas configurations follows from the large jump in ionization energy once a noble-gas core is reached.7,11 Removing a second electron from sodium (breaking into the 2p6 core) costs 47.29 eV, more than nine times the first ionization energy, and removing a third electron from magnesium costs 80.14 eV.5 No lattice energy can repay such costs, so sodium forms Na+ and magnesium Mg2+, never Na2+ or Mg3+. Anions are limited in the same way from the other side: adding a second electron to O− is strongly endothermic (Worked example 6), yet O2− exists in oxides because the extra lattice energy from the doubled charge more than pays for it.11 Transition-metal cations commonly do not have noble-gas configurations (Fe2+ is [Ar]3d6, Fe3+ is [Ar]3d5); their electron configurations are treated in Quantum Chemistry and Electronic Structure.15

1.2 Ionic compounds

Ionic compounds form when an element of low ionization energy (typically a metal of groups 1 and 2) combines with one of high electron affinity (typically a halogen, oxygen or sulfur). Electroneutrality fixes their empirical formulas: the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge, giving NaCl, MgCl2, Al2O3 and so on.11 The formula describes the ratio of ions in the lattice, not a molecule.

The characteristic physical properties of ionic solids follow from strong, non-directional electrostatic bonding in an extended lattice:11,15

  • High melting and boiling points. Sodium chloride melts at 800.7 °C;16 magnesium oxide, with doubly charged ions, melts above 2800 °C.16
  • Hardness and brittleness. Displacing one layer of ions relative to the next by half a lattice spacing brings ions of like charge into contact; the resulting repulsion cleaves the crystal rather than letting it deform plastically.
  • Electrical conduction only when molten or dissolved. In the solid the ions are fixed at lattice sites; in the melt or in solution they carry current.
  • Solubility in polar solvents. Water dissolves many ionic solids because ion–dipole interactions with the solvent compensate for the lattice energy.

No real compound is completely ionic. A small, highly charged cation distorts (polarizes) the electron cloud of a large, polarizable anion, drawing electron density into the region between the nuclei and conferring partial covalent character. Fajans summarised the conditions that favour this in 1923: high cation charge, small cation size, large anion size and high anion charge.17 Thus AlCl3, with a small, triply charged cation, sublimes as Al2Cl6 molecules at 180 °C rather than behaving as a salt, while NaCl is close to the ionic limit.15 The quantitative measure of ionic character is taken up in section 4.3.

1.3 Crystal lattices

A crystal is described by a lattice (a regular array of points) and a basis (the group of atoms or ions attached to each point); the smallest repeating parallelepiped that shows the full symmetry is the unit cell.18 In an ionic crystal each ion is surrounded by a coordination shell of counter-ions, and the number in that shell is the coordination number. Shannon's effective ionic radii are 102 pm for six-coordinate Na+, 174 pm for eight-coordinate Cs+ and 181 pm for Cl−.20 For NaCl, r+/r− = 102/181 = 0.56, within the octahedral range 0.414–0.732, consistent with the observed 6:6 rock-salt structure. 1.4 Lattice energy

The lattice energy is the energy required to separate one mole of a crystalline ionic solid into its gaseous ions at infinite separation, for example NaCl(s) → Na+(g) + Cl−(g). Defined this way it is positive; the corresponding enthalpy change ΔHL at 298 K differs from the 0 K energy UL by only a few kilojoules per mole (about 2RT = 5 kJ mol−1 for a 1:1 salt).11,13 Lattice energy cannot be measured directly. It is calculated from an electrostatic model or obtained indirectly from a thermochemical cycle (section 1.5).

Madelung energy

Summing the Coulomb interactions of one ion with all others in the lattice gives an electrostatic energy proportional to that of a single ion pair at the nearest-neighbour distance r0. The proportionality constant, which depends only on the geometry of the structure, is the Madelung constant M, first evaluated by Madelung in 1918.21 For one mole of a lattice with ionic charges z+ and z−,

ECoulomb=−NAM|z+z−|e24πε0r0(1)

The Madelung constant exceeds 1 (Table 1): in rock salt, for instance, the attraction of the six nearest neighbours is partly offset by the repulsion of twelve next-nearest like ions, then reinforced by eight further counter-ions, and so on, and the alternating series converges to 1.7476.18

Born–Landé and Born–Mayer equations

Ions cannot interpenetrate, so a short-range repulsion opposes the Coulomb attraction. Born and Landé represented it by a term proportional to 1/rn; minimizing the total energy with respect to r gives the Born–Landé equation11,22

UL=NAM|z+z−|e24πε0r0(1−1n)(2)

The Born exponent n is obtained from compressibility data and depends on the electron configuration of the ions: about 5 for a He-like core, 7 for Ne, 9 for Ar, 10 for Kr and 12 for Xe, averaged over cation and anion.1,11 Born and Mayer later replaced the power law with an exponential repulsion, B e−r/ρ, which is better founded quantum mechanically; this replaces (1 − 1/n) by (1 − ρ/r0), with ρ ≈ 0.345 Å for most salts.11,23

Worked example 3 · Electrostatic lattice energy of NaCl

For rock-salt NaCl, M = 1.7476, z+ = z− = 1 and r0 = a/2 = 281.5 pm.18 Using NAe2/4πε0 = 1.38935 × 10−4 J m mol−1 (from CODATA constants),14 the Madelung energy is 1.7476 × 1.38935 × 10−4/2.815 × 10−10 = 862.5 kJ mol−1. Na+ is Ne-like (n = 7) and Cl− Ar-like (n = 9), so n = 8 and the Born–Landé equation gives UL = 862.5 × (1 − 1/8) = 755 kJ mol−1. The Born–Mayer form with ρ = 34.5 pm gives 862.5 × (1 − 34.5/281.5) = 757 kJ mol−1. Both lie about 4% below the thermochemical value of 787 kJ mol−1 found in Worked example 5; most of the gap is the dispersion (van der Waals) attraction between the ions, which the simple models omit, and the small difference between UL and ΔHL.11

Kapustinskii equation

Kapustinskii observed that M divided by the number of ions in the formula unit, ν, is nearly the same for many structure types, and that r0 can be approximated by the sum of the ionic radii. This allows the lattice energy to be estimated without knowing the crystal structure:24

UL≈Kν|z+z−|r++r−(1−dr++r−)(3)

with K ≈ 1.20 × 10−4 J m mol−1 and d = 0.345 Å (the Born–Mayer ρ).11,24 (Slightly different values of K appear in the secondary literature, depending on unit conversions and on the radius set used.) The equation is most valuable for estimating the lattice energies of compounds whose structures are unknown, and thereby the stability of hypothetical compounds.

Worked example 4 · Kapustinskii estimates for NaCl and MgO

With six-coordinate Shannon radii (Na+ 102 pm, Mg2+ 72 pm, Cl− 181 pm, O2− 140 pm)20 and ν = 2:

NaCl: UL ≈ (1.2025 × 10−4 × 2 × 1/2.83 × 10−10)(1 − 0.345/2.83) = 850 × 0.878 = 746 kJ mol−1.

MgO: UL ≈ (1.2025 × 10−4 × 2 × 4/2.12 × 10−10)(1 − 0.345/2.12) = 4538 × 0.837 = 3800 kJ mol−1.

Doubling both ionic charges multiplies the lattice energy by four through the |z+z−| factor, and the smaller ions add a further 30%. This is why MgO melts more than 2000 °C higher than NaCl even though the two have the same structure.

1.5 Born–Haber cycles

In 1919 Born and Haber independently showed how the lattice energy can be found from measurable quantities by applying Hess's law to a closed cycle linking the elements, the gaseous atoms, the gaseous ions and the solid.25,26 Because enthalpy is a state function, the standard enthalpy of formation of the solid equals the sum of the enthalpies of the individual steps along any path from the elements. For a 1:1 halide MX:

ΔfH°(MX,s)=ΔfH°(M,g)+ΔfH°(X,g)+I(M)−Eea(X)−ΔLH(4)

All terms except ΔLH are measured: enthalpies of atomization by calorimetry and vapour-pressure measurements, ionization energies spectroscopically, electron affinities by photodetachment, and enthalpies of formation by calorimetry.11,13 (Ionization energies and electron affinities are 0 K energies; converting them to 298 K enthalpies adds +5⁄2RT to the ionization step and −5⁄2RT to the attachment step, which cancel in equation (4).)13

Worked example 5 · Born–Haber cycle for NaCl

The steps, in kJ mol−1:

  • Na(s) → Na(g): ΔfH°(Na,g) = +107.52
  • ½Cl2(g) → Cl(g): ΔfH°(Cl,g) = +121.32
  • Na(g) → Na+(g) + e−: I = 5.1391 eV = +495.85
  • Cl(g) + e− → Cl−(g): −Eea = −3.6127 eV = −348.66
  • Na(s) + ½Cl2(g) → NaCl(s): ΔfH° = −411.13

Rearranging equation (4): ΔLH = 107.5 + 121.3 + 495.8 − 348.6 − (−411.1) = 787.2 kJ mol−1. The agreement to within 4% with the purely electrostatic Born–Landé value (755 kJ mol−1, Worked example 3) is the main quantitative evidence that bonding in NaCl is very largely ionic.11

Comparing Born–Haber and electrostatic lattice energies is a diagnostic for covalency. For the alkali halides the two agree to within a few per cent; for salts of polarizing cations with polarizable anions, such as the silver halides, the thermochemical value exceeds the ionic-model value by much more, because the extra covalent bonding is not captured by the electrostatic model.11,15 The cycle can also be run in reverse, using a calculated lattice energy to find a quantity that cannot be measured, as the next example does.

Worked example 6 · MgO and the second electron affinity of oxygen

The cycle for MgO needs the enthalpy of O−(g) + e− → O2−(g), which cannot be measured because O2− is not bound as a free ion. Known terms (kJ mol−1): ΔfH°(Mg,g) = 147.1 and ΔfH°(O,g) = 249.2;2 I1 + I2 of Mg = 7.6462 + 15.0353 eV = 2188.4;5 first electron affinity of O = 1.4611 eV = 141.0;6 ΔfH°(MgO,s) = −601.6.27 Take the lattice energy from the Born–Mayer equation with M = 1.7476, |z+z−| = 4 and r0 = 4.20 Å/2 = 210 pm:18 UL = 4625 × (1 − 34.5/210) = 3865 kJ mol−1. Then

ΔHea2=−601.6−(147.1+249.2+2188.4−141.0)+3865≈+820 kJ mol−1

(The Born–Landé form, with n = 7, gives +920 kJ mol−1; the result is model-dependent, but its sign and magnitude are not in doubt.) Forming O2− from O− is strongly endothermic, as is ionizing Mg+ to Mg2+. Magnesium oxide is nevertheless very stable (ΔfH° = −601.6 kJ mol−1) because its lattice energy, roughly five times that of NaCl, repays both costs.11

2 Covalent bonding

A covalent bond is formed when two atoms share one or more pairs of electrons. Unlike the ionic bond it is directional, which is why covalently bonded substances form discrete molecules of definite shape (or, in solids such as diamond and silicon, networks with definite bond angles).11 Covalent bonding dominates between atoms of similar, fairly high electronegativity, that is, among the nonmetals.

2.1 Electron sharing

Lewis proposed in 1916 that "two atoms may conform to the rule of eight … by holding one or more pairs of electrons in common", the shared pair counting towards the valence shell of both atoms.8 The physical reason sharing lowers the energy was supplied by Heitler and London's 1927 quantum-mechanical treatment of H2. Writing the molecular wavefunction as a combination of products of hydrogen-atom orbitals, with each electron allowed to be on either nucleus, they found a strongly attractive energy only for the combination with the two electron spins paired; the binding arises from an exchange term with no classical analogue, and corresponds to a build-up of electron density between the nuclei that attracts both.10,13 The opposite (spin-parallel) combination is repulsive at all distances. This is the origin of the electron pair as the unit of the covalent bond. The orbital descriptions that grew from this work, valence-bond theory and molecular-orbital theory, are developed in Molecular Structure.

Figure 3 shows the potential-energy curve that results: as two atoms approach, the energy falls to a minimum at the equilibrium bond length re and then rises steeply as nuclear and core repulsion take over. The depth of the well below the separated atoms is the equilibrium dissociation energy De; the energy actually needed to dissociate the molecule from its lowest vibrational level is the smaller quantity D0, the difference being the zero-point vibrational energy.13

The number of shared electron pairs between two atoms is the bond order. Single, double and triple bonds have bond orders 1, 2 and 3, and fractional orders arise naturally from resonance (section 3.5) and from molecular-orbital theory. Two empirical regularities hold across almost all of chemistry: for a given pair of atoms, higher bond order means a shorter and stronger bond (Table 3).1,11

2.2 Single bonds

A single bond is one shared pair. In H2 it joins two atoms that each contribute one 1s electron; in Cl2, each chlorine contributes one 3p electron and keeps three lone pairs; in ethane, the C–C bond joins two tetrahedral carbon atoms. In orbital language a single bond is a σ bond, with cylindrical symmetry about the internuclear axis.13 That symmetry means rotation about a single bond does not break it, so groups joined by single bonds rotate relatively freely at room temperature, giving rise to conformations rather than separable isomers.29 Single bonds are the longest and weakest of the bonds between a given pair of atoms: the C–C bond in ethane is 153.6 pm long, the N–N bond in hydrazine 144.6 pm and the O–O bond in hydrogen peroxide 147.5 pm.4 Bonds between atoms carrying lone pairs, such as N–N, O–O and F–F, are unusually weak for their length, which is commonly attributed to repulsion between the lone pairs on adjacent atoms.11

2.3 Double bonds

A double bond comprises two shared pairs, in orbital terms one σ bond and one π bond, the π bond formed by side-on overlap of p orbitals perpendicular to the bond axis.13 The π overlap requires the p orbitals on the two atoms to stay parallel, so rotation about a double bond would break the π bond. As a result the six atoms of ethylene lie in one plane, and substituted alkenes exist as separable cis and trans (Z and E) isomers that do not interconvert at room temperature.29 The C=C bond in ethylene is 133.9 pm, 20 pm shorter than the single bond in ethane.4 Other common double bonds include C=O in carbonyl compounds and CO2 (116.2 pm in CO2) and O=O in dioxygen (120.75 pm).4,28 Dioxygen is the standard example of a molecule that a simple Lewis structure describes incorrectly: the structure O=O with all electrons paired predicts a diamagnetic molecule, but O2 is paramagnetic with two unpaired electrons, a result explained by molecular-orbital theory.11

2.4 Triple bonds

A triple bond comprises three shared pairs: one σ bond and two mutually perpendicular π bonds. Molecules with a triple bond between two atoms are linear about that bond, as in acetylene (H–C≡C–H) and hydrogen cyanide.13 Triple bonds are the shortest and strongest bonds between a given pair of atoms. The C≡C bond in acetylene is 120.3 pm long;4 the N≡N bond in dinitrogen is 109.8 pm long and requires 945 kJ mol−1 to break (Table 3), which is the principal reason N2 is so unreactive and why fixing atmospheric nitrogen is energetically demanding.15 Carbon monoxide, isoelectronic with N2, has a C≡O bond of 112.8 pm and the largest dissociation energy of any diatomic molecule listed here, 1076 kJ mol−1.2,4

2.5 Bond length

The bond length is the equilibrium distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms. It is measured by rotational (microwave) spectroscopy and gas-phase electron diffraction for free molecules, and by X-ray and neutron diffraction in crystals.13 Different methods measure slightly different quantities (the equilibrium distance re at the potential minimum, or vibrationally averaged distances such as r0), which differ typically by less than 1 pm.28 For a given pair of atoms, bond length depends chiefly on bond order and only weakly on the rest of the molecule, so bond lengths are approximately additive. Each atom can be assigned a covalent radius, and a bond length estimated as the sum of the two radii. Pyykkö and co-workers have derived self-consistent radii separately for single, double and triple bonds; for carbon they are 75, 67 and 60 pm.30–32

Worked example 7 · Estimating carbon–carbon bond lengths from covalent radii

From the Pyykkö radii,30–32 predicted lengths are C–C 2 × 75 = 150 pm, C=C 2 × 67 = 134 pm and C≡C 2 × 60 = 120 pm. The experimental values in ethane, ethylene and acetylene are 153.6, 133.9 and 120.3 pm.4 Each step up in bond order shortens the bond by roughly 15–20 pm. 2.6 Bond strength

"Bond strength" is used informally for how much energy it takes to break a bond, and it is quantified by the bond energies of section 2.7. Two general patterns stand out in Table 3. First, for a given pair of atoms, strength rises with bond order. It does not rise in proportion, however: a C=C bond is less than twice as strong as a C–C bond and a C≡C bond less than three times as strong, because a π bond is weaker than a σ bond. For nitrogen the pattern is reversed, and N≡N is more than three times as strong as the N–N bond in hydrazine, partly because of the lone-pair repulsion that weakens the single bond.11,15 These contrasts explain why carbon forms long chains of single bonds whereas elemental nitrogen exists as N2 rather than as single-bonded chains or rings.15

Second, down a group bonds generally become longer and weaker as the atoms become larger and their valence orbitals more diffuse. The hydrogen halides show this cleanly: the H–X bond energy falls from 570.7 kJ mol−1 for HF to 298.3 kJ mol−1 for HI while the bond length rises from 91.7 to 160.9 pm (Table 3).2,4 Fluorine is an exception among the dihalogens: F–F (158.8 kJ mol−1) is weaker than Cl–Cl (242.6 kJ mol−1), again attributed to repulsion between lone pairs held close together on the small fluorine atoms.11,15 Weak bonds make molecules reactive; the weakness of F–F is one reason fluorine is the most reactive element.15

2.7 Bond energy

Two different quantities are both loosely called "bond energy", and they must be distinguished:34

  • The bond dissociation energy D is the enthalpy (per mole) required to break one specific bond of a specific molecule by homolysis, with the fragments in their ground states, for example HO–H → HO + H. It is a measurable property of that bond in that molecule. Values at 298 K are written D298 or DH298.
  • The mean bond energy (mean bond enthalpy) is the average of the gas-phase bond energies, usually at 298 K, for all bonds of the same type in the same molecule, for example one half of the enthalpy of H2O(g) → 2H(g) + O(g). Tabulated "average bond enthalpies" go one step further and average over many different molecules, and are therefore only transferable estimates.

For a diatomic molecule the two coincide. For any gaseous molecule the total atomization enthalpy is exactly the sum of its successive bond dissociation energies, and it can be obtained from standard enthalpies of formation by Hess's law. All the diatomic bond energies in Table 3 were calculated this way from CODATA enthalpies of formation; for example, D298(H–Cl) = ΔfH°(H,g) + ΔfH°(Cl,g) − ΔfH°(HCl,g) = 218.0 + 121.3 + 92.3 = 431.6 kJ mol−1.2

Worked example 8 · Bond dissociation energies and mean bond energy in water

Atomization enthalpy of H2O(g): 2ΔfH°(H,g) + ΔfH°(O,g) − ΔfH°(H2O,g) = 2(218.0) + 249.2 + 241.8 = 927.0 kJ mol−1.2 The mean O–H bond energy is therefore 927.0/2 = 463.5 kJ mol−1.

The first bond dissociation energy, D298(HO–H), is 118.82 kcal mol−1 = 497.1 kJ mol−1.33 The second, for the OH radical, follows by difference: 927.0 − 497.1 = 429.9 kJ mol−1. Neither individual bond dissociation energy equals the mean bond energy. Removing the first hydrogen leaves an OH radical whose remaining bond is weaker, because the electronic structure of the fragment differs from that of the parent.33

Mean bond enthalpies are useful for estimating enthalpies of reaction when thermochemical data are lacking: ΔrH ≈ Σ(bond energies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond energies of bonds formed), for gas-phase species.13 The estimates are typically good to some tens of kilojoules per mole, the error arising because a given bond type has somewhat different energies in different molecules (compare 377.0 kJ mol−1 for the C–C bond dissociation energy of ethane with a tabulated average C–C bond enthalpy of 348 kJ mol−1).13,33

2.8 Bond polarity

When the two atoms of a bond differ in electronegativity, the shared electrons are drawn towards the more electronegative atom, which acquires a partial negative charge δ− while its partner acquires δ+. The bond then has an electric dipole moment μ = qr, of magnitude equal to the separated charge times the bond length, conventionally pointing from the negative to the positive end in physics (IUPAC) usage; chemists often draw the arrow from δ+ to δ−.13,35 Dipole moments are reported in debye, 1 D = 3.33564 × 10−30 C m.35 Bond polarity affects reactivity (polar bonds are attacked at their δ+ or δ− ends), bond strength (Pauling's ionic-resonance excess, section 4.2) and intermolecular forces.

HCl has μ = 1.093 D and re = 127.46 pm.4 Treating the bond dipole as two point charges ±δe separated by re:

3 Lewis theory

Lewis theory is a set of bookkeeping rules for distributing valence electrons in a molecule as bonding pairs and lone pairs. It makes no use of quantum mechanics, yet it predicts connectivity, bond order, the location of charge and (combined with VSEPR theory) molecular shape for most compounds of the main-group elements.8,11,36 Its limitations are equally instructive and are collected in sections 3.6–3.8.

3.1 Lewis symbols

A Lewis symbol is the chemical symbol of an element surrounded by one dot for each valence electron.8 For main-group elements the number of valence electrons equals the group number for groups 1 and 2 and the group number minus 10 for groups 13–18. The dots are placed singly on the four sides of the symbol before any are paired, so that unpaired dots show the number of bonds an atom typically forms (Figure 4).11 Carbon, with four single dots, typically forms four bonds; nitrogen three; oxygen two; fluorine one. The noble gases have a complete set of four pairs.

Lewis symbols also describe ion formation: Na· + ·Cl (with its seven dots) gives Na+ and [:Cl:]− with a full octet, the electron-transfer picture of section 1.1 in Lewis notation.11

3.2 Lewis structures

A Lewis structure shows every valence electron of a molecule or ion, with each shared pair drawn as a line (a bond) and each unshared pair as two dots (a lone pair). A systematic procedure:11,29

  1. Count the total valence electrons, adding one for each negative charge and subtracting one for each positive charge.
  2. Choose a skeleton. The central atom is usually the least electronegative atom other than hydrogen; hydrogen and fluorine are always terminal.
  3. Join each terminal atom to the central atom with a single bond (two electrons each).
  4. Complete the octets of the terminal atoms with lone pairs (hydrogen needs only two electrons).
  5. Place any remaining electrons on the central atom.
  6. If the central atom lacks an octet, convert lone pairs on neighbouring atoms into multiple bonds until it has one.
  7. Assign formal charges (section 3.4) and, if more than one structure is possible, prefer the one with the smallest formal charges.

Worked example 10 · Lewis structure of hydrogen cyanide

Valence electrons: H 1 + C 4 + N 5 = 10. Skeleton H–C–N (carbon is the less electronegative heavy atom, hydrogen is terminal). Two single bonds use 4 electrons. Completing nitrogen's octet with three lone pairs uses 6 more, leaving carbon with only four electrons. Converting two of nitrogen's lone pairs into bonding pairs gives H–C≡N:, in which carbon (two bonds to H and four shared with N) and nitrogen (six shared plus one lone pair) both have octets and every atom has zero formal charge. Consistent with a triple bond, the C≡N bond in HCN is short, 115.6 pm,4 close to the sum of the triple-bond covalent radii of carbon and nitrogen (60 + 54 = 114 pm).32

3.3 Octet rule

The octet rule states that atoms of the main-group elements tend to gain, lose or share electrons until each is surrounded by eight valence electrons, the configuration of the following noble gas (two, the duplet, for hydrogen).8,9 Lewis's 1916 paper stated it as the "rule of eight" in connection with his model of the cubic atom; Langmuir gave it the name "octet".8,9 The quantum-mechanical rationale is that eight electrons fill the valence s and three p orbitals (ns2np6), beyond which the next available orbitals lie much higher in energy.11 The rule is reliable for the period 2 elements carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine, which have only four valence orbitals. It fails in three recognised ways: some molecules have more than eight electrons around an atom (section 3.6), some fewer (section 3.7), and molecules with an odd total number of electrons cannot give every atom an octet (section 3.8).11

3.4 Formal charge

The formal charge of an atom in a Lewis structure is the charge it would carry if the electrons in every bond were shared exactly equally, regardless of electronegativity.37 IUPAC defines it as

Zformal=Nvalence−Nlone-pair electrons−12Nbonding electrons(5)

where Nvalence is the number of valence electrons of the free atom.37 The formal charges in a structure always sum to the overall charge of the species. Formal charge is a bookkeeping device and should not be confused with oxidation state, which assigns all bonding electrons to the more electronegative atom, or with the real partial charge, which lies somewhere between the two extremes.11 Its main use is choosing among alternative Lewis structures, with three guidelines: structures with formal charges closest to zero are preferred; adjacent atoms should not carry formal charges of the same sign; and negative formal charge should reside preferentially on the more electronegative atoms.11,29

Worked example 11 · Formal charges in N2O

In structure (a), N=N=O: the terminal N has 5 valence electrons, 4 lone-pair electrons and 4 bonding electrons, so Z = 5 − 4 − 2 = −1; the central N has 5 − 0 − 4 = +1; oxygen 6 − 4 − 2 = 0. In (b), N≡N–O: terminal N 5 − 2 − 3 = 0, central N +1, oxygen 6 − 6 − 1 = −1. In (c), N–N≡O: terminal N 5 − 6 − 1 = −2, central N +1, oxygen 6 − 2 − 3 = +1.

(c) is rejected. (b) places the negative charge on the more electronegative oxygen, and (a) also has charges of only ±1, so both contribute significantly. The measured bond lengths, N–N 112.8 pm and N–O 118.4 pm,4 are consistent with this: the N–N bond is close to the 109.8 pm triple bond of N2 but slightly longer, as expected from a mixture of N≡N and N=N character. The small measured dipole moment, 0.161 D,4 reflects the near-cancellation of the opposite charge separations in (a) and (b).

3.5 Resonance

For many species no single Lewis structure is adequate, and the real electron distribution is represented as a weighted combination of two or more structures that differ only in the placement of electrons, not of nuclei. The individual structures are called resonance (canonical, contributing) structures and the real species a resonance hybrid; the structures are linked by a double-headed arrow ↔.1,38 Resonance in this sense was introduced by Pauling as a qualitative expression of the quantum-mechanical superposition of valence-bond wavefunctions.39,40 The contributing structures do not exist separately, and the molecule does not oscillate between them; the hybrid is a single, time-independent electronic structure.29,38

Resonance has three measurable consequences. Equivalent bonds have equal lengths, intermediate between those of the single and multiple bonds they average. The hybrid is lower in energy than any single contributing structure, the difference being the resonance (delocalization) energy. And charge is spread over several atoms rather than localized on one.1,11

Worked example 12 · Bond lengths and resonance energy

Ozone. The two Lewis structures O=O+–O− and −O–O+=O are equivalent, so the hybrid has two identical bonds of order 1.5. The measured O–O distance is 127.8 pm,4 between the single bond in H2O2 (147.5 pm) and the double bond in O2 (120.75 pm).4,28

Benzene. The two Kekulé structures give each C–C bond order 1.5, and the measured length is 139.7 pm, between 153.6 pm (ethane) and 133.9 pm (ethylene).4 Thermochemically, Kistiakowsky and co-workers measured the enthalpy of hydrogenation of benzene to cyclohexane as −49.8 kcal mol−1 (−208 kJ mol−1), far less exothermic than three times the value for an isolated cyclohexene-type double bond.41 The shortfall, about 150 kJ mol−1 (36 kcal mol−1), is the conventional thermochemical resonance energy of benzene.1,41

Not all contributing structures carry equal weight. Structures that keep octets, minimise formal charges and place negative charge on electronegative atoms dominate, as in the N2O example above.11,29

3.6 Expanded octets

Elements of period 3 and beyond form many compounds in which the central atom appears to have more than eight valence electrons: PCl5 (ten), SF6 (twelve), XeF2 (ten), XeF4 (twelve), the triiodide ion I3− (ten) and the sulfate ion when drawn with two S=O double bonds (twelve).11,15 Musher named such molecules "hypervalent" in 1969,42 and IUPAC defines hypervalency as "the ability of an atom in a molecular entity to expand its valence shell beyond the limits of the Lewis octet rule".38 No period 2 element forms such compounds: nitrogen forms NF3 but not NF5, while phosphorus forms both PF3 and PF5.15

The older explanation, that the central atom uses empty nd orbitals to form sp3d or sp3d2 hybrids (see Molecular Structure), is now regarded as incorrect for main-group compounds. Quantum-chemical analyses show that d-orbital occupation in molecules such as SF6 is small: the d functions act as polarization functions rather than as true valence orbitals.43 The accepted description instead uses three-centre four-electron bonding, proposed by Pimentel and by Rundle: in a linear F–Xe–F or I–I–I unit, one p orbital of the central atom and one orbital on each terminal atom combine into a bonding, a nonbonding and an antibonding molecular orbital, and four electrons fill the first two.11,44 The bond order of each X–A bond is ½, the nonbonding pair resides on the terminal atoms, and the central atom never actually holds more than eight electrons. This explains why hypervalent compounds need electronegative ligands (F, O, Cl) to accommodate that electron density, and why the larger atoms of period 3 and below, which can accommodate more ligands around them, are the ones that form them.11,43

In Lewis terms, structures with apparent expanded octets are often replaced by structures with octets and formal charges. Sulfate, for example, can be drawn with four S–O single bonds, a formal charge of +2 on sulfur and −1 on each oxygen, which obeys the octet rule but has large formal charges; or with two S=O double bonds, which minimizes formal charge but expands the octet. Modern calculations generally favour the charge-separated, octet-obeying picture with highly polar S–O bonds.11 The disagreement is a reminder that Lewis structures are models.

3.7 Incomplete octets

Atoms with fewer than four valence electrons, notably beryllium, boron and aluminium, often form compounds in which they have fewer than eight electrons. Boron trifluoride, BF3, has six electrons around boron in its simplest Lewis structure.11,15 Such molecules are electron-deficient and strongly electrophilic: BF3 accepts a lone pair from ammonia to form the adduct F3B–NH3, in which boron has an octet. Lewis generalised this behaviour in 1923 into his definition of acids and bases: an acid is an electron-pair acceptor and a base an electron-pair donor.36 Aluminium chloride relieves its electron deficiency differently, by dimerizing to Al2Cl6 through bridging chlorine atoms that donate lone pairs to the neighbouring aluminium.15

For BF3, structures with a B=F double bond (formal charges B−, F+) can be drawn, and their contribution is often invoked to explain the short B–F bond, 130.7 pm,4 compared with 149 pm for the sum of the single-bond covalent radii of boron and fluorine (85 + 64 pm).11,30 Because the double-bonded structures put positive formal charge on the most electronegative element, the structure with the incomplete octet is generally regarded as the main contributor, and a large part of the bond shortening is attributed to the high polarity of the B–F bond.11

3.8 Odd-electron species

A molecule with an odd total number of valence electrons cannot have all its electrons paired, so at least one atom must lack an octet. Such species, with one or more unpaired electrons, are radicals.37 Nitric oxide (NO, 11 valence electrons), nitrogen dioxide (NO2, 17) and chlorine dioxide (ClO2, 19) are stable inorganic examples.15 Gomberg's 1900 preparation of triphenylmethyl, (C6H5)3C·, established that carbon-centred radicals can be persistent in solution.45 Most organic radicals are highly reactive transient intermediates.

Worked example 13 · Nitric oxide and nitrogen dioxide

NO. With 5 + 6 = 11 valence electrons, the best Lewis structure is N=O with one lone pair and the odd electron on nitrogen and two lone pairs on oxygen (Figure 8): oxygen has an octet, nitrogen has seven electrons, and both formal charges are zero. Lewis theory suggests a bond order of 2, but the measured bond length, 115.4 pm,4 is shorter than the 117 pm sum of the double-bond covalent radii of nitrogen and oxygen (60 + 57 pm);31 molecular-orbital theory gives a bond order of 2.5 with the odd electron in a π* orbital, explaining both the short bond and the paramagnetism.11

NO2. With 17 electrons, the structure O=N–O· with the odd electron formally on nitrogen (or on an oxygen) has two equivalent resonance forms, so both N–O bonds are the same length, 119.3 pm, and the O–N–O angle is 134.1°.4 The odd electron makes NO2 reactive towards itself: two molecules pair their odd electrons to form colourless dinitrogen tetroxide, 2NO2 ⇌ N2O4, with an N–N bond.15

4 Electronegativity and polarity

Pauling defined electronegativity as "the power of an atom in a molecule to attract electrons to itself".1 The Pauling, Mulliken, Allred–Rochow and Allen scales, and the periodic trend (rising across a period, falling down a group), are developed in The Periodic Table and Periodic Trends. This section uses Pauling values as revised by Allred (H 2.20, C 2.55, N 3.04, O 3.44, F 3.98, Na 0.93, Cl 3.16, Br 2.96, I 2.66)46 to classify bonds by polarity. The governing idea is that the electronegativity difference Δχ between two bonded atoms determines where a bond lies on the continuum from nonpolar covalent through polar covalent to ionic. There is no sharp boundary between the classes.1,11

4.1 Nonpolar covalent bonds

A bond between identical atoms, as in H2, N2, Cl2 or the C–C bond of ethane, has Δχ = 0. The shared pair is distributed symmetrically, the bond has no dipole moment, and the measured dipole moments of homonuclear diatomic molecules are exactly zero by symmetry (for example, 0.000 D for N2).4 Bonds between different atoms of very similar electronegativity are close to nonpolar. The C–H bond (Δχ = 0.35) is the most important example; its low polarity is why hydrocarbons are insoluble in water and interact mainly through dispersion forces.29 The C–S bond (2.55 vs 2.58) and the P–H bond (2.19 vs 2.20) are nearly nonpolar on the Pauling scale.46

4.2 Polar covalent bonds

When Δχ is appreciable but not large, the electrons are shared unequally and the bond is polar covalent: the hydrogen halides, O–H, N–H, C–O, C–F and C–Cl bonds are examples. Pauling noticed that such bonds are stronger than the average of the corresponding homonuclear bonds, and attributed the excess to the contribution of ionic structures (A+B−) to the bond.47 His electronegativity scale was built from this excess, Δ, through |χA − χB| = √(Δ/eV); a worked example for HCl is given in the periodic-trends article.

4.4 Molecular dipole moments

The dipole moment of a molecule is, to a first approximation, the vector sum of its bond dipoles together with contributions from lone pairs.13 It is measured from the Stark splitting of rotational lines in microwave spectra or from the temperature dependence of the dielectric constant of a gas.13 Whether a molecule is polar depends on both bond polarity and geometry: polar bonds arranged symmetrically give a zero resultant. Linear CO2, trigonal planar BF3 and tetrahedral CCl4 are nonpolar even though their bonds are polar, while bent H2O and pyramidal NH3 are polar.4 The geometries themselves are explained by VSEPR theory in Molecular Structure.

Chloromethanes. 5 Metallic bonding

About three-quarters of the elements are metals.15 Their characteristic properties are high electrical and thermal conductivity, lustre, malleability and ductility, and an electrical resistivity that increases with temperature.18 Neither the ionic nor the localized covalent model accounts for these: metal atoms have too few valence electrons to form electron-pair bonds to all of their eight or twelve nearest neighbours. Metallic bonding is instead described in terms of valence electrons delocalized over the whole crystal.

5.1 Electron-sea model

In 1900 Drude proposed that the valence electrons of a metal form a gas of free charged particles moving among the fixed positive ion cores, and applied the kinetic theory of gases to them.50 In this electron-sea (free-electron) picture the bonding is the electrostatic attraction between the lattice of cations and the delocalized electron gas that surrounds them. Because that attraction does not depend on the direction to any particular neighbour, the bonding is non-directional.11,51

The model explains the main metallic properties qualitatively. Free electrons accelerate in an applied field, giving high electrical conductivity σ = ne2τ/me, where n is the electron density and τ the mean time between collisions. The same mobile electrons carry heat, so good electrical conductors are good thermal conductors. They absorb and re-emit visible light over a wide range of frequencies, giving lustre. Layers of cations can slide past one another without breaking directional bonds, because the electron sea adjusts around them, giving malleability and ductility in place of the brittleness of ionic solids.18,51

The strength of metallic bonding is measured by the enthalpy of atomization (Table 6). It rises with the number of valence electrons per atom across a period (Na, Mg, Al) and falls down a group as atoms grow larger (Li to Cs). Mercury, with its filled 5d106s2 shell and strongly bound 6s electrons, has the smallest atomization enthalpy in the table and is the only metal that is liquid at room temperature.2,15

The free-electron model remains a good description of the simple s- and p-block metals. It cannot, however, explain why some elements and compounds are insulators or semiconductors, why some metals have Hall coefficients of the "wrong" (positive) sign, or why conductivity depends on crystal direction. These require the effect of the periodic lattice on the electrons, which is the subject of band theory.18,51

5.2 Band theory

Bloch showed in 1929 that the wavefunctions of an electron in a periodic potential have the form of a plane wave modulated by a function with the periodicity of the lattice, ψk(r) = uk(r) eik·r (Bloch's theorem).53 The allowed energies form continuous bands separated by forbidden energy gaps, which open where electron waves are Bragg-reflected by the lattice.18

Chemists usually reach the same result from the other direction, by extending molecular-orbital theory (developed in Molecular Structure) to an infinite solid.54 Combining one atomic orbital from each of N atoms gives N molecular orbitals, ranging from fully bonding at the bottom to fully antibonding at the top. As N grows towards 1023, the spacing between these levels becomes vanishingly small while the total width, set by the strength of overlap between neighbouring orbitals, stays finite, so the levels merge into a band (Figure 10).11,54 Each band built from N orbitals holds 2N electrons. s orbitals give an s band, p orbitals a p band, and so on; bands from different atomic orbitals may be separated by a gap or may overlap.

Wilson used this band picture in 1931 to explain the difference between metals and insulators.55 Electrons can carry a current only if there are empty states at energies just above the occupied ones into which they can be accelerated by a field:

  • Metals have a partly filled band. In sodium the 3s band, built from one 3s orbital per atom, holds 2N electrons but contains only N, so it is half full and the Fermi level lies in the middle of the band.11,18 Magnesium, with two 3s electrons per atom, would fill its 3s band exactly and be an insulator if the 3s band were isolated; it is a metal because the 3s and 3p bands overlap, leaving both partly filled.11
  • Insulators have completely filled bands separated from the next empty band by a large gap. Diamond's gap is about 5.4 eV, so essentially no electrons are thermally excited across it at room temperature.18
  • Intrinsic semiconductors are insulators with small gaps (Si 1.11 eV, Ge 0.66 eV, GaAs 1.43 eV at 300 K),18 across which a small fraction of electrons is thermally excited, leaving mobile holes behind. Their conductivity rises steeply with temperature, the opposite of the behaviour of metals.18,55

Worked example 17 · Why the size of the gap matters

The density of thermally excited carriers in an intrinsic semiconductor varies approximately as exp(−Eg/2kBT).18 At 300 K, kBT = 0.02585 eV.14 For silicon (Eg = 1.11 eV) the exponential factor is exp(−1.11/0.0517) ≈ 5 × 10−10; for diamond (5.4 eV) it is exp(−5.4/0.0517) ≈ 4 × 10−46. A fivefold larger gap therefore reduces the carrier concentration by about 36 orders of magnitude (ignoring the weaker temperature-dependent prefactor), which is why diamond is an excellent insulator and silicon a semiconductor.

Band theory also explains the temperature dependence of metallic resistivity. In a perfect, static lattice, Bloch electrons would travel without scattering; resistance arises from lattice vibrations (phonons) and imperfections, and since phonon populations increase with temperature, the resistivity of a metal rises as it is heated.18,53 For a chemist, the band picture unifies the three limiting bond types: a metal is a solid whose highest occupied band is partly filled, while ionic and covalent solids are insulators or semiconductors whose filled valence bands are derived mainly from anion orbitals (in ionic solids) or from bonding combinations (in covalent networks such as diamond and silicon).54
