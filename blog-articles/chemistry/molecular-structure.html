Chemistry Series

Molecular Structure

How the valence-shell electron-pair repulsion model predicts molecular shape, how the eleven common geometries compare with measured structures, and how valence bond and molecular orbital theory explain the bonds that hold those shapes together.

Abstract

The three-dimensional arrangement of atoms in a molecule fixes its symmetry, polarity, spectroscopy and reactivity. This article treats molecular structure through three complementary models. The valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) model, which grew out of work by Tsuchida, Sidgwick and Powell, and Gillespie and Nyholm,1–3 predicts the arrangement of electron domains around a central atom and from it the molecular shape; its predictions for eleven common geometries are compared with gas-phase structures from the NIST Computational Chemistry Comparison and Benchmark Database and the spectroscopic literature.4 Valence bond theory, founded on the Heitler–London treatment of H2 and extended by Pauling and Slater,5–7 describes bonds as overlapping orbitals, distinguishes σ from π bonds and introduces hybrid orbitals; the article also sets out the modern evidence against d-orbital participation in "sp3d" and "sp3d2" main-group molecules.8,9 Molecular orbital theory, developed by Hund, Mulliken and Lennard-Jones,10,11 builds delocalized orbitals from atomic ones and classifies them as bonding, antibonding or nonbonding; it supplies bond orders, the frontier HOMO and LUMO, and the magnetic behaviour of molecules such as O2. Lewis structures, formal charge, resonance, bond polarity and dipole moments are developed in the companion article Chemical Bonding, and the shapes of atomic orbitals in Quantum Chemistry and Electronic Structure; both are used here without re-derivation.

1 VSEPR theory

The idea that the shape of a molecule follows from the number of electron pairs in the valence shell of its central atom was proposed by Tsuchida in 19391 and, independently, by Sidgwick and Powell in their 1940 Bakerian Lecture, who surveyed the known structures and noted that the arrangement of bonds depended on the total number of valence-shell electron pairs, shared and unshared.2 Gillespie and Nyholm turned this observation into a predictive model in 1957 by adding rules for the different sizes of lone-pair and bonding-pair domains and for multiple bonds,3 and Gillespie developed it over the following five decades into what is now called the valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) model.12,13

The model rests on one premise: the electron pairs in the valence shell of a central atom A behave as if they repel one another and adopt the arrangement that keeps them as far apart as possible.12 Gillespie later reframed "repulsion" in terms of the Pauli exclusion principle: electrons of the same spin are kept apart, so each pair occupies its own region of space, or domain, and domains behave as if they avoid one another.13,14 The model needs only a Lewis structure (see Chemical Bonding) and no quantum-mechanical calculation, which is why it remains the first tool used to predict molecular shape.

Molecules are classified with the AXmEn notation, where A is the central atom, X a bonded atom (ligand) and E a lone pair.12 The steric number (SN) is m + n, the total number of electron domains around A.

1.1 Electron-domain geometry

The electron-domain geometry is the arrangement of all the electron domains (bonding and nonbonding) around the central atom. A domain is counted as follows:3,12

  • each single bond counts as one domain;
  • a double or triple bond also counts as one domain, because its two or three electron pairs are held in the same region between the two nuclei;
  • each lone pair counts as one domain;
  • a single unpaired electron, as in a radical, occupies a smaller domain and is usually counted as one.

For SN = 2 to 6, the arrangements that maximise the separation of equivalent domains on a sphere around the nucleus are the five shown in Table 1.3,12 All but the trigonal bipyramid have every position equivalent. In the trigonal bipyramid the two axial positions each have three nearest neighbours at 90°, while the three equatorial positions have only two neighbours at 90°, so the two kinds of site are not equivalent, and this matters greatly when lone pairs are present (Section 1.3).3,12

Worked example 1. Counting electron domains

SO2. Sulfur has six valence electrons. In the Lewis structure each oxygen is joined to S by a bond that counts as one domain whatever its order, and one lone pair remains on S. SN = 2 + 1 = 3, so the electron-domain geometry is trigonal planar (AX2E).

ClF3. Chlorine has seven valence electrons; three are used in Cl–F bonds and four remain as two lone pairs. SN = 3 + 2 = 5: trigonal bipyramidal electron-domain geometry (AX3E2).

XeF4. Xenon has eight valence electrons; four form Xe–F bonds and four remain as two lone pairs. SN = 4 + 2 = 6: octahedral electron-domain geometry (AX4E2).

I3−. The central iodine carries seven valence electrons plus the extra electron, eight in all; two are used in bonds to the terminal iodines and six remain as three lone pairs. SN = 2 + 3 = 5: trigonal bipyramidal (AX2E3). The molecular shapes that follow are derived in Sections 2.1, 2.2, 2.8 and 2.11.

1.2 Molecular geometry

The molecular geometry (or molecular shape) describes the positions of the atoms only. Lone pairs influence the shape but are not part of its name, because experimental structure methods such as microwave spectroscopy, gas-phase electron diffraction and X-ray crystallography locate nuclei, not lone pairs.12,15 When every domain is a bond (n = 0), the molecular geometry and the electron-domain geometry coincide. When lone pairs are present, the molecular geometry is the shape traced out by the remaining atoms. Table 2 lists every combination for SN = 2 to 6; the eleven shapes named in the right-hand column are the subjects of Section 2.

Worked example 2. Same electron-domain geometry, different shapes

CH4, NH3 and H2O each have SN = 4 and a tetrahedral electron-domain geometry. Replacing one bond by a lone pair (NH3, AX3E) leaves the three hydrogen atoms at the base of a pyramid with N at the apex: a trigonal pyramid. Replacing two (H2O, AX2E2) leaves three atoms that define a plane: a bent molecule. By contrast BF3 (AX3, SN = 3) and NH3 (AX3E, SN = 4) both have three ligands but different shapes, because the lone pair on nitrogen is a fourth domain. The measured structures bear this out: BF3 is planar with F–B–F = 120°, while NH3 is pyramidal with H–N–H = 106.67°.4,16,17

1.3 Lone-pair effects

Gillespie and Nyholm's key refinement was that electron domains are not all the same size. A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so its domain is larger and more spread out around the central atom than a bonding domain, which is drawn out towards the ligand.3,12 Domains therefore push one another apart in the order

lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair

and this ordering produces three observable consequences.3,13

Compression of bond angles

Each lone pair that replaces a bond squeezes the remaining bonding domains closer together. The series CH4, NH3, H2O shows the effect cleanly (Table 3): the H–X–H angle falls from the tetrahedral value as lone pairs are added.4,17–19

Placement of lone pairs

Where the sites are inequivalent, lone pairs occupy the positions that minimise lone pair–lone pair and lone pair–bond interactions at 90°, the shortest distance at which domains meet.3,12 In a trigonal bipyramid an equatorial site has two 90° neighbours and an axial site has three, so lone pairs go equatorial: this gives the seesaw (one lone pair), T-shaped (two) and linear (three) structures. In an octahedron all sites are equivalent for the first lone pair; a second goes trans to the first, at 180°, rather than cis at 90°, which gives the square planar AX4E2 shape.3,15

Ligand electronegativity, atom size and multiple bonds

Three further rules extend the model.12,13 First, an electronegative ligand draws its bonding pair away from the central atom, shrinking that domain and letting the angle close: F–O–F in OF2 (103.1°) is smaller than H–O–H in H2O (104.48°).4,19,20 Second, a larger, less electronegative central atom holds its bonding pairs less tightly near the nucleus, so bonding domains take up a smaller fraction of the valence shell and the angles close further; in PH3 and H2S they approach 90°.4,17,21 Third, a double or triple bond forms a larger domain than a single bond, so the angle between two single bonds next to a multiple bond is compressed: H–C–H is 116.13° in formaldehyde and 117.6° in ethylene, not 120°.4,17,22

Worked example 3. Ranking bond angles

Problem. Rank NH3, CH4 and H2O by bond angle, and predict whether SO2 has an O–S–O angle above or below 120°.

Solution. All three hydrides have SN = 4. Lone pairs exert the stronger push, so the angle falls with the number of lone pairs: CH4 (0) > NH3 (1) > H2O (2). The measured values are 109.47°, 106.67° and 104.48°.4 SO2 is AX2E with SN = 3; one lone pair should compress the angle below 120°, and the measured angle is 119.5°.4,17 The compression is small because the two S–O bond domains carry multiple-bond character and are themselves large.12

Limits of the model

VSEPR is a qualitative model, and its known failures help mark where it applies. The gas-phase dihalides of the heavier alkaline-earth metals, such as SrF2 and BaF2, are bent rather than linear as AX2 predicts; calculations attribute this to polarisation of the metal's outer core and to small covalent contributions from its d orbitals.13,23 Transition-metal compounds are generally outside the model, because their nonbonding d electrons do not form stereochemically active domains in the way main-group lone pairs do; their shapes are better described by ligand-field and molecular orbital arguments.12,15 Finally, the "rabbit-ear" picture of two identical lone pairs on water is a convenience of the model, not a physical result: symmetry requires the two lone-pair orbitals of H2O to be inequivalent, one of σ type in the molecular plane and one a pure p orbital perpendicular to it (Section 4.3).24 VSEPR predicts the shape correctly while describing the lone pairs only schematically.

2 Common geometries

This section takes the eleven molecular geometries in turn. For each one it gives the VSEPR class, the ideal angles, a representative molecule and its measured gas-phase structure. Figure 1 shows all eleven together. Unless noted, structural data are from the NIST Computational Chemistry Comparison and Benchmark Database (CCCBDB), which collects experimental geometries together with their primary sources;4 the primary source is cited alongside each value.

2.1 Linear

Classes AX2 and AX2E3; angle 180°; point group D∞h for symmetric A–X2. Two domains on opposite sides of A give a linear molecule. Carbon dioxide is the standard example: each C=O double bond is a single domain, so O=C=O is linear, with C–O = 1.162 Å.4,17 Acetylene, H–C≡C–H, is linear about each carbon for the same reason (C≡C 1.203 Å, C–H 1.063 Å).4,16

The second route to linearity is AX2E3. Xenon difluoride has SN = 5; its three lone pairs take the three equatorial sites of a trigonal bipyramid, leaving the two fluorines axial at 180°.3,12 The Xe–F distance is 1.974 Å.4,25 The triiodide ion I3− is linear for the same reason.15

2.2 Bent

Classes AX2E (from trigonal planar) and AX2E2 (from tetrahedral); point group C2v. A bent (V-shaped or angular) molecule has three atoms that are not collinear. Two different parent geometries give bent shapes, and they lead to different angles:

  • AX2E, ideal angle just under 120°: SO2, O–S–O = 119.5°, S–O = 1.432 Å;4,17 O3, O–O–O = 116.8°, O–O = 1.278 Å.4,17
  • AX2E2, ideal angle under 109.5°: H2O, H–O–H = 104.48°, O–H = 0.958 Å;4,19 OF2, 103.1°;4,20 H2S, 92.11°.4,21

The bent shape of water is responsible for its permanent dipole moment, discussed in Chemical Bonding.

2.3 Trigonal planar

Class AX3; angle 120°; point group D3h for AX3 with identical ligands. Three domains lie in a plane at 120°. Boron trifluoride is the archetype, with B–F = 1.307 Å and F–B–F = 120°.4,16 Boron has only six valence electrons in BF3, and the vacant orbital perpendicular to the molecular plane makes BF3 a Lewis acid (Section 4.6).15 Carbon centres with one double bond, as in formaldehyde, ethylene and the carbonate ion, are also trigonal planar, although inequivalent ligands distort the angles (H–C–H 116.13°, H–C–O 121.9° in H2CO).4,22

2.4 Trigonal pyramidal

Class AX3E; ideal angle < 109.5°; point group C3v. One lone pair at a tetrahedral site leaves three ligands forming the base of a pyramid with A at the apex. Ammonia has N–H = 1.012 Å and H–N–H = 106.67°;4,17 phosphine has H–P–H = 93.3°.4,17 A trigonal pyramid with three different substituents is chiral, but amines invert rapidly through a planar transition state, so their enantiomers usually cannot be isolated.15

2.5 Tetrahedral

Class AX4; angle 109.47° (Eq. 1); point group Td. Four bonding domains point to the corners of a regular tetrahedron. In methane every H–C–H angle is 109.47° and C–H = 1.087 Å.4,18 The tetrahedral carbon is the structural basis of organic stereochemistry, and the same geometry appears in NH4+, SiCl4, SO42− and many other species.15 Unequal substituents lower the symmetry: in ethane, H–C–C is 110.91°.4,17

2.6 Trigonal bipyramidal

Class AX5; angles 90°, 120°, 180°; point group D3h. Three equatorial ligands lie in a plane at 120°, and two axial ligands sit above and below it. Because the axial sites have more neighbours at 90° (Section 1.1), axial bonds are generally longer than equatorial ones. In PF5, P–Fax = 1.577 Å and P–Feq = 1.534 Å;4,26 in gaseous PCl5, P–Clax = 2.124 Å and P–Cleq = 2.020 Å.4,22

Although axial and equatorial fluorines are structurally distinct, the 19F NMR spectrum of PF5 shows them as equivalent on the NMR timescale. Berry explained this in 1960 by a low-energy vibration, now called Berry pseudorotation, that passes through a square-pyramidal configuration and exchanges two axial with two equatorial ligands.27

2.7 Seesaw

Class AX4E; point group C2v. One lone pair occupies an equatorial site of the trigonal bipyramid. The two remaining equatorial ligands and the two axial ligands form a shape like a seesaw (or "disphenoid"), with the axial pair as the plank. In SF4, measured by microwave spectroscopy, Fax–S–Fax = 173.07° and Feq–S–Feq = 101.55°, both well below the ideal 180° and 120° because the lone pair pushes the bonds away. S–Fax is 1.646 Å and S–Feq 1.545 Å.4,28

Worked example 4. Why the lone pair in SF4 is equatorial

Count the 90° interactions involving the lone pair in each candidate structure. With the lone pair axial, it sits at 90° to all three equatorial S–F bonds: three lone pair–bond interactions at 90°. With the lone pair equatorial, it is at 90° only to the two axial bonds: two such interactions (its contacts with the other equatorial bonds are at 120°). The equatorial structure has fewer of the strongest close contacts and is preferred, in agreement with the measured C2v seesaw.3,28 The same count places both lone pairs of ClF3 and all three of XeF2 in equatorial sites.

2.8 T-shaped

Class AX3E2; point group C2v. Two equatorial lone pairs leave one equatorial and two axial ligands, which form a T. Chlorine trifluoride is the classic case. Its microwave structure has one short Cl–F bond of 1.597 Å (the stem of the T, the equatorial position) and two long Cl–F bonds of 1.697 Å (the axial arms). The arms are bent back from the lone pairs, making an angle of 87.45° with the stem and about 175° with each other.4,29,30

2.9 Octahedral

Class AX6; angles 90° and 180°; point group Oh. Six domains point along ±x, ±y and ±z. Sulfur hexafluoride is a regular octahedron with S–F = 1.561 Å.4,16 Octahedral coordination is also the most common geometry for transition-metal complexes such as [Co(NH3)6]3+, although their shapes are better explained by ligand-field theory than by VSEPR.15

2.10 Square pyramidal

Class AX5E; point group C4v. One lone pair at an octahedral site leaves four basal ligands in a square and one apical ligand opposite the lone pair. In BrF5 the apical Br–F bond (1.689 Å) is shorter than the four basal bonds (1.774 Å). The angle between the apical and basal bonds is 84.8°, less than 90°, so the basal fluorines lie slightly above the bromine, displaced away from the lone pair.4,22 IF5 has the same C4v structure.4,31

2.11 Square planar

Class AX4E2; angles 90° and 180°; point group D4h. Two lone pairs take trans octahedral sites, so they are 180° apart, and the four ligands lie in a square plane. Xenon tetrafluoride is the main-group example, with Xe–F = 1.935 Å.4,32 Square-planar geometry is also characteristic of four-coordinate d8 transition-metal complexes such as [PtCl4]2− and [Ni(CN)4]2−, where it results from ligand-field stabilisation rather than from lone pairs.15

3 Valence bond theory

VSEPR predicts shapes but does not say what a bond is. The first quantum-mechanical answer came in 1927, when Heitler and London calculated the energy of two hydrogen atoms as a function of their separation. They found that a wavefunction in which the two electrons, with opposite spins, are exchanged between the two atoms gives a bound molecule, whereas the same spins give only repulsion.5 The covalent bond thus appeared as a quantum effect of electron exchange, the counterpart of Lewis's shared electron pair. In 1931 Pauling and Slater independently generalised the treatment to polyatomic molecules, introducing the ideas of maximum overlap and directed, hybridised orbitals.6,7 This framework is valence bond (VB) theory. In its simplest form a molecule is described by localised two-centre, two-electron bonds, each formed by spin-pairing one electron from each of two overlapping atomic or hybrid orbitals.33,34

3.1 Orbital overlap

The extent to which two orbitals φA and φB on neighbouring atoms occupy the same region of space is measured by the overlap integral35,36

S=∫φA*φBdτ(2)

For normalised orbitals, S runs from 0, when the orbitals do not interact at all, to 1, when they are identical (complete overlap). It is positive when regions of the same phase overlap, negative when regions of opposite phase overlap, and zero when positive and negative contributions cancel exactly by symmetry.35,37 Pauling and Slater proposed the criterion of maximum overlap: of the possible bonding orbitals on an atom, the one that overlaps most with the orbital of its partner forms the strongest bond, and bonds form in the directions where overlap is greatest.6,7 This criterion explains why p-orbital bonds are directional and why hybrid orbitals, which concentrate density on one side of the atom, form stronger bonds than pure s or p orbitals.33,38

Worked example 5. Which orbital pairs can bond?

Take the internuclear axis as z. An s orbital on A is symmetric under reflection through the xz and yz planes. A px orbital on B changes sign under reflection through the yz plane. The integrand φsφpx is therefore odd, and its integral over all space vanishes: S(s, px) = 0. By the same argument px overlaps only with px (or dxz), never with py or pz. Only orbitals of the same symmetry with respect to the bond axis can combine. This is the rule that separates σ from π bonding and that later controls which atomic orbitals mix in molecular orbital theory (Section 4).35,37

3.2 Sigma bonds

A σ bond is one whose electron density is cylindrically symmetric about the internuclear axis; in IUPAC terms, a two-centre σ bond has no nodal plane containing the bond axis.39 It arises from end-on overlap of s–s, s–p, p–p (both along the axis), or hybrid–hybrid orbitals (Figure 2a, b).15,33 Every single bond is a σ bond, and every multiple bond contains exactly one σ bond. Because σ density is cylindrically symmetric, rotating one end of the bond about its axis does not change the overlap. Rotation about single bonds is therefore relatively easy, which is why ethane and other alkanes interconvert rapidly between conformations at room temperature.15,35

3.3 Pi bonds

A π bond forms by side-on overlap of two parallel p orbitals (or a p and a d orbital) perpendicular to the bond axis (Figure 2c). It has a nodal plane containing the internuclear axis, with electron density above and below that plane.39 Side-on overlap is smaller than end-on overlap at the same distance, so a π bond is generally weaker than the σ bond it accompanies.15,35 A double bond is one σ plus one π bond; a triple bond is one σ plus two π bonds, in mutually perpendicular planes. The N≡N bond in N2, for example, is one σ bond and two π bonds.33

Adding π bonds shortens a bond. The carbon–carbon distances in ethane, ethylene and acetylene are 1.536 Å, 1.339 Å and 1.203 Å.4,16,17 A π bond also makes the bond rigid. Twisting one end of a double bond by 90° moves the two p orbitals to perpendicular orientations where their overlap is zero, which breaks the π bond. This is why cis and trans isomers of alkenes such as but-2-ene do not interconvert at room temperature, and why all six atoms of ethylene are coplanar.15,35

3.4 Hybridization

A ground-state carbon atom (2s22p2) has two unpaired electrons, in orbitals at 90° to each other, yet methane has four equivalent C–H bonds at 109.47°. Pauling resolved this in 1931 by showing that linear combinations of the 2s and 2p orbitals of one atom, called hybrid orbitals, are themselves valid orbitals. Four equivalent sp3 hybrids point to the corners of a tetrahedron and overlap more strongly with ligand orbitals than unhybridised s or p orbitals do.6 Slater reached the same conclusion independently.7 Hybridisation is a mathematical re-description of the atom's valence orbitals chosen to match a known geometry, not a physical process that precedes bonding. The number of hybrids equals the number of atomic orbitals mixed.33,38

Hybrids are written h = s + √λ p, where λ sets the p:s ratio; spn means λ = n. The fractional s character of a hybrid is f = 1/(1 + λ). Two hybrids on the same atom must be orthogonal, and for two equivalent hybrids that condition fixes the angle between them:38,40

1+λcosθ=0⇒cosθ=−1λ=−f1−f(3)

Equation 3 gives 180° for sp (f = ½), 120° for sp2 (f = ⅓), 109.47° for sp3 (f = ¼) and 90° for pure p (f = 0) (Figure 3). Bent's rule adds a chemical principle to this geometry: an atom directs hybrids richer in s character towards electropositive substituents and hybrids richer in p character towards electronegative ones.40 The rule accounts for the small F–O–F angle in OF2 and, in trigonal bipyramids, for the preference of electronegative ligands for the axial sites, whose bonding orbitals have more p character.15,40

Worked example 6. Hybridisation of oxygen in water from its bond angle

Problem. Use the measured H–O–H angle, 104.48°,19 to find the hybridisation of the oxygen orbitals that bond to hydrogen.

Solution. cos 104.48° = −0.2506, so by Eq. 3 λ = 1/0.2506 = 3.99 ≈ 4. The O–H bonding hybrids are close to sp4, with f = 1/(1 + 4) = 0.20 (20% s character each). The two bonds together use 0.40 of oxygen's single 2s orbital, so 0.60 of it remains for the lone pairs. If the lone pairs are treated as two equivalent hybrids, each is 30% s, the "rabbit-ear" picture. Symmetry favours a different partition: one lone pair is a pure 2p orbital perpendicular to the molecular plane (0% s) and the other is an in-plane hybrid carrying all 60% of the remaining s character.24 Either way, the bonds have less s character than sp3 and the angle is smaller than tetrahedral, consistent with the VSEPR result. In PH3 and H2S, angles near 90° correspond to bonds that are almost pure p (Figure 3).

3.4.1 sp

Mixing the s orbital with one p orbital (taken along z) gives two equivalent hybrids pointing in opposite directions:33,35

h1=12(s+pz),h2=12(s−pz)(4)

The two unhybridised p orbitals (px, py) are free to form two π bonds. In acetylene each carbon uses one sp hybrid for the C–H σ bond and one for the C–C σ bond, and the two p orbitals on each carbon pair up into two perpendicular π bonds, making the C≡C triple bond.15,33 The carbon of CO2 is described the same way, with one π bond to each oxygen in perpendicular planes. Monomeric BeCl2, which exists only in the gas phase, is linear and is described with sp hybrids on beryllium.15

3.4.2 sp²

Mixing s with two p orbitals gives three equivalent hybrids in a plane at 120°:35

h1=13s+23pxh2,3=13s−16px±12py(5)

The remaining pz orbital is perpendicular to the plane. In ethylene each carbon forms three σ bonds with its sp2 hybrids (two C–H and one C–C), and the two pz orbitals overlap side on to form the π bond. This is why the molecule is planar and why twisting it breaks the π bond (Section 3.3).33,35 In BF3, boron uses three sp2 hybrids for the B–F σ bonds and its empty pz orbital is available to accept an electron pair.15

3.4.3 sp³

Mixing s with all three p orbitals gives four equivalent hybrids directed to alternate corners of a cube, at 109.47° (Eq. 1):6,35

h1=12(s+px+py+pz),h2=12(s+px−py−pz),…(6)

sp3 hybridisation describes saturated carbon in methane, ethane and all alkanes, and, with lone pairs in some of the hybrids, nitrogen in amines and oxygen in water and alcohols. For NH3 and H2O the measured angles show that the bonding hybrids are not exactly sp3 (Worked example 6).33,40

Worked example 7. Counting σ and π bonds and assigning hybridisation

Problem. For acrylonitrile, H2C=CH–C≡N, count the σ and π bonds and give the hybridisation of each carbon and of nitrogen.

Solution. Bonds: three C–H, one C=C, one C–C and one C≡N. Every bond contains one σ, so there are 3 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 6 σ bonds. The double bond adds one π and the triple bond two: 3 π bonds. Hybridisation follows the number of σ bonds plus lone pairs on each atom: C1 (=CH2) has 3 σ, so sp2; C2 (=CH–) has 3 σ, so sp2; C3 (–C≡) has 2 σ, so sp; N has 1 σ plus one lone pair, so sp. The C=C–C≡N skeleton is therefore planar at C1 and C2 and linear at C3 and N.15,33

3.4.4 sp³d

For SN = 5, Pauling's 1931 scheme added one d orbital (dz²) to s and three p orbitals to make five hybrids directed to the corners of a trigonal bipyramid.6 Unlike the earlier sets these hybrids are not equivalent: they divide into three equatorial hybrids (built from s, px, py) and two axial hybrids (from pz and dz²).15,33 The scheme is still used as a label for PCl5, SF4, ClF3 and XeF2.

Modern calculations show that it is not physically correct for main-group atoms. The 3d orbitals of phosphorus and sulfur lie too high in energy to take part in bonding to a significant extent. In ab initio wavefunctions their occupancy is small, and their role is that of polarisation functions that refine the shape of the s and p orbitals.8,9 The bonding in these molecules is instead described by the three-centre, four-electron (3c–4e) bond proposed by Pimentel and Rundle.41,42 A p orbital on the central atom combines with the orbitals of the two opposite (axial) ligands to give one bonding, one nonbonding and one antibonding molecular orbital. Four electrons fill the bonding and nonbonding orbitals, so the two axial bonds share a single bonding pair: each has a bond order of about one half, with negative charge built up on the ligands.15,41 This explains why axial bonds are longer than equatorial bonds in PF5, SF4 and ClF3 (Section 2) and why the most electronegative ligands prefer axial sites.15,40

3.4.5 sp³d²

For SN = 6, adding dz² and dx²−y² to s and three p orbitals gives six equivalent hybrids pointing to the vertices of an octahedron. Pauling introduced this set for octahedral transition-metal complexes, where it is written d2sp3 to show that the inner (n − 1)d orbitals are used.6 For transition metals, whose (n − 1)d orbitals are true valence orbitals, this is reasonable.15 For main-group molecules such as SF6, the same objection applies as for sp3d. Reed and Weinhold's natural-population analysis of SF6 found only a small electron population in the sulfur d orbitals, which act as polarisation functions rather than as hybridising valence orbitals, and showed that the S–F bonds are strongly polar.8 The bonding is consistent with three-centre, four-electron interactions along each F–S–F axis.8,15 Magnusson reached the same conclusion for a broad range of second-row "hypervalent" molecules.9 sp3d2 remains a convenient way to say "octahedral" but should not be read as a statement about which orbitals the atom uses.

4 Molecular orbital theory

Molecular orbital (MO) theory takes a different starting point from valence bond theory. Instead of pairing electrons in localised bonds, it places every electron in an orbital that may extend over the whole molecule. The approach grew out of the interpretation of molecular band spectra by Hund and Mulliken in 1926–1928.10,43 In 1929 Lennard-Jones introduced the practice of building molecular orbitals as linear combinations of atomic orbitals (LCAO) and used it to explain the electronic structures of the first-row diatomic molecules, including the paramagnetism of O2.11 Mulliken, who extended the method to polyatomic molecules, received the 1966 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.43,44

For a homonuclear diatomic molecule built from one orbital on each atom, the two LCAO combinations are35,36

ψ±=φA±φB2(1±S),E±=α±β1±S(7)

where α is the Coulomb integral (roughly the energy of an electron in the atomic orbital), β the resonance (exchange) integral, which is negative, and S the overlap integral of Eq. 2. Three general rules follow and apply to any molecule:35,37

  • Conservation of orbitals. N atomic orbitals combine to give N molecular orbitals.
  • Symmetry and overlap. Only atomic orbitals of the same symmetry with respect to the molecular framework combine, and the strength of their interaction grows with their overlap (Worked example 5).
  • Energy matching. Orbitals interact strongly only when their energies are similar; an orbital with no partner of suitable energy and symmetry remains essentially atomic.

Electrons are then placed in the MOs in order of increasing energy, following the Pauli principle and Hund's rule exactly as for atoms (see Quantum Chemistry and Electronic Structure). Each MO is classified by its effect on the bond as bonding, antibonding or nonbonding.

4.1 Bonding orbitals

The in-phase combination ψ+ builds up electron density between the nuclei, where it is attracted by both and screens their mutual repulsion. Its energy E+ lies below α, and an electron in it holds the atoms together.35,37 Bonding orbitals are labelled by their symmetry about the bond axis: σ (no nodal plane containing the axis) or π (one such plane), with the subscript g (gerade, symmetric under inversion through the centre) or u (ungerade, antisymmetric) for homonuclear molecules.35,39 The simplest example is H2. Its two electrons occupy the 1σg bonding orbital formed from the two 1s orbitals, giving a molecule with an equilibrium bond length of 0.7414 Å and a bond dissociation energy of 435.78 kJ mol−1 at 298 K.45,46

4.2 Antibonding orbitals

The out-of-phase combination ψ− has a nodal plane perpendicular to the bond axis, midway between the nuclei, so electron density is removed from the bonding region. IUPAC defines an antibonding MO as one whose occupation diminishes the overall bonding and increases the total energy, lying above the average of the contributing atomic orbitals.47 Antibonding orbitals are marked with an asterisk (σ*, π*). Because S > 0, Eq. 7 shows that the antibonding orbital is raised above α by more than the bonding orbital is lowered:35,36

E−−α=αS−β1−S>α−E+=αS−β1+S(8)

This asymmetry explains why He2, with two electrons in 1σg and two in 1σu*, is not a chemically bound molecule: filling a bonding and an antibonding orbital together gives a net repulsion.15,35 It also explains the repulsion between filled orbitals that underlies the size of atoms in contact (steric repulsion).37

4.3 Nonbonding orbitals

A nonbonding MO is one whose occupation contributes nothing, or almost nothing, to the binding energy of the molecule.47 Nonbonding orbitals arise in three ways.15,37

  • No symmetry match. In HF the internuclear axis is z; the hydrogen 1s orbital can combine only with fluorine 2s and 2pz. The fluorine 2px and 2py orbitals have zero overlap with H 1s (Worked example 5) and remain as a degenerate pair of nonbonding π orbitals. These are the MO counterparts of the fluorine lone pairs.
  • Poor energy match. An atomic orbital much lower in energy than any partner, such as F 2s in HF, mixes only slightly and is largely nonbonding.
  • Nodes on the linking atoms. In polyatomic systems an MO can have nodes at the atoms that would connect bonding regions. In the allyl π system, Hückel theory gives three MOs with energies α + √2β, α and α − √2β; the middle one has a node at the central carbon and is exactly nonbonding.36,37

Water illustrates how MO theory describes lone pairs. Its two highest occupied orbitals are not equivalent "rabbit ears" but a nonbonding 1b1 orbital, an oxygen 2p orbital perpendicular to the molecular plane, and a σ-type 3a1 orbital that lies in the plane and is partly bonding.24,37 The 3c–4e bond of Section 3.4.4 also contains a nonbonding orbital, which places the extra electron density on the terminal ligands.41

4.4 Bond order

In MO theory the bond order is defined as15,35

b=12(n−n*)(9)

where n is the number of electrons in bonding orbitals and n* the number in antibonding orbitals; nonbonding electrons do not count. Unlike a Lewis bond order it can be a half-integer. For a given pair of atoms a higher bond order means a shorter, stronger bond. IUPAC also recognises a computational definition, the Mulliken overlap population, which measures the electron population built up between two atoms.47

The second-period homonuclear diatomic molecules are the standard test of Eq. 9. Their valence MOs are formed from 2s and 2p orbitals (Figure 4). For Li2 to N2, the 2s and 2pz orbitals are close enough in energy to mix (s–p mixing), which raises the 3σg orbital above the 1πu pair. For O2 and F2 the larger 2s–2p separation suppresses the mixing, and 3σg lies below 1πu.15,37 The resulting configurations are listed in Table 6. The bond orders follow the measured bond lengths and dissociation energies closely (Figure 5).45,46

4.5 HOMO

The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) is, as its name says, the highest-energy MO that contains electrons.39 Its electrons are the most easily removed and the most available for donation to an electron acceptor. Koopmans' theorem states that, within Hartree–Fock theory, the energy needed to remove an electron from an orbital is approximately the negative of that orbital's energy.49 Photoelectron spectroscopy, which measures these ionisation energies directly, therefore maps out the occupied orbital energies, starting with the HOMO.35

N2 is a good test. Its first ionisation energy is 15.581 eV,50 and the photoelectron spectrum shows that the electron removed comes from the 3σg orbital, not from 1πu. This confirms the s–p-mixed order of Figure 4.15,37 The HOMO of O2 is the antibonding 1πg*, and its first ionisation energy, 12.0697 eV, is correspondingly lower.50 In CO the HOMO is a σ orbital concentrated on carbon, which is why CO binds to transition metals through carbon.15,37 In NH3 the HOMO is the nitrogen lone pair, the orbital that makes ammonia a Lewis base.15

4.6 LUMO

The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) is the lowest-energy MO that is empty.39 It is where an added electron goes, and the orbital through which a molecule acts as an electron acceptor. Together the HOMO and LUMO are the frontier orbitals. Fukui and co-workers showed in 1952 that the positions at which aromatic hydrocarbons react are predicted by the electron density in the HOMO,51 and the resulting frontier molecular orbital theory, in which reactivity is governed largely by the interaction between the HOMO of one partner and the LUMO of the other, earned Fukui a share of the 1981 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.39,52

Three examples show how frontier orbitals are used.15,37

  • Lewis acid–base adducts. The LUMO of BF3 is essentially the empty boron 2p orbital perpendicular to the molecular plane (Section 2.3). The lone-pair HOMO of NH3 donates into it to form F3B–NH3, and boron becomes tetrahedral.
  • Cycloadditions. In the Diels–Alder reaction the HOMO of the diene overlaps in phase at both ends with the LUMO of the dienophile, so both new σ bonds can form at once. The symmetry match of these frontier orbitals underlies the selection rules for pericyclic reactions.52
  • The HOMO–LUMO gap and colour. The lowest electronic absorption of many molecules corresponds roughly to promoting an electron from HOMO to LUMO. In Hückel theory the gap for ethylene is 2|β|, while for butadiene, whose π levels lie at α ± 1.618β and α ± 0.618β, it is 1.236|β|. Extending conjugation narrows the gap and moves absorption to longer wavelengths, which is why long polyenes such as β-carotene are coloured.35–37

In open-shell molecules such as O2, the half-filled 1πg* orbitals are singly occupied molecular orbitals (SOMOs) that serve as both donor and acceptor. An added electron goes into 1πg* to give superoxide, and the electron affinity of O2 is 0.448 eV.50

4.7 Paramagnetism

A paramagnetic substance has a positive magnetic susceptibility and is drawn into a magnetic field.39 At the molecular level, paramagnetism comes from unpaired electrons, whose spin magnetic moments line up partly with an applied field. For molecules whose orbital angular momentum is quenched, the effective magnetic moment is given by the spin-only formula15

μeff=n(n+2)μB(10)

where n is the number of unpaired electrons and μB the Bohr magneton.

Dioxygen is the textbook demonstration of MO theory's advantage over simple Lewis structures. Every acceptable Lewis structure for O2 pairs all twelve valence electrons, yet liquid oxygen is drawn into the gap between the poles of a strong magnet.15,48 MO theory places the last two electrons in the degenerate 1πg* pair; by Hund's rule they occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins, giving the 3Σg− triplet ground state (Figure 4).11,45 Lennard-Jones gave this explanation in 1929,11 and Pauling's valence-bond description had to invoke two three-electron bonds to reach the same result.33 B2 is also paramagnetic, with two unpaired electrons in 1πu. Its triplet ground state is direct evidence for s–p mixing, because without mixing the last two electrons would pair in 3σg and B2 would be diamagnetic.15,45 NO, NO2 and ClO2 are paramagnetic because they have an odd number of electrons.48

Worked example 9. Spin-only moment of O2

O2 has n = 2 unpaired electrons. By Eq. 10, μeff = √(2 × 4) μB = √8 μB = 2.83 μB. For superoxide (n = 1), μeff = √3 μB = 1.73 μB; for peroxide (n = 0) the spin-only moment is zero.15

4.8 Diamagnetism

A diamagnetic substance has a negative magnetic susceptibility and is pushed out of a magnetic field.39 Diamagnetism arises because an applied field induces circulating currents in the electron cloud, and those currents set up a field that opposes the applied one. Every substance has this contribution, but it is weak. It is observed as the dominant effect only when there are no unpaired electrons to produce the much larger paramagnetic response.15,35 Molecules with all electrons paired (closed-shell molecules) are therefore diamagnetic. Among the second-period diatomics these are Li2, C2, N2 and F2 (Table 6); CO, H2O, CH4 and most stable organic molecules are also diamagnetic.15

The magnetic behaviour of C2 and B2 is a sensitive test of orbital ordering. With s–p mixing, the four highest electrons of C2 fill the 1πu pair, giving a diamagnetic singlet (1Σg+). Without mixing, two of them would occupy 3σg and two would be left unpaired in 1πu, making C2 paramagnetic. The observed ground states (B2 triplet, C2 singlet) agree only with the mixed ordering.15,45 Converting between para- and diamagnetic forms is also chemically useful. Reducing paramagnetic O2 to diamagnetic peroxide, O22− (Worked example 8), for instance, can be followed by magnetic measurement.48

5 Comparing the models

The three models answer different questions and are best used together.13,34,37 VSEPR, with only a Lewis structure as input, predicts the shapes of main-group molecules and the direction in which their angles deviate from ideal values. Valence bond theory, with hybridisation, turns those shapes into localised σ and π bonds that match the chemist's structural formulae, and it is the natural language for discussing rotation about bonds, stereochemistry and resonance. Molecular orbital theory is needed whenever electrons are delocalised or unpaired: it gives fractional bond orders, the magnetism of O2 and B2, the ordering of ionisation energies, and the frontier orbitals that control reactivity and spectra. Valence bond and MO theory are approximations to the same quantum mechanics; when both are carried to high accuracy, they converge on the same wavefunction.34,36
