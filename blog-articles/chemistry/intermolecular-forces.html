Chemistry Series

Intermolecular Forces

The attractions between molecules, from the fleeting dipoles behind London dispersion to hydrogen bonds and ion–dipole binding, how polarizability and induced dipoles tie them together, and how they set boiling point, melting point, vapour pressure, viscosity, surface tension and solubility.

Abstract

Intermolecular forces are the attractions and repulsions between molecules, or between separate parts of one large molecule, that do not involve forming chemical bonds.1 They are one to two orders of magnitude weaker than covalent bonds, yet they decide whether a substance is a gas, a liquid or a solid at room temperature, and they control almost every bulk property of liquids. This article treats the forces in the order of the outline. London dispersion forces, explained quantum-mechanically by London in 1930,2,3 act between all molecules and usually dominate. Dipole–dipole (Keesom) forces4 act between polar molecules and are weakened by thermal tumbling. Hydrogen bonds, defined by IUPAC in 2011,5 are the strongest and most directional of the neutral interactions. Ion–dipole forces bind solvent molecules to ions and drive the hydration of salts.6,7 Induced-dipole (Debye) forces8 arise when a charge or a permanent dipole polarizes a neighbour, and the ease of that distortion, the polarizability,1 links all of these interactions together. The last section relates the forces quantitatively to boiling point, melting point, vapour pressure, viscosity, surface tension and solubility, using reference data from NIST, IAPWS and the primary literature. Fourteen worked examples compute interaction energies and property estimates from measured dipole moments, polarizabilities and ionization energies. Bond polarity and molecular dipole moments are developed in Chemical Bonding, molecular shape in Molecular Structure, and electronegativity in The Periodic Table and Periodic Trends; they are used here without re-derivation.

0 Overview

Van der Waals introduced attractive forces between molecules in 1873 to explain why real gases depart from ideal behaviour and condense to liquids; the constant a in his equation of state measures their strength.9,10 IUPAC now uses the term van der Waals forces for the attractive or repulsive forces between molecular entities, or between groups within the same entity, other than those due to bond formation or to the electrostatic interaction of ions with one another or with neutral molecules; the term includes dipole–dipole, dipole–induced dipole and London (instantaneous induced dipole–induced dipole) forces.1 Hydrogen bonds and ion–dipole forces are treated separately because they are stronger and, in the case of hydrogen bonds, partly covalent.5,11

All of these interactions are electrical in origin. They differ in which charge distributions interact (net charges, permanent dipoles or fluctuating dipoles) and in how steeply they fall off with distance r.11,12 The three attractions between neutral molecules that are averaged over thermal motion, the Keesom (dipole–dipole), Debye (dipole–induced dipole) and London (dispersion) energies, all vary as r−6, so their sum can be written as a single coefficient C6:11,12

VvdW(r)=−C6r6,C6=CKeesom+CDebye+CLondon(1)

At short range the electron clouds of two closed-shell molecules overlap, and the Pauli exclusion principle produces a steep repulsion.12 Lennard-Jones represented this by an inverse power of r; with the twelfth power, the now-standard 12-6 potential is10,13

V(r)=4ε[(σr)12−(σr)6](2)

where ε is the depth of the potential well and σ the separation at which V = 0. The minimum lies at rm = 21/6σ ≈ 1.12σ (Figure 1).10 The well depth for two argon atoms is about 1.2 kJ mol−1 (Worked example 1), while a C–C single bond is about 350 kJ mol−1, which is why intermolecular forces are overcome by temperatures of tens to hundreds of kelvin while covalent bonds survive.11 Table 1 summarizes the interactions treated in this article.

1 London dispersion forces

1.1 Origin

Noble gases liquefy, and nonpolar molecules such as N2, CH4 and CCl4 condense, even though they have no permanent dipole to attract one another. Classical electrostatics could not explain this attraction. London showed in 1930 that it follows from quantum mechanics: the electron distribution of an atom fluctuates, so at any instant the atom carries an instantaneous dipole even though its time-averaged dipole is zero.2,3 The field of that instantaneous dipole polarizes a neighbouring atom, and the two fluctuating dipoles become correlated so that they attract on average. London called the effect dispersion because the same oscillator strengths that govern it also govern the dispersion of light, the variation of refractive index with frequency.3,11 IUPAC defines London forces as attractive forces between apolar molecules due to their mutual polarizability, and lists them among the van der Waals forces.1

Dispersion is present between every pair of atoms or molecules, polar or not, because every electron cloud can be polarized.11 It is non-directional to a first approximation, approximately additive over pairs of atoms, and, as Section 2.5 shows, usually the largest single contribution to the attraction between neutral molecules, including most polar ones.11,12

1.2 The London formula

Treating each atom as a single oscillator of characteristic energy close to its first ionization energy I, London obtained an approximate expression for the dispersion energy between molecules 1 and 2:3,11

VLondon=−32I1I2I1+I2α1′α2′r6⇒CLondon=34Iα′2(identical molecules)(3)

Here α′ = α/4πε0 is the polarizability volume (Section 6.1). The formula captures the two factors that control dispersion: it grows with the square of the polarizability and with the energy scale of the electronic excitations. Because I varies much less from molecule to molecule than α′ does, polarizability is the dominant factor in practice.11 Accurate dispersion coefficients are obtained instead from measured or calculated dipole oscillator strength distributions, and these are the benchmark against which the London formula is tested.14,15

Worked example 1. The dispersion coefficient of argon

Data. Polarizability volume of Ar, α′ = 1.664 Å3 = 1.664 × 10−30 m3;16 first ionization energy I = 15.760 eV = 2.525 × 10−18 J.17

London estimate. C6 = ¾ I α′2 = 0.75 × (2.525 × 10−18 J) × (1.664 × 10−30 m3)2 = 5.24 × 10−78 J m6. In atomic units (1 Eha06 = 9.573 × 10−80 J m6) this is 54.8 au.

Comparison. The value derived from Kumar and Meath's dipole oscillator strength distribution is C6 = 64.3 au, with an estimated uncertainty of about 1%.14,15 The one-oscillator London formula therefore underestimates the true coefficient by about 15%, because real atoms absorb over a range of frequencies extending well above the ionization threshold.

Size of the energy. At r = 3.76 Å, close to the separation of the potential minimum for Ar2, the accurate coefficient gives −C6/r6 = −1.31 kJ mol−1. This is comparable to RT at the normal boiling point of argon (87.3 K, RT = 0.73 kJ mol−1),18 which is why argon condenses only at very low temperature.

1.3 Dependence on size and polarizability

Larger atoms and molecules have more electrons, held less tightly and farther from the nuclei, so they are more polarizable (Section 6.2) and attract more strongly by dispersion.11,19 The noble gases, which interact only by dispersion, show the effect cleanly: from helium to xenon the polarizability rises twenty-fold and the boiling point forty-fold (Table 2, Figure 2).

1.4 Dependence on shape and contact area

Because dispersion falls off as r−6, it is dominated by the atoms that come closest together, so molecules that can make extensive surface contact attract more strongly than compact molecules of the same composition.11 The three isomers of C5H12 have the same molar mass and almost the same polarizability, yet their boiling points fall as the shape becomes more nearly spherical: n-pentane boils at 309.2 K (36.1 °C), 2-methylbutane (isopentane) at 301.1 K (27.9 °C) and 2,2-dimethylpropane (neopentane) at 282.6 K (9.5 °C).20 The enthalpies of vaporization near the boiling point follow the same order, 25.79, 24.69 and 22.74 kJ mol−1.20 The melting points of the same three isomers behave quite differently, for reasons discussed in Section 7.2.

1.5 Additivity and large-scale effects

To a good approximation, the dispersion energy between two molecules is the sum of contributions from all pairs of atoms, one in each molecule.11,12 Summing the r−6 pair attraction over two macroscopic bodies, as Hamaker did, gives an interaction that falls off much more slowly than r−6: for two flat surfaces the energy per unit area varies as D−2, where D is the gap.11 This is why dispersion forces matter for colloids, thin films and adhesion. A striking example is the gecko: Autumn and colleagues measured the adhesion of single gecko setae and showed that it depends on the polarizability of the surface rather than its polarity or wettability, which identified van der Waals (dispersion) forces, not capillary forces, as the adhesive mechanism.21

2 Dipole–dipole forces

2.1 Permanent dipoles

A molecule whose centre of positive charge does not coincide with its centre of negative charge has a permanent electric dipole moment μ, measured in debye (1 D = 3.33564 × 10−30 C m).10,22 How bond polarity and molecular geometry combine to give the dipole moment is developed in Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure. Here only the measured moments are needed: for example, 1.093 D for HCl, 1.857 D for H2O, 1.47 D for NH3 and 3.92 D for CH3CN.16,22 The interaction between such dipoles was analysed by Keesom, and the thermally averaged dipole–dipole attraction is called the Keesom interaction.4,11

2.2 Interaction at fixed orientation

For two point dipoles μ1 and μ2 a distance r apart, the electrostatic energy depends on their relative orientation:11,12

V=−μ1μ24πε0r3(2cosθ1cosθ2−sinθ1sinθ2cosφ)(4)

where θ1 and θ2 are the angles between each dipole and the line joining them and φ is the dihedral angle between the dipoles. The most favourable arrangement is head-to-tail in line (θ1 = θ2 = 0), with energy −2μ1μ2/4πε0r3; the side-by-side antiparallel arrangement gives half that.11 The r−3 dependence applies in solids and in other situations where the molecules cannot rotate freely.

2.3 Thermal averaging: the Keesom interaction

In a gas or a liquid the molecules rotate, and if all orientations were equally likely the average of Equation (4) would be zero. Orientations of lower energy are, however, slightly more probable by the Boltzmann factor, so a net attraction survives. When the interaction is weak compared with kT, the Boltzmann-weighted average is:4,11

VKeesom=−2μ12μ223(4πε0)2kTr6(5)

Two features distinguish it from the other r−6 terms: it depends on the fourth power of the dipole moment for identical molecules, and it weakens as temperature rises because thermal motion randomizes the orientations.11

Worked example 2. Two HCl molecules at 0.40 nm

Data. μ(HCl) = 1.093 D = 3.646 × 10−30 C m;16 r = 0.40 nm; T = 298.15 K; 1/4πε0 = 8.988 × 109 J m C−2.

Fixed, head-to-tail. V = −2μ2/(4πε0r3) = −2 × (3.646 × 10−30)2 × 8.988 × 109 / (4.0 × 10−10)3 = −3.73 × 10−21 J, or −2.25 kJ mol−1 after multiplying by NA.

Rotating (Keesom). Equation (5) gives −0.34 kJ mol−1, about one-seventh of the aligned value.

Interpretation. Both energies are smaller than RT = 2.48 kJ mol−1 at 298 K. The aligned energy being comparable to RT means the alignment is only partial; that is exactly the condition under which Equation (5) is valid.11

2.4 Effect on boiling point

Comparing molecules of nearly equal molar mass and size isolates the effect of the dipole moment (Table 3). Propane and dimethyl ether have almost the same molar mass, but the ether's dipole of 1.30 D raises its boiling point by 17 K; acetaldehyde, with 2.69 D, boils 63 K above propane. Ethanol, an isomer of dimethyl ether with a smaller dipole moment, boils 103 K higher still because it can form hydrogen bonds (Section 3).20,22,23

3 Hydrogen bonding

3.1 Definition

Latimer and Rodebush proposed in 1920 that a hydrogen atom held by two octets could act as a bond between two atoms, to explain the association of water, hydrogen fluoride and ammonia and the weakness of NH4OH as a base.24 Pauling brought the concept into general use, describing the hydrogen bond as largely ionic (electrostatic) and formed only with the most electronegative atoms.25 The current IUPAC definition, adopted in 2011, is broader:5

The hydrogen bond is an attractive interaction between a hydrogen atom from a molecule or a molecular fragment X–H in which X is more electronegative than H, and an atom or a group of atoms in the same or a different molecule, in which there is evidence of bond formation.

A hydrogen bond is written X–H···Y–Z, where X–H is the donor and Y the acceptor, which may be an atom, an anion, or a π-electron system.5 The recommendation lists criteria for evidence: the forces involved include electrostatic, charge-transfer and dispersion contributions; the X–H bond is polarized and lengthens on hydrogen bond formation; the X–H···Y angle is usually close to 180°, and the closer to linear the stronger the bond; the X–H stretching frequency shifts to lower wavenumber (red shift), and the 1H NMR signal shifts downfield; and the Gibbs energy of formation must be larger than the thermal energy of the system for the bond to be detected experimentally.5,26 Electronegativity, which determines whether X–H is polarized enough to act as a donor, is treated in The Periodic Table and Periodic Trends. The classical donors are N–H, O–H and F–H and the classical acceptors are N, O and F with lone pairs, but C–H groups and π systems can also take part in weaker hydrogen bonds.27,28

3.2 Geometry and strength

Hydrogen bonds cover a wide range of energies. Jeffrey classified them as strong (roughly 60–170 kJ mol−1, mostly in ions with nearly symmetric X···H···X), moderate (16–60 kJ mol−1, the common bonds of water, alcohols, acids and biological molecules) and weak (below 16 kJ mol−1, for example C–H···O).28 Some measured values follow:

  • Water dimer. In (H2O)2 one molecule donates a nearly linear O–H···O hydrogen bond to the other, with an O···O distance of 2.98 Å determined by molecular-beam electric resonance spectroscopy.29 Rocher-Casterline and co-workers measured the dissociation energy by velocity-map imaging as D0 = 1105 ± 10 cm−1 = 13.2 ± 0.12 kJ mol−1.30
  • Ammonia–water. For H3N···H–OH, in which water donates to the nitrogen lone pair, the same group measured D0 = 1538 ± 10 cm−1 (18.4 kJ mol−1).31 Nitrogen is the better acceptor because its lone pair is less tightly held than oxygen's.
  • Hydrogen difluoride ion. In [F–H–F]−, a strong, symmetric hydrogen bond, Harrell and McDaniel obtained a lower limit of 155 kJ mol−1 from the thermochemistry of tetramethylammonium salts;32 Emsley reviewed this and other very strong hydrogen bonds.33

Strength correlates with geometry: shorter X···Y distances and more linear X–H···Y angles accompany stronger bonds.5,27 The dissociation energy D0 is measured from the lowest vibrational level and is smaller than the depth of the potential well because of zero-point energy.30

3.3 Effect on boiling points of the hydrides

The clearest evidence for hydrogen bonding in bulk is the boiling points of the molecular hydrides (Figure 3). In group 14, where the hydrides are nonpolar and cannot form hydrogen bonds, the boiling point rises steadily from CH4 to SnH4 as polarizability, and therefore dispersion, increases. In groups 15, 16 and 17 the heavier hydrides follow the same rising trend, but NH3, H2O and HF lie far above the line extrapolated from their heavier congeners.18,25 Water boils at 100 °C; extrapolation from H2Te, H2Se and H2S would predict roughly −80 °C. The extra 180 K or so is the signature of hydrogen bonding between small, highly electronegative first-row atoms that carry lone pairs.25

Water is the extreme case because each molecule has two O–H donors and two lone pairs, so it can take part in four hydrogen bonds and build an extended three-dimensional network. HF has three lone pairs but only one donor hydrogen, and NH3 has three donors but only one lone pair, so both form chains or rings rather than a fully connected network, and both boil well below water.25,28

3.4 Hydrogen bonding in ice and water

In ordinary ice (ice Ih) every oxygen atom is tetrahedrally surrounded by four others, each linked by one hydrogen bond, so there are two hydrogen bonds per molecule. Pauling showed that the random orientation of the hydrogen-bonded protons in this lattice accounts for the residual entropy of ice.25 The open tetrahedral network is less dense than the liquid, which is why ice floats.25

Worked example 4. An estimate of the hydrogen-bond energy in ice

Data. The enthalpy of sublimation of ice Ih at the triple point is 51.06 kJ mol−1;34 it equals the enthalpy of fusion (6.01 kJ mol−1) plus the enthalpy of vaporization of the liquid at the same temperature (45.05 kJ mol−1).35

Estimate. Subliming one mole of ice breaks two moles of hydrogen bonds (each bond is shared between two molecules), so the energy per hydrogen bond is at most 51.06/2 ≈ 25.5 kJ mol−1.

Interpretation. This is an upper bound, because the sublimation enthalpy also contains dispersion and other attractions between non-bonded neighbours. Even so, it is about twice the 13.2 kJ mol−1 measured for the isolated water dimer.30 The difference reflects cooperativity: a water molecule that accepts a hydrogen bond becomes a better donor, so hydrogen bonds in an extended network are stronger than an isolated one.27,28

3.5 Hydrogen bonding in biological molecules

Hydrogen bonds fix the three-dimensional structures of proteins and nucleic acids. Pauling, Corey and Branson predicted the α-helix in 1951 as a structure in which each backbone N–H group forms a hydrogen bond to the C=O group of the residue four positions along the chain.36 Watson and Crick's 1953 model of DNA joined the two strands by specific hydrogen bonding between adenine and thymine and between guanine and cytosine, which immediately suggested a copying mechanism for genetic material.37 Because each individual hydrogen bond is only moderately strong, these structures can be assembled and taken apart at physiological temperatures.28

4 Ion–dipole interactions

4.1 Energy of an ion–dipole pair

An ion of charge ze attracts the oppositely charged end of a polar molecule. For a point dipole μ at distance r from the ion, with angle θ between the dipole and the line to the ion, the energy is11

V=−zeμcosθ4πε0r2(6)

It falls off only as r−2, more slowly than any interaction between neutral molecules, and near a small ion it is strong enough to hold solvent molecules in fixed orientations: a cation binds water through the oxygen, an anion through a hydrogen.6,11 Only at long range, where the energy is small compared with kT, does the dipole rotate freely; the orientation-averaged energy is then −(ze)2μ2/[6(4πε0)2kTr4].11

Worked example 5. Na+ bound to one water molecule

Data. μ(H2O) = 1.857 D = 6.194 × 10−30 C m.16 For the Na+···O distance, take the sum of the six-coordinate ionic radius of Na+, 1.02 Å,38 and, as an approximate radius for the water oxygen, the six-coordinate radius of the oxide ion, 1.40 Å,38 giving r ≈ 2.42 Å.

Calculation. With θ = 0, V = −eμ/(4πε0r2) = −(1.602 × 10−19)(6.194 × 10−30)(8.988 × 109)/(2.42 × 10−10)2 = −1.52 × 10−19 J, or −92 kJ mol−1.

Comparison. Džidić and Kebarle measured the enthalpy of the gas-phase reaction Na+ + H2O → Na+(H2O) by high-pressure mass spectrometry as −24.0 kcal mol−1 (−100 kJ mol−1).7 The point-dipole estimate gets close; the remainder is mostly the induced dipole that the ion's field creates in the water molecule (Section 5.2). The interaction is roughly forty times RT at room temperature, so the water molecule is held in a fixed orientation.

4.2 Hydration of ions

In solution, each ion is surrounded by a shell of oriented solvent molecules, and the sum of the ion–dipole energies, together with the polarization of the more distant solvent, makes up the free energy of hydration. Born modelled the ion as a charged sphere of radius a in a dielectric continuum and obtained ΔhydG ∝ −z2(1 − 1/εr)/a, which captures the two main trends: hydration is stronger for higher charge and for smaller ions.10,39 Table 4 shows the second trend for the alkali-metal cations, using Marcus's compiled single-ion Gibbs energies of hydration.6 Ionic radii and their trends are covered in The Periodic Table and Periodic Trends.

4.3 Consequences

Ion–dipole forces explain why ionic solids dissolve in polar solvents. Dissolving NaCl in water requires the lattice energy to be supplied (see Chemical Bonding), and this is very nearly repaid by the hydration of the separated ions, so the net enthalpy of solution is small and the dissolution is driven by the entropy of mixing.10 Nonpolar solvents such as hexane cannot provide this compensation, so salts are practically insoluble in them (Section 7.6). The same forces account for the hydrated ions found in crystals, such as [Mg(H2O)6]2+ in many magnesium salts, and for the strong binding of water by small, highly charged cations.6,40

5 Induced dipoles

5.1 Induction

An electric field E displaces a molecule's electrons relative to its nuclei and so induces a dipole moment proportional to the field:1,10

μind=αE=4πε0α′E,Vind=−12αE2(7)

The proportionality constant α is the polarizability (Section 6). The induced dipole always points so that it is attracted to the source of the field, so induction is always attractive. The factor ½ in the energy appears because half of the work done by the field goes into distorting the molecule.11 The source of the field can be an ion (Section 5.2), a permanent dipole (Section 5.3) or the instantaneous dipole of a neighbour, which gives the London force of Section 1.3,11

5.2 Ion–induced dipole

The field of an ion of charge ze at distance r is ze/4πε0r2, so from Equation (7) the ion–induced dipole energy is11

V=−(ze)2α′2(4πε0)r4(8)

This interaction acts even between an ion and a nonpolar molecule or atom, and it does not depend on temperature because the induced dipole follows the field instantly.11

Worked example 6. The dipole induced in argon by a sodium ion

Data. α′(Ar) = 1.664 Å3;16 r = 0.30 nm; z = +1.

Field. E = e/4πε0r2 = (1.602 × 10−19 × 8.988 × 109)/(3.0 × 10−10)2 = 1.60 × 1010 V m−1.

Induced dipole. μind = 4πε0α′E = (1.113 × 10−10)(1.664 × 10−30)(1.60 × 1010) = 2.96 × 10−30 C m = 0.89 D, about half the permanent dipole of a water molecule.

Energy. V = −½μindE = −2.37 × 10−20 J, or −14 kJ mol−1, more than five times RT at 298 K. This is the same mechanism that adds to the ion–dipole energy in Worked example 5.

5.3 Dipole–induced dipole: the Debye interaction

Debye showed in 1920 that a permanent dipole polarizes a neighbouring molecule, and that the resulting attraction does not average to zero as the molecules rotate.8 For a polar molecule 1 and any molecule 2, the orientation-averaged energy is11,12

VDebye=−μ12α2′+μ22α1′4πε0r6(9)

Unlike the Keesom energy, the Debye energy does not depend on temperature, because the induced dipole always lines up with the inducing field whatever the orientation of the permanent dipole.11 It is usually the smallest of the three r−6 contributions (Worked example 3), but it is the main polarity-dependent term between a polar and a nonpolar molecule. For example, the solubility of nonpolar gases such as O2 and N2 in water, though small, depends on dipole–induced dipole and dispersion attractions to the solvent.11

5.4 Induced dipole–induced dipole

When neither molecule has a permanent dipole, the only source of field is the instantaneous dipole of the electron cloud. The correlated fluctuation of two such instantaneous and induced dipoles is the London dispersion force of Section 1.2,3 Induction is not exactly pairwise additive: a third molecule alters the field felt by the first two. For dispersion, the leading three-body correction is the Axilrod–Teller–Muto term, which is small for gases but contributes a few per cent of the cohesive energy of condensed rare gases.12

6 Polarizability

6.1 Definition and units

IUPAC defines polarizability as the ease of distortion of the electron cloud of a molecular entity by an electric field, given quantitatively by the ratio of the induced dipole moment to the field that induces it, α = μind/E.1 Its SI unit is C2 m2 J−1 (equivalently C m2 V−1). It is more convenient to quote the polarizability volume,10

α′=α4πε0(10)

which has the dimensions of volume and is usually given in Å3 (10−30 m3). Polarizability volumes are of the same order as molecular volumes, which reflects the fact that the electrons of larger molecules are less tightly held.10,11 In atomic units, 1 au of polarizability corresponds to 0.1482 Å3.19

For a molecule that is not spherical, the induced dipole depends on the direction of the field, and polarizability is a tensor with different principal values along different axes. The mean polarizability, the average of the three principal components, is the quantity normally tabulated.1 Linear and planar molecules, such as CO2 and benzene, are more polarizable along the molecular axis or in the plane than perpendicular to it.12

6.2 Trends

Polarizability increases with the number of electrons and with how loosely they are bound.11,19 Within a group of the periodic table it increases down the group: He 0.208, Ne 0.381, Ar 1.664, Kr 2.498 and Xe 4.005 Å3. The hydrogen halides follow the same pattern: HF 0.80, HCl 2.52, HBr 3.62 and HI 5.45 Å3.16 Across a period, the increasing nuclear charge holds the electrons more tightly, so polarizability falls; among the neutral atoms, the alkali metals, with a single loosely held valence electron, are the most polarizable in each period, and caesium is one of the most polarizable of all the elements.19 Polarizability is roughly additive over the atoms or bonds of a molecule, so it rises steadily along a homologous series: CH4 2.45, C2H6 4.23, C3H8 5.92 and n-C4H10 8.02 Å3, an increment of about 1.8–2.1 Å3 per CH2.16

6.3 Polarizability from refractive index

The polarizability at optical frequencies can be obtained from the refractive index n of a substance through the Lorentz–Lorenz equation,10

Rm=Mρn2−1n2+2=4πNA3α′(11)

where Rm is the molar refraction, M the molar mass and ρ the density. At optical frequencies only the electrons can follow the field, so this gives the electronic polarizability.10

Worked example 7. Polarizability of benzene from its refractive index

Data. For liquid benzene at 20 °C, nD = 1.5011 and ρ = 0.8765 g cm−3; M = 78.11 g mol−1.18

Molar refraction. (n2 − 1)/(n2 + 2) = 1.2533/4.2533 = 0.2947; M/ρ = 89.12 cm3 mol−1; Rm = 26.26 cm3 mol−1.

Polarizability volume. α′ = 3Rm/(4πNA) = 3 × 26.26 cm3 mol−1 / (4π × 6.022 × 1023 mol−1) = 1.041 × 10−23 cm3 = 10.4 Å3.

Comparison. The tabulated gas-phase static value is 9.96 Å3.16 The optical value is about 4% higher because polarizability increases with frequency as it approaches the electronic absorption bands; this frequency dependence is the "dispersion" from which London's force takes its name.3

6.4 Chemical consequences

Polarizability links the different intermolecular forces: it sets the London energy (Equation 3), the Debye energy (Equation 9) and the ion–induced dipole energy (Equation 8). It also appears in Pearson's classification of Lewis acids and bases, where soft species are large and polarizable (I−, RS−, Hg2+, Ag+) and hard species are small and weakly polarizable (F−, OH−, Li+, Mg2+), with soft–soft and hard–hard combinations preferred.41

7 Relationship to physical properties

Every property in this section reflects the balance between the cohesive energy of the condensed phase, which comes from the intermolecular forces, and the thermal energy that disrupts it. In each case the comparison of molecules that differ in only one respect, such as size, shape, polarity or hydrogen-bonding ability, isolates the contribution of a particular force.

7.1 Boiling point

At the normal boiling point Tb the vapour pressure of a liquid equals 1 atm (101.325 kPa), and liquid and vapour are in equilibrium, so ΔvapG = 0 and Tb = ΔvapH/ΔvapS.10 Stronger intermolecular forces increase ΔvapH, and so raise the boiling point. Trouton observed in 1884 that the molar entropy of vaporization at the boiling point is roughly the same for many liquids, about 85–88 J K−1 mol−1 (Trouton's rule), because vaporization produces a similar increase in disorder whatever the liquid.10,42

Worked example 8. Trouton's rule and its exceptions

Data. Boiling points and enthalpies of vaporization at Tb: benzene 353.3 K, 30.72 kJ mol−1; n-pentane 309.2 K, 25.79 kJ mol−1; propane 231.1 K, 19.04 kJ mol−1; ethanol 351.5 K, 38.56 kJ mol−1; 1-butanol 390.9 K, 43.29 kJ mol−1;20 water 373.12 K, 40.66 kJ mol−1.35

Entropies of vaporization. ΔvapS = ΔvapH/Tb: benzene 87.0, n-pentane 83.4, propane 82.4, ethanol 109.7, 1-butanol 110.7 and water 109.0 J K−1 mol−1.

Estimate. For benzene, Trouton's rule with 88 J K−1 mol−1 predicts ΔvapH ≈ 88 × 353.3 = 31.1 kJ mol−1, within 1.3% of the measured value.

Interpretation. The nonpolar liquids obey the rule. The hydrogen-bonded liquids exceed it by about 20 J K−1 mol−1, because hydrogen bonding imposes extra order on the liquid that is lost on vaporization.10

Boiling points in a homologous series increase smoothly with chain length, reflecting the growth of dispersion with molecular size (Figure 4). For the n-alkanes the boiling point rises from 111.7 K for methane to 447.3 K for decane.18,20 Branching lowers the boiling point by reducing the contact area (Section 1.4). Polarity raises it for molecules of equal size (Table 3), and hydrogen bonding raises it much further (Figure 3).

7.2 Melting point

Melting is also a balance of enthalpy and entropy, Tm = ΔfusH/ΔfusS, but it depends on how efficiently molecules pack in the crystal and on how much freedom they gain on melting, not just on the strength of the attractions. Melting points therefore correlate less smoothly with intermolecular forces than boiling points do.10 Two effects are especially clear in the alkanes.

Molecular symmetry. Among the pentane isomers, the most compact and symmetrical, neopentane, has the lowest boiling point but by far the highest melting point: 256.6 K, compared with 143.5 K for n-pentane and 113.4 K for isopentane.20 Nearly spherical molecules can rotate within the crystal lattice before it melts, forming what Timmermans called plastic crystals, which are characterized by an unusually low entropy of fusion, below about 17 J K−1 mol−1.44

Worked example 9. Why neopentane melts so high

Data. Enthalpies of fusion and triple-point temperatures: n-pentane 8.40 kJ mol−1 at 143.5 K; isopentane 5.13 kJ mol−1 at 113.4 K; neopentane 3.26 kJ mol−1 at 256.5 K.20

Entropies of fusion. ΔfusS = ΔfusH/Tm: n-pentane 58.6, isopentane 45.2, neopentane 12.7 J K−1 mol−1.

Interpretation. Neopentane has the smallest enthalpy of fusion, consistent with weaker intermolecular attraction, but its entropy of fusion is smaller still, by a factor of nearly five relative to n-pentane. The rotationally disordered solid has already gained most of the entropy that the other isomers gain only on melting, so the ratio ΔfusH/ΔfusS is large. Its ΔfusS is below Timmermans's plastic-crystal threshold.44

Odd–even alternation. The melting points of the short n-alkanes do not rise smoothly with chain length; chains with an even number of carbon atoms melt relatively higher than their odd neighbours (Figure 4). Boese, Weiss and Bläser determined the crystal structures of the C3–C9 alkanes at low temperature and traced the alternation to packing: even-numbered chains pack more densely, with more favourable contacts at the chain ends, than odd-numbered chains.43 Hydrogen bonding, like dispersion, also raises melting points: water melts at 0 °C whereas H2S melts at about −86 °C.18

7.3 Vapour pressure

The vapour pressure of a liquid is the pressure of its vapour in equilibrium with the liquid at a given temperature. Molecules escape from the liquid only if they have enough energy to overcome the intermolecular attractions, so stronger forces mean lower vapour pressure at a given temperature, and a liquid with a high vapour pressure at room temperature is called volatile.10 The temperature dependence follows the Clausius–Clapeyron equation, which, if ΔvapH is taken as constant, integrates to10

lnp2p1=−ΔvapHR(1T2−1T1)(12)

In practice, vapour pressures are reported with empirical fits such as the Antoine equation, log10(p/bar) = A − B/(T/K + C), whose parameters for many compounds are compiled in the NIST Chemistry WebBook.20

Worked example 10. Two isomers of C4H10O

Data. Antoine parameters: diethyl ether, A = 4.13377, B = 1102.878, C = −40.46 (212–293 K); 1-butanol, A = 4.54607, B = 1351.555, C = −93.34 (296–391 K).20

Calculation at 293.15 K (20 °C). Diethyl ether: log10p = 4.13377 − 1102.878/252.69 = −0.2308, so p = 0.588 bar = 58.8 kPa. 1-Butanol: log10p = 4.54607 − 1351.555/199.81 = −2.2181, so p = 0.0060 bar = 0.61 kPa (a short extrapolation, 2.6 K below the fitted range).

Interpretation. The two isomers have the same molar mass and similar dispersion forces, but the vapour pressure of the ether is almost 100 times that of the alcohol, because only the alcohol's molecules are held together by O–H···O hydrogen bonds. Correspondingly, diethyl ether boils at 307.7 K and 1-butanol at 390.6 K.20

Worked example 11. The vapour pressure of water at 25 °C from its boiling point

Data. T1 = 373.12 K, p1 = 101.325 kPa; ΔvapH = 40.66 kJ mol−1 at 100 °C and 43.99 kJ mol−1 at 25 °C.35

Calculation. Using the mean value, 42.33 kJ mol−1, in Equation (12) with T2 = 298.15 K: ln(p2/p1) = −(42 330/8.3145)(1/298.15 − 1/373.12) = −3.432, so p2 = 101.325 × e−3.432 = 3.28 kPa.

Comparison. The IAPWS-95 reference value is 3.17 kPa,35 so the estimate is within 4%. Using ΔvapH at 100 °C alone gives 3.75 kPa, and using the 25 °C value alone gives 2.87 kPa, which shows the effect of the temperature dependence of ΔvapH that Equation (12) neglects.

7.4 Viscosity

Viscosity η is a liquid's resistance to flow, defined as the ratio of shear stress to the velocity gradient; its SI unit is Pa s, and 1 mPa s equals the older unit of 1 centipoise.10 In Eyring's model, a molecule in a liquid can move only by jumping into a neighbouring vacancy over an energy barrier set by the attractions to its neighbours, so viscosity falls steeply with temperature, approximately as η ∝ exp(Ea/RT).45 Stronger intermolecular forces raise the barrier, and molecules that are long and can entangle, or that form multiple hydrogen bonds, flow especially slowly (Table 6).

Glycerol, HOCH2CH(OH)CH2OH, has three hydroxyl groups per molecule and a viscosity about 1400 times that of water at 20 °C.48 Hexane, held together only by dispersion, is about one-third as viscous as water although its molecules are nearly five times as heavy.20,46

Worked example 12. Activation energy for viscous flow of water

Data. η(H2O) = 1.0014 mPa s at 293.16 K and 0.6526 mPa s at 313.16 K, from the IAPWS 2008 viscosity formulation.20,46

Calculation. From η = A exp(Ea/RT), Ea = R ln(η1/η2)/(1/T1 − 1/T2) = 8.3145 × ln(1.5345)/(2.178 × 10−4 K−1) = 16.3 kJ mol−1.

Comparison. The same calculation for n-hexane between 20 °C (0.3132 mPa s) and 25 °C (0.2980 mPa s) gives about 7 kJ mol−1.20 The barrier for water is of the same order as the energy of a hydrogen bond (Section 3.2), consistent with flow requiring hydrogen bonds to be broken and re-formed. The Arrhenius form is only approximate for water: between 0 and 50 °C the same method gives 17.4 kJ mol−1, because the hydrogen-bond network weakens as temperature rises.

7.5 Surface tension

A molecule in the interior of a liquid is attracted equally in all directions, but a molecule at the surface has neighbours only on one side and is pulled inward. Creating new surface therefore costs energy, and the surface tension γ is the work needed to increase the area of the surface by one unit, measured in J m−2 or, equivalently, N m−1.10,11 Liquids with strong intermolecular forces have high surface tensions (Table 6). IAPWS gives the surface tension of water along the saturation curve as γ = Bτμ(1 + bτ), with τ = 1 − T/Tc, Tc = 647.096 K, B = 235.8 mN m−1, b = −0.625 and μ = 1.256; this gives 72.74 mN m−1 at 20 °C and 71.97 mN m−1 at 25 °C.47,49

Fowkes proposed that the surface tension can be split into a dispersion component γd, present in every liquid, and a component from polar and hydrogen-bonding interactions.50 For saturated hydrocarbons the whole surface tension is dispersive, whereas for water only about 22 of the 72.8 mN m−1 at 20 °C is dispersive; the remainder comes from hydrogen bonding.50 This partition underlies the methods used to measure the surface energies of solids from contact angles.

Worked example 13. Capillary rise of water and hexane

Data. At 25 °C: water γ = 71.97 mN m−1,47 ρ = 997.05 kg m−3;20 hexane γ = 17.87 mN m−1, ρ = 654.85 kg m−3.20 Clean glass capillary of radius r = 0.25 mm, both liquids assumed to wet the glass completely (contact angle θ = 0).

Calculation. The height of rise is h = 2γ cos θ/(ρgr).10 Water: h = 2 × 0.07197/(997.05 × 9.807 × 2.5 × 10−4) = 0.0589 m = 5.9 cm. Hexane: h = 2 × 0.01787/(654.85 × 9.807 × 2.5 × 10−4) = 0.0223 m = 2.2 cm.

Interpretation. Water's surface tension is four times that of hexane. Its higher density offsets some of that, so it rises 2.6 times higher. The difference is due to water's hydrogen-bonded network.

7.6 Solubility

The rule of thumb that "like dissolves like" expresses the energetics of mixing. A solute dissolves readily when the attractions between solute and solvent molecules are comparable to the solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions they replace; it dissolves poorly when one component's self-attraction is much stronger, because separating its molecules costs energy that is not repaid.10,51 Hildebrand quantified this for nonpolar mixtures with the solubility parameter δ, the square root of the cohesive energy density:51

δ=ΔvapH−RTVm(13)

where Vm is the molar volume of the liquid. Liquids with similar δ tend to be miscible, and the enthalpy of mixing in regular-solution theory is proportional to (δ1 − δ2)2.51 Hansen extended the idea by dividing δ into dispersion, polar and hydrogen-bonding components, δ2 = δD2 + δP2 + δH2, which makes the parameter useful for polar and hydrogen-bonding liquids as well.52

Worked example 14. Why hexane and water do not mix

Data at 298.15 K. Hexane: ΔvapH = 31.6 kJ mol−1, M = 86.18 g mol−1, ρ = 0.6549 g cm−3.20 Water: ΔvapH = 43.99 kJ mol−1,35 M = 18.015 g mol−1, ρ = 0.9970 g cm−3.20 RT = 2.48 kJ mol−1.

Hexane. Vm = 86.18/0.6549 = 131.6 cm3 mol−1; δ = √[(31 600 − 2480) J mol−1/131.6 × 10−6 m3 mol−1] = √(2.21 × 108 Pa) = 14.9 MPa1/2.

Water. Vm = 18.07 cm3 mol−1; δ = √[(43 990 − 2480)/18.07 × 10−6] = √(2.30 × 109 Pa) = 47.9 MPa1/2.

Interpretation. The cohesive energy density of water is ten times that of hexane, almost entirely because of hydrogen bonding. Mixing would break water–water hydrogen bonds that hexane cannot replace, so the two liquids separate. The calculated values agree with those tabulated by Hansen (14.9 and 47.8 MPa1/2).52

Hydrogen bonding and the hydrophobic effect. Small alcohols mix with water in all proportions because their O–H groups take part in water's hydrogen-bond network. As the hydrocarbon chain grows, the nonpolar part dominates and the solubility falls steeply, so that the higher alcohols are only sparingly soluble.10 The tendency of nonpolar groups to be expelled from water and to associate with one another, which Kauzmann identified as a major force stabilizing folded proteins, is called the hydrophobic effect.53 For small solutes it is mainly entropic, arising from the restructuring of water around the solute; for large solutes it is dominated by the cost of creating an interface, as Chandler showed by analysing the length scales involved.54

Ions. Ionic compounds dissolve in water and other polar solvents because ion–dipole interactions with the solvent compensate for the lattice energy (Section 4.3), and in nonpolar solvents they do not dissolve because no such compensation is available.6,10 Nonpolar gases dissolve slightly in water through dispersion and dipole–induced dipole attractions (Section 5.3).11

8 Summary

The interactions between molecules can be ranked roughly by strength: ion–dipole interactions and the strongest hydrogen bonds are strongest, followed by ordinary hydrogen bonds, then dipole–dipole, induction and dispersion forces. The total cohesion of a substance does not follow this ranking, however, because dispersion grows with molecular size and acts between every pair of atoms. The forces of attraction in iodine or a long-chain alkane, held together by dispersion alone, exceed those in small polar molecules such as HCl. Three quantities summarize most of the chemistry: the dipole moment (Keesom and Debye forces), the polarizability (London, Debye and ion–induced dipole forces), and the presence of X–H donors and lone-pair acceptors on electronegative atoms (hydrogen bonds).
