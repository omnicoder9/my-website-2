Chemistry Series

Quantum Chemistry and Electronic Structure

From the quantisation of light to the electron configurations of the elements: the physical evidence, the mathematical framework, and the rules that organise the periodic table.

Abstract

Chemistry is, at bottom, the behaviour of electrons bound to nuclei, and electrons obey quantum mechanics. This article develops the subject in four stages. It first reviews the experimental facts that forced the abandonment of classical physics at atomic scales: the spectrum of blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect, discrete atomic spectra and the wave behaviour of matter. It then sets out the formal machinery of quantum mechanics (wavefunctions, the Schrödinger equation, the Born probability rule, operators and eigenvalue problems) and shows how four quantum numbers arise. The third part describes the shapes, energies and nodal structure of s, p, d and f atomic orbitals using the exactly solvable hydrogen atom. The final part explains how the Pauli exclusion principle, the aufbau principle and Hund's rule generate the ground-state electron configurations of atoms and ions, and why a number of elements deviate from simple filling rules. Numerical values of constants are the CODATA 2022 recommended values1 and spectroscopic data are taken from the NIST Atomic Spectra Database.2

1 Quantum foundations

By the end of the nineteenth century classical mechanics and Maxwell's electrodynamics appeared to explain almost every physical phenomenon. A small number of stubborn observations involving the interaction of light with matter did not fit, and resolving them produced quantum theory. Because those observations are also the experimental basis of modern spectroscopy, they are treated here in some detail.

1.1 Electromagnetic radiation

Electromagnetic radiation is a self-propagating oscillation of coupled electric and magnetic fields. Maxwell showed in 1865 that his field equations admit wave solutions travelling at a speed fixed by the electric and magnetic constants of free space, and that this speed equals the measured speed of light, from which he concluded that light is an electromagnetic disturbance.3 In a plane wave travelling in vacuum the electric field E and magnetic field B are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation, so the wave is transverse, and its polarisation is the direction of oscillation of E.4,5

The various "kinds" of radiation (radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and γ-rays) differ only in wavelength, and therefore in frequency and photon energy (Figure 1). For chemists the division matters because each region couples to a different molecular degree of freedom: microwaves drive rotational transitions, infrared radiation drives vibrational transitions, visible and ultraviolet radiation promotes valence electrons, and X-rays eject or excite core electrons.5

1.2 Wavelength

The wavelength, λ, is the distance between successive points of equal phase (for example, adjacent crests) of a wave; its SI unit is the metre, and nanometres (1 nm = 10−9 m) are customary for visible and ultraviolet light.6 Spectroscopists frequently use the wavenumber, ν̃ = 1/λ, conventionally in cm−1, because it is directly proportional to photon energy.5,6 A wavelength of 500 nm corresponds to a wavenumber of 20 000 cm−1.

1.3 Frequency

The frequency, ν, is the number of complete oscillations that pass a fixed point per unit time; its SI unit is the hertz (1 Hz = 1 s−1).7 The angular frequency is ω = 2πν. Frequency, unlike wavelength, does not change when light passes from one medium into another, which is why it, rather than the wavelength in air, is the more fundamental label for a spectral transition.4

1.4 Speed of light

All electromagnetic radiation travels in vacuum at the same speed, c. Wavelength and frequency are therefore linked by

c=λν(1)

Since 1983 the metre has been defined in terms of c, so the speed of light in vacuum is now an exact defined constant, c = 299 792 458 m s−1, rather than a measured quantity.1,7 In a material medium of refractive index n the phase velocity is c/n; this is why tabulated spectral lines specify whether a wavelength refers to vacuum or to standard air.2

Worked example 1

Green light of vacuum wavelength 500 nm has frequency ν = c/λ = (2.997 924 58 × 108 m s−1)/(5.00 × 10−7 m) = 6.00 × 1014 Hz.

1.5 Photon energy

Light is absorbed and emitted in discrete quanta, photons, each carrying energy proportional to its frequency:5,8

E=hν=hcλ=hcν~(2)

A photon also carries linear momentum p = h/λ, which Compton confirmed in 1923 by showing that X-rays scattered from electrons are shifted to longer wavelength exactly as expected for a collision between particles.9 A convenient working form of equation (2) is E/eV ≈ 1239.84/(λ/nm), obtained from the exact SI values of h, c and the elementary charge e.1

Worked example 2

For λ = 500 nm, E = hc/λ = (6.626 070 15 × 10−34 J s)(2.997 924 58 × 108 m s−1)/(5.00 × 10−7 m) = 3.97 × 10−19 J = 2.48 eV. Per mole of photons (multiplying by the Avogadro constant, 6.022 140 76 × 1023 mol−11) this is 239 kJ mol−1, comparable to the energy of a chemical bond, which is why visible and ultraviolet light can drive photochemistry.5

1.6 Planck's constant

The proportionality constant h in equation (2) is the Planck constant. It was introduced by Planck in his 1900–1901 derivation of the blackbody radiation law,10 and it sets the scale at which quantum effects become important. Since the 2019 revision of the SI, h has an exact defined value, h = 6.626 070 15 × 10−34 J s, and it is used to define the kilogram.1,7 The reduced Planck constant ħ = h/2π = 1.054 571 817… × 10−34 J s appears naturally wherever angular frequency or angular momentum is involved.1 Because h has the dimensions of action (energy × time, or momentum × length), quantum effects dominate whenever the characteristic action of a system is comparable to h, as it is for electrons in atoms but not for macroscopic objects.4

1.7 Blackbody radiation

A blackbody is an idealised object that absorbs all radiation falling on it; in thermal equilibrium it emits radiation whose spectrum depends only on its temperature. A small hole in the wall of a cavity held at uniform temperature is an excellent practical realisation.5 Classical statistical mechanics, which assigns an average energy kBT to every electromagnetic mode in the cavity, gives the Rayleigh–Jeans law, Bλ = 2ckBT/λ4. This agrees with experiment at long wavelengths but diverges as λ → 0, predicting an infinite total emitted power, the so-called ultraviolet catastrophe.5,11

Planck resolved the problem by postulating that the oscillators exchanging energy with the radiation field could hold energy only in integer multiples of hν.10 High-frequency modes then require a large quantum of energy that is rarely available thermally, so they are suppressed. The resulting spectral radiance per unit wavelength is

Bλ(λ,T)=2hc2λ51ehc/λkBT−1(3)

which reduces to the Rayleigh–Jeans law when hc/λ ≪ kBT (Figure 2).5,11 Two classical empirical laws follow from equation (3). Wien's displacement law states that the wavelength of maximum emission is inversely proportional to temperature, λmaxT = b, with b = 2.897 771 955… × 10−3 m K; the Stefan–Boltzmann law states that the total emitted power per unit area is σT4, with σ = 5.670 374 419… × 10−8 W m−2 K−4.1 Both constants are now exact because h, c and kB are exact.7

Worked example 3

The nominal effective temperature of the Sun adopted by the International Astronomical Union is 5772 K.12 Wien's law gives λmax = (2.898 × 10−3 m K)/(5772 K) ≈ 502 nm, in the middle of the visible region. The cosmic microwave background, measured by the COBE FIRAS instrument to be a near-perfect blackbody at 2.728 K,13 peaks at about 1.06 mm, in the microwave region.

1.8 Photoelectric effect

When light of sufficiently high frequency strikes a clean metal surface, electrons are ejected. The experimental facts are that (i) no electrons are emitted below a threshold frequency characteristic of the metal, however intense the light; (ii) above threshold, the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons increases linearly with frequency and is independent of intensity; and (iii) emission begins without measurable delay even at very low intensity, while the number of electrons emitted is proportional to intensity.5,11 None of these features follows from a classical wave picture, in which energy is delivered continuously and in proportion to intensity.

Einstein explained all three in 1905 by proposing that light itself consists of quanta of energy hν, each of which is absorbed by a single electron.8 Energy conservation then gives

Ek,max=12mevmax2=hν−Φ(4)

where Φ is the work function, the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the surface; the threshold frequency is ν0 = Φ/h. Millikan's careful measurements of stopping potentials, published in 1916, confirmed the linear relation of equation (4) and yielded a value of h in agreement with Planck's.14 The same principle underlies photoelectron spectroscopy, in which the kinetic energies of electrons ejected by ultraviolet or X-ray photons are used to measure orbital ionisation energies.5

Worked example 4

Ultraviolet light of wavelength 250 nm (photon energy 4.96 eV) falls on a metal with an assumed work function of 4.50 eV. The maximum photoelectron kinetic energy is 4.96 − 4.50 = 0.46 eV, and the stopping potential is 0.46 V. Doubling the light intensity doubles the photocurrent but leaves 0.46 eV unchanged; red light (λ = 700 nm, 1.77 eV) ejects no electrons at any intensity.

1.9 Atomic spectra

Atoms in a gas discharge emit light only at a set of sharp, element-specific wavelengths, and absorb at the same wavelengths; this is the basis of atomic emission and absorption spectroscopy.5 In 1885 Balmer found that the four visible lines of hydrogen fit a simple formula,15 and the general empirical result for hydrogen, the Rydberg formula, is

ν~=1λ=RH(1n12−1n22),n2>n1(5)

with n1 = 1 (Lyman series, ultraviolet), 2 (Balmer, visible) and 3 (Paschen, infrared).5 The Rydberg constant for infinite nuclear mass is R∞ = 10 973 731.568 157 m−1,1 and for hydrogen it is reduced by the factor 1/(1 + me/mp) to RH ≈ 1.096 776 × 107 m−1.5 The calculated Balmer lines are shown in Figure 3; the computed vacuum wavelength of Hα, 656.47 nm, corresponds to the familiar air wavelength of 656.28 nm.2

The existence of sharp lines implies that the energy of an atom is quantised: a photon is emitted when the atom drops between two allowed energies, and the Bohr frequency condition hν = |Eupper − Elower| connects the line to the levels.5,16 Bohr's 1913 model reproduced the hydrogen levels, En = −hcRH/n2, by quantising the angular momentum of circular electron orbits.16 The model failed for atoms with more than one electron and is inconsistent with the uncertainty principle (section 1.12); it is discussed further in the companion article on atomic structure. Its energy formula, however, is reproduced exactly by the Schrödinger equation (section 2.2). Not every pair of levels gives an observed line: transitions obey selection rules, of which the most important for one-electron electric-dipole transitions is Δl = ±1.5,17

1.10 Wave–particle duality

The photoelectric and Compton effects show that light, classically a wave, transfers energy and momentum in localised particle-like quanta, while diffraction and interference show that it propagates as a wave.8,9 Matter displays the converse duality. In 1927 Davisson and Germer observed that a beam of electrons scattered from a nickel crystal produces diffraction maxima at angles consistent with Bragg reflection of waves.18 Tonomura and co-workers later recorded the arrival of electrons one at a time in a biprism interferometer: each electron registered as a single localised spot, yet the accumulation of many thousands of spots built up an interference pattern.19 Interference has since been demonstrated for objects as large as C60 fullerene molecules.20

Duality does not mean that an electron is sometimes a particle and sometimes a wave. In quantum mechanics it is described by a wavefunction whose evolution is wave-like (so that amplitudes interfere) and whose squared modulus gives the probability of detecting the particle at a point (section 2.3).4,21

In his 1924 doctoral thesis, published in 1925, de Broglie proposed that the relation p = h/λ, valid for photons, applies to all particles:22

1.12 Heisenberg uncertainty principle

Heisenberg argued in 1927 that the position and momentum of a particle cannot both be specified with arbitrary precision.23 The precise modern statement, proved by Kennard the same year, concerns the standard deviations of position and momentum measured on identically prepared systems:24

σxσpx≥ℏ2(7)

Robertson generalised the result to any two observables A and B: σAσB ≥ ½|⟨[Â, B̂]⟩|, where [Â, B̂] = ÂB̂ − B̂Â is the commutator of the corresponding operators (section 2.4).25 Equation (7) follows because [x̂, p̂x] = iħ.4 The principle is not a statement about clumsy measuring instruments; it is a property of the wavefunction itself, since a function sharply localised in space is necessarily composed of a broad range of wavelengths and hence momenta.4,17

Worked example 6

An electron confined to a region of atomic size, σx ≈ 0.1 nm, has σp ≥ ħ/(2σx) = 5.3 × 10−25 kg m s−1, corresponding to a velocity spread of at least 5.8 × 105 m s−1. A well-defined classical orbit of the kind assumed in the Bohr model is therefore impossible, and the electron must instead be described by a probability distribution.

2 Quantum mechanics

Quantum mechanics replaces the classical trajectory with a wavefunction and replaces classical dynamical variables with operators. The framework rests on a small number of postulates, stated here in the form used in standard texts.4,11,17

2.1 Wavefunctions

The state of a quantum system is completely specified by its wavefunction, Ψ(r, t), a generally complex-valued function of the coordinates of all the particles and of time; all measurable information about the system can be extracted from it.17 For Ψ to represent a physical state it must be single-valued, continuous and square-integrable (so that it can be normalised), and in most chemical problems its first derivative must also be continuous wherever the potential is finite.5,17 These "well-behaved" conditions, together with the boundary conditions of a particular problem, are what restrict the allowed energies to discrete values and so produce quantisation.5

Because the governing equation is linear, any linear combination of allowed wavefunctions is also an allowed wavefunction (the superposition principle). Wavefunctions also carry a phase: multiplying Ψ by a constant eiα changes no observable, but relative phases between superposed components govern interference, and the relative signs (phases) of atomic orbital lobes determine how orbitals overlap to form bonds.4,5

2.2 Schrödinger equation

The wavefunction evolves in time according to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation:4,26

iℏ∂Ψ∂t=H^Ψ,H^=−ℏ22m∇2+V(r)(8)

where Ĥ is the Hamiltonian (total energy) operator, the first term is the kinetic energy operator and V is the potential energy. When V does not depend on time, solutions of the form Ψ = ψ(r)e−iEt/ħ exist, and the spatial factor satisfies the time-independent Schrödinger equation4,17

H^ψ=Eψ(9)

Such solutions are stationary states: their probability density does not change with time. Schrödinger introduced the equation in 1926 and in the same paper solved it for the hydrogen atom, recovering the Bohr energy levels without any ad hoc quantisation rule.26

Example: particle in a one-dimensional box. For a particle of mass m confined between infinitely high walls at x = 0 and x = L, the requirement that ψ vanish at the walls gives5,11

ψn(x)=(2L)1/2sinnπxL,En=n2h28mL2,n=1,2,3,…(10)

Three general lessons emerge. Energy is quantised by the boundary conditions; the lowest energy is not zero (a zero-point energy, consistent with the uncertainty principle); and the nth state has n − 1 nodes, so higher energy goes with more nodes.5 The model also gives a first estimate of the electronic absorption of conjugated polyenes, whose π electrons are delocalised along the carbon chain.5,11

The hydrogen atom. For a one-electron atom of nuclear charge Ze the potential is V = −Ze2/(4πε0r). Because it depends only on r, equation (9) separates in spherical polar coordinates into a radial function and an angular function, ψnlm = Rnl(r)Ylm(θ, φ), where the Ylm are spherical harmonics.4,17 The bound-state energies are5,17

En=−μmeZ2hcR∞n2,n=1,2,…(11)

where μ is the reduced mass of the electron–nucleus pair and hcR∞ = 13.605 693 122 990 eV.1 For hydrogen this gives an ionisation energy of 13.5983 eV, in agreement with the measured value of 13.598 434 6 eV2 to about one part in 105; the small residual reflects relativistic and quantum-electrodynamic corrections that the non-relativistic Schrödinger equation omits.17

Many-electron atoms. For an atom with more than one electron the electron–electron repulsion terms prevent separation of the Schrödinger equation, and no exact analytic solution exists. Practical work relies on the orbital approximation, in which each electron is assigned a one-electron wavefunction (an orbital) and moves in the average field of the nucleus and the other electrons; the Hartree–Fock method is the systematic version of this idea, and it is the foundation for modern computational quantum chemistry.17,27

2.3 Probability density

Born proposed in 1926 that the wavefunction is a probability amplitude: the probability of finding a particle in a small volume element dτ around r is |ψ(r)|2dτ = ψ*ψ dτ.21 The quantity |ψ|2 is the probability density. Because the particle must be somewhere, the wavefunction is normalised so that ∫|ψ|2dτ = 1 over all space.4,17

For atoms it is often more informative to ask how likely the electron is to be found at a given distance from the nucleus, regardless of direction. This is given by the radial distribution function5

P(r)=r2[Rnl(r)]2(12)

so that P(r)dr is the probability of finding the electron in a spherical shell of radius r and thickness dr.

Worked example 7

The hydrogen 1s orbital is ψ1s = (πa03)−1/2e−r/a0, where a0 = 5.291 772 105 44 × 10−11 m is the Bohr radius.1,17 Its probability density is greatest at the nucleus, but P(r) = (4/a03)r2e−2r/a0 vanishes at r = 0 and peaks at r = a0 (for infinite nuclear mass), because the volume of a spherical shell grows as r2. Integrating P(r) from 0 to a0 gives 1 − 5e−2 ≈ 0.323: the electron is found inside the Bohr radius only about a third of the time.

2.4 Operators

Each observable property corresponds to a linear Hermitian operator, constructed by writing the classical expression in terms of Cartesian coordinates and momenta and then replacing each momentum component by its operator, p̂x = −iħ ∂/∂x, while position operators simply multiply by the coordinate.11,17 Thus the kinetic energy p2/2m becomes −(ħ2/2m)∇2, and the Hamiltonian of equation (8) is obtained.

Hermiticity guarantees that the operator's eigenvalues are real, as measured quantities must be, and that eigenfunctions belonging to different eigenvalues are orthogonal.4,17 The average result of many measurements of an observable A on systems in state ψ is the expectation value4

⟨A⟩=∫ψ*A^ψdτ(13)

Operators need not commute. The order of operation matters when the commutator [Â, B̂] is non-zero, and in that case the two observables cannot in general have simultaneously well-defined values (section 1.12). Conversely, commuting operators possess a common set of eigenfunctions, which is why the hydrogen-atom orbitals can simultaneously have definite energy, definite squared orbital angular momentum L̂2 and definite z-component L̂z.4,17

2.5 Eigenvalues and eigenfunctions

If an operator acting on a function returns the same function multiplied by a constant, Âψ = aψ, then ψ is an eigenfunction of Â and a is the corresponding eigenvalue.17 Equation (9) is therefore an eigenvalue equation: the allowed energies are the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian and the stationary states are its eigenfunctions. The measurement postulate states that any single measurement of A yields one of the eigenvalues of Â. If the system is in an eigenstate, that eigenvalue is obtained with certainty; if it is in a superposition ψ = Σ ciψi of normalised eigenfunctions, the eigenvalue ai is obtained with probability |ci|2.4,17

Worked example 8

The function eikx is an eigenfunction of p̂x: −iħ d(eikx)/dx = ħk eikx, with eigenvalue p = ħk = h/λ, recovering the de Broglie relation. By contrast, the particle-in-a-box function sin(kx) = (eikx − e−ikx)/2i is not an eigenfunction of p̂x; it is an equal superposition of momenta +ħk and −ħk, each of which is found with probability ½.4,5

When two or more linearly independent eigenfunctions share the same eigenvalue they are said to be degenerate. Any linear combination of degenerate eigenfunctions is also an eigenfunction with that eigenvalue; this freedom is used to convert the complex hydrogen-atom orbitals into the real orbitals (px, py, dxy and so on) used in chemistry (section 3).17

Solving the hydrogen-atom Schrödinger equation subject to the well-behaved conditions introduces three integers, n, l and ml, which label the orbitals. A fourth, the spin magnetic quantum number ms, is needed to describe the electron completely.5,17 Table 1 summarises them.

2.6.1 Principal quantum number

The principal quantum number n takes positive integer values and labels the shell (historically K, L, M, … for n = 1, 2, 3, …). In a one-electron atom it alone fixes the energy (equation 11), and the average distance of the electron from the nucleus increases roughly as n2.5,17 A shell contains n2 orbitals and so can hold 2n2 electrons; the total number of nodes in an orbital is n − 1.5

2.6.2 Angular momentum quantum number

The orbital angular momentum (azimuthal) quantum number l takes the values 0, 1, …, n − 1 and specifies the magnitude of the electron's orbital angular momentum, [l(l + 1)]1/2ħ.17 Orbitals with l = 0, 1, 2, 3 are called s, p, d and f; the letters derive from the spectroscopists' descriptions of series of lines as "sharp", "principal", "diffuse" and "fundamental".5,17 The value of l fixes the shape of the orbital and the number of angular nodes. In many-electron atoms orbitals of the same n but different l are no longer degenerate: an s electron penetrates closer to the nucleus than a p electron of the same shell and is less shielded by inner electrons, so for a given n the energies increase in the order s < p < d < f.5,28

2.6.3 Magnetic quantum number

The magnetic quantum number ml takes the 2l + 1 integer values from −l to +l and specifies the component of orbital angular momentum along a chosen axis, Lz = mlħ.17 A subshell therefore contains one s, three p, five d or seven f orbitals. In a free atom these orbitals are degenerate; the name reflects the fact that an applied magnetic field lifts the degeneracy because the orbital magnetic moment is proportional to ml (the Zeeman effect).5 Since only one component of angular momentum can be sharp at a time (the components do not commute), the angular momentum vector can be pictured as lying anywhere on a cone about the z axis.4,5

2.6.4 Spin quantum number

In 1922 Gerlach and Stern passed a beam of silver atoms through an inhomogeneous magnetic field and observed it split into two discrete components rather than spreading continuously, a direct demonstration of space quantisation.29 Silver has a single unpaired 5s electron with l = 0 and no orbital angular momentum, so the two components could not be explained by ml. Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit proposed in 1925 that the electron possesses an intrinsic angular momentum, spin, to account for features of atomic spectra.30 The electron has spin quantum number s = ½, so the magnitude of its spin angular momentum is [s(s + 1)]1/2ħ = (√3/2)ħ, and its component along an axis is msħ with ms = +½ ("spin up", α) or −½ ("spin down", β).5,17 The associated magnetic moment is governed by the electron g-factor, ge = −2.002 319 304 360 92, whose slight deviation from −2 is a quantum-electrodynamic effect.1 Spin emerges naturally from Dirac's relativistic theory of the electron but must be added to the non-relativistic Schrödinger theory as a postulate.17

3 Atomic orbitals

An atomic orbital is a one-electron wavefunction for an electron in an atom.5,6 The hydrogen-atom solutions ψnlm are exact; for many-electron atoms, orbitals of the same symmetry and qualitatively similar shape are used within the orbital approximation.17 Shapes are conventionally depicted as boundary surfaces enclosing a high fraction (often 90 per cent) of the probability density, with the sign of the wavefunction in each lobe indicated by shading.5 The radial functions Rnl used below are taken from standard tabulations.11,17

3.1 s orbitals

For l = 0 the angular function Y00 = (4π)−1/2 is a constant, so s orbitals are spherically symmetric. They are the only orbitals with non-zero amplitude at the nucleus.5,17 The 1s orbital decays exponentially from the nucleus; 2s and 3s orbitals have one and two radial nodes respectively, at which the radial function changes sign (Figure 4). This non-zero density at the nucleus underlies the penetration effect described in section 2.6.2, and it is also why s electrons determine properties such as hyperfine coupling and NMR contact shifts.5

3.2 p orbitals

For l = 1 there are three orbitals in each shell from n = 2 upward. The ml = 0 function is real and proportional to cos θ = z/r, so it is labelled pz; the ml = ±1 functions are complex, proportional to sin θ e±iφ, and their sum and difference give real functions proportional to x/r and y/r, labelled px and py.5,17 The real px and py orbitals are therefore not eigenfunctions of L̂z, but they remain eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian and of L̂2 because they are combinations of degenerate functions (section 2.5). Each p orbital has two lobes of opposite sign separated by a nodal plane through the nucleus, and the three are directed along the Cartesian axes (Figure 5).5

3.3 d orbitals

For l = 2 there are five orbitals in each shell from n = 3 upward. The conventional real set consists of dxy, dyz and dzx, whose four lobes lie between the axes; dx²−y², whose lobes lie along the x and y axes; and dz² (more fully d3z²−r²), which has two lobes along z and a toroidal collar of opposite sign in the xy plane.5,28 The angular part of dz² is proportional to 3cos2θ − 1, which vanishes on two cones at θ = 54.7° and 125.3°.17 The orientation of the d orbitals relative to surrounding ligands is the basis of crystal-field and ligand-field theory: in an octahedral complex, dx²−y² and dz² point at the ligands and are raised in energy (the eg set) relative to dxy, dyz and dzx (the t2g set).28

3.4 f orbitals

For l = 3 there are seven orbitals in each shell from n = 4 upward, each with three angular nodes. Two different real sets are in common use: a "general" set convenient for describing complexes of axial symmetry, and a "cubic" set adapted to octahedral and tetrahedral environments; the two are related by linear combination and are physically equivalent in the free atom.28 The 4f orbitals are filled across the lanthanides and the 5f across the actinides. Because the 4f orbitals have no radial node and lie largely inside the filled 5s and 5p subshells, they are radially contracted and participate little in bonding. Lanthanide chemistry is therefore dominated by the +3 oxidation state, and the electronic spectra of lanthanide ions show sharp, ligand-insensitive f–f bands.28 The poor shielding provided by 4f electrons produces the lanthanide contraction, the steady decrease in radius across the series, which makes the second- and third-row transition metals (for example Zr and Hf) almost identical in size.28

3.5 Nodes

A node is a point, surface or region where the wavefunction passes through zero and changes sign, so the probability density vanishes there.5 An orbital with quantum numbers n and l has n − 1 nodes in total, of which l are angular and n − l − 1 are radial.5,17 The rule that energy rises with the number of nodes, already seen for the particle in a box, holds here too: more nodes means greater curvature of the wavefunction and therefore greater kinetic energy.5

3.5.1 Radial nodes

Radial nodes are spherical surfaces at fixed distances from the nucleus where the radial function Rnl(r) is zero. Their positions follow from the roots of the polynomial part of Rnl.17 For hydrogen the 2s radial function is proportional to (2 − r/a0)e−r/2a0, so its node lies at r = 2a0. The 3s radial function is proportional to (27 − 18ρ + 2ρ2)e−ρ/3 with ρ = r/a0, whose roots are ρ = (9 ± 3√3)/2, that is, r = 1.90a0 and 7.10a0 (Figure 4).11,17 For a hydrogen-like ion of nuclear charge Z all these distances scale as 1/Z. Radial nodes are not visible in a boundary-surface picture, which is one reason the radial distribution function is a useful complement to orbital drawings.5

3.5.2 Angular nodes

Angular nodes are surfaces, independent of r, on which the angular function Ylm(θ, φ) vanishes; they pass through the nucleus. For real orbitals they are planes or cones.5,17 A p orbital has one nodal plane (for example the xy plane for pz). Four of the five d orbitals have two perpendicular nodal planes (for example the xz and yz planes for dxy, and the planes x = ±y for dx²−y²), while dz² has two conical nodes. The number of angular nodes equals l and determines the behaviour of the orbital under inversion through the nucleus: orbitals with even l (s, d) are symmetric (gerade) and those with odd l (p, f) are antisymmetric (ungerade).5,17 This parity underlies the Laporte selection rule that forbids electric-dipole transitions between states of the same parity, such as d–d transitions in centrosymmetric complexes.28

4 Electron configurations

An electron configuration specifies how many electrons occupy each subshell, for example 1s22s22p4 for oxygen. It is a statement within the orbital approximation, and it labels a group of quantum states rather than a single state.5,17 The ground-state configurations of the elements, which underlie the structure of the periodic table, are established experimentally by atomic spectroscopy; those quoted below are the evaluated data of the NIST Atomic Spectra Database.2 Three principles rationalise them.

4.1 Aufbau principle

The aufbau ("building-up") principle states that the ground-state configuration of an atom can be obtained by adding electrons one at a time to the lowest-energy available orbitals, subject to the Pauli principle and Hund's rule, while the nuclear charge increases by one unit at each step.5,28 For neutral atoms the empirical filling order is

1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d < 5p < 6s < 4f < 5d < 6p < 7s < 5f < 6d < 7p

which is summarised by the Madelung (or Klechkowski) n + l rule: subshells fill in order of increasing n + l, and for equal n + l, in order of increasing n.31,32 The ordering is a consequence of penetration and shielding (section 2.6.2), which lower the energies of low-l subshells in many-electron atoms enough that, for example, 4s is occupied before 3d in potassium and calcium.5,28

The aufbau order is a rule for the sequence of ground-state configurations as Z increases. It does not mean that, within a given atom, the 4s orbital is lower in energy than 3d. In the transition elements the 3d orbitals lie below 4s, and the occupation of 4s in neutral atoms reflects the total energy of the whole configuration, including electron–electron repulsion, rather than the ordering of individual orbital energies. That is why transition-metal atoms lose their 4s electrons first on ionisation (section 4.7).32,33

4.2 Pauli exclusion principle

Pauli formulated the exclusion principle in 1925 to account for the closing of electron shells and the structure of atomic spectra: no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers n, l, ml and ms.34 Consequently an orbital holds at most two electrons, and if it holds two their spins are paired (one α, one β). A subshell of angular momentum l can therefore accommodate 2(2l + 1) electrons: 2 for s, 6 for p, 10 for d and 14 for f.5

The exclusion principle is a special case of a more general postulate: the total wavefunction of a system of identical fermions (particles of half-integer spin, such as electrons) must be antisymmetric with respect to the interchange of any two of them.4,17 A wavefunction in which two electrons occupy the same spin-orbital would be identical to its negative and so would vanish. Antisymmetry is built into many-electron wavefunctions by writing them as Slater determinants, the starting point of Hartree–Fock theory.27 Without the exclusion principle every electron would occupy the 1s orbital, and there would be no shell structure and no periodic table.5

4.3 Hund's rule

Hund deduced a set of rules from his analysis of the complex spectra of the elements scandium to nickel.35 The first, usually called simply Hund's rule or the rule of maximum multiplicity, states that for a given configuration the term of lowest energy is the one with the greatest total spin. Operationally, when electrons occupy a set of degenerate orbitals they enter separate orbitals with parallel spins before any orbital is doubly occupied.5,17

The traditional explanation is that electrons with parallel spins tend to avoid one another (a consequence of antisymmetry often called Fermi or exchange correlation), which reduces their mutual repulsion. Accurate calculations show the more subtle reality that in the high-spin state the electron–electron repulsion is in fact slightly larger. The stabilisation arises because the parallel-spin electrons shield each other less effectively, so the orbitals contract and electron–nucleus attraction increases by more than the repulsion does.17 Experimental ground levels confirm the rule. Carbon (2p2) has a 3P ground term, nitrogen (2p3) 4S° and oxygen (2p4) 3P, chromium (3d54s), with six parallel spins, has a septet 7S ground term, and manganese (3d54s2), with five, has a sextet 6S ground term.2

4.5 Ground states

The ground state is the lowest-energy state of an atom. Its configuration is commonly written in condensed form, with the preceding noble-gas core in brackets: sodium is [Ne]3s1, and iron is [Ar]3d64s2.2,28 Electrons outside the noble-gas core are the valence electrons, and atoms with analogous valence configurations fall into the same group of the periodic table, which explains the recurrence of chemical properties. Lithium, sodium and potassium all have an ns1 configuration and are reactive metals that readily form +1 ions; the halogens are all ns2np5, one electron short of a noble-gas configuration.28,32 The blocks of the periodic table (s, p, d, f) correspond to the subshell that is being filled.28

4.6 Excited states

An excited state is any state of higher energy than the ground state. In the simplest picture an electron is promoted to a higher orbital, giving an excited configuration, and the absorption or emission lines of the atom correspond to transitions between such states.5 Three examples illustrate the main features.

  • Sodium. Promotion of the 3s electron to 3p gives the [Ne]3p excited configuration. Spin–orbit coupling splits it into two levels, 2P1/2 and 2P3/2, and transitions from these to the ground state produce the yellow sodium D lines at 589.59 and 589.00 nm (air wavelengths), which are responsible for the colour of sodium street lamps and sodium flame tests.2,5
  • Helium. The 1s12s1 excited configuration gives a singlet state (spins paired) and a triplet state (spins parallel). Consistent with Hund's rule the triplet lies lower, about 19.82 eV above the ground state compared with about 20.62 eV for the singlet.2,5
  • Carbon. The 1s22s12p3 excited configuration, with four unpaired electrons, is the starting point of the valence-bond description of tetravalent carbon, in which the energy cost of promotion is repaid by the formation of two additional bonds.5

Highly excited (Rydberg) states, in which an electron occupies an orbital of very large n, have hydrogen-like energies that converge on the ionisation limit, just as the Balmer lines converge in Figure 3.5

4.7 Electron configurations of ions

Anions are formed by adding electrons according to the aufbau principle, typically completing the valence p subshell: O2− and F− are 1s22s22p6, isoelectronic with neon, and Cl− is [Ne]3s23p6, isoelectronic with argon.28

Main-group cations lose electrons from the subshell of highest n first, in reverse order of filling within the valence shell: Na+ is [Ne], Mg2+ is [Ne], and Al3+ is [Ne]. Heavier p-block elements can lose only their np electrons, giving ions such as Sn2+ ([Kr]4d105s2) and Pb2+ ([Xe]4f145d106s2) that retain an ns2 pair, a feature associated with the inert-pair effect.2,28

Transition-metal cations lose their ns electrons before their (n−1)d electrons, even though ns is filled first in the neutral atoms.28,33 The free-ion ground configurations from spectroscopy are Fe2+ [Ar]3d6, Cr+ [Ar]3d5 and Cu+ [Ar]3d10.2 Once the nuclear charge is no longer balanced by a full complement of electrons, the (n−1)d orbitals fall well below ns, so ions with a net positive charge strongly favour dn configurations.33 The preference is not absolute in the gas-phase singly charged ions: Fe+ ([Ar]3d64s) and Mn+ ([Ar]3d54s) keep one 4s electron in their ground states.2 In coordination compounds and solids, however, transition-metal ions are described as dn (Fe3+ as d5, Cu2+ as d9), which is the starting point of ligand-field theory.28

Worked example 9

Write the configuration of Co3+. Neutral cobalt (Z = 27) is [Ar]3d74s2.2 Remove the two 4s electrons first and then one 3d electron: Co3+ = [Ar]3d6. In an octahedral complex such as [Co(NH3)6]3+ these six d electrons occupy the lower t2g set.28

Several factors combine to produce these anomalies.

  • Near-degeneracy of (n−1)d and ns. In the transition series the (n−1)d and ns orbital energies are close, so configurations such as dns2, dn+1s1 and dn+2 lie within a few electronvolts of one another, and small changes in electron–electron repulsion and exchange decide which is lowest.31,33
  • Exchange stabilisation of half-filled and filled subshells. The d5s1 configurations of Cr and Mo, with six parallel spins, gain exchange energy relative to d4s2. The d10s1 configurations of Cu, Ag and Au benefit from a filled d subshell.28 The popular statement that half-filled and filled subshells are "especially stable" is a shorthand for this exchange effect and should not be over-applied: tungsten, directly below molybdenum, has the regular [Xe]4f145d46s2 configuration.2,33
  • Relativistic effects. In heavy atoms the inner electrons move at a substantial fraction of the speed of light. Relativity contracts and stabilises s (and to a lesser degree p) orbitals and indirectly expands and destabilises d and f orbitals.36 The 6s contraction in gold accounts for its high ionisation energy, its unusually large electron affinity and, through a reduced 5d–6s energy gap, its colour, and relativistic effects strongly influence the 5d/6s competition in platinum.36
  • Lanthanides and actinides. At the start of the f block the 4f (or 5f) and 5d (or 6d) orbitals are close in energy. La and Th place electrons in d rather than f, and Gd takes 4f75d6s2 to preserve a half-filled 4f subshell.2,28

These exceptions do not invalidate the aufbau approach. They show that the configuration of lowest total energy is determined by the whole many-electron energy, not by the order of orbital energies alone, and that the Madelung rule is an empirical summary of typical behaviour rather than a law.31–33 Chemically, the exceptions in neutral atoms have limited consequences: the chemistry of these elements is dominated by their ions and compounds, where dn configurations prevail (section 4.7).33
