1 Development of Atomic Theory

The modern atom was not discovered in a single stroke. It was assembled over roughly 2,400 years, first as a philosophical postulate, then as a quantitative hypothesis that explained the laws of chemical combination, and finally, between 1897 and the late 1920s, as a physical object whose internal structure could be probed by experiment and described by quantum mechanics. Each model in the sequence below was retained for what it explained and revised for what it could not.

1.1 Democritus and ancient atomism

Atomism originated in the fifth century BCE with Leucippus and his student Democritus of Abdera. They held that reality consists of two things only: indivisible, unchangeable bodies (atomoi, "uncuttables") and the empty void in which they move [1,2]. Atoms were thought to differ only in shape, arrangement and position; their number was infinite and their motion eternal. The qualities we perceive, such as colour, taste and warmth, were attributed to the configuration of atoms rather than to the atoms themselves, a position captured in the fragment attributed to Democritus: "by convention sweet, by convention bitter … but in reality atoms and void" [2].

The atomist argument was primarily logical. It responded to the Eleatic claim (Parmenides) that change and plurality are impossible by accepting that "what is" cannot come from "what is not", but locating permanence in the atoms and change in their rearrangement [1]. Infinite divisibility was rejected because a body divided without limit would be reduced to nothing, or to parts of no magnitude from which no magnitude could be reconstituted [1,2].

Atomism was later developed by Epicurus and given its fullest surviving exposition in Lucretius's De rerum natura, but Aristotle's continuum theory of matter dominated natural philosophy for most of the following two millennia [1,3].

Assessment

Democritean atomism anticipated two durable ideas: that macroscopic diversity arises from the combination of a small number of kinds of constituent, and that observable properties are emergent rather than intrinsic to those constituents. It was not, however, a scientific theory in the modern sense. It made no quantitative predictions, it assigned no measurable properties to atoms, and it was not tested by experiment [1].

1.2 Dalton's atomic theory

Atomism became a chemical theory only after the quantitative laws of combination were established. Lavoisier's systematic use of the balance underpinned the law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction the total mass of the products equals that of the reactants [3,4]. Proust's law of definite proportions (around 1794–1799) stated that a given compound always contains its constituent elements in the same proportion by mass [3].

John Dalton's A New System of Chemical Philosophy (1808) supplied a mechanism for these regularities [5]. His postulates, in modern wording, were [3,5,6]:

  1. Each element is composed of extremely small particles called atoms.
  2. All atoms of a given element are identical in mass and other properties; atoms of different elements differ.
  3. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions; reactions rearrange them.
  4. Compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in fixed, small whole-number ratios.

Postulate 3 explains conservation of mass and postulate 4 explains definite proportions. The theory also predicted a new regularity, the law of multiple proportions: when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a ratio of small whole numbers [3,5].

Worked example: multiple proportions in the carbon oxides

Carbon monoxide contains 42.88 % C and 57.12 % O by mass; carbon dioxide contains 27.29 % C and 72.71 % O (calculated from the conventional atomic weights Ar(C) = 12.011 and Ar(O) = 15.999 [7]).

  • Mass of O per gram of C in CO: 57.12 / 42.88 = 1.332 g
  • Mass of O per gram of C in CO2: 72.71 / 27.29 = 2.664 g
  • Ratio: 2.664 / 1.332 = 2.000, a small whole number, as required if CO and CO2 contain one and two oxygen atoms per carbon atom.

Dalton also published the first table of relative atomic weights, taking hydrogen as unity [5]. Because he had no independent way of determining molecular formulas, he assumed the simplest possible combinations (for example, water as HO), and several of his values were therefore wrong by integer factors [3]. The difficulty was resolved by Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes of gases and by Avogadro's hypothesis (1811) that equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules, which allowed molecular formulas, and hence consistent atomic weights, to be deduced [3,8]. Avogadro's hypothesis was not widely accepted until Cannizzaro's advocacy at the Karlsruhe Congress of 1860 [3].

What survived and what did not

Postulates 3 and 4 remain valid for ordinary chemical reactions. Postulate 1 requires qualification: atoms are divisible into subatomic particles (Sections 1.3, 1.5, 2). Postulate 2 is false in detail: most elements consist of several isotopes with different masses (Section 2.6), which is why tabulated atomic weights are averages (Section 3.3) [6,7].

1.3 Thomson and the electron

Late nineteenth-century experiments on electrical discharges in evacuated tubes produced "cathode rays", whose nature was disputed: were they waves in the ether or streams of charged particles? In 1897 J. J. Thomson showed that cathode rays carry negative charge, are deflected by both electric and magnetic fields, and behave as streams of particles whose charge-to-mass ratio is independent of the gas in the tube and of the metal used for the cathode [9]. He concluded that these "corpuscles" were a universal constituent of matter.

The crossed-field method

A particle of charge e and mass m moving at speed v through perpendicular electric (E) and magnetic (B) fields experiences no net deflection when the electric and magnetic forces balance:

eE = evB   ⟹   v = E/B

With the speed thus known, the radius of curvature r in the magnetic field alone gives the charge-to-mass ratio:

evB = mv2/r   ⟹   e/m = v/(Br) = E/(B2r)

Thomson's determinations of m/e were of order 10−7 in the electromagnetic units he used, roughly a thousandth of the value for the hydrogen ion in electrolysis [9]. Either the corpuscle carried far more charge than the hydrogen ion or it was far lighter; Thomson argued for the latter [9]. The modern CODATA value of the electron's charge-to-mass quotient is −e/me = −1.758 820 008 38(55) × 1011 C kg−1 [10,11], and the proton is 1836.152 673 426(32) times as massive as the electron [10].

The "plum-pudding" model

If neutral atoms contain negatively charged electrons, they must also contain an equal amount of positive charge. In 1904 Thomson proposed that the electrons are arranged in rings inside a uniform sphere of positive charge, and analysed the mechanical stability of such arrangements, suggesting that the periodic recurrence of stable electron configurations might underlie chemical periodicity [12]. The model made the testable assumption that the positive charge, and with it most of the atom's mass, is spread throughout the atomic volume. That assumption was refuted by Rutherford's scattering experiments (Section 1.5).

1.4 Millikan oil-drop experiment

Thomson's experiment measured only the ratio e/m. Robert Millikan measured the charge itself by observing individual charged oil droplets between horizontal capacitor plates [13].

Principle

A droplet of radius a and density ρ falling under gravity in air of viscosity η quickly reaches a terminal speed vg at which its weight (less buoyancy) is balanced by Stokes drag:

4⁄3πa3(ρ − ρair)g = 6πηavg

This determines the droplet's radius and hence its mass. When a vertical electric field E is applied, a droplet carrying charge q moves upward at a new terminal speed vE, and

q = 6πηa(vg + vE) / E

By ionising the air (with X-rays or radium), Millikan could change the charge on a single drop many times while it remained in view. Every measured charge, and every change in charge, was an integer multiple of a single elementary value [13].

Worked example: inferring the elementary charge

Suppose successive determinations on several droplets give charges of 3.20, 4.81, 8.01 and 11.21 × 10−19 C. Dividing each by 1.602 × 10−19 C gives 2.00, 3.00, 5.00 and 7.00. The data are consistent with integer multiples of a single unit, and the smallest common divisor consistent with all four values is the elementary charge. (These figures are illustrative; Millikan's 1913 analysis used 58 drops [13].)

Millikan's 1913 result was e = 4.774(9) × 10−10 electrostatic units, equivalent to about 1.592 × 10−19 C [13]. This is about 0.6 % below the modern value [10,13]. Combined with e/m, the measurement fixed the electron's mass; combined with the Faraday constant, it gave the Avogadro constant [13].

Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the elementary charge is no longer measured: it is one of the defining constants of the system, fixed at exactly e = 1.602 176 634 × 10−19 C [10,14].

1.5 Rutherford nuclear model

Under Rutherford's direction at Manchester, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden directed α particles (helium nuclei emitted by a radioactive source) at thin metal foils and counted the scintillations they produced on a zinc sulfide screen. In 1909 they reported that about 1 in 8,000 α particles incident on platinum was reflected back through an angle greater than 90° [15].

This result was incompatible with Thomson's atom. A diffuse sphere of positive charge produces only weak electric fields, so an α particle could be turned through a large angle only by the cumulative effect of a very large number of small deflections, whose probability is vanishingly small [16]. In 1911 Rutherford showed instead that the observations follow if the atom's positive charge and nearly all of its mass are concentrated in a very small central region, now called the nucleus, so that a single close encounter produces the large deflection [16].

Treating the α particle (charge 2e) and a nucleus of charge Ze as point charges interacting by Coulomb's law, Rutherford derived the number of particles scattered per unit solid angle [16,17]:

dσ/dΩ = ( zZe2 / (4πε0 · 4Ek) )2 · 1 / sin4(θ/2)

where z = 2 for the α particle, Ek is its kinetic energy and θ the scattering angle. The predicted 1/sin4(θ/2) angular dependence, and the dependence on foil thickness, Z2 and 1/Ek2, were confirmed by Geiger and Marsden's systematic measurements of 1913 [17].

Worked example: an upper bound on nuclear size

In a head-on collision the α particle stops momentarily at the distance of closest approach d, where its kinetic energy equals the Coulomb potential energy:

d = zZe2 / (4πε0Ek)

Using e2/(4πε0) = 1.440 MeV fm [10,17], a 7.7 MeV α particle and gold (Z = 79):

d = (2 × 79 × 1.440 MeV fm) / 7.7 MeV ≈ 30 fm = 3 × 10−14 m

Since pure Coulomb scattering was still observed, the gold nucleus must be smaller than about 3 × 10−14 m, some ten thousand times smaller than the atom (atomic radii are of order 10−10 m) [17].

The nuclear model left a serious problem. According to classical electrodynamics an electron orbiting a nucleus is continuously accelerated and should radiate energy, spiralling into the nucleus in a tiny fraction of a second [6,18]. Atoms are evidently stable, and they emit light only at discrete wavelengths. Resolving this contradiction required quantum theory.

1.6 Bohr model

Hydrogen's visible emission lines had been fitted empirically by Balmer in 1885 [19]; in the form later generalised by Rydberg, the wavenumbers of all hydrogen lines satisfy

1/λ = RH ( 1/n12 − 1/n22 ),    n2 > n1

with n1 = 1 (Lyman series, ultraviolet), 2 (Balmer, visible), 3 (Paschen, infrared), and so on [6]. In 1913 Niels Bohr derived this formula from Rutherford's nuclear atom by introducing quantum postulates [20]:

  1. The electron moves in certain circular orbits, the stationary states, without radiating.
  2. Radiation is emitted or absorbed only when the electron passes between stationary states, as a single quantum of energy hν = |E2 − E1| (drawing on Planck's and Einstein's quantum hypotheses [21,22]).
  3. The allowed orbits are those for which the electron's angular momentum is an integer multiple of ħ = h/2π: mevr = nħ, n = 1, 2, 3, …

Combining the quantisation condition with the balance of Coulomb attraction and centripetal force gives orbit radii and energies for a one-electron atom of nuclear charge Ze [6,20]:

rn = n2a0/Z,     a0 = 4πε0ħ2/(mee2) = 5.291 772 105 44(82) × 10−11 m
En = −Z2 hcR∞ / n2,     hcR∞ = 13.605 693 122 990(15) eV

(numerical values from CODATA 2022 [10]). The Bohr radius a0 and the Rydberg energy remain fundamental scales of atomic physics.

Worked example: the Hα line

The red Balmer line Hα corresponds to the transition n = 3 → 2. With the infinite-nuclear-mass Rydberg energy,

ΔE = 13.6057 eV × (1/4 − 1/9) = 1.8897 eV,    λ = hc/ΔE = 1239.84 eV nm / 1.8897 eV = 656.1 nm

Allowing for nuclear motion replaces R∞ by RH = R∞/(1 + me/mp) = 1.096 776 × 107 m−1, giving λvac = 36/(5RH) = 656.47 nm [6,10], in agreement with the observed vacuum wavelength of about 656.46 nm [23]. The same correction gives the hydrogen ionisation energy 13.598 eV [23].

Independent support came in 1914, when Franck and Hertz showed that electrons colliding with mercury atoms lose energy only in discrete amounts (4.9 eV), demonstrating quantised atomic energy levels directly [24]. In the same period Moseley found that the frequencies of characteristic X-ray lines vary smoothly with an integer, the atomic number, in a way that Bohr's theory accounts for (Section 2.4) [25].

Limitations

The Bohr model gives exact energies for one-electron systems (H, He+, Li2+, …) in the non-relativistic limit, but it fails for atoms with two or more electrons, cannot predict relative line intensities, and does not explain the fine structure of spectral lines or chemical bonding [6,18]. Conceptually, it assigns the electron a definite trajectory, which is incompatible with the uncertainty principle, and it predicts non-zero orbital angular momentum for the ground state of hydrogen, whereas quantum mechanics gives zero [18]. It is best viewed as a transitional theory that introduced quantised stationary states.

1.7 Modern quantum-mechanical model

Wave–particle duality

In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that every particle of momentum p has an associated wavelength λ = h/p [26]. Bohr's quantisation condition then has a natural interpretation: an allowed orbit contains a whole number of electron wavelengths. Davisson and Germer confirmed electron diffraction from a nickel crystal in 1927 [27].

Worked example: de Broglie wavelength of a 54 eV electron

λ = h / √(2meEk) = 1.226 nm / √(Ek/eV) = 1.226 nm / √54 = 0.167 nm

This is comparable to interatomic spacings in crystals, which is why a nickel crystal acts as a diffraction grating for such electrons. Davisson and Germer observed a strong diffraction maximum at 54 eV [27].

The Schrödinger equation and the wavefunction

In 1926 Erwin Schrödinger formulated a wave equation whose stationary solutions reproduce the hydrogen energy levels without ad hoc postulates [28]. For an electron in a potential V:

−(ħ2/2me) ∇2ψ + Vψ = Eψ

Quantisation arises because physically acceptable solutions (single-valued, finite and normalisable) exist only for certain energies [18,28]. Max Born interpreted |ψ|2 as a probability density: |ψ(r)|2dτ is the probability of finding the electron in the volume element dτ at r [29]. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, ΔxΔp ≥ ħ/2, shows that a definite trajectory cannot be assigned to the electron [18,30]. The Bohr orbit is therefore replaced by an orbital: a one-electron wavefunction whose square describes a probability distribution in space.

Quantum numbers

For the hydrogen atom, solutions are labelled by three quantum numbers arising from the radial and angular parts of the equation, plus a fourth for electron spin [6,18]:

Table 1. Quantum numbers of an electron in an atom [6,18,31]
SymbolNameAllowed valuesDetermines
nprincipal1, 2, 3, …shell; energy in hydrogen (En ∝ −1/n2); size
lorbital angular momentum0, 1, …, n − 1 (s, p, d, f, …)subshell; orbital shape; |L| = √(l(l+1)) ħ
mlmagnetic−l, …, 0, …, +lorientation; Lz = mlħ
msspin magnetic+½, −½spin orientation; Sz = msħ

Each shell n therefore contains n2 orbitals and up to 2n2 electrons. An orbital has n − 1 nodes in total, of which l are angular and n − l − 1 are radial [6]. For example, the 1s orbital has no nodes and its radial probability density is greatest at r = a0, so the Bohr radius survives as the most probable electron–nucleus distance in the ground state [6,18]. The 2s orbital has one radial node; each 2p orbital has one angular (planar) node through the nucleus.

Electron spin was proposed by Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit in 1925 to explain the doubling of spectral lines [31], and it arises naturally in Dirac's relativistic theory of the electron (1928) [32].

Many-electron atoms

The Schrödinger equation cannot be solved exactly for atoms with more than one electron, but the orbital approximation, in which each electron occupies a hydrogen-like orbital modified by the screening of the nuclear charge by the other electrons, is the basis of chemical descriptions of atomic structure [6,33]. Screening removes the hydrogenic degeneracy of subshells: within a shell, energies increase in the order s < p < d < f, because s electrons penetrate closer to the nucleus and experience a larger effective nuclear charge [6,33]. Ground-state configurations follow three rules:

  • Pauli exclusion principle: no two electrons in an atom may have the same set of four quantum numbers, so an orbital holds at most two electrons, with paired spins [34].
  • Aufbau (building-up) principle: electrons occupy the lowest available orbitals, in the approximate order 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, … [6,33].
  • Hund's rule: electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly, with parallel spins, before pairing [6,33].

Examples: ground-state electron configurations

  • N (Z = 7): 1s2 2s2 2p3, with the three 2p electrons unpaired in separate orbitals (Hund's rule).
  • Fe (Z = 26): [Ar] 3d6 4s2.
  • Cr (Z = 24): [Ar] 3d5 4s1, and Cu (Z = 29): [Ar] 3d10 4s1. These depart from the simple aufbau order because the 3d and 4s orbitals are close in energy and the resulting configurations are lower in total energy [23,33].

In this model the atom has no sharp boundary; its "size" is defined operationally (covalent, ionic or van der Waals radius). The quantum-mechanical atom accounts for the structure of the periodic table, ionisation energies, spectra, magnetism and, through molecular orbital and valence bond theories, chemical bonding [6,33].

2.1 Protons

The proton is the nucleus of the hydrogen-1 atom. In 1919 Rutherford found that α particles passing through nitrogen produce fast hydrogen nuclei, and concluded that hydrogen nuclei are constituents of other nuclei [36]. This was the first artificially induced nuclear reaction, now written 14N + 4He → 17O + 1H [17]. By 1920 Rutherford was treating the hydrogen nucleus as a fundamental building block of all nuclei [37].

The proton carries a charge of +e, equal in magnitude and opposite in sign to the electron's, and is 1836.15 times as massive as the electron [10]. It is not elementary: it is a bound state of two up quarks and one down quark held together by the strong interaction [35]. Its root-mean-square charge radius is 0.840 75(64) fm [10]. The number of protons in a nucleus defines the atomic number and hence the chemical element (Section 2.4).

2.2 Neutrons

Atomic masses are roughly twice the nuclear charge for light elements, which implied that nuclei contain mass not accounted for by protons. Rutherford speculated in 1920 that a neutral proton–electron combination might exist [37]. In 1932 James Chadwick identified the neutron as the penetrating, uncharged radiation produced when beryllium is bombarded with α particles, 9Be + 4He → 12C + n. He showed that the recoil of hydrogen and nitrogen nuclei struck by the radiation required a neutral particle of mass close to that of the proton, rather than γ rays [38,39].

The neutron is a bound state of one up and two down quarks [35]. It is slightly more massive than the proton (by 1.293 MeV/c2, about 0.14 %) [10]. A free neutron is unstable and β-decays, n → p + e− + ν̄e, with a mean life of about 880 s (just under 15 min) [35]; neutrons bound in stable nuclei do not decay. Neutrons contribute to nuclear binding through the strong force without adding Coulomb repulsion, which is why heavier stable nuclei have progressively more neutrons than protons [17].

2.3 Electrons

The electron, discovered by Thomson (Section 1.3), is an elementary particle: a lepton with no known internal structure [35]. Its charge is −e and its mass 9.109 383 7139(28) × 10−31 kg, about 1/1836 of the proton's [10]. Although electrons contribute less than 0.06 % of an atom's mass, they occupy nearly all of its volume and determine its chemistry: bonding, oxidation states, spectra and ionisation all concern the electrons, particularly the outermost (valence) electrons [6,33]. A neutral atom contains as many electrons as protons.

2.4 Atomic number

The atomic number (proton number), Z, is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom [40,41]. It equals the nuclear charge in units of e, and in a neutral atom it also equals the number of electrons. Atomic number defines the element: every atom with Z = 6 is carbon, whatever its mass.

The physical meaning of Z was established by Moseley in 1913. He measured the characteristic Kα X-ray frequencies of elements from calcium to zinc and found that √ν increases linearly with an integer that rises by one from each element to the next [25]:

√ν ∝ (Z − 1)    (Kα lines)

The (Z − 1) form follows from Bohr's theory for an n = 2 → 1 transition with the nuclear charge screened by the one remaining 1s electron [6,17]. Moseley's law showed that the periodic table should be ordered by nuclear charge rather than atomic weight. It explained the anomalous order of pairs such as Co (Z = 27, Ar 58.93) and Ni (Z = 28, Ar 58.69), and it identified gaps at Z = 43, 61 and 75 for elements then undiscovered [7,25].

2.5 Mass number

The mass number (nucleon number), A, is the total number of protons and neutrons in a nucleus [40,41]:

A = Z + N

where N is the neutron number. A nuclide is written with the mass number as a left superscript and, optionally, the atomic number as a left subscript: AZX, for example 23592U, or more simply 235U or uranium-235 [41].

A is an integer count, not a mass. It is close to, but not equal to, the mass of the atom expressed in daltons; for instance A = 35 for chlorine-35, whose atomic mass is 34.968 852 682 u (Section 3.1) [42].

Worked example: counting nucleons and electrons

  • 5626Fe: 26 protons, 56 − 26 = 30 neutrons, 26 electrons in the neutral atom.
  • 23892U: 92 protons, 146 neutrons, 92 electrons.
  • 3717Cl−: 17 protons, 20 neutrons, 18 electrons.

2.6 Isotopes

Isotopes are nuclides with the same atomic number but different mass numbers; that is, atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons [40]. The term was coined by Frederick Soddy in 1913 (Greek isos topos, "same place") for radioactive species that are chemically inseparable and therefore occupy the same place in the periodic table [43]. In the same year J. J. Thomson's positive-ray analysis showed that neon gives two parabolas, at masses 20 and 22 [44], and Aston's mass spectrograph confirmed that stable elements are commonly mixtures of isotopes [45].

Because chemical behaviour is governed by the electrons, and hence by Z, isotopes of an element have nearly identical chemistry. Small differences in mass do produce measurable isotope effects on vibrational frequencies, reaction rates and equilibria; these are largest for hydrogen, where deuterium is twice as massive as protium [6]. Natural variations in isotopic composition caused by such effects are the reason that some standard atomic weights are given as intervals (Section 3.2) [7].

2.7 Nuclides

A nuclide is "a species of atom, characterized by its atomic number, mass number and nuclear energy state, provided that the lifetime in that state is long enough to be observable" [40]. "Isotope" describes a relationship between nuclides of the same element; "nuclide" names an individual species. Thus 12C, 16O and 235U are three nuclides, while 12C and 13C are isotopes of each other. The NUBASE2020 evaluation gives recommended properties for 3,340 nuclides in their ground states and a further 1,938 long-lived excited (isomeric) states [48].

Nuclides are classified by which of Z, N and A they share [17,40]:

Nuclear isomers illustrate why the definition includes the energy state. Technetium-99m is a metastable excited state of 99Tc that decays to the ground state by γ emission with a half-life of about 6 h, whereas 99Tc itself has a half-life of about 2 × 105 years [48].

Nuclear binding and the mass defect

The mass of a nucleus is less than the sum of the masses of its free nucleons. The difference, the mass defect, corresponds through E = mc2 to the binding energy that holds the nucleus together [17].

Worked example: binding energy of helium-4

Using atomic masses (so that electron masses cancel) [10,42]:

2m(1H) + 2mn = 2(1.007 825 032) + 2(1.008 664 916) = 4.032 979 897 u
m(4He) = 4.002 603 254 u  ⟹  Δm = 0.030 376 642 u
EB = 0.030 376 642 u × 931.494 104 MeV u−1 = 28.30 MeV  (7.07 MeV per nucleon)

The conversion factor 931.494 103 72(29) MeV per dalton is the CODATA 2022 value [10]. Binding energies per nucleon of this order, about a million times larger than chemical bond energies, are why nuclear transformations release so much more energy than chemical reactions [17].

2.8 Ions: cations and anions

An ion is an atomic or molecular particle with a net electric charge [40]. Ions form when atoms or molecules lose or gain electrons; the nucleus, and therefore the identity of the element, is unchanged. The charge number z is written as a right superscript: Na+, Ca2+, Cl−, O2−, SO42− [41]. For a monatomic ion,

number of electrons = Z − z

Cations

A cation is a positively charged ion, formed by loss of one or more electrons [40]. The energy required to remove the outermost electron from a gaseous atom is its first ionisation energy; for sodium,

Na(g) → Na+(g) + e−    Ei = 5.139 eV [23]

Metals, whose valence electrons are weakly bound, typically form cations. Main-group metals often attain the configuration of the preceding noble gas (Na+ and Mg2+ are isoelectronic with Ne) [33]. Transition metals lose their 4s electrons before their 3d electrons, so Fe ([Ar] 3d6 4s2) gives Fe2+ [Ar] 3d6 and Fe3+ [Ar] 3d5 [33]. Successive ionisation energies rise steeply once a noble-gas core is reached, which is why magnesium forms Mg2+ but not Mg3+ in chemical compounds [23,33].

Anions

An anion is a negatively charged ion, formed by gain of one or more electrons [40]. The energy released when a gaseous atom captures an electron is its electron affinity; for chlorine it is about 349 kJ mol−1 (3.61 eV per atom), so the process Cl(g) + e− → Cl−(g) is exothermic [6,33]. Non-metals typically form anions with noble-gas configurations, such as F−, Cl−, O2− and N3−. The second electron affinity of oxygen is negative (O− repels an incoming electron), so O2− is stable in ionic lattices only because of the lattice energy [33].

Ion size

Removing electrons reduces electron–electron repulsion and often empties an outer shell, so cations are smaller than their parent atoms; adding electrons has the opposite effect, so anions are larger. Across an isoelectronic series, radius decreases as nuclear charge increases [33,49]:

3 Atomic Mass

Several related quantities describe the mass of atoms. Using them correctly requires distinguishing between the mass of a particular nuclide, a dimensionless ratio of masses, and an abundance-weighted average [7,41].

3.1 Isotopic mass

The isotopic mass (atomic mass of a nuclide), ma, is the rest mass of a neutral atom of that nuclide in its nuclear and electronic ground state [40,41]. It is usually expressed in daltons (unified atomic mass units). The most accurate values come from Penning-trap and storage-ring mass spectrometry, critically evaluated in the Atomic Mass Evaluation (AME2020) [42].

Isotopic masses are not integers (except for 12C, by definition) for two reasons [17]:

  1. The proton and neutron masses are not exactly 1 u (1.007 28 u and 1.008 66 u respectively), and atoms also contain electrons (0.000 549 u each) [10].
  2. The nuclear binding energy reduces the mass of the nucleus relative to its free nucleons (Section 2.7).

Nuclear physicists express this using the mass excess, Δ = (ma − A u)c2, which is tabulated in AME2020 in keV [42]. For example, the mass excess of 35Cl is negative because its atomic mass, 34.968 852 682 u, is less than 35 u [42].

3.2 Relative atomic mass

The relative atomic mass, Ar, is the ratio of the average mass of the atom to the unified atomic mass constant mu = m(12C)/12 [40,41]:

Ar(X) = ma(X) / mu

It is a dimensionless number. The term atomic weight is an accepted synonym for relative atomic mass of an element, despite the historical use of the word "weight" [7,41]. For a single nuclide, Ar is numerically equal to its isotopic mass in daltons; for example, Ar(35Cl) = 34.968 852 682 [42].

Standard atomic weights

The relative atomic mass of an element depends on its isotopic composition, which varies between natural sources. IUPAC's Commission on Isotopic Abundances and Atomic Weights therefore publishes standard atomic weights, Ar°(E), that apply to all normal terrestrial materials [7]. Since 2009, for elements whose natural variation exceeds the measurement uncertainty, the standard atomic weight has been given as an interval [a, b] rather than a single value with an uncertainty [7,50]. For calculations where a single number is needed, IUPAC also provides abridged and conventional values [7].

3.3 Average atomic mass

For a sample of an element, the average atomic mass is the abundance-weighted mean of the masses of its isotopes [6,41]:

Ar(E) = Σi xi · Ar(iE),    Σi xi = 1

where xi is the amount (number) fraction of isotope i. Note that the weighting uses amount fractions (atom fractions), not mass fractions. Expressed in daltons, this average is the mean mass of one atom of the element; the same number in grams per mole is the element's molar mass (Section 3.4).

Worked example 1: chlorine

Using the isotopic masses and representative abundances in Table 3 [42,46]:

Ar(Cl) = 0.7576 × 34.968 853 + 0.2424 × 36.965 903 = 26.4924 + 8.9605 = 35.453

The result lies within the IUPAC interval [35.446, 35.457] [7]. No chlorine atom has a mass of 35.45 u; the value is an average over a mixture of atoms of mass about 34.97 u and about 36.97 u.

Worked example 2: magnesium (three isotopes)

Isotopic data [42,46]: 24Mg 23.985 042 u (78.99 %), 25Mg 24.985 837 u (10.00 %), 26Mg 25.982 593 u (11.01 %).

Ar(Mg) = 0.7899(23.985 042) + 0.1000(24.985 837) + 0.1101(25.982 593) = 18.9457 + 2.4986 + 2.8607 = 24.305

This agrees with the conventional value 24.305 [7].

Worked example 3: the inverse problem (copper)

Copper has two stable isotopes, 63Cu (62.929 598 u) and 65Cu (64.927 790 u), and Ar(Cu) = 63.546 [7,42]. If x is the fraction of 63Cu:

63.546 = 62.929 598x + 64.927 790(1 − x)  ⟹  x = (64.927 790 − 63.546)/(64.927 790 − 62.929 598) = 0.6915

The result, 69.15 % 63Cu and 30.85 % 65Cu, matches the tabulated representative composition [46,47].

In mass spectrometry, the same principle explains characteristic isotope patterns. A molecule containing one chlorine atom shows two molecular-ion peaks separated by 2 mass units with intensities in the ratio of about 3 : 1 (75.76 : 24.24), a pattern used routinely to recognise chlorine in unknown compounds [6,47].

3.4 Atomic mass unit

The unified atomic mass unit (symbol u), also called the dalton (symbol Da), is defined as one twelfth of the mass of a free carbon-12 atom at rest in its nuclear and electronic ground state [14,41]:

1 u = 1 Da = mu = m(12C)/12 = 1.660 539 068 92(52) × 10−27 kg

(CODATA 2022 [10]). The dalton is not an SI unit but is accepted for use with the SI [14]. Its energy equivalent is muc2 = 931.494 103 72(29) MeV [10]. The dalton is conventionally used for the masses of macromolecules; for example, the kilodalton (kDa) for proteins [14].

History of the scale

Dalton's original scale took hydrogen as 1 [5]. Chemists later adopted natural oxygen = 16, because most elements form oxides and atomic weights were determined by chemical analysis. After the discovery of oxygen isotopes, physicists used a separate scale based on 16O = 16, and the two scales differed by about 0.03 % [3,41]. In 1960–61 the International Union of Pure and Applied Physics and IUPAC agreed on the single scale 12C = 12, which remains in use [41]. The symbol "amu", associated with the older oxygen-based scales, is obsolete; u or Da should be used [41].

Connection to the mole

Before 2019 the mole was defined as the amount of substance containing as many entities as there are atoms in 0.012 kg of carbon-12, so the molar mass of 12C was exactly 12 g mol−1 and the molar mass constant Mu = NAmu was exactly 1 g mol−1 [14]. Since the 2019 SI revision the Avogadro constant is fixed exactly at NA = 6.022 140 76 × 1023 mol−1, and Mu is determined experimentally [10,14]. Its CODATA 2022 value is 1.000 000 001 05(31) g mol−1, equal to 1 g mol−1 to about one part in 109, so for all practical chemistry the numerical value of an atomic weight is still the molar mass in g mol−1 [10,14].

Worked example: from atomic weight to atoms

How many atoms are in 1.000 g of copper (Ar = 63.546)?

n = 1.000 g / 63.546 g mol−1 = 0.015 737 mol
N = nNA = 0.015 737 mol × 6.022 141 × 1023 mol−1 = 9.477 × 1021 atoms

Conversely, the mean mass of one copper atom is 63.546 u × 1.660 539 × 10−27 kg u−1 = 1.0552 × 10−25 kg [7,10].

4 Summary

  • Ancient atomism (Leucippus, Democritus) proposed indivisible particles and void as a philosophical answer to the problem of change; it made no quantitative predictions [1,2].
  • Dalton's theory (1808) made atoms quantitative by assigning each element a characteristic relative weight, explaining the laws of conservation of mass, definite proportions and multiple proportions [3,5].
  • Thomson (1897) showed that atoms contain electrons with a universal charge-to-mass ratio; Millikan (1913) showed that charge is quantised and measured e [9,13].
  • Rutherford (1911) deduced from α scattering that the positive charge and nearly all the mass are concentrated in a nucleus at least ten thousand times smaller than the atom [15,16].
  • Bohr (1913) introduced quantised stationary states, reproducing the hydrogen spectrum; quantum mechanics (1924–1928) replaced orbits with orbitals described by four quantum numbers [20,28,29,34].
  • An atom of element X with atomic number Z and mass number A contains Z protons, A − Z neutrons and, if neutral, Z electrons. Isotopes share Z; ions differ from atoms only in their number of electrons [40,41].
  • Isotopic masses are measured in daltons (1 Da = m(12C)/12); the relative atomic mass of an element is the abundance-weighted mean of its isotopic masses, and IUPAC standard atomic weights are given as intervals where natural variation requires it [7,14,42].
